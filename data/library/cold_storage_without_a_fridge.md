# Cold Storage Without a Fridge

People kept food cold long before refrigerators. The Food Safety and
Inspection Service (FSIS), the US Department of Agriculture's food
safety agency, starts its own history of refrigeration there: "In
prehistoric times, man found that his game would last longer if stored
in the coolness of a cave or packed in snow." "Later, ice was harvested
in the winter to be used in the summer." Root cellars, storage pits,
springhouses and cold porches all come from that long practice.

But "keeping food cold" is two different jobs, and mixing them up is
how people get hurt.

1. **Keeping vegetables and fruit alive.** Potatoes, carrots, cabbages,
   onions, squash and apples are still living tissue after harvest.
   Stored cool, at the right dampness, they keep for months. A cellar,
   a pit or a cold room can do this job well.
2. **Keeping perishable food safe.** Meat, fish, milk, eggs, cooked food
   and leftovers grow bacteria that make people ill, and the bacteria do
   not show. These need a steady 40 F (4 C) or below, and without a
   refrigerator the reliable way to get that is ice: bought, or in
   winter made outdoors.

This guide covers both, and is plain about which methods can do which.
It builds on [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew), which
gives the three storage climates for common crops and the other ways to
keep a harvest (freezing, drying, canning), and on [Telling Spoiled From
Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe), which explains why food that looks
and smells fine can still make you ill.

Its sources are FSIS, the USDA Agricultural Research Service (ARS), the
USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS), a 1960 USDA
bulletin on home storage, the National Oceanic and Atmospheric
Administration (NOAA), the Occupational Safety and Health Administration
(OSHA) and the Agency for Toxic Substances and Disease Registry
(ATSDR), all federal and public domain, and an evaluation of clay pot
coolers by MIT D-Lab, whose facts are restated in our own words. Where
something is general practice rather than a published rule, the text
says so.

## First, the rules that keep people safe

### Perishable food: 40 F, and two hours

FSIS calls the range between 40 F and 140 F (4 C and 60 C) the danger
zone, because bacteria grow fastest there, doubling in number in as
little as 20 minutes. Its rule for perishable food: "Never leave food
out of refrigeration over 2 hours." Above 90 F (32 C) the limit is 1
hour. Cold food is kept at or below 40 F.

You cannot judge this by looking or smelling. FSIS explains why: the
bacteria that cause illness grow quickly in the danger zone but "do not
generally affect the taste, smell, or appearance of a food. In other
words, one cannot tell that a pathogen is present." And cold alone is
not a cure: FSIS notes that "some bacteria such as Listeria
monocytogenes thrive at cold temperatures", multiplying in a
refrigerator over time. Cold storage slows trouble; it does not make
food safe forever.

FSIS's list of foods to throw away after more than 4 hours in a
refrigerator without power is a good list of the foods that need cold.
It includes raw, cooked or leftover meat, poultry, fish and eggs; lunch
meats and hot dogs; casseroles, soups and stews; mixed salads; milk, cream,
yogurt, sour cream and soft cheeses; cut fresh fruit and vegetables and
cooked vegetables; opened juices; creamy dressings; batters and doughs;
custard and cream pies; and garlic stored in oil. Its list of foods that
do not actually need refrigeration includes whole fresh fruit and
vegetables, unopened juices, dried fruit, most baked goods, hard and
processed cheeses, butter, flour and nuts.

**Eggs belong on the first list.** FSIS says eggs should be refrigerated
as soon as possible after they are gathered, and that once cold they need
to stay cold: "A cold egg left out at room temperature can sweat,
facilitating the movement of bacteria into the egg." "Refrigerated eggs
should not be left out more than 2 hours." [Chickens and
Eggs](/library#chickens-and-eggs) covers eggs from your own hens.

### Measure, do not guess

FSIS says to keep an appliance thermometer in a refrigerator and a
freezer, and explains why that kind: "Appliance thermometers are
specifically designed to provide accuracy at cold temperatures." The
same goes for a cellar, a cold room or a cooler. The 1960 USDA bulletin
on home storage asked for a thermometer that records the lowest and
highest temperatures, placed in the coldest part of the cellar, with a
second one outside. A cold storage you have not measured is a guess
(general practice).

### Not in the snow

It is tempting, in a winter power cut, to put food outside. FSIS's
advice is direct: "During a snowstorm, do not place perishable food out
in the snow. Outside temperatures can vary and food can be exposed to
unsanitary conditions and animals." "Instead, make ice." Its method:
fill buckets, empty milk containers or cans with water, leave them
outside to freeze, and bring the ice in to the refrigerator, freezer or
coolers.

### Ice and dry ice

FSIS's emergency advice works for any cooler: pack it with enough ice to
keep the food at 40 F or below, and add more as it melts. A full freezer
holds its temperature for about 48 hours with the door closed, a
half-full one about 24, and a refrigerator keeps food safe for up to 4
hours. For dry ice, it gives a caution in brackets: "Do not touch dry
ice with bare hands or place it in direct contact with food." Dry ice
turns into carbon dioxide gas as it warms, so keep it out of small
closed rooms and cars where people breathe (general knowledge).

### Never taste to decide

"Never taste food to determine its safety." That is FSIS, and it is the
end of every argument about whether something kept cold enough.

### Digging and building below ground

A root cellar or storage pit is a hole in the ground with a roof on it,
and both parts have their hazards.

- **The hole.** Soil walls collapse. OSHA's rule for work in
  excavations requires protection from cave-ins for anyone in an
  excavation 5 feet deep or more unless it is in stable rock, and at less
  than 5 feet only where a competent person finds no sign of a cave-in
  (29 CFR 1926.652(a)(1)). [Square, Level and
  Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb) has more on digging safely, and on
  calling to have buried utilities marked first.
- **The roof.** The 1960 bulletin says it plainly: "The structure of an
  underground cellar must be strong to support the weight of earth over
  the roof." A roof carrying feet of wet soil is structural work for an
  engineer or a builder with a permit (general practice).
- **The air.** Stored produce is alive and breathes out carbon dioxide
  (the USDA handbook below gives each crop's rate), so a closed space
  under the ground full of it can hold bad air (our reading). The 1960
  bulletin gives every storage room and cellar a way to let air in and
  out, and every pit a vent. Open up and air a cellar that has been shut
  before you go in, and never climb into a closed, unventilated pit or
  cellar to retrieve something that fell in. The rules for confined
  spaces, and why rescuers die, are in [Wells and
  Groundwater](/library#wells-and-groundwater).

### Water that is cold is still water

A spring, a stream or a well bucket can keep sealed food cool, but the
water is not clean, and a child or an animal can drown in very little of
it. Keep food in sealed, waterproof containers clear of the water, and
fence or cover anything a child could fall into (general practice).
FSIS's flood advice gives a sense of how far contamination reaches:
food that is not in a waterproof container is thrown away if flood
water may have touched it.

## What produce actually needs

The temperature that keeps a vegetable longest is not the same for every
vegetable. Too cold injures some crops; too warm lets others sprout and
rot. The authority here is the USDA's commercial storage handbook
(Agriculture Handbook 66, revised February 2016), restated below. It is
written for warehouses, but the biology is the same in a cellar.

| Crop | Best storage | Notes from the handbook |
|---|---|---|
| Carrots (topped) | 32 to 34 F (0 to 1 C), very damp (98 to 100 percent humidity) | 7 to 9 months at best; not harmed by cold; turn bitter near ethylene gas |
| Beets (topped) | 33 to 36 F (1 to 2 C), very damp (98 percent) | 4 to 6 months in ventilated storage; trim tops, sort out damaged roots |
| Potatoes for eating | 45 to 50 F (7 to 10 C), after curing | colder storage turns starch to sugar; dark storage is essential |
| Onions (dry) | 32 F (0 C), fairly dry (65 to 75 percent) | damp makes roots grow; warmth makes them sprout |
| Pumpkins and winter squash | 50 to 55 F (10 to 13 C), dry (50 to 70 percent) | chilling injury below 50 F; keep the surface dry |
| Apples | 30 to 39 F (-1 to 4 C), damp (90 to 95 percent), by variety | give off ethylene, a ripening gas |

Four lessons follow (our reading of the table):

- **There is no single "cellar".** Carrots want cold and wet, squash
  wants cool and dry, potatoes want somewhere in between. [Keeping What
  You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) calls these the three climates. Its
  figure for potatoes, from Iowa State University, is 40 F; the USDA
  handbook's warmer range is for potatoes you want to eat without them
  turning sweet. The 1960 bulletin noted the same sweetening, and that a
  week or two in a warm room usually corrects it.
- **Keep fruit and vegetables apart.** The handbook explains ethylene:
  apples make it, and carrots exposed to it turn bitter. The 1960
  bulletin adds the smell: "Apples and pears absorb odors readily and
  should be stored apart from vegetables." "Dairy products and eggs
  readily absorb odors and should not be stored in the same space as
  fruits and vegetables."
- **Keep potatoes in the dark.** The handbook says light, even a week or
  two of dim light, turns potatoes green and builds bitter, toxic
  glycoalkaloids such as solanine, which cooking does little to remove.
  The cure is prevention: darkness, from the day they are dug.
- **Store only sound produce.** The 1960 bulletin's first rules: discard
  anything with decay or serious injury before storing, handle it gently,
  and remove rotting produce as soon as you find it.

## The ways to keep things cold without power

### A cold room or a basement corner

The 1960 bulletin's simplest method is a corner of a basement,
partitioned off and insulated, on the north or east side, with no
heating pipes through it, at least one window or vent to the outside,
and the windows darkened. Shelves and a slatted floor keep containers off
the ground and let air move; a wet floor or damp sawdust raises the
humidity. An unheated garage, porch or spare room can serve the same
way, if a thermometer shows it stays in the right range (general
practice; [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) says the
same).

### A root cellar

A cellar dug into the ground keeps a steadier temperature than anything
above it. Why: the ground's temperature swings less and less with depth.
The NRCS's Soil Taxonomy explains that the daily and yearly swings fade
with depth, that a single reading 6 metres down is within 1 C of the
soil's yearly average, and that the yearly average soil temperature in
much of the United States is commonly estimated as the yearly average
air temperature plus 1 C (2 to 3 C more in some places).

That is both the strength and the limit of a cellar. Deep ground stays
close to the year's average temperature: cool in summer, but no colder
than that average in winter. The cold that brings a cellar down to the
32 to 40 F that roots like comes from outside air, let in at the right
times. The 1960 bulletin's method: open the vents at night and on cool
days when the outside air will cool the produce without freezing it, and
close them in the early morning and whenever it is warmer outside than
in. It says this usually means working the vents every day, and that
the right pattern has to be found for each place.

Its other rules for outdoor cellars: proper drainage, insulation, light
and rodents kept out, the whole structure except the door covered with
earth (2 to 3 feet in the north), and screened air vents to keep out
birds and small animals. A cellar is cleaned out once a year and rotting
produce removed as soon as it is found.

### A pit or clamp

For potatoes, carrots, beets, turnips, parsnips and cabbage, the 1960
bulletin describes a cone-shaped outdoor pit: a layer of straw or leaves
on well-drained ground, the produce piled on it, more straw over it,
then 3 or 4 inches of soil, firmed with the back of a shovel to shed
water, with a drainage ditch around it and a little straw poking through
the top as a vent. Several small pits are better than one big one,
because a pit is best emptied all at once. And: "Pits should be made in
a different place every year to avoid decay from contaminated material
remaining in an old pit." The bulletin does not recommend pits for sweet
potatoes, even in warm climates, because the dampness encourages decay.

### Leaving roots in the ground

Some roots keep where they grew. The 1960 bulletin says parsnips,
salsify and horseradish can stay undug, can stand freezing but not
repeated freezing and thawing, and should be mulched to stay frozen. It
also warns that holding potatoes undug is not advisable where it is hot
and the rain is heavy, because they rot.

### A clay pot cooler

Where the air is hot and dry, evaporating water cools things. MIT
D-Lab's 2018 evaluation of cooling devices in Mali found that clay pot
coolers kept vegetables several degrees cooler than the air (the best
design, a pot set inside a larger pot, about 6.7 C cooler on average)
and very humid inside, which slows wilting, and kept animals and
insects out. Those results were measured with the outside
air below 40 percent humidity and between 29 and 37 C (84 and 99 F), and
the report says these coolers need low humidity, high temperatures,
water, and a shady, well-ventilated place to work.

Two limits follow (our reading, from the figures). A cooler that takes a
95 F day down by about 12 F still holds food near 83 F, so it is a way to
keep vegetables, never a way to keep meat, milk or leftovers safe. And in
a damp climate, or a cold season, it does very little.

### Ice

Ice is the only way on this page to hold perishable food at 40 F or below
in warm weather. A cooler or an insulated box packed with enough ice,
checked with a thermometer and topped up as the ice melts, is FSIS's own
emergency method. In winter, FSIS's advice to make ice outside, rather
than to put the food outside, is the safe way to use the cold.

### Springs and running water

A springhouse lets cold spring water run past sealed containers. Spring
water comes from the ground, and water from any depth tends toward the
ground's yearly average temperature (our reading of Soil Taxonomy's
account of soil temperature). In a place whose yearly average is above
40 F, spring water cannot hold perishable food at FSIS's 40 F (our
reading). It can keep vegetables cool and drinks cold.

## Worked example: cold storage at Silverdale, Washington

The project's canonical place is Silverdale, on Puget Sound (see
[Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa)). Its winters are
mild, and that changes what cold storage can do there.

**The climate.** NOAA's 1991 to 2020 normals for Bremerton, the nearest
full weather station, give an average temperature of 41.1 F in January
and 40.3 F in December, and 41.2 F for December to February as a whole,
with average winter nights at 35.3 F and average January afternoons at
46.6 F. The year's average is 52.3 F.

**What that means for a cellar.** The 1960 bulletin said that elaborate
storage buildings are not practical unless winter temperatures outside
average 30 F or lower, and that home storage is not practical for most
fruit and vegetables unless the storage can be kept below 40 F.
Silverdale's winter averages 41 F. By the bulletin's rule, a cellar here
cannot be kept at 32 F, and holding it under 40 F would take working the
vents to catch the colder nights (our reading). The deep ground is no
help with that: Soil Taxonomy's rule of thumb puts the soil's yearly
average about 1 C above the air's, around 54 F here (our arithmetic).

**What still works.** Read the table again: potatoes for eating keep
best at 45 to 50 F and squash at 50 to 55 F, which Silverdale's mild
winter and cool ground suit well (our reading). Onions and squash want
it dry: the 1960 bulletin sends onions to a dry, well-ventilated attic
or unheated room, not a cellar, and says damp places such as root
cellars should not be used for pumpkins and squash. Carrots and apples,
which want 32 F, will keep here, but not as long; the bulletin says
that when storage cannot be kept near 32 F, storage life is shorter.

**What the ground does in winter.** The soil survey for the common
Alderwood soil around Silverdale records a seasonal perched water table
as shallow as about 65 cm (26 inches), above dense glacial till, and
rates it limited for dwellings with basements because of it (USDA NRCS
soil data, as gathered in the project's locale file). A dug cellar or
pit on such ground can fill with water in the wet season, exactly when
it is full of food (our reading). Look up your own parcel in the NRCS's
Web Soil Survey before digging.

**What does not work here.** Snow, for anything: Bremerton's normals
record about 3 inches of snow a year. A porch as a refrigerator: with
average winter afternoons around 46 F, it is above 40 F much of most
days (our reading of the normals). And a springhouse for milk or meat:
groundwater here sits near the ground's mid-50s F (our reading).

## An old bulletin, read carefully

The 1960 bulletin quoted throughout this guide is a federal publication,
and much of its storage advice agrees with the 2016 handbook, but not
all of it: its 35 to 40 F for potatoes over winter is colder than the
handbook's range for potatoes to eat. The copy on the Internet Archive
carries the National Agricultural Library's stamp: "Do not assume
content reflects current scientific knowledge, policies, or practices."
It means it. The same bulletin recommends treating stored beans with
carbon tetrachloride, a chemical whose uses as a pesticide and a
cleaning fluid, ATSDR says, are now banned because of its harmful
effects. This guide takes only the bulletin's methods for cellars, pits
and storage rooms, and checks its temperatures against the 2016
handbook. Do the same with any old storage guide you find (general
practice).

## Know where your own work stops

- **A cellar dug into the ground, or under a house,** is excavation and
  structural work: a builder or an engineer, a permit, and utilities
  marked before digging (general practice; the cave-in rule above).
- **Any doubt about perishable food that was not kept at 40 F or below**
  ends with FSIS's rule: when in doubt, throw it out.
- **Anyone ill after eating stored food** needs the advice in [Telling
  Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) and, if it is serious,
  a doctor.

Yours to do: choosing the right place for each crop, measuring it with a
thermometer, working the vents, sorting out what is rotting, and keeping
perishable food on ice or not at all.

## Traps

- **"It feels cold in here."** Measure it. A cellar at 45 F is good for
  potatoes and wrong for milk.
- **Food in the snow.** Changing temperatures, dirt and animals. Make
  ice instead.
- **A clay pot cooler for meat.** It cools by degrees, not to 40 F.
- **Apples beside the carrots.** Bitter carrots and tainted apples.
- **Potatoes in the light.** Green potatoes are a toxin, not a colour.
- **A cellar dug into a winter water table.** It floods when it is full.
- **An old guide, followed whole.** Check its chemicals and its numbers.
- **Tasting to decide.** Never.

## How the game models it

The game has spoiling food, and a freezer, but no cold storage.

- **Food spoils on a clock.** Every food item has a shelf life
  (`spoilage_rate_hours` in `data/food_system.ron`, matched to items in
  `data/food/item_profiles.ron`). For the newer foods it is the shortest
  time the USDA's FoodKeeper data gives for that food stored the way it
  normally is, in a refrigerator for perishables and a pantry for the
  rest. The clock runs on game time, so speeding up time spoils food
  faster.
- **Spoiled food makes you ill.** Eating a spoiled item gives a quarter
  of its nutrition and always causes food poisoning
  (`src/systems/food.rs`).
- **Only carried food spoils.** The clock runs on food in an inventory,
  such as your pack. Food you move into one of the home's storage
  vessels from its card, such as a pantry cabinet or the family home's
  Freezer, is not aged at all while it sits there.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: no
temperature anywhere changes how fast food spoils. The food data
describes temperature zones (frozen, cold, cool, room temperature and
hot), each with a spoilage multiplier, but no code uses them yet, so a
cellar, a cold room, snow, ice or a warm kitchen all keep food the
same. The
Freezer is a storage vessel like the others: whether or not it has
power makes no difference to the food in it, and any vessel stops the
clock entirely. There is no root cellar, pit or springhouse to build,
produce has no best temperature or humidity, nothing turns green or
sprouts, and nothing absorbs a smell. The clock is not saved, either:
when the game starts again, food you were carrying starts fresh.

**Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
every session from the default home" is on, which is the default during
development, only your character carries between launches, so the
home's stores, and whatever you were carrying, start each session as
they are in the default home.

## You own this when

- You can say which foods need 40 F or below and which do not, and you
  keep perishables on ice or not at all.
- You follow the two-hour rule, one hour above 90 F, and never taste to
  decide.
- You put a thermometer in every place you store food, and believe it
  over your hand.
- You make ice in a winter power cut instead of putting food in the
  snow.
- You can give the best temperature and dampness for carrots, potatoes,
  onions, squash and apples, and keep fruit away from vegetables.
- You can run a cellar's vents to catch the cold nights, and you keep
  potatoes in the dark.
- You can build a storage pit that sheds water and move it each year.
- You know what your own winter and your own soil allow, and you would
  not dig a cellar into a winter water table or under an unengineered
  roof.
