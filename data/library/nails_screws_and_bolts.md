# Nails, Screws and Bolts

A shelf, a fence, a chicken house and a roof all come down to the same
question: what holds this piece to that one, and will it still be
holding in ten years, in the rain, with a load on it? Often it is the
joint, not the board, that fails. FEMA, looking at wood-framed
buildings after high winds, floods and earthquakes, reports that "structural failures
frequently occur at connections rather than in framing members". The
Forest Products Laboratory's Wood Handbook opens its chapter on
fastenings the same way: the strength and stability of any structure
depend heavily on the fastenings that hold its parts together.

This guide is about choosing and using the common fasteners: nails,
screws, lag screws, bolts, rivets and wall anchors. It covers what each
one is good at, how it fails, and the habits that keep it, and you, in
one piece.

Its facts come from the Wood Handbook (the US Forest Service's reference
on wood as an engineering material), the Army's carpentry manual (FM
5-426), the armed services' joint hand tool manual (TM 9-243), NASA's
Fastener Design Manual, the joint NIOSH and OSHA guide to nail gun
safety, the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) and FEMA. All are
United States government publications. Their own text is in the public
domain; the few parts they credit to someone else (a table in NASA's
manual, material in FEMA's bulletin) are restated here, not quoted.
Where something is general practice rather than a published rule, the
text says so.

Three guides sit beside this one. [Working with
Wood](/library#working-with-wood) sets out the Wood Handbook's
numbers for how much a fastener holds in different woods; they are
summarised here, not repeated. [Hand Tools](/library#hand-tools)
covers the motion of hammering and driving a screw. [Square, Level and
Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb) covers getting the pieces in the right
place before you fasten them.

## First: the ways fastening work hurts people

Before the fasteners, the tools that drive them. These are the hazards
to know before you start.

### Hammering

TM 9-243's warning over its instructions for the carpenter's hammer is
short: "WEAR EYE PROTECTION AND WATCH THE FINGERS." A nail that bends and
flies, or a chip off a hammer face, is what it guards against (our
explanation). The same manual
says never to use a claw hammer on hardened steel-cut or masonry nails,
because the face is too soft and could chip. To start a nail, hold it
about halfway up between thumb and finger, tap it until it stands by
itself, then take your fingers away and drive it; keep the hand that is
steadying the work out of line with the blows. The full technique is in
[Hand Tools](/library#hand-tools).

The Army's carpentry manual adds that nails left sticking out should be
bent over, to prevent damage to materials and injury to people.

### Screwdrivers

TM 9-243's safety rules for screwdrivers include: do not hold the work
in one hand while using the screwdriver with the other, because if the
screwdriver slips out of the slot you are most likely to gash your hand;
do not use a screwdriver for prying, punching, chiselling, scoring or
scraping; and do not use one near a live wire or to find out whether a
circuit is live. Clamp the work instead.

### Wrenches

From TM 9-243's list of wrench safety rules:

- Use a wrench that fits the nut or bolt. A loose fit slips and rounds
  the corners.
- Do not extend the handle to get more leverage. The manual says this
  may damage the wrench or the work.
- Do not strike a wrench with a hammer.
- Pull on the wrench rather than push, where you can, so that your
  knuckles are protected if it slips.
- On an adjustable wrench, do not let the pulling force fall on the
  adjustable jaw.
- For a rusted nut, apply penetrating oil and give it time to soak in.

### Nail guns

A nail gun drives a nail in a fraction of a second with no chance to
change your mind. The joint NIOSH and OSHA guide (2011) says nail guns
are responsible for an estimated 37,000 emergency room visits each
year, that more than half of reported injuries are to the hand and
fingers, and that injuries have
caused blindness, paralysis and death.

What the guide says to do:

- **Use a full sequential trigger.** It fires only when the tip is
  pressed against the work first and the trigger is pulled second, and
  both must be released before the next nail. The guide calls it the
  safest type and reports that the risk of injury with a contact
  ("bump") trigger is twice as high as with a sequential trigger.
- **Keep the hand that holds the work at least 12 inches (30 cm) from
  the nailing point**, or use clamps instead of your hand. Nails can pass
  straight through the wood, or bounce off a knot or metal and fly.
- **Shoot away from your body and away from other people.** In the
  guide's words: "Always shoot nail guns away from your body and away
  from co-workers."
- **Look before you nail.** Check for knots, nails, straps and hangers
  that can deflect a nail, and use a hammer for metal hardware and
  awkward spots.
- **Use your dominant hand only.** The guide says never to use the
  nailer with the non-dominant hand, and to use a hammer if you cannot
  reach the work while holding the nailer in your dominant hand.
- **Disconnect the air** when you leave the gun, climb a ladder or
  stairs, hand it to someone, clear a jam or do any maintenance. A
  cordless nailer has no air line: remove its battery, and its fuel cell
  if it has one, at the same moments (general practice; the guide covers
  only air nailers).
- **Be careful on ladders.** The guide suggests a scaffold instead of a
  ladder where possible. If the work must be done from a ladder, use a
  full sequential trigger, so that bumping your leg on the way up or
  down cannot fire a nail. Keep three points of contact with the ladder
  at all times: holding the nailer in one hand and the work in the
  other leaves only your feet, which is two, so clamp the work instead.
  Place the ladder so you do not reach too far; the guide says your belt
  buckle should stay between the side rails.
- **Never bypass or disable a safety feature**, and keep your finger off
  the trigger while carrying the gun.
- **Wear safety glasses or goggles marked ANSI Z87.1 and hearing
  protection**, and a hard hat on a building site. The guide adds that
  safety shoes help protect your toes.
- **Get any nail gun injury seen by a doctor at once, even one that
  looks minor.** The guide says one in four hand injuries can involve
  damage such as a fracture, that glue, plastic or clothing carried in
  can cause infection, and that barbs on a nail can do more harm if it
  is pulled out the wrong way.

Tools that fire nails into concrete or steel with a gunpowder charge are
a different class. OSHA's construction rule allows only people trained
on the particular tool to operate one (29 CFR 1926.302(e)(1)). At home,
leave them to someone trained.

### What is behind the surface

Before you drive a nail or a screw into a wall, floor or ceiling, think
about what may be inside it: wiring, water pipes, gas pipes and drains
all run there. Ways to find out before you drive anything long enough
to reach them (general practice):

- Look at what is on the wall nearby. Cables and pipes often run
  straight up, down or across from switches, sockets, taps and radiators,
  so keep well clear of the line between them.
- Use a detector that finds studs, pipes and live cables, but treat a
  clear reading as a hint, not proof.
- Where you can, switch off the circuits in that wall at the breaker
  before you drill, and make sure nobody switches them back on while you
  work. [Keeping Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working) explains how
  to make sure a circuit stays off.
- Stop at once if you hit something that does not feel like wood.

Before digging, including for a fence post, see the 811 call in
[Square, Level and Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb).

## Two ways a fastener is loaded

The Wood Handbook says nails in use resist **withdrawal** loads,
**lateral** loads, or a combination of the two, and the same is true of
screws and bolts.

- **Withdrawal** is the load that tries to pull the fastener straight
  out along its length, like a shelf bracket being pulled off a wall.
- **Lateral** load, also called shear, pushes sideways across the
  fastener, like the weight of a board resting on nails driven through
  it into a post.

The difference matters because withdrawal is the less dependable of the
two. The handbook says that variations in the wood, the nail and the
conditions have a more pronounced effect on withdrawal resistance than
on lateral resistance, and that nails in wood that goes through changes
in moisture can lose so much grip that withdrawal resistance is as low
as 25 percent of its value just after driving.

So, where you have the choice, **arrange a joint so the load comes
sideways across the fasteners rather than pulling them out** (our
summary of the handbook's figures; the details of grain direction and
density are in [Working with Wood](/library#working-with-wood)). A
shelf that sits on a cleat screwed to the wall loads the screws
sideways. A shelf hung from screws driven up into it from below loads
them in withdrawal.

The same idea shows up in walls. A CPSC staff study of furniture
anchors (May 2015) found that drywall anchors are strongest when pulled
at an angle parallel to the wall (shear) and weakest when pulled
straight out from it, and that drywall cannot typically withstand the 50
lb pull the voluntary furniture standard required at the time. That is
why, it says,
furniture anchor kits usually come with a wood screw that must go into a
stud rather than a drywall anchor.

## Nails

The Army's carpentry manual calls nails the most common type of metal
fastener, and says they are generally considered the cheapest and
easiest to apply.

### Kinds

From the carpentry manual: **common** nails for rough framing; **box**
nails, slightly thinner, for toenailing and light framing work;
**casing** nails for door and window trim; **finishing** nails and
brads for light trim, easy to set below the surface with a nail set;
**annular** (ring) and **spiral** nails, threaded for more holding power
and good for panelling and plywood floors; **roofing** nails with large
heads; and **duplex** (double headed) nails for temporary work such as
concrete forms, because the second head makes them easy to pull.
Zinc-coated (galvanised) nails are for places where corrosion and
staining matter, which the Wood Handbook says is their main purpose.

### Sizes

In the United States, nail lengths are still given in the old **penny**
system, written with a small d. The carpentry manual's examples are a
6d nail, 2 inches long, and a 10d nail, 3 inches long. The Wood
Handbook's table of common wire nails includes:

| Size | Length | Diameter |
|---|---|---|
| 6d | 2 in (50.8 mm) | 0.113 in (2.87 mm) |
| 8d | 2 1/2 in (63.5 mm) | 0.131 in (3.33 mm) |
| 10d | 3 in (76.2 mm) | 0.148 in (3.76 mm) |
| 16d | 3 1/2 in (88.9 mm) | 0.162 in (4.11 mm) |

The handbook also warns that penny sizes should be used cautiously:
some producers do not keep to these dimensions, so it calls penny sizes
obsolete and recommends giving nails by length and diameter. Box nails
of the same penny size are about the same length but thinner, and
coated and threaded nails are often thinner or slightly shorter. When it
matters, read the length and diameter on the box.

### How long, and how to drive it

The carpentry manual's rules:

- **Length.** A nail should be at least three times as long as the
  thickness of the wood it holds, so that two thirds of its length goes
  into the piece underneath.
- **Angle.** Drive nails at a slight angle toward each other to improve
  their hold.
- **Grain.** Nails driven with the grain do not hold as well as nails
  driven across it.
- **Number.** A few nails of the right type and size, properly placed
  and driven, hold better than a great many driven close together.

**Worked example.** You are nailing a 3/4 inch (19 mm) fence board to a
post. Three times 3/4 inch is 2 1/4 inches, so a 2 1/2 inch nail (8d)
is the shortest size in the table above that meets the rule.
(Arithmetic on the manual's rule.) If the post is treated lumber, as
fence posts often are, the nail should be hot-dip galvanised or
stainless steel; see the section on rust below.

From the Wood Handbook:

- **Edges and ends split.** As a general rule, keep nails no closer to
  the edge of the outer piece than half its thickness, and no closer to
  the end than its full thickness. Smaller nails can go closer.
- **A pilot hole helps.** A hole about 90 percent of the nail's diameter
  gives somewhat greater withdrawal resistance than no hole, and reduces
  splitting, especially in dense wood.
- **End grain holds poorly.** A nail driven into the end of a board
  resists withdrawal at only 50 to 75 percent of its grip in the side.
- **Toenailing** (driving a nail at a slant through the side of one
  piece into another) gives the strongest joint when you use the largest
  nail that will not split the wood, start it about one third of the
  nail's length from the end, drive it at about 30 degrees, and bury the
  whole shank without battering the wood.
- **Clinching** (bending over the point where it comes through) greatly
  increases a nail's hold, but the handbook says it is generally limited
  to boxes, crates and similar containers.

## Screws

The carpentry manual says screws cost more than nails in time and money
but hold better, can be tightened to pull pieces together, look neater,
and can be taken out without damaging the work.

### Pilot holes

The carpentry manual's method uses two holes:

1. In the piece being fastened (the top piece), bore a **pilot hole**
   the diameter of the screw. (In our explanation: the screw then slides
   through the top piece and draws it down, instead of gripping both
   pieces and holding them apart.)
2. In the piece that holds the threads, bore a smaller **starter hole**,
   narrower than the threads, to a depth of one half to two thirds of
   the threaded length.

It says this places the screw accurately, reduces splitting and saves
time. The Wood Handbook's figures for how well a screw holds assume a
starter hole of about 70 percent of the thread's root diameter in
softwoods and about 90 percent in hardwoods, and it recommends rubbing
soap or a similar lubricant on the screw to make
it easier to drive in dense wood, which it says has little effect on how
well the screw holds.

### Driving and tightening

- **Turn a screw in. Never hammer it.** The Wood Handbook says starting
  or driving a screw with a hammer tears the wood fibres and damages the
  threads, seriously reducing what the screw can carry.
- **Match the driver to the screw.** TM 9-243 says to match the size of
  the screwdriver to the job and its type to the head of the screw. A
  tip that does not fit chews the slot.
- **Do not over-tighten in particleboard.** The handbook says to take
  care not to strip the threads when tightening screws in particleboard,
  and that modest tightening is often the best compromise. The same
  holds for soft wood and drywall (general practice).
- **End grain is weaker here too**: screws in end grain average about 75
  percent of their hold in side grain, and their results are erratic.

## Lag screws

A lag screw is a large, heavy screw with a hexagon or square head,
turned with a wrench; the Army calls it a lag bolt, wood-screw type. The
carpentry manual says it is used when an ordinary wood screw would be
too short or too light and a spike would not be strong enough.

The Wood Handbook's rules:

- **Bore the right holes.** The hole for the plain shank should be the
  same diameter as the shank. The hole for the threaded part depends on
  the wood: 40 to 70 percent of the shank diameter for light softwoods
  such as cedars and white pine, 60 to 75 percent for Douglas-fir and
  southern pine, and 65 to 85 percent for dense hardwoods such as oak.
  The smaller figure goes with smaller lag screws.
- **Turn it with a wrench, never drive it with a hammer**, and use soap,
  beeswax or another lubricant in dense wood.
- **Not in end grain.** The handbook says lag screws should not be used
  in end grain, because the wood may split under a sideways load.

## Bolts

The carpentry manual says bolts are used when great strength is needed
or when the work has to be taken apart often.

- **Washers.** A washer between the nut and wood, or under both the head
  and the nut, avoids marring the surface and lets you tighten the bolt
  further, the manual says. Use one under the nut of a carriage bolt.
- **Carriage bolts** have a square, finned or ribbed neck under a round
  head. The neck grips the wood so the bolt cannot turn while you
  tighten the nut. They are chiefly for wood to wood.
- **Machine bolts** have a hexagon or square head and are for close-fit
  work, mostly metal to metal; the hole is bored to the same diameter as
  the bolt.
- **Hole fit in wood.** The Wood Handbook says a hole that is too large
  makes the bolt bear unevenly, and one too small splits the wood. A bolt
  should fit so that it goes in with light taps of a wooden mallet, and
  a smooth hole bears better than a rough one.
- **Spacing.** Along the grain, the handbook puts bolts at least four
  bolt diameters apart, centre to centre, for loads along the grain.
- **Wood shrinks; check the nuts.** Where bolted timber joints with
  connectors are built from green or not fully dried wood, the handbook
  says to inspect them and tighten the bolts as needed while the wood
  dries, normally during the first year.

## How tight is tight

For most household work in solid wood or metal, "firm, then a bit
more" is the instruction, and it is fine (general practice). The
exception is particleboard, soft wood and drywall, where modest
tightening is better, as the section on screws says. Where a
manufacturer gives a tightening torque, as for vehicle wheels, engines and machinery, use a
torque wrench and the manufacturer's number. TM 9-243 describes torque
wrenches as precision instruments that must be calibrated at regular
intervals, and says they are for the final tightening of nuts and bolts.

Why the number matters, from NASA's Fastener Design Manual (1990):

- **A torque figure assumes a friction.** A wrench measures how hard
  the bolt is to turn, not how hard it is clamping (our explanation).
  NASA says the
  friction between threads, and between the nut and the surface under
  it, can vary from 0.04 to 1.10 depending on the materials and
  lubricants, which is why a torque figure is only right for the
  conditions it was worked out for. Oil a thread that the figure assumed
  was dry and the same torque clamps harder (our reading of NASA's
  explanation). Follow the manufacturer's instruction on whether threads
  are oiled.
- **Feel is not a measurement.** A table NASA reproduces from another
  source puts a mechanic's feel at roughly plus or minus 35 percent and a
  torque wrench at roughly plus or minus 25 percent, with more accurate
  methods costing more.
- **More is not safer.** NASA advises against tightening close to the
  bolt's yield point, because a bolt taken past it yields and loses
  its clamping load.
- **A split spring lock washer does not lock.** NASA's manual says the
  common helical lock washer is flat by the time the bolt is fully
  tightened, behaves like a plain washer, and has no locking ability.
  Where a nut must not come loose, it lists other methods: locking nuts,
  cotter pins through castellated nuts, and thread-locking compounds.

## Rivets

A rivet is a permanent fastener, set by deforming its end. NASA's manual
says rivets are cheap, light and quick to install, which is why aircraft
use so many, but that they should not be used in thick materials or
where they would be pulled along their length, because they are much
weaker in tension than in shear. Riveted joints are neither airtight nor
watertight without seals or coatings, and a rivet comes out only by
drilling it out.

## Rust and the wrong metal

Outdoors, the enemy of a fastener is corrosion. FEMA reports that in
coastal areas, corroded connectors and fasteners have been seen to
contribute to the loss of the load path that holds a building together.
Three causes, from FEMA's technical bulletin on corrosion (June 2019),
the Wood Handbook and NASA:

- **Treated lumber eats ordinary steel.** FEMA says the preservative
  chromated copper arsenate (CCA) was removed from most building uses in
  2004, and that some of the formulas that replaced it are more
  corrosive to metal, so makers of several of them specify stainless
  steel or hot-dip galvanised fasteners with a stated minimum coating.
  Wood treated for ground contact carries more chemical and can corrode
  fasteners faster. Read the tag on the treated wood and the
  instructions of the connector maker.
- **Two different metals touching make a battery.** FEMA and NASA both
  describe galvanic corrosion: two dissimilar metals in contact, with
  water present, and one of them corrodes. FEMA says fasteners and
  connectors should be made of similar metals, and says that metal
  connectors and fasteners of the same metal, either hot-dip galvanised
  or stainless, perform better. NASA adds that the metal that corrodes
  should be the larger piece, so plain steel fasteners in a stainless or
  copper assembly are poor practice, while stainless fasteners in a plain
  steel assembly are acceptable.
- **Salt air is worse.** FEMA says corrosion rates are dramatically
  higher in coastal areas, and that studies have shown stainless steel
  and thick hot-dip galvanised (G185 or heavier) connectors and
  fasteners improve corrosion protection.

The Wood Handbook adds which nail metals have performed well in treated
wood, and the list depends on the preservative. Nails of copper,
silicon bronze and 300 series stainless steel have performed well in
wood treated with ammoniacal copper arsenate (ACA) and chromated copper
arsenate (CCA). In wood treated with copper azole or alkaline copper
quaternary (ACQ), it names only 300 series
stainless steel. So read the tag on the wood before you choose the
metal. The handbook also says that a joist hanger and its fasteners
should be the same metal, and that stainless steel, aluminium or
hot-dipped galvanised nails avoid the black staining some woods give
with plain steel. FEMA's last word on the subject applies to every
outdoor joint: whatever metal you choose, inspect it regularly so you
know when it needs replacing.

## Fastening to walls, and stopping furniture falling

Furniture and televisions that tip over are one of the household jobs
where the fastener choice is a safety decision. The CPSC (release 25-409,
29 July 2025) says to anchor TVs and furniture, such as bookcases and
dressers, securely to the wall, and where anchoring is not possible, to
put a TV on a sturdy, low base, pushed back as far as it will go, with
the cable cords kept out of reach. It also says not to store things
children want, such as toys and remotes, where they may climb to reach
them, and to store heavier items on lower shelves. And it says a
federal stability standard for dressers and other clothing storage units
took effect in September 2023.

How to make the anchor actually hold, from the CPSC staff study above.
The study dates from 2015 and describes the voluntary standards as they
were then; the mandatory standard for clothing storage units came later.

- In 2015, the voluntary furniture standard sized the restraint for a
  50 lb pull, which it based on the weight of a large five-year-old
  child (the 95th percentile).
- That standard covered the strap, but the study points out it set no
  strength requirement for the anchor that holds the strap to the wall.
- A short strap pulls the anchor straight out of the wall, the direction
  drywall anchors resist worst, and drywall typically cannot take 50 lb
  that way. So the screw belongs **in a stud**, which is why kits supply
  a wood screw and expect you to find one.

The study was produced by CPSC staff and, it says, was not reviewed or
approved by the Commission. Drive the screw into the stud, not beside it:
a screw that misses the stud and goes only into drywall gives the hold
of drywall (general practice).

## Choosing a fastener

A starting point, summarising the sections above. Where a manufacturer,
a building code or a plan says otherwise, follow it.

| Job | Usual choice | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Framing, fences, crates | Common or box nails; hot-dip galvanised or stainless outdoors and in treated wood | Cheap and fast; loaded sideways, nails hold well |
| Trim and finish work | Finishing or casing nails, or small screws | Small heads that can be set below the surface |
| Plywood floors, panelling | Ring or spiral nails, or screws | Threads resist the loosening that moisture causes |
| Anything you may take apart, or that must pull tight | Screws, in pilot holes | Can be tightened and removed |
| Heavier timber, such as a woodshed frame or a gate post brace | Lag screws or through bolts with washers | Larger diameter for larger loads |
| A joint that must be very strong or taken apart often | Bolts with washers and nuts | The carpentry manual's choice for great strength |
| Thin sheet metal | Rivets or sheet-metal screws | Rivets are permanent and strong in shear |
| Furniture to a wall | The kit's screw into a stud | Drywall alone does not hold the pull |
| Treated lumber or salt air | Hot-dip galvanised or stainless, matching the connectors | Ordinary steel corrodes |

## Know where your own work stops

Fastening a shelf, a fence board or a chicken run is yours to do. Some
joints are not:

- **Anything people stand on, sit under or rely on to hold them up**:
  deck ledgers and railings, stairs, balconies, lofts, roof framing,
  swings and anything hung overhead. These connections are designed to a
  code: the Wood Handbook says its own research figures are often
  modified for safety in design documents, and points to the National
  Design Specification for design criteria. Most places require a permit
  and an inspection for such work (general practice). Get the design
  from a code, a tested product's instructions or an engineer.
- **The metal connectors of a house**: hurricane straps, joist hangers
  and truss plates. FEMA lists them as connectors that matter to the load
  path. Use the exact fasteners the connector maker specifies, never a
  substitute (general practice).
- **Vehicle wheels, brakes and steering.** Use the manufacturer's
  fasteners and torque, or have it done by someone trained (general
  practice).
- **Powder-actuated tools**, which OSHA restricts to trained operators.
- **Anything near gas, electrical panels or wiring you cannot see.**

## How the game models it

The game has fasteners as materials you make and use up.

- **Three kinds, by the box.** A Nail Box holds 100 nails, a Screw
  Box 100 screws, and a Bolt Box 50 bolts (`data/items.csv`).
- **You forge them.** At a forge, Forge Nails turns one iron ingot into
  four boxes of nails, and Forge Screws and Forge Bolts each turn one
  steel ingot into three boxes of screws or two boxes of bolts
  (`data/recipes.csv`). Making them trains Metalworking.
- **Woodwork uses nails; some machines use bolts.** Furniture, doors,
  crates and other woodwork at the workbench take boxes of nails: a
  chair, for example, takes four planks and one box of nails. A few of
  the machines you assemble take bolts: the sawmill takes three boxes,
  and the workbench, anvil station, grain mill and grain silo take them
  too. So do some metal goods, such as a vise, clamps and safes. The
  sawmill recipe trains the Engineering skill.
- **Screws are not used yet.** Boxes of screws can be forged, but no
  recipe in the game uses them.
- **The right tools.** Woodwork at the workbench needs a hammer and a
  hand saw in your backpack, and assembling machines there needs an
  adjustable wrench and a flat screwdriver, as set out in
  `data/crafting/tools.ron`; forging needs a hammer. Each craft wears
  those tools, as [Keeping Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working)
  explains.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** The game starts in the Normal play mode (Settings >
  Gameplay > Play mode), where a craft uses up the boxes of nails or
  bolts it calls for and wears its tools. In Creative, or in Dev if you
  choose it, materials are free while the Creative mode switch on the
  Inventory page is on, which it is unless you turn it off: crafting
  then uses up no boxes of nails or bolts and wears no tools.
- **Starting from the default home.** Settings > Gameplay > "Start every
  session from the default home" is off by default, so boxes you forged,
  and what you used them for, are kept between launches. With the
  setting on, only your character carries between launches, and boxes
  you forged or used start the next session as they were in the default
  home.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
one kind of nail, screw and bolt, with no sizes, lengths, coatings or
metals to choose; no pilot holes; no difference between withdrawal and
sideways loads; no corrosion; and no joint that loosens or fails. In the
real world, all of the choices in this guide are the work.

## You own this when

- You wear eye protection to hammer, keep your fingers out of the line
  of the blow, and clamp work instead of holding it.
- You pull wrenches rather than push them, use one that fits, and never
  lengthen the handle.
- If you use a nail gun, it has a full sequential trigger, you hold it
  in your dominant hand and point it away from yourself and others, your
  other hand is at least 12 inches from the nail, the air (or battery)
  is off before you clear a jam or climb a ladder, and any injury goes to
  a doctor.
- You find out what is inside a wall before you drive into it.
- You can say whether a joint loads its fasteners sideways or pulls them
  out, and arrange it sideways when you can.
- You pick a nail at least three times as long as the board is thick,
  and keep it back from edges and ends.
- You drill pilot holes for screws and lag screws, turn them in, and
  never hammer them.
- You put washers under bolts in wood, and recheck bolts in timber that
  is still drying.
- You use a torque wrench where a manufacturer gives a torque, and you
  know a split lock washer does not lock.
- You use hot-dip galvanised or stainless fasteners with treated wood
  and outdoors, matched to the connectors.
- You anchor tall furniture and TVs to the wall with the screw in a stud,
  and keep heavy things on low shelves and toys and remotes where
  children will not climb for them.
- You know which joints are for a code, a permit or a professional.

- Arranging joints so fasteners are loaded sideways, and the shelf
