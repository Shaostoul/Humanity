# How Animals Work

A goat, a hen, a pig or a deer is doing the same few things all day.
It takes in food and water, turns them into warmth, movement, growth,
milk or eggs, keeps its body at a steady temperature, watches for
danger, and, when the season and its body allow, makes more of its
kind. Every job a keeper does, from carrying water to building a shed
to moving a flock through a gate, is help with one of those things.
Understanding them is what turns a list of rules into common sense.

This guide is about the body and the behaviour of the animals a
homestead keeps, and of the wild animals a forager meets. Most of its
numbers come from the National Range and Pasture Handbook of the US
Department of Agriculture's Natural Resources Conservation Service
(NRCS), whose chapter on livestock nutrition, husbandry and behaviour
(June 2022) is written for people who manage grazing animals. Its
definitions come from federal regulations, and its wildlife and rabies
advice from the National Park Service and the Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention (CDC). Those are United States government works,
in the public domain. A few facts come from university extension
services in Oklahoma, Virginia, Nebraska and Florida; those are
copyrighted, so their facts are restated here in our own words, never
quoted. Where something is general practice or our own reading, the
text says so.

Three other guides sit beside this one. [Keeping Animals](/library#keeping-animals)
turns what is here into the daily work of water, feed, shelter and
fences. [Animal Health and Disease](/library#animal-health-and-disease) is
about noticing when a body stops working and what can pass from
animals to people. [Chickens and Eggs](/library#chickens-and-eggs) covers
the one animal most households start with. The plants on the other end
of the grazing are in [How Plants Work](/library#how-plants-work), and where
animals fit in a wider web of life is in [How an Ecosystem Holds
Together](/library#how-an-ecosystem-holds-together).

## Every animal is running a budget

Food is fuel and building material at once. The NRCS handbook describes
where the energy in grazed plants goes: first to keeping the body
running (breathing, blood flow and the nervous system), then to new
tissue in a growing animal, and then to products such as milk and wool.
It calls the first part maintenance: the amount of feed energy that
leaves the animal neither gaining nor losing, which covers keeping its
body temperature, its basic chemistry and its physical activity.

Only what is left over after maintenance becomes growth, milk, wool or
a healthy pregnancy. That is the single most useful idea in animal
keeping, and the handbook gives several examples of how the balance
shifts:

- **Work and walking cost energy.** An animal walking up a slope spends
  12 to 20 times more energy than one walking on a slope under 15
  percent, according to the handbook, so animals grazing steep or
  widely spread ground need more feed for the same result.
- **Milk is the most expensive thing an animal makes.** The handbook
  says lactation puts more nutritional stress on a mature animal than any
  other stage except heavy, continuous muscular work, and that a high
  producing cow can give so much milk she cannot eat enough to stop
  losing weight.
- **Pregnancy matters most at the end.** For most species, the handbook
  says, energy needs during pregnancy are most critical in the last
  third of the term, and a pregnant animal has a bigger appetite and
  spends longer grazing.
- **Young animals and males differ.** Young animals have a higher basal
  metabolism and need more protein and energy; males have a higher
  basal metabolism than non-pregnant females of the same size; and
  castration lowers it by 5 to 10 percent.

So the same goat needs more and better feed when she is in milk or late
in pregnancy than when she is dry and idle. That is our reading of the
handbook, and it is the reason a feeding plan changes through the year.

### Body condition: the budget you can see

Weight is a poor guide to whether an animal is doing well. The
handbook explains why: the fill of the gut and the products of
pregnancy change the weight without changing how fat or thin the animal
really is. Keepers use a body condition score instead, judged by eye
and by hand over the backbone, ribs, hips and tail head.

The handbook gives the scales. Beef cattle and horses are scored from 1
to 9, sheep and goats from 1 to 5. On the cattle scale, 1 is severely
emaciated, with the bones of the shoulder, ribs, hooks and pins sharp
and easily seen, and 9, rarely seen, is very obese, with movement
possibly impaired by fat. It
recommends a score of 5 or more (at least 14 percent body fat) for a
beef cow at calving and through breeding, and a score of 3 to 3.5 for a
doe going into lactation.

Feel with your hands rather than trusting your eyes, because a thick
winter coat or a full fleece can hide a thin animal (general practice).
Score each animal at the same time every month and write it down, so you
see the trend rather than one number.

## Water comes first

The handbook is blunt: "Animals are more sensitive to the lack of water
than food." When water runs short, it says, the first sign is that the
animal eats less; as the shortage gets worse, weight falls fast and the
body dries out. It counts the loss of 10 percent of the body's water as
severe dehydration, and 20 percent as fatal.

An animal gets water in three ways, the handbook explains: by drinking,
from the water in its food, and from metabolic water made inside the
body as it burns food. Green forage and silage are 70 to 90 percent
water, while hay and concentrates such as grain are only about 7 to 15
percent. So a goat moved from lush spring grass onto dry hay has to
drink a great deal more to take in the same water (our reading). The handbook adds that
burning fat yields about twice the metabolic water of burning
carbohydrate, about 110 grams of water from 100 grams of fat against 55
grams from carbohydrate, and it notes that many desert animals carry
large reserves of fat.

Milk is 80 to 88 percent water, according to the handbook, which is why
an animal in milk needs so much to drink. How much each kind of animal
drinks in a day is set out in [Keeping Animals](/library#keeping-animals).

## Keeping warm, keeping cool

Farm animals, like people, are warm-blooded: their bodies hold a steady
temperature whatever the weather. A body warmer than the air around it
loses heat, and the handbook lists the ways: conduction, convection and
radiation from the skin, and evaporation of water from the skin, the
lungs and the mouth. How fast the heat goes depends on fat under the
skin, skin thickness and hair, and in the handbook's words: "Insulation
benefits are also greatly reduced by air movement or when the body
surface is wet." [Heat and How It Moves](/library#heat-and-how-it-moves)
explains these routes for buildings, and they work the same way on an
animal.

### The comfortable range

Each species has a range of air temperatures in which it keeps warm or
cool without spending extra energy. The handbook calls it the
thermoneutral zone. Inside it, the body adjusts by widening or
narrowing the blood vessels near the skin and by a little sweating.
Below it, the animal shivers to make heat. Above it, the animal pants,
breathes faster and sweats.

The handbook gives the zone for some species, the first four from its
table of typical zones and the rest from its sections on each animal:

| Animal | Comfortable range |
|---|---|
| Cattle | 41 to 68 F (5 to 20 C) |
| Calves | 50 to 68 F (10 to 20 C) |
| Sheep | 70 to 88 F (21 to 31 C) |
| Goats | 50 to 68 F (10 to 20 C) |
| Horse | about 41 to 77 F (5 to 25 C) |
| Foal | about 60 to 72 F (16 to 22 C) |
| Pig | 50 to 75 F (10 to 24 C) |
| Most poultry | 60 to 75 F (16 to 24 C) |

The sheep's range stands out. The handbook says it is almost 20 degrees
higher than for most other farm animals, probably because of the wool,
which insulates the sheep against both heat and cold, and that without
wool a sheep's energy needs would be higher. It gives the body
temperature of a market pig as about 102 F (39 C), and of chickens and
turkeys as 105 to 107 F (41 to 42 C). The Celsius figures are our
conversions. The handbook also notes that heavier horse breeds, with
more body mass and fat, stand cold better than light ones, and light
breeds stand heat better.

### Cold

The handbook says rain, snow and mud cut the time animals spend
grazing, so they eat less, while dry cold tends to make them eat more.
Cold is exactly when an animal below its comfortable range needs more
energy to stay warm (our reading). The handbook's advice for winter
includes giving warm drinking water in cold areas, so the animal does
not have to spend body energy warming icy water. For sheep it adds that
windbreaks, shade and dry ground help them cope with the weather.
Because wet and wind strip away insulation, a windbreak and a dry place
to lie down do more for most livestock in winter than a closed, heated
barn (our reading).

### Heat

Heat is often the greater danger. The handbook explains that when the
air is hotter than the body, "Evaporation is the only way an animal can
cool itself (other than immersion in cool water) if the environmental
temperature exceeds body temperature." High humidity slows evaporation,
so a humid hot day is worse than a dry one, and so is short water. It
reports that animals can eat about half as much as usual in the first 8
days of heat, recovering to about a 10 percent drop after 17 to 24 days
as they adjust.

Two species show how differently animals cope:

- **Pigs.** The handbook says a hot pig eats less, moves less, and looks
  for shade, water and mud to wallow in; a cold pig moves more, huddles
  with others, gets out of the wind and eats more.
- **Poultry.** As the air warms towards 85 F (29 C), the handbook says,
  birds change their behaviour and eat and lay less; towards 100 F
  (38 C), their core temperature rises to lethal levels unless they get
  relief, from shade, ventilation, evaporative cooling, or feeding late
  in the day when it is cooler.

So in a heat wave, shade, moving air and unlimited water are not
comforts but life support (our reading).

## How animals eat

### Ruminants: a fermentation tank on legs

Cattle, sheep and goats cannot digest grass on their own. Federal
regulation (21 CFR 589.2000) defines a ruminant as any member of the
order of animals "which has a stomach with four chambers (rumen,
reticulum, omasum, and abomasum) through which feed passes in
digestion", and names cattle, buffalo, sheep, goats, deer, elk and
antelopes among them. The first chamber, the rumen, holds microbes, and
the NRCS handbook describes those micro-organisms as doing the work of
digesting forage.

That arrangement explains a lot of keeping rules:

- **The microbes need feeding too.** The handbook says: "Protein is
  required by rumen micro-organisms to digest forages; therefore, if
  protein is inadequate, intake will be reduced." An animal on poor,
  dry, low-protein forage can starve with a full belly.
- **Graze, then chew the cud.** Ruminants swallow food quickly and later
  bring it back up to chew again. The handbook gives cattle 4 to 9 hours
  a day of grazing and 4 to 9 of ruminating, and sheep and goats 9 to 11
  hours of grazing, with sheep ruminating 7 to 10. An animal that stops
  chewing its cud has stopped doing something it does for hours every
  day (our reading).
- **Fermentation makes gas.** The Nebraska extension service explains
  that digestion in the rumen makes gas after every meal, and that most
  of it leaves by belching. When it cannot, the rumen swells. That is
  bloat, an emergency covered in [Animal Health and
  Disease](/library#animal-health-and-disease).
- **Old forage is worth less.** As a plant matures, the handbook says,
  its cell walls thicken and it becomes less digestible; and as the
  quality of forage falls, animals eat drastically less of it.
- **Different ruminants suit different feed.** Sheep digest highly
  digestible feed better than cattle, the handbook says, while cattle
  do better than sheep on coarse, fibrous feed.

### Pigs and poultry: one simple stomach

Pigs and chickens are not ruminants, and grass alone cannot keep them.
The handbook says swine and poultry get only about 5 to 20 percent of
their nutrition from forage, and the rest must come from grain or other
feeds. Turning them onto pasture gives
them insects, seeds and exercise, not a full diet.

A bird has no teeth. As [Chickens and Eggs](/library#chickens-and-eggs)
explains from the University of Florida's guide, grinding is done in
the gizzard, a muscular stomach that uses small hard stones, called
grit, which the bird swallows.

### Horses

The handbook groups horses, ponies, mules and donkeys with the
non-ruminants, but unlike pigs and poultry they live mainly on grass
and hay. It says a mature horse that is not working probably needs
little more than pasture and forage, while working, pregnant and
nursing horses need extra energy, protein, vitamins and minerals.

### Choosing what to eat

Grazing animals are choosy. The handbook says herbivores can select a
balanced diet when given choices, and that cattle, sheep and horses
have preferences and find it hard to switch from one kind of vegetation
to another. The Department of Agriculture's Agricultural Research
Service (ARS), which runs a laboratory on plants poisonous to
livestock, adds the catch: livestock eat what tastes good to them, some
poisonous plants taste good, and a hungry enough animal may eat plants
it would normally leave alone. What that means for pasture and feeding
is in [Keeping Animals](/library#keeping-animals).

## What animals sense, and why they act as they do

### Seeing the world as prey

Cattle, sheep, goats and horses are prey animals, and their senses are
built for spotting danger (general knowledge). The Oklahoma State
University extension service
describes cattle: they see in almost every direction without turning
their heads, except directly behind them; they judge depth poorly and
must stop and lower their heads to do it, which is why a shadow or an
unfamiliar object on the ground makes them stop and refuse to move;
their hearing is very sensitive; and they tend to move towards light.

Two consequences matter for anyone working with livestock. The CDC says
to avoid standing directly behind farm animals or approaching them from
the rear, and the blind spot is the reason: an animal that cannot see
you may kick at a sound or a touch it did not expect (that reason is our
reading). And a loud, shouting keeper is far more alarming to an animal
than to a person (our reading of the fact sheet).

### Personal space and the herd

The same fact sheet explains the flight zone: an animal's personal
space. Step inside it and the animal moves away; step back out and it
stops. Its size depends on how tame or fearful the animal is, how you
approach, and how excited it already is, from about 5 to more than 25
feet (1.5 to 7.6 metres) for feedlot cattle, kept in pens close to
people, to as much as 300 feet (91 metres) for cattle raised on open
range. The fact sheet's advice is to avoid approaching cattle head on
and to work at the edge of the flight zone, at 45 to 60 degrees behind
the shoulder, and the cattle will circle away from you.

It also describes the herd instinct. Cattle follow a leader and want to
see others ahead of them, and an animal left alone in a pen may become
agitated and try to jump the fence to get back to its herd. A herd or
flock animal kept on its own is a stressed animal (our reading), which is
one reason [Keeping Animals](/library#keeping-animals) says never to keep
one alone.

### How animals learn

The handbook describes behaviour as a mix of what an animal inherits
and what it learns. Animals are born with instinctive reflexes; they
learn by habituation, coming to respond without thinking to things they
meet again and again; and they learn by trial and error, by reasoning
and by imprinting. It gives two kinds of conditioning, each with a farm
example:

- **Learning by association.** An animal that learns you bring feed
  starts to react the same way when it hears the barn door where the
  feed is kept.
- **Learning by consequence.** An animal that touches an electric fence
  learns to keep away from it.

Both are tools (general practice). Animals that are fed and handled
calmly at the same times each day learn to come when called and to walk
through gates. Animals that are chased and shouted at learn to avoid
you. The Oklahoma fact sheet notes that the size of the flight zone
depends on how fearful and how excited the animal is, so a frightened
herd is a harder herd to move (our reading).

### Habits of the body

The handbook records some everyday habits that make useful benchmarks:
cattle, sheep and goats drop their dung anywhere, cattle 12 to 18 times
a day and horses 5 to 12, and both urinate 7 to 11 times a day. A big
change in how often an animal eats, drinks, dungs or urinates is worth
noticing (our reading).

## Making more animals

Breeding is driven by a cycle in the female. For part of each cycle she
is in heat (oestrus): receptive to the male and able to conceive. The
handbook gives these figures, including the length of the whole cycle,
from the start of one heat to the start of the next:

| Animal | Length of heat | Cycle | Pregnancy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Horse | 6 to 7 days | 22 days | 336 to 340 days in its table; 320 to 380 days in its text on horses, shorter in small breeds |
| Cow | 12 to 18 hours | 19.5 days | 283 days |
| Ewe (sheep) | 29 to 36 hours | 17 days | 142 to 150 days |
| Doe (goat) | 24 to 26 hours | 20 to 22 days | 151 days; about 150 days, roughly 5 months, in its text on goats |
| Sow (pig) | | | about 115 days |

It gives the age of puberty in the United States as the second spring
(as a yearling) for horses, 5 to 13 months for cattle depending on breed
and condition, the first autumn for sheep, and 7 to 8 months for goats;
and says a sow becomes sexually mature at 5 to 6 months. A young male
running with females is a pregnancy waiting to happen once he reaches
that age, so keepers separate young males from females they do not want
bred (general practice).

After birth comes lactation, the most demanding stage, as above. The
handbook says peak milk in a mature cow comes 30 to 45 days after
calving and then slowly tapers off, and that dairy goat breeds such as
the Nubian and Alpine lactate for about 284 days. It notes that horses
commonly live 20 to 25 years and can live past 30.

## Reading the body from outside

A body that is working has a temperature, a pulse and a breathing rate
within a normal range. The Virginia Cooperative Extension gives these
normal adult ranges:

| Animal | Temperature | Pulse (beats a minute) | Breaths a minute at rest |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cattle | 100.4 to 103.1 F (38.0 to 39.5 C) | 40 to 70 | 10 to 30 beef, 18 to 28 dairy |
| Sheep and goats | 102.2 to 104.9 F (39.0 to 40.5 C) | 60 to 90 | 12 to 20 |
| Pigs | 100.4 to 104 F (38.0 to 40.0 C) | 60 to 100 | 8 to 18 |
| Horses | 99.5 to 101.3 F (37.5 to 38.5 C) | 28 to 40 | 8 to 16 |

The Celsius figures are our conversions. Notice that farm animals run
warmer than people, and birds warmer still (above). Exercise, excitement
and hot weather all push these numbers up for a while, so they mean the
most when the animal is calm and at rest. How to take them safely, and
what a reading outside the range means, is in [Animal Health and
Disease](/library#animal-health-and-disease).

## Wild animals

Wild animals have the same bodies but none of the habituation to people
that makes a farm animal manageable, and they defend themselves, their
food and their young.

**Keep your distance.** The National Park Service's rule for Yellowstone
is: "Always stay at least 100 yards (91 m) away from bears, wolves, and
cougars, and at least 25 yards (23 m) away from all other animals,
including bison and elk." It adds that if an animal moves closer, you
back away to keep that distance, and that cow elk protecting calves in
spring (May to early July in Yellowstone) may run at people or kick even
beyond 25 yards. Those are one park's rules; they are a sound minimum
anywhere you meet large wild animals (our reading). Never step between a
mother and her young (general practice).

**Never feed wildlife.** The Park Service says animals that come to
depend on human food may become aggressive towards people and have to be
killed, and that animals carry diseases that can pass to people.

**Strange behaviour is a warning.** The CDC says: "Any mammal can get
rabies, but some are at higher risk." In the United States more than 90
percent of reported rabies cases in animals are in wildlife, and the
wild species that carry it include bats, raccoons, skunks and foxes. Its
advice: "Never approach animals who appear to be injured, sick, or dead,
especially if you see animals during the day who are usually active at
night (bats, raccoons, etc.)"
Call animal control instead. If a wild or unfamiliar animal bites or
scratches you, the CDC says to wash the wound at once with soap and
water for 15 minutes and to talk to a healthcare or public health
professional urgently, because rabies treatment has to start before
symptoms do. [Bleeding and
Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) covers bites in more detail.

[The Species Where You Live](/library#the-species-where-you-live) is where to
learn which animals share your ground.

## Traps

- **"It's eating, so it's fine."** Eating less is often the first sign of
  thirst, heat or illness. Watch for the change, not just the eating.
- **Judging condition by eye.** Hair and wool hide ribs. Use your hands.
- **Thinking grass feeds everything.** It feeds ruminants and horses.
  Pigs and poultry need grain or other feed.
- **Standing where the animal cannot see you.** Directly behind is the
  blind spot, and the kick zone.
- **Chasing.** Pushing deep into a flight zone frightens animals, and a
  frightened animal is hard to move. Work the edge (our reading of the
  Oklahoma fact sheet).
- **One of anything.** A herd animal alone is a stressed animal.
- **Feeding a wild animal.** It teaches the animal to approach people,
  which is how wild animals end up hurting someone and being killed.
- **Touching a sick or dead wild animal.** Leave it, and call animal
  control.

## How the game models it

The game keeps a creature database of 101 species, 51 of them typed as
animals; the others are fantasy creatures, robots and gatherable
resource nodes such as berry bushes and clay pits. It is in
`data/creatures.csv`.

- **What each row records.** A species name, a typical mass, a top speed,
  the ways it can move (walk, run, swim, fly, climb, burrow), a diet
  class (herbivore, omnivore or carnivore), how it behaves (wander, flee,
  hunt and so on), what it drops if it dies, and, for farm animals, what
  you can collect from it alive. The diet column is recorded but nothing
  in the game reads it yet: no animal eats.
- **Size from mass.** Animals are still simple box figures. Each one's
  body is scaled as if its typical mass were water, which gives a scale
  of about 15 centimetres for a hen and 43 for a sheep (`body_side` in
  `src/systems/livestock.rs`).
- **The home's animals.** Three hens, two goats and two sheep live by the
  fields of the home (`data/entities/livestock.ron`). They amble around
  the field they were placed at, at about a third of their species'
  listed speed, and run directly away, at twice that pace, from any
  predator within 12 metres.
- **Predators.** No wild predator is placed in the default world: wolves
  were taken out of the starting area so the home stays safe. In the
  default Dev play mode, with dev cheats on (both are the defaults during
  development), the Dev page under Platform can spawn any species in
  front of you, wolves included, and a spawned hunting species stalks
  prey, the player among them.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** The Dev page and its spawn tool work only in the Dev
  play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode) with the "Enable dev
  cheats" switch on (Settings > Animations). Both are on by default
  during development.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches; the
  home's animals start each session as the game data places them.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: its
animals have no body temperature, no water or feed needs, no digestion,
no senses beyond noticing a predator, no flight zone, no herd instinct,
no learning, no breeding and no ageing. Real animals are all of those
things at once, and everything in this guide is the part the game does
not yet simulate.

## You own this when

- You can explain why a milking or late-pregnant animal needs more and
  better feed than the same animal idle.
- You can score an animal's body condition by hand and keep a monthly
  record.
- You know water matters more than feed, and why an animal on dry hay
  drinks more than one on fresh grass.
- You can name the ways an animal loses heat, and why wind and wet make
  cold worse and humidity makes heat worse.
- You can explain what the rumen does, why ruminants need protein for
  their microbes, and why pigs and poultry cannot live on grass.
- You know where an animal's blind spot is, work at the edge of its
  flight zone, and never keep a herd animal alone.
- You know the length of pregnancy for the animals you keep, and when
  their young males become able to breed.
- You keep the Park Service's distances from wild animals, never feed
  them, and treat a strangely behaving or nocturnal animal seen by day as
  a reason to keep away and call animal control.

  Hubert, D. J. *Cattle Handling Safety in Working Facilities*, BAE-1738,
  published February 2017 (panoramic vision except directly behind; poor
  depth perception and balking at shadows; sensitive hearing; moving
  towards light; the flight zone and its size; working at 45 to 60
  degrees behind the shoulder; the herd instinct and the agitated lone
  animal).
  https://extension.okstate.edu/fact-sheets/cattle-handling-safety-in-working-facilities.html
- Virginia Cooperative Extension. Gregg, C., Clarke, T. and Greiner,
  S. P. *Monitoring Livestock Vital Signs*, APSC-169NP, publication date
  29 May 2025 (normal adult temperature, pulse and resting breathing
  rates by species; activity and heat raising the readings).
  https://www.pubs.ext.vt.edu/APSC/APSC-169/APSC-169.html
- Nebraska Extension. Rasby, R., Waechter-Mead, L. and Redfern, D.
  *Bloat Prevention and Treatment in Cattle*, G2018, revised 2024 (gas
  made in the rumen and normally belched out).
  https://extensionpubs.unl.edu/publication/g2018/pdf/view
- University of Florida IFAS Extension. *Raising Backyard Chickens for
  Eggs*, AN239, 18 January 2024, as cited in Chickens and Eggs (grit in
  the gizzard).
  https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/AN239

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- That a milking or late-pregnant animal needs more and better feed than
  the same animal idle is our reading of the handbook.
- Feeling for condition by hand because coats hide it, and scoring
  monthly, are general practice.
- That an animal moved from grass to hay must drink more is our reading
  of the handbook's water figures.
- That cold is when an animal below its comfortable range needs more
  energy is our reading of the handbook's thermoneutral zone.
- That the blind spot is the reason for the CDC's advice not to stand
  behind animals, and that a frightened herd is harder to move, are our
  reading.
- That a windbreak and a dry bed matter more than a heated barn, and that
  shade, air and water are life support in a heat wave, are our reading.
- That a ruminant which stops chewing its cud is a concern, and that a
  change in dunging or urinating is worth noticing, are our reading.
- That shouting alarms animals, and that a lone herd animal is stressed,
  are our reading of the Oklahoma fact sheet.
- Training animals with calm, regular handling is general practice.
- Separating young males from females is general practice.
- Treating the Yellowstone distances as a minimum elsewhere, and never
  stepping between a mother and her young, are our reading and general
  practice.
- The Celsius and metric conversions are arithmetic.
