# Handling Conflict

Every household, street and work crew has disagreements: the dishes,
the dog that barks at five in the morning, the branch over the fence,
the share of the power bill, who did more on the work day. Most are
settled in a five-minute conversation. A few turn into feuds: years of
not speaking, letters from lawyers, a street that takes sides. The
difference is rarely the size of the problem. It is how the first few
conversations go (our observation, not a published finding).

This guide is about those first conversations, and what to do when they
fail: noticing when you are too angry to talk, and stepping away;
listening until you understand; naming the problem so it can be solved;
writing down what you agree; bringing in a mediator; and recognising
when it is not a disagreement any more but a matter for the police, or
for people trained to help someone who is being abused. The worked
example is a street near Silverdale, Washington, the place the
simulation is built on.

The Humanity Accord's [Conflict Resolution](/library#conflict-resolution)
sets out the principles: use the least harmful method that works,
starting from clearing up the facts and moving through needs, mediation
and separation, and only in rare cases containment. This guide is the
practice of those first steps. It sits beside [Making an Agreement That
Holds](/library#making-an-agreement-that-holds), which covers writing an
agreement clearly enough to settle a later argument; [Organising Shared
Work](/library#organising-shared-work), whose after-action review is a way of
talking about what went wrong without blaming people; [The Law Where You
Live](/library#the-law-where-you-live), for courts and free legal help; and
[Mental Health Under Strain](/library#mental-health-under-strain), for when
someone in the conflict is in crisis.

Its sources are the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services
Administration's anger management manual, the Federal Emergency
Management Agency's (FEMA) course on effective communication and the
Department of Justice, all US federal and public domain; and, for the
worked example, Washington law, the Kitsap County Code, the Kitsap
County District Court, Kitsap 911 and the Dispute Resolution Center of
Kitsap County, restated in our own words. Local details were read on
4 October 2026 and can change. **This guide is not legal advice.**
Where something is general practice or our reading, the text says so.

## First, when it is not a disagreement any more

Some situations look like a quarrel and are something else. Deal with
these before anything below.

- **Violence, threats or a weapon: leave and call 911.** Kitsap 911's
  list of reasons to call includes crimes in progress, life-threatening
  situations, suspicious activity, and dangerous or threatening animals
  (restated). Do not stay to finish the argument (general practice).
- **When one person is afraid of the other.** The Department of
  Justice's Office on Violence Against Women describes domestic violence
  as a pattern of abusive behaviour that one partner uses to gain or keep
  power and control over the other, and lists its forms: physical,
  sexual, emotional, economic, psychological and through technology
  (restated, because that page credits outside organisations for its
  wording). That is not a disagreement between equals, and the tools in
  this guide, which assume two people who can speak freely, are the
  wrong tools for it (our reading). The Accord's Conflict Resolution
  says the same in general terms: "Neutrality in asymmetric conflict
  often favors the powerful." The National Domestic Violence Hotline is
  1-800-799-SAFE (7233), TTY 1-800-787-3224, with chat available around
  the clock, and in immediate danger the call is 911 (Office on Violence
  Against Women, restated).
- **Someone talks about wanting to die.** Stop the argument. [Mental
  Health Under Strain](/library#mental-health-under-strain) says what to do,
  and 988 takes calls and texts at any hour.

## Step away before you say it

Most feuds start with something said in anger that cannot be unsaid
(our observation). The anger management manual published by the
Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration (SAMHSA),
written for counselors who run anger groups, teaches people to watch
their anger rise before it peaks.

**The anger meter.** Rate your anger from 1, completely calm, to 10, the
point where you lose control and something bad follows. The manual's
point is that nobody goes from 1 to 10 instantly, even when it feels
that way: "There is always time, provided one has learned effective
coping skills, to stop anger from escalating to a 10."

**The warning signs.** The manual sorts the signs that anger is
climbing into four kinds:

- **In the body:** a racing heart, a tight chest, feeling hot and
  flushed.
- **In what you do:** clenched fists, pacing, slamming a door, raising
  your voice.
- **In other feelings underneath:** the manual names feeling abandoned,
  afraid, disrespected, humiliated or rejected, among others, as the
  primary feelings that anger often covers.
- **In your thoughts:** reading a comment as an attack, hostile
  self-talk, picturing revenge.

**The timeout.** The manual's basic strategy, recommended for everyone:
"A timeout involves leaving a situation if you feel your anger is
escalating out of control." It can mean stopping a conversation, or
leaving the room. It works better agreed in advance: a formal timeout,
in the manual's words, "requires an agreement or a plan that has been
drawn up in advance" with family, friends or co-workers, so that anyone
can call it, and the person who calls it agrees to come back and either
finish the discussion or postpone it. Use the time to calm down: a walk,
a call to a friend you trust, writing it down (SAMHSA's manual).

Two habits that follow, both general practice: do not send the message
you wrote at midnight, and do not knock on the neighbour's door while
you are still at 7.

## Listen first

Most people in a dispute are waiting for their turn to talk. FEMA's
course on effective communication (independent study course IS-242.c)
lists the techniques of active listening, and they are worth learning
as a set:

- Decide to listen, and concentrate on the speaker.
- "Use your imagination and enter the speaker's situation", their frame
  of reference and point of view.
- Notice the tone of voice, the energy or lack of it, and, face to face,
  the expression and body language.
- "Listen without interruption." Note the key phrases.
- "Use paraphrasing or clarifying questions to confirm that you received
  the intended message."
- Check what you think they are feeling.

Paraphrasing, in FEMA's description, means summarising what the person
said and how they feel, in your own words rather than parroting theirs,
while staying neutral, showing neither agreement nor disagreement. It is
not agreeing. It is proving you heard (our reading). An example of our
own: "So the dog barking at five is waking the baby, and you have not
slept properly in a week. Have I got that right?"

Why it comes first: the Accord's Conflict Resolution starts with clearing
up facts, because many conflicts arise from incomplete or incorrect
information, and it says that if understanding resolves the issue,
nothing more is needed.

## Name the problem

A complaint about a person ("you're so inconsiderate") starts a fight. A
complaint about a problem can be solved (general practice). SAMHSA's
manual gives a five-step Conflict Resolution Model that turns one into
the other:

1. **Identify the problem that is causing the conflict.** "It is
   important to be specific when identifying the problem." Not "the
   noise", but "the band practice in the garage after 10 at night".
2. **Identify the feelings** that go with it: annoyed, frustrated, taken
   for granted.
3. **Identify the impact**: what actually happens because of it. "I'm
   not sleeping before a 6 a.m. shift."
4. **Decide whether to resolve it, or let it go.** The manual puts the
   test as two questions: "Is the conflict important enough to bring up?
   If I do not try to resolve this issue, will it lead to feelings of
   anger and resentment?"
5. **Work for a resolution.** Ask for a time to talk first, because, the
   manual says, you might otherwise raise it when the other person has no
   time for it or is preoccupied with something else. Then describe the
   problem, your feelings and the impact, and ask for what you want.

The manual's distinctions are worth having words for. Aggression aims to
control or hurt; it includes verbal abuse and threats. Passivity lets
your rights be ignored and builds resentment. Passive-aggression refuses
quietly: turning up late, "forgetting". Assertiveness stands up for
yourself while respecting the other person. Its basic message, in the
manual's words: "My feelings, thoughts, and beliefs are important, and
your feelings, thoughts, and beliefs are equally important."

The model helps even when it fails. The manual says it "is useful even
when conflicts are not resolved", because you know you tried to settle
it fairly before doing anything else.

The Accord's second level is the reason this works: separate what each
person is demanding (their position) from what they actually need, look
for the overlap, and avoid outcomes where one side's win is the other's
loss. In its words, "Most durable resolutions occur at this level."

## Find options, and write down what you agree

Once each side understands the other, list the possible ways out before
judging any of them, then pick one you can both live with (general
practice). Write it down in words you could both check later: [Making an
Agreement That Holds](/library#making-an-agreement-that-holds) explains how,
with a worked example of a household's chores. The same method turns
"keep it down" into "no amplified music after 9 p.m. on school nights"
(our example). Put a date on it to look at it again.

If someone has caused harm, the Accord's principle of repair over
retribution applies: acknowledge the harm, make it good where possible,
and fix whatever caused it. An apology that names what happened and
what will change does more than one that does not (our reading).

## When talking does not work: a third person

### Someone you both trust

A respected neighbour, a friend of both, an elder, a faith leader: a
third person can slow the conversation down and keep it fair (general
practice). The Accord's description of good mediation applies to them
too: voluntary, trusted by everyone, about the process and not the
verdict. "Mediators facilitate understanding; they do not impose
outcomes."

### A community dispute resolution center

In Washington, a state law (chapter 7.75 RCW, read 4 October 2026)
sets up community dispute resolution centers as an alternative to court.
Restated:

- A center is run by a county or city, or by a nonprofit whose plan the
  county or city has approved.
- Taking part is voluntary for everyone, and the trained mediators make
  no decisions on the issues; they help the people involved negotiate
  their own resolution.
- Its services are free or charged on a fee based on what you can pay.
- You sign an agreement on how you will try to settle it at the start,
  and a written settlement at the end, which can be used as evidence in
  a later court or administrative case.
- The center's files are confidential, and you can withdraw in writing
  at any point before a settlement, without penalty.
- While the process runs, the deadlines for going to court (statutes of
  limitations) are paused.

Kitsap County's center is the Dispute Resolution Center of Kitsap
County, a nonprofit at 9321 Bay Shore Drive NW in Silverdale, telephone
360-698-0968 (phone hours Monday to Friday, 9 a.m. to 3:30 p.m.). From
its pages (restated):

- It mediates neighbourhood conflicts, landlord and tenant disputes,
  family and workplace conflicts and small claims, among others, and
  says it is one of 19 centers working under the state law.
- You start with an intake call; with your permission, it contacts the
  other party to see whether they are willing to take part.
- A session usually lasts 2 to 3 hours, and fees are on a sliding scale.
- The mediators do not give legal advice or counselling, or tell people
  how to settle; the people in the dispute control the outcome, and
  nobody should sign an agreement they are not satisfied with.
- A written agreement may be signed by everyone and filed with the
  court as a binding contract, and the center advises having a lawyer
  review it first.
- Its list of what makes mediation work includes that everyone agrees
  to mediate, follows ordinary courtesy and is able to make a decision
  and keep to it. Where fear stops someone doing that, see the first
  section of this guide (our reading).

### Tension across a whole community

When a dispute is not between two households but across a town, over
discrimination based on race, colour or national origin, or after mass
violence or civil disorder, the Department of Justice's Community
Relations Service offers mediation, facilitated dialogue, training and
consultation. Its pages say its services are confidential and free to
communities that ask for or accept them, that it works with all sides as
a neutral party, and that it is not an investigating or prosecuting
agency and has no law enforcement authority. Requests go to
202-252-5550 (restated, read 4 October 2026).

### Separation

Sometimes two people cannot share a thing, a house or a project. The
Accord's fourth level is separation: physical distance, new roles,
clear boundaries or dividing what was shared. "Separation is a
resolution, not a failure." Moving out, ending the shared-tool
arrangement or drawing a written line down the shared driveway can end a
conflict that talking could not (our examples).

## When it is about money or property

If the disagreement is over money and talking and mediation have
failed, Washington's small claims courts exist so that people can settle
it without lawyers. From the state law (chapter 12.40 RCW) and the
Kitsap County District Court's small claims page, restated:

- Small claims is a department of the district court. It can award
  **money only**, up to $10,000 when the person suing is an individual
  and $5,000 in other cases. It cannot order your property returned, and
  district courts cannot decide who owns land.
- Lawyers and paralegals may not take part without the judge's
  permission, and the hearing is informal.
- The state filing fee is $35 plus any county surcharge. In Kitsap
  County the fee is $50, and the court's page cites both the state fee
  and the surcharge that state law lets a county add, up to $15 on a
  small claims filing, to fund dispute resolution centers (the
  arithmetic is ours). The court waives fees for people who cannot pay
  and qualify.
- A claim is generally filed in the district court of the county where
  the person you are suing lives, and time limits for bringing a case
  run from one to ten years depending on the kind of claim.
- Court staff may not fill out forms for you or give legal advice.

Once a small claims case has been filed, Kitsap's dispute resolution
center mediates it free of charge, and its mediators are at the
courthouse during small claims sessions; both sides must agree to try
it, and either can still go back to the judge. The state law also lets a
court offer dispute resolution at the first hearing instead of a trial.

Anything about who owns land, where the boundary runs, or a large sum
needs a lawyer; [The Law Where You Live](/library#the-law-where-you-live) says
where free legal help is.

## Neighbours: trees and noise

Two of the commonest neighbour fights have law behind them. Know it
before you act.

**Trees.** In Washington, when someone cuts down, girdles or otherwise
injures a tree or shrub on someone else's land without lawful
authority, a court judgment against them is for three times the damages
(RCW 64.12.030). It drops to the actual damages only in narrow cases,
such as a trespass that was casual or involuntary, or a real reason to
believe the land was their own (RCW 64.12.040). So: do not cut anything
that might be on the other side of the line without the owner's written
agreement, and if nobody knows where the line is, a licensed land
surveyor does (general practice). Both statutes read 4 October 2026.

**Noise.** The Kitsap County Code's noise chapter (chapter 10.28, read 4
October 2026 in the online code) applies in the unincorporated county,
which includes Silverdale. Restated:

- The limits for noise reaching a home are 10 decibels lower between
  10 p.m. and 7 a.m.
- Between 7 a.m. and 10 p.m., the sounds of temporary repair and
  maintenance work on a home and its grounds are exempt, and building
  work is exempt too except at night where it reaches a home.
- Separately, the code forbids public disturbance noises. Its examples
  include frequent, repetitive or continuous loud music from a property,
  naming a band session or a social gathering, that unreasonably
  disturbs the neighbours' peace and rest, and car or portable audio that
  a person with normal hearing can clearly hear 50 feet away in
  residential areas.
- The sheriff's office enforces the public disturbance rule, and for
  most noise only after a complaint from someone who lives or works in
  the area it reaches.

The code is the backstop, not the first move: talk first (general
practice). If a party at 1 a.m. will not stop, that is a non-emergency
call, and Kitsap 911's non-emergency number is 360-328-7711 (the number
from Kitsap 911; that late noise belongs on it rather than on 911 is our
reading). Save 911 for the emergencies in the first section.

Fences are a third flashpoint: whether one needs a permit is in [The Law
Where You Live](/library#the-law-where-you-live).

## Housemates

Sharing a house means daily, small frictions, which is why they are best
handled on a routine rather than in a blow-up (general practice):

- **A short house meeting** at a fixed time, every week or two, with a
  list anyone can add to.
- **A written house agreement** for chores, guests, noise, shared food
  and bills, with a date to review it. [Making an Agreement That
  Holds](/library#making-an-agreement-that-holds) has a worked example of a
  chores rota that anyone can check.
- **A formal timeout rule,** agreed at the first meeting, as in the
  section on stepping away above (SAMHSA's manual).
- **Money in writing.** Who pays what share of which bill, and by when.
  An unpaid share can end up in small claims, but between housemates
  that usually ends the household as well (our reading).
- **Renting.** A dispute with a landlord has its own law. Kitsap's
  dispute resolution center mediates landlord and tenant disputes, and
  the Northwest Justice Project, a legal aid organisation, runs an
  eviction help line at 1-855-657-8387 (both restated, read 4 October
  2026).

If one housemate frightens another, go back to the first section of
this guide.

## Small groups and online spaces

- **Decide how you will decide before the first disagreement.**
  [Making an Agreement That Holds](/library#making-an-agreement-that-holds)
  covers agreements in a group, and the Accord's [Governance
  Models](/library#governance-models) the ways a group can make
  decisions.
- **Review the work, not the people.** The after-action review in
  [Organising Shared Work](/library#organising-shared-work) is built to look at
  what went wrong rather than who.
- **Online.** The Accord's [Communication and
  Association](/library#communication-and-association) says a
  member must always be able to leave a space, that moderation should
  usually warn or limit before removing, and that forming an
  alternative space is legitimate. Leaving a group chat is sometimes the
  Accord's separation, done in one click (our reading).

## Worked example: the alder and the band practice

An illustration with made-up names and details, set west of Silverdale,
in unincorporated Kitsap County.

The Okafors' new garden shed sits near the boundary. A red alder on the
Lees' side leans over it, and in a November storm a branch dents the
shed roof. The Lees' teenager's band also practises in their garage
until about 11 on school nights.

1. **Timeout.** Femi Okafor finds the dent at 7 p.m. and is at 8 on the
   anger meter. He does not go round, and he does not touch the tree: it
   is on the Lees' land, and cutting it without their agreement could
   cost three times the damage (SAMHSA's manual; RCW 64.12.030).
2. **Ask for a time.** Next day he leaves a note asking whether they
   could talk on Saturday morning about the tree and the music (SAMHSA's
   manual).
3. **Listen first.** On Saturday the Lees say the alder worries them
   too, and that the band is the one thing keeping their son off his
   phone. Femi paraphrases before he answers (FEMA).
4. **Name it.** "The branch over the shed dented the roof, and the band
   after 10 means I'm not sleeping before a six o'clock start. I'm
   worried about the next storm, and I'm tired." (SAMHSA's model: the
   problem, the feelings, the impact.)
5. **Options and an agreement.** They agree to share the cost of an
   arborist to take the branches back to the line, the Lees to pay for
   the roof panel, and band practice to end at 9 on school nights. They
   write it on one page with a date to look at it again in spring
   ([Making an Agreement That Holds](/library#making-an-agreement-that-holds)).

Had Saturday gone badly, the next step would have been the Dispute
Resolution Center of Kitsap County: an intake call, the center asking
the Lees whether they would take part, and a session of two or three
hours on a sliding fee. Had the Lees refused to pay for the roof, the
roof panel is a money claim within the small claims limits. And had the
band played at one in the morning, the county's noise rule and the
non-emergency number would have been the backstop, not the front door
(all restated from the sources above).

**Three housemates.** In a rented house in Silverdale, Ana keeps paying
the power bill late and Ben does all the washing up. At the fortnightly
house meeting they name the two problems separately (SAMHSA's model),
agree a rota and a date for transfers to Ana, and add a timeout rule
after one meeting nearly turned into a shouting match. All of it goes
on one page on the fridge.

## Know where your own effort stops

- **Violence, threats and weapons** belong to the police: 911.
- **Abuse** belongs to people trained in it: the National Domestic
  Violence Hotline, and 911 in immediate danger.
- **A mental health crisis** belongs to 988 or 911 ([Mental Health Under
  Strain](/library#mental-health-under-strain)).
- **Who owns what, and where the line runs,** belong to a lawyer and a
  licensed surveyor ([The Law Where You Live](/library#the-law-where-you-live)).
- **Mediation is not therapy and not legal advice.** The Kitsap center
  says so of its own mediators.

Yours to do: noticing your own anger, stepping away, listening,
naming the problem, keeping your word, and knowing who to call.

## Traps

- **"I'll just go round and sort it out now."** At 8 on the meter, you
  will not.
- **"If I ignore it, it will go away."** SAMHSA's manual warns that
  passivity breeds resentment, and you are likely to be angrier next
  time.
- **"It's only a branch."** Cutting a neighbour's tree can cost three
  times the damage in Washington.
- **Bringing friends to back you up.** Sides turn a disagreement into a
  feud (our observation).
- **Talking it out with someone who frightens you.** That is not a
  conflict to mediate; see the first section.
- **Calling 911 for a noisy party.** Use the non-emergency line, and
  talk first.
- **"We agreed, so we don't need to write it down."** The page is what
  you look at when you remember it differently.

## How HumanityOS and the game model it

The game has a list of ways a settlement might settle a dispute, twelve
of them from mediation and arbitration to a popular vote, a coin flip
and a duel, in `data/governance.ron`, alongside templates for laws and
civic roles. No code in the game reads that file yet, so in practice
nothing in the game lets players or characters disagree, and nothing
settles a dispute. The Leadership skill, which this topic belongs to,
appears in the skill list, but nothing in the game levels it yet.

Outside the game, HumanityOS's chat has a Report entry on a message's
menu. It files a report about the sender with the server and tells the
server's admins and moderators who are online; the server accepts only
a few reports from each person per hour. The Governance page is how a
group on a server writes down a decision; [Making an Agreement That
Holds](/library#making-an-agreement-that-holds) describes it. And the Accord's
promise that you can close a contact pathway without asking a moderator
is not yet kept in one respect: a friendship certificate, once given,
cannot be withdrawn. [Where the Software Falls
Short](/library#conformance-gaps) says so plainly.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the
whole of this guide. People, real ones, get angry, misunderstand each
other and make up again, and no bar tells you when.

## You own this when

- You would leave and call 911 at violence, threats or a weapon, and you
  know that when one person is afraid of the other, it is not a conflict
  to mediate.
- You know your own warning signs on the anger meter, and you take a
  timeout before you say the thing you cannot unsay.
- You have a formal timeout rule with the people you live with.
- You can listen without interrupting and paraphrase what you heard,
  feelings included.
- You can name a problem specifically, with its feelings and impact,
  and ask for a time to talk about it.
- You know the difference between aggressive, passive,
  passive-aggressive and assertive.
- You write down what you agree, with a date to review it.
- You know where your local dispute resolution center is and what
  mediation can and cannot do.
- You know what small claims can decide, and what it cannot.
- You would not cut a neighbour's tree, and you know the noise rules
  where you live.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- That feuds turn on the first conversations, that most start with
  something said in anger, and that taking sides turns a disagreement
  into a feud are our observation.
- Not staying to finish an argument when there is violence; not sending
  the midnight message or knocking on the door while still angry;
  complaining about a problem rather than a person; listing options
  before judging them; a trusted third person; asking a surveyor where a
  line runs; talking before invoking the noise code; house meetings,
  written house agreements and money in writing are general practice.
- That this guide's tools are the wrong ones where one person fears the
  other, that fear undermines the conditions for mediation, that
  paraphrasing proves you heard rather than agreeing, that a specific
  apology does more, that a late-night noise complaint belongs on the
  non-emergency line, that an unpaid share in small claims usually ends
  a household, and that leaving a group chat can be the Accord's
  separation are our reading.
- The $15 surcharge inside Kitsap's $50 small claims fee is our
  arithmetic from the court's page and the two statutes it cites.
- The worked example's names, places, times and agreements are made up
  to illustrate the steps.
