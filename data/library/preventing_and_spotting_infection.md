# Preventing and Spotting Infection

Most cuts heal and most colds pass. Skin keeps most germs out, and the
body deals with most of the ones that get in. An infection is what
happens when germs get past those defences and start to grow, and most
infections stay small and go away. A few do not. A scratch from a
blackberry cane turns into a hot, swollen arm; a flu that seemed to be
getting better comes back with a fever and a cough that will not stop;
an infection anywhere in the body turns into sepsis, which the Centers
for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) calls a life-threatening medical
emergency.

This guide is about the space between those two outcomes. It covers how
to keep a cut from becoming infected; how to keep an ordinary illness
from turning into something worse, and from spreading through the house;
the signs that an infection is winning; and when to wait, when to see a
doctor, and when to call 911. It says plainly where your help stops and
trained help takes over. The worked example is set in Silverdale, Kitsap
County, Washington, where the simulation is set.

It leans on other guides and does not repeat them. [Bleeding and
Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) is the companion for every wound in this
guide: how to clean one properly, which wounds must not be closed,
tetanus, the full signs of a wound infection, and the wounds you cannot
manage at home. [How Your Body Works](/library#how-your-body-works) has the
CDC's signs of sepsis and the signs of dehydration. [The Germs That
Matter](/library#the-germs-that-matter) covers stomach bugs and the germs in
food and water. [Handwashing and Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene)
covers the hands. [First Aid Until Help Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives)
covers calling 911. [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns) covers infection
in burns, and [Teeth](/library#teeth) the swelling from a tooth that means an
emergency.

Its sources are the CDC's pages on wounds, skin infections, sepsis, flu,
urinary infections and antibiotics, with NIDDK, the National Institutes
of Health's digestive diseases institute, on when diarrhoea needs a
doctor, all US federal and in the public domain; and, restated in our
own words, the Washington State Department of Health on Vibrio in
Washington's waters and the Kitsap Public Health District's beach
advisories. Where something is general practice or our own reading, the
text says so. This guide does not diagnose anything and recommends no
medicine (it names only what not to give).

## The short version

1. **Wash your hands** before you touch a wound, and before food.
2. **Clean every minor cut or scrape with soap and water,** and keep it
   covered with a clean, dry bandage until it heals. A large or bleeding
   wound is a different job ([Bleeding and
   Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds)).
3. **Look at a wound every day.** Pain and redness should ease, not
   grow.
4. **Keep an open wound out of the sea, lakes, rivers, hot tubs and
   pools** until it heals.
5. **Do not close a dirty wound, a bite or a puncture.** Get it seen.
6. **When you are ill, stay home, cover coughs, rest and drink,** and
   take antibiotics only when a clinician prescribes them.
7. **Know the turn for the worse:** spreading redness, fever, an illness
   that improves and then comes back worse.
8. **Know the emergency.** In someone with an infection, confusion,
   trouble breathing, a racing heart, clammy skin or extreme pain are
   signs of sepsis, which the CDC calls a medical emergency. Get medical
   care at once, and call 911 if the signs are severe or the person is
   confused, struggling to breathe or hard to wake.

## What an infection is

The CDC puts it simply: "When germs get into a person's body, they can
cause an infection. If that infection doesn't stop, it can cause
sepsis."

Germs get in through a small number of doors (our grouping of the CDC
pages below):

- **The skin.** The CDC lists ways the bacteria that cause cellulitis,
  an infection of the deeper layers of the skin, get in, including
  injuries that break the skin, such as cuts, ulcers, bites, punctures,
  tattoos and piercings, and skin conditions such as athlete's foot and
  eczema. Burns and cracked hands are doors too ([Treating
  Burns](/library#treating-burns), [Handwashing and
  Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene)).
- **The mouth,** with food and water: the subject of [The Germs That
  Matter](/library#the-germs-that-matter).
- **The nose and lungs,** with the viruses that cause colds, flu and
  other respiratory illnesses.
- **The urinary tract.** The CDC says urinary tract infections happen
  when bacteria, often from the skin or the rectum, enter the urethra.

The CDC says the infections that lead to sepsis most often start in the
gut, the lungs, the skin or the urinary tract: the same doors.

**Bacteria or virus makes a difference.** The CDC says antibiotics treat
only certain infections caused by bacteria, and do not work on viruses,
such as those that cause colds and runny noses, flu, most sore throats
and most chest colds. Most of what a household catches is viral, and gets
better on its own (our reading of the CDC's list).

## Keeping a cut from becoming infected

[Bleeding and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) covers the cleaning in
depth: why flushing the wound with plenty of clean water is the step that
prevents infection, how much water, and which wounds should not be
flushed or scrubbed. These are the CDC's steps around it.

1. **Clean hands first.** The CDC: wash your hands with soap and water
   before touching any wound, or use an alcohol-based hand rub if you
   cannot wash. Its disaster wound-care sheet adds: avoid touching the
   wound with your fingers, and use disposable gloves if you have them.
2. **Clean the wound.** For minor cuts and scrapes, the CDC says to clean
   them with soap and water. Its disaster sheet says to flood the wound
   gently with bottled water or clean running water, and to clean gently
   around it with soap and clean water. Look for dirt and anything stuck
   in it, without poking around inside ([Bleeding and
   Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) says why).
3. **Cover it.** After cleaning, the CDC says to cover a draining or open
   wound with a clean, dry bandage and change it at least every few days
   until the wound heals. Change it sooner if it gets wet or dirty
   (general practice).
4. **Leave it alone.** The CDC: do not scratch or pick at the scab, and
   do not pick at or pop a sore.
5. **Leave dirty wounds open.** The CDC's disaster sheet: "Leave unclean
   wounds, bites and punctures open. Wounds that are not cleaned
   correctly can trap bacteria and result in infection." [Bleeding and
   Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) explains how closing them can do real
   and lasting harm.
6. **Look at it every day.** The CDC's disaster sheet: "Review the wound
   every 24 hours."
7. **Keep it out of the water.** The CDC says that with an open wound or
   a skin infection, you should avoid hot tubs, natural bodies of water
   such as lakes, rivers and oceans, and swimming pools.
8. **Do not share towels, washcloths or razors.** The CDC says MRSA,
   methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus, can survive on items like
   these for hours, days or even weeks, and cause an infection if it gets
   into a cut or scrape.

**Puget Sound and an open wound.** Vibrio, a bacterium that lives
naturally in coastal salt and brackish water, can infect a wound. The
CDC's advice: if you have an open wound, including a recent surgery
site, piercing or tattoo, stay out of salt water and brackish water if
you can, wading included; cover the wound with a waterproof bandage if it
could meet coastal water, raw seafood or its juices; and wash any wound
with soap and clean running water straight after such contact. Its
reason is stark: "About 1 in 5 people with this infection die,
sometimes within a day or two of becoming ill." That figure is for
Vibrio vulnificus, which the CDC says can need intensive care or the
loss of a limb. The CDC says these steps matter most for people with an
underlying medical condition, especially liver disease. The Washington
State Department of Health says that, as of 2025, no V. vulnificus
infection had been traced to Washington shellfish or to skin contact
with Washington's marine waters, but that other Vibrio species found
here do infect wounds (restated). So a cut from an oyster shell on a
Dyes Inlet beach is the case these steps are for, even though the
deadliest kind has not been recorded here (our reading). Sewage spills
add germs of their own: the Kitsap Public Health District posts swimming
beach and sewage spill advisories, which you can get by email or text
(restated, page read 5 October 2026).

**Wounds to have seen, not just cleaned.** The CDC's disaster sheet says
to seek medical attention as soon as possible if something is embedded
in a wound, if it is an animal bite, or if it is a puncture from a dirty
object, and the CDC says to see a healthcare provider for punctures and
other deep or serious wounds. The full list, from nerve damage to bites
on the hand, is in [Bleeding and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds).

**Tetanus.** A puncture, or a wound with soil, faeces or saliva in it,
may need a tetanus booster: the CDC says to have a health care
professional decide from your own records. [Bleeding and
Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) has the intervals. If you do not know
when your last shot was, that is a reason to ask.

**Feet, if you have diabetes.** The CDC says to check your feet every
day, looking for injuries or signs of infection. [Bleeding and
Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) reports that diabetes was one of the
strongest predictors of a cut becoming infected.

**Burns and teeth** have their own guides: [Treating
Burns](/library#treating-burns) and [Teeth](/library#teeth).

## Keeping an illness from turning into something worse

Most colds, flu and stomach bugs run their course. The work at home is
to help the body do that, to keep the illness from spreading, and to
notice early if it turns.

**Rest, fluids, and the right medicine or none.** For a virus,
antibiotics will not help, and the CDC says to ask a healthcare
professional for tips on how to feel better while your body fights off
the infection. For a stomach bug, drink to replace what is lost ([When
Food or Water Makes You Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick) has
what to give, and when it needs a doctor).

**No aspirin for a child or teenager with flu or chickenpox.** The CDC:
"Aspirin and salicylate-containing products should not be used for
children or adolescents because it may increase the risk for developing
Reye syndrome." The CDC says Reye syndrome happens almost only in
children taking aspirin, mostly with flu or chickenpox, and that it
brings severe vomiting and confusion and can lead to a coma. Check the
labels of cold and flu remedies for aspirin or salicylate (general
practice). Some stomach remedies contain one too, bismuth
subsalicylate, which the CDC also says not to give a child under 12
([The Germs That Matter](/library#the-germs-that-matter)).

**Stay away from other people while you are ill.** The CDC's advice for
respiratory viruses: stay home and away from others, including the
people you live with who are not sick. You can go back to your normal
activities when, for at least 24 hours, your symptoms have been getting
better overall and you have had no fever, and have not been taking
fever-reducing medicine; then take extra care for the next 5 days, with
cleaner air, hygiene, a mask or distance, because you may still spread
the virus. If you get a fever or feel worse after going back, stay home
again.

**The everyday habits.** The CDC's list: clean your hands with soap and
water for at least 20 seconds, or with a sanitiser of at least 60
percent alcohol; cover your mouth and nose with a tissue when you cough
or sneeze; stay home when you are sick; avoid touching your eyes, nose
and mouth with unwashed hands; and avoid close contact with people who
have colds. For a stomach bug, use soap and water, not sanitiser ([The
Germs That Matter](/library#the-germs-that-matter)).

**Vaccines.** The CDC says vaccines can prevent some of the infections
that lead to sepsis, or make them less severe, and lists getting
recommended vaccines, such as the flu vaccine, among its healthy habits.
Which ones you are due for depends on your age, your health and what you
have had before: ask a doctor or pharmacist (general practice).

**Long-term conditions.** The CDC lists taking good care of chronic
conditions, such as diabetes, lung disease, cancer and kidney disease,
among the ways to prevent the infections that lead to sepsis.

**Watch for the turn.** The CDC says most people who get flu recover in
a few days to less than two weeks, but some develop complications, such
as pneumonia. One of its warning signs is a fever or a cough that gets
better and then comes back or gets worse. A urinary infection can be
more serious too: the CDC says a kidney infection is less common than a
bladder infection but more serious, and lists fever, chills, pain in the
lower back or the side of the back, and nausea or vomiting among its
symptoms.

## The signs that it is getting worse

Three levels. The first is yours to watch. The second needs medical care
now: for an infected wound, spreading redness or an infection that is
getting worse, the CDC's word is immediately, and for the urinary and
stomach signs a same-day appointment is the outside limit (our reading).
The third is an emergency.

### Watch at home

A wound that is healing hurts less from one day to the next, and an
illness that is passing gets better overall, day by day (our reading).
[Bleeding and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) gives the pattern that
says otherwise: a wound that hurt on day one, less on day two and more on
day three is telling you something.

### Get medical care now, not tomorrow

- **A wound that looks infected.** The CDC's signs: pain and soreness
  that increase, swelling, redness, draining or oozing, or a fever. Its
  disaster sheet says to seek medical care immediately when a wound
  develops redness, swelling or oozing, and its floodwater page says the
  same. Do not wait for it to clear by itself (our reading).
- **Skin that is red, swollen, warm, tender and painful,** which can be
  cellulitis. The CDC: "Seek medical attention immediately if the red
  area of the skin spreads quickly or you develop a fever or chills." Its
  MRSA page adds that MRSA skin infections are sometimes confused with
  spider bites.
- **A red streak running from a wound, or pain far worse than the wound
  looks.** [Bleeding and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) explains why
  these do not wait until morning.
- **Signs of a urinary infection:** pain or burning when you pass urine,
  needing to go often, blood in the urine, or pressure low in the belly.
  The CDC says to see a healthcare professional for one or more of these,
  or for any symptom that is severe or worrying. The kidney signs above
  make it more urgent (our reading).
- **A stomach bug that needs a doctor:** blood in the diarrhoea;
  diarrhoea for more than 2 days in an adult (NIDDK and the CDC's
  E. coli page; the CDC's general food poisoning list says 3), or for
  more than a day in a baby or child (NIDDK); a fever over 102 F;
  vomiting so often that liquids will not stay down; signs of
  dehydration; and, in pregnancy, a fever with other flu-like symptoms.
  A baby under 12 months with diarrhoea needs a doctor right away
  (NIDDK). [When Food or Water Makes You
  Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick) has the full lists.
- **Any infection that is not getting better, or is getting worse.** The
  CDC's words: "act fast. Get medical care immediately." It suggests
  asking a healthcare provider, "Could this infection be leading to
  sepsis?" and whether you should go to the emergency room.

If you cannot tell how urgent it is, ring your doctor's office or your
health plan's nurse line, if it has one, and describe what you see
(general practice). If what you see is on the next list, do not wait for
a call back.

### Emergency: care at once, and 911 when it is severe

- **Signs of sepsis.** The CDC's list: clammy or sweaty skin; confusion
  or disorientation; extreme pain or discomfort; fever, shivering or
  feeling very cold; a high heart rate or a weak pulse; shortness of
  breath. The CDC says a person with sepsis might have one or more of
  these, so do not wait for several: anyone with an infection who shows
  any of them needs medical care immediately, and the CDC suggests asking
  whether to go to the emergency room. Call 911 if the signs are severe,
  as the CDC's patient sheet puts it in [How Your Body
  Works](/library#how-your-body-works), or if the person is confused,
  struggling to breathe or hard to wake (our reading).
- **Trouble breathing or chest pain** in someone who is ill. The CDC
  says to seek emergency medical care immediately.
- **The CDC's emergency warning signs of flu.** In adults: difficulty
  breathing or shortness of breath; persistent pain or pressure in the
  chest or abdomen; persistent dizziness, confusion or inability to
  wake; seizures; not urinating; severe muscle pain; severe weakness or
  unsteadiness; a fever or cough that improves and then returns or
  worsens; and chronic conditions getting worse. In children it adds
  fast breathing, bluish lips or face, ribs pulling in with each breath,
  dehydration (no urine for 8 hours, a dry mouth, no tears when crying),
  not being alert or interacting when awake, a fever above 104 F that
  fever-reducing medicine does not control, and any fever at all in a
  baby younger than 12 weeks. The CDC says anyone with these signs
  should get medical care right away; for a person who cannot breathe
  properly, who is confused or who cannot be woken, that means 911 (our
  reading, as in [First Aid Until Help
  Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives)).
- **A baby under 3 months with a fever.** Besides the flu list above,
  the CDC's urinary infection page says to talk to a healthcare
  professional right away if a child younger than 3 months has a fever of
  100.4 F (38 C) or higher. Call the doctor now, day or night, and if you
  cannot reach one at once, go to an emergency department. Do not wait to
  see whether it passes (our reading).
- **Swelling of the face, neck or floor of the mouth from a tooth:**
  [Teeth](/library#teeth).

How to make the call, and what to do while you wait, is in [First Aid
Until Help Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives).

### Who needs watching most closely

The CDC says anyone can get sepsis, but that adults 65 or older, children
younger than one, people with chronic conditions such as diabetes or lung
disease, people with weakened immune systems, and women who are pregnant
or have just given birth are at higher risk, and that most people who
develop sepsis have at least one underlying condition or a recent
hospital stay. Its list of people at higher risk from flu adds children
younger than 5, especially those under 2. For these people, act at the
first sign rather than the second (our reading).

## Antibiotics: what they can and cannot do

[Bleeding and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) explains why this
collection never names an antibiotic or a dose. The CDC's rules for the
person taking one:

- **Only when you need them.** They treat certain infections caused by
  bacteria, such as strep throat, whooping cough and urinary tract
  infections, and the CDC says many sinus infections and some ear
  infections get better without them. They do not work on viruses.
- **Exactly as prescribed.** Do not share them, do not save them for
  later, and do not take antibiotics prescribed for someone else. The
  CDC says the wrong medicine for a future illness can delay the right
  treatment and cause severe side effects.
- **Side effects are real.** The CDC lists rash, dizziness, nausea,
  diarrhoea and yeast infections, and, more seriously, C. diff
  infection, which causes diarrhoea that can lead to severe colon damage
  and death, severe allergic reactions, and infections that resist
  antibiotics. Call a healthcare professional about any side effect.
- **Getting rid of unused or expired medicine.** The CDC says to use a
  drug take-back programme, often at a pharmacy, or to mix it in a
  plastic bag with dirt, cat litter or used coffee grounds and put it in
  the household rubbish, and to ask a pharmacist.

## Worked example: a scratch in September, a flu in January

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on.

**The scratch.** On a September Saturday in Silverdale, a man clears
blackberry canes from the edge of his garden and comes away with a deep
scratch along his forearm and a puncture from a thorn near the wrist.

1. **That afternoon.** He washes his hands, flushes both wounds under
   running water, the puncture for at least five minutes, cleans around
   them with soap, and covers them with a clean, dry dressing. He leaves
   the puncture open under the dressing rather than closing it, as
   punctures should be. A puncture is a wound to have seen, and a
   tetanus-prone one, and his last tetanus shot was twelve years ago, so
   he goes to an urgent care clinic that afternoon, where the clinician
   looks at the puncture and settles the booster ([Bleeding and
   Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) has the intervals; going the same day
   is general practice).
2. **Sunday.** The family's plan was a low-tide walk on a Dyes Inlet
   beach. He goes, keeps the dressings on under a waterproof cover, stays
   out of the water, and washes the arm with soap and clean water when
   they get home.
3. **Tuesday evening.** The scratch is fine. The area around the puncture
   is red, warm and swollen, wider than it was in the morning, and it hurts
   more than on Sunday. That is the CDC's picture of a wound infection,
   and the redness is spreading. He goes to an urgent care clinic that
   evening instead of waiting for the weekend.
4. **After.** The clinician prescribes an antibiotic. He takes it exactly
   as prescribed, keeps the dressing clean, looks at the arm every day,
   and knows to go back at once if the redness spreads past the line the
   clinician drew or he gets a fever.

**The flu.** In January his father, 72, catches flu.

1. **The first days.** He stays home, rests and drinks. The family keeps
   him in his own room, washes hands, and covers coughs.
2. **Day five.** He seemed better yesterday. Today the fever is back. A
   fever that improves and then returns is on the CDC's list of
   emergency warning signs for flu, which it says need medical care right
   away, and he is over 65. His son rings the doctor's office, which tells
   them to come in now, and they get ready to go at once.
3. **Minutes later,** before they are out of the door, he is confused
   about where he is, his skin is clammy and he is breathing fast. Those
   are signs of sepsis, and confusion and trouble breathing are reasons
   to call 911, not to drive. His son calls 911, gives the address, and
   does what the call-taker says ([First Aid Until Help
   Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives)).

Notice who made which decision. The family did the cleaning, the
watching and the noticing. The diagnosis, the medicine and the treatment
were the clinicians'.

## Know where your own effort stops

- **Diagnosis and antibiotics** belong to clinicians. This guide
  recommends no medicine, and nothing you can make at home is an
  antibiotic (our reading).
- **Wounds that need a professional:** punctures, bites, deep wounds,
  anything embedded, and the rest of the list in [Bleeding and
  Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds).
- **A wound or skin infection that is spreading, or comes with a
  fever:** medical care straight away.
- **Any sign of sepsis:** medical care at once; and 911 if the signs are
  severe, or there is trouble breathing, chest pain, confusion, or a
  person who cannot be woken.
- **A baby under 3 months with a fever:** the doctor now, day or night,
  or an emergency department.
- **Infections that keep coming back,** or a skin infection in more than
  one person in the house: a doctor, who may look for a cause (general
  practice).

Yours to do: washing hands, cleaning and covering wounds, keeping them
out of the water, staying home when ill, keeping vaccines up to date,
looking every day, and acting early.

## Traps

- **"It's only a scratch."** Punctures and bites look like nothing and
  carry germs deep. Clean them and watch them.
- **"Pour peroxide on it."** Water does the cleaning. [Bleeding and
  Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) explains why: household peroxide has no
  shown healing benefit, and the National Library of Medicine's wound
  care advice keeps peroxide and strong antiseptics out of a wound
  because they can damage the tissue and slow healing.
- **"Glue it shut."** Not a dirty wound, a bite or a puncture.
- **"A swim in the Sound will clean it."** Coastal water carries Vibrio.
  Keep open wounds out of it.
- **"There are antibiotics left over from last time."** The CDC: do not
  save them, and do not take someone else's.
- **"It's a virus, so the doctor can't do anything."** Antibiotics will
  not touch the virus, but a doctor can catch the complications the CDC
  warns about, such as pneumonia.
- **"He was getting better."** A fever or cough that improves and then
  returns is one of the CDC's warning signs.
- **"The fever's gone, so I'm not catching."** You may still spread the
  virus; the CDC's rule is 24 hours of getting better with no fever and
  no fever-reducing medicine, then 5 days of extra care.
- **"Let's see how it is in the morning."** Not with spreading redness,
  a red streak, or any sign of sepsis.
- **"Babies run fevers."** Not under 3 months, as far as the CDC is
  concerned. Call the doctor now.
- **"It's a spider bite."** The CDC says MRSA skin infections are
  sometimes confused with spider bites.

## How the game models it

The game has no infections. Its data describes them, but nothing in the
game gives one to you.

- **A catalogue that is not in play.** The game's medical data describes
  a Deep Laceration that becomes an infected wound if it is not treated,
  a Puncture Wound that leads to a Bacterial Infection, and a Bacterial
  Infection that leads to Sepsis, with treatments such as bandaging and
  a course of medicine (`data/medical.ron`). The system that would apply
  it (`src/systems/medical.rs`) is not registered with the game, and the
  Infected Wound, Infection and Plague conditions in
  `data/status_effects.csv` are not applied by anything either.
- **Medical items treat little yet.** The item list includes Bandage,
  Antiseptic, Painkillers, Antibiotics and Medkit (`data/items.csv`), and
  each has a Use button in the Inventory. Antibiotics end every condition
  the game marks as caused by bacteria (the `bacterial` tag in
  `data/status_effects.csv`), which is three of them: Infected Wound,
  Infection and Plague. They end nothing else. Since nothing gives you
  any of the three, using them keeps them in your pack and says why, and
  they do not touch the game's Food Poisoning, which carries no such tag.
  A Bandage or a Medkit puts back health and would stop Bleeding, which
  nothing gives you either, and Antiseptic does nothing on its own: it
  goes into a Medkit (`data/medical/treatments.ron`,
  `src/systems/treatment.rs`, `src/gui/pages/inventory.rs`).
- **The game brews antibiotics.** The Crafting page has a recipe called
  Culture Antibiotics that makes Antibiotics from Purified Water, Flour
  and Sugar at a Chemistry Set, at Medicine level 4 (`data/recipes.csv`).
  Real antibiotics come from a pharmacy, on a prescription, and the CDC's
  rule is to take them only when needed and exactly as prescribed.
- **Healing is instant.** The First Aid ability puts 35 health back at
  once ([First Aid Until Help Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives)
  has the detail), and the one illness you can catch is Food Poisoning,
  from food ([The Germs That Matter](/library#the-germs-that-matter)).
- **The Medicine skill rises by crafting.** Crafting a recipe trains the
  skill that recipe names, and the medical recipes name Medicine
  (`src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`). The Profile page's "Dev: max skills"
  button, which sets every skill to its maximum, shows only in the Dev
  play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), not in Normal, where the
  game starts (`src/gui/pages/profile.rs`).

**The default settings.** No play mode turns infections on: the medical
system is not running in any of them. Your health and the conditions on
you are saved with your game, and "Start every session from the default
home" is off by default, so the next launch brings them back as you left
them (`src/save_load.rs`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: a wound
that turns bad over two or three days; the redness, the warmth and the
fever that tell you; a flu that comes back worse; sepsis; the decision
to see a doctor, and the one to call 911; and the fact that an antibiotic
is a prescription, not a recipe. In the game, health comes back with a
button. In a real body, it comes back because someone noticed in time.

## You own this when

- You wash your hands before touching a minor wound, clean it with water
  and soap, cover it, and look at it every day.
- You leave dirty wounds, bites and punctures open, and get them seen.
- You keep open wounds out of the sea, lakes, rivers, pools and hot
  tubs, and you know why Puget Sound in particular.
- You know your last tetanus date, or know to ask.
- You stay home when ill until you have had 24 hours of getting better
  with no fever and no fever-reducing medicine, and take care for 5 days
  after.
- You know that antibiotics do nothing for colds and flu, and you never
  take leftovers or someone else's.
- You would never give aspirin, or anything with salicylate in it, to a
  child or teenager with flu or chickenpox.
- You can name the signs of a wound infection and of cellulitis, and
  you know that spreading redness or a fever means medical care straight
  away.
- You can name the CDC's signs of sepsis, you would get medical care at
  once for any of them, and you would call 911 for severe signs, trouble
  breathing or confusion.
- You know that an illness that gets better and then worse is a warning
  sign.
- You would call the doctor at once about any fever in a baby under 3
  months.
- You know who in your household is at higher risk, and you act sooner
  for them.
