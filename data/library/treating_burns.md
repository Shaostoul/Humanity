# Burns

Someone has been burned. Before you read another word, the first minute
matters more than anything else in this guide:

1. **Stop the burning.** Get the person away from the heat. If clothing
   is alight, stop them, get them on the ground, and roll them or smother
   the flames with something heavy. If it is electricity, the power goes
   off before anyone touches them.
2. **Get things off before the swelling starts.** Rings, watch,
   bracelets, belt, boots, and any clothing that is not stuck to the
   skin. Anything still stuck to the skin stays where it is.
3. **Cool the burn under running water.** Twenty minutes, cool not cold.
   No ice, ever.
4. **While the water is running, decide whether this needs a hospital.**
   The list is a few paragraphs down. If you are not sure, it does.

Everything after this is the explanation. Do the four things first.

One honest boundary before we go further. **This guide is not a
substitute for medical care, and it does not qualify you to manage a
serious burn at home.** What it can do is help you act correctly in the
first minutes, tell you which burns you must hand to someone trained,
and stop you doing the things that make a burn worse. Many of the
remedies people reach for instinctively cause real harm, and that is the
single most useful thing in here. The moment a burn is on the list of
burns that need professional care, your job stops being treatment and
becomes getting the person to someone who can treat it.

Its companion, [Building a Fire Staff](/library#fire-staff-materials),
covers the other side of this: how to build a thing that burns, and how
to not get burned by it in the first place.

## Cooling: the numbers, and why ice is not a shortcut

Cool running water is the whole of burn first aid. It is not a comfort
measure. A systematic review in the *Journal of Burn Care & Research*
covering seven studies and 11,383 patients found that people who got
twenty minutes of cool running water within three hours of injury had
"significantly decreased odds of requiring skin grafting or other
surgical intervention." That is the difference between a burn that heals
and a burn that gets operated on.

The numbers:

- **How long.** MedlinePlus says to keep the area under water for at
  least 5 to 30 minutes. The burn-care guidance summarised by CADTH is
  firmer, calling for at least twenty minutes for any major burn or burn
  over 10 percent of the body. Twenty minutes is the number to aim at.
- **How cool.** The 2024 international consensus on second-degree burn
  wounds recommends running water between 12 and 25 degrees Celsius,
  roughly 54 to 77 Fahrenheit. CADTH's summary cites 15 degrees Celsius,
  about 59 Fahrenheit. As a rule of thumb, an ordinary cold tap is
  usually in that band already, which is exactly why this works without
  a thermometer.
- **How soon.** Sooner is better, but the window is wider than people
  assume. The studies that found the grafting benefit measured cooling
  begun within three hours of injury. If you did not cool it at the time,
  it is still worth doing.

**Ice and iced water are harmful, and this is not a small point.** The
2024 consensus is explicit about the mechanism: ice cubes are cold enough
to cause "vasoconstriction of the wound blood vessels, leading to
ischaemic necrosis," and also carry a risk of frostbite and hypothermia.
Read that plainly. The burn has already damaged the tissue, and the blood
supply is what will repair it. Ice clamps that blood supply shut. You can
take living tissue at the edge of a burn and kill it with an ice pack.
The CADTH summary gives the same two reasons, hypothermia and impaired
blood flow, and says ice or ice water should not be used. MedlinePlus
lists ice among the things never to put on a burn.

There is one more wrinkle worth understanding, because it looks like a
contradiction. MedlinePlus says not to put a severe burn into cold water,
because doing so can cause shock. Both things are true. Running cool water
over a burn is right. Immersing a large burn, or a whole person, in cold
water is not, because burned skin loses heat fast and a cold person is a
new emergency on top of the burn. Military burn guidance notes that
casualties with burns over 20 percent of the body are at high risk of
hypothermia and says plainly to keep the patient warm.

So for a large burn: cool the burned area with running water, then cover
the person and keep them warm. Cool the burn, warm the patient.

## Call for help now if any of these are true

MedlinePlus says to call emergency services if:

- The burn is large, about the size of your palm or bigger
- The burn is full thickness
- You are not sure how serious it is
- The burn was caused by chemicals or electricity
- The person shows signs of shock
- The person breathed in smoke
- Physical abuse is the known or suspected cause
- There are other symptoms alongside the burn

Notice the third one. "You are not sure how serious it is" is on the
official list, in those words. You are allowed to not know.

## How deep is it

Three depths, described by what you can actually see and feel. These
descriptions come from US military burn care guidance and from the
National Library of Medicine, and they agree with each other.

**Superficial, also called first degree.** The outer layer of skin only.
Red, painful, swollen. It does not blister, and it blanches readily,
meaning the redness goes pale when you press it and floods back when you
let go. Sunburn is the everyday example.

**Partial thickness, also called second degree.** Outer and underlying
layers. Red, moist, swollen, blistered, and it still blanches. Military
guidance describes these as "moist and sensate," meaning they hurt, and
they hurt a lot. A shallower partial thickness burn is red and wet and
extremely painful. A deeper one turns paler, drier, mottled white, and
blanches less and hurts less.

**Full thickness, also called third degree.** All the layers of skin,
sometimes deeper. It looks leathery and dry, white or brown or blackened.
It does not blanch. And here is the thing nobody expects:

> **The deepest burns can hurt the least.** Military guidance describes
> full thickness burns as "insensate," and the National Library of
> Medicine describes them as causing little or no pain, with skin that
> may be numb.

The nerve endings that report pain are in the skin. A burn deep enough to
destroy them destroys their ability to complain. So "it does not hurt
much" is not reassurance. On a burn that is white, leathery, dry and
numb, it is the finding that tells you this is the worst kind.

Two practical consequences. A big burn is usually a patchwork of all
three depths, so judge the worst part, not the average. And a burn often
declares itself over the first day or two, so the depth you see in the
first hour is a first guess, not a verdict.

## How big is it

Two methods, one for small burns and one for large.

**The rule of palm.** The flat of a hand is about 1 percent of that
person's body. Federal emergency medicine guidance gives the palmar hand
surface as approximately 1 percent of total body surface area, stated
there for a child. Published clinical teaching gives the finer version
for patients generally, separating the palm alone, about 0.5 percent,
from the whole hand including the fingers, about 1 percent. Use the
injured person's own hand, not yours, because the whole point is that it
scales with the body you are measuring, and MedlinePlus uses exactly this
as the household threshold: a burn about the size of your palm or larger
means call for help.

**The rule of nines.** For larger burns, the body is divided into
regions worth 9 percent or a multiple of it. For an adult:

| Region | Percent |
|---|---|
| Head and neck | 9 |
| Each arm | 9 |
| Front of the trunk | 18 |
| Back of the trunk | 18 |
| Each leg | 18 |
| Genitals | 1 |

Those add to 100. It is a rough field estimate, deliberately coarse, and
in hospital it gets refined against a proper chart.

**Children are not small adults, and the rule of nines does not fit
them.** Federal guidance notes that children have proportionally larger
heads, up to 20 percent, and smaller legs, 13 percent in infants. This is
one of several reasons a child's burn is not something to size up by eye
at home.

**The counterintuitive rule: superficial burns do not count.** Military
guidance states that first degree burns are not included in the estimate
of burn size at all. Only partial thickness and full thickness are
counted. So a whole sunburned back is a 0 percent burn by this
measurement, which sounds absurd until you realise that what is being
measured is how much skin has stopped working, and a first degree burn
has not stopped working.

**The size thresholds that matter.** The American Burn Association is
the authority here, and its criteria are copyrighted, so this is a
summary in our own words rather than their text. A published consensus
study that updated those criteria, in the *Journal of Burn Care &
Research*, puts the transfer thresholds at full thickness burns over
5 percent of the body, and partial thickness burns over 10 percent.
Below those, it still recommends outpatient burn referral rather than
nothing, and recommends specialist consultation for any burn that might
be deep, at any size.

Translated for a household: a partial thickness burn bigger than about
ten palms, or any full thickness burn bigger than about five, is a
hospital problem. In practice you will not be measuring. MedlinePlus
gives the household version, which is that one palm or larger means call.

## The burns that need professional care whatever their size

Size is only one of the reasons a burn goes to a specialist. These go
regardless of how small they look. The list is drawn from the American
Burn Association's referral criteria, restated here, and from the
published consensus study that updated them.

- **Face.** Also because of what it implies about the airway.
- **Hands.** Scarring across a hand costs function permanently.
- **Feet.**
- **Genitals and perineum.**
- **Anything over a joint**, for the same reason as hands. Healing burn
  tissue contracts as it matures, and a contracture across a joint locks
  it.
- **Circumferential burns**, meaning a burn that goes all the way around
  a limb, a finger, the neck, or the chest. Military guidance explains
  why: a deep circumferential burn to an arm or leg can act "like a
  tourniquet, reducing arterial circulation," and around the chest it can
  physically stop the ribcage expanding. The burned skin is stiff, the
  limb underneath swells, and the pressure has nowhere to go. This needs
  a surgeon, sometimes urgently.
- **Every chemical burn.** The consensus criteria say all chemical
  injuries should be cared for in a burn center.
- **Every electrical burn.** High voltage and lightning injuries go
  straight in; even low voltage injuries warrant follow-up, because some
  of the damage shows up late.
- **Any inhalation injury**, or a good reason to suspect one.
- **Burns in young children and in older adults.** The published
  criteria treat both groups as benefiting from specialist involvement
  beyond what the size thresholds alone would trigger, and the American
  Burn Association's guidance is that paediatric burns generally warrant
  referral. As a rule of thumb for a household: have a child's burn or an
  older person's burn looked at even when you would shrug off the same
  burn on yourself. The reasons are real. The same patch of skin is a
  bigger fraction of a smaller body, older skin is more fragile, and
  older adults are already the group most at risk around fire. The US
  Fire Administration recorded adults aged 65 and over as having 2.5
  times the risk of dying in a fire compared with the population as a
  whole in 2020.
- **Any burn on someone with other significant health problems**, or who
  was injured in some other way at the same time.

## Smoke, enclosed spaces, and why the airway comes first

A burn that happened in an enclosed space is an airway emergency until
proven otherwise, even if the skin looks fine.

The blunt number, from MedlinePlus: "Over half of deaths from fires are
due to inhalation injuries." Not the flames. The air.

Three separate things are going on in a house fire or a vehicle fire or
any fire in a closed room:

- **Heat and smoke particles damage the lining of the airway.** Federal
  emergency medicine guidance notes that the lung symptoms of inhalation
  injury "rarely grossly manifest within first 24hrs" and generally
  evolve over 48 to 72 hours. Someone can walk and talk and look fine and
  be in serious trouble the next day.
- **The airway swells shut.** The same guidance describes swelling at the
  vocal cords, from local injury or from the whole-body swelling that
  follows a large burn, which "can eventually occlude airway." Swelling
  in a tube that is already narrow is a race against a clock.
- **The smoke poisons.** Carbon monoxide binds to haemoglobin and
  displaces oxygen. Hydrogen cyanide, released when synthetic materials
  burn, is described as "a potent and rapid cellular poison." Neither has
  anything to do with the burn on the skin.

Signs worth taking seriously: the fire was in an enclosed space, soot
around the nose or mouth, singed nose or facial hair, a hoarse or changed
voice, a cough with black phlegm, a scratchy throat, chest tightness,
shortness of breath, confusion, or drowsiness. Any of those, or just the
fact that they were breathing smoke in a closed room, means emergency
care now rather than a wait-and-see.

MedlinePlus adds one small mechanical detail for someone with an airway
burn: do not put a pillow under their head. It closes the airway further.

## What not to put on a burn

MedlinePlus is unambiguous, and names the list: no oil, butter, ice,
medicines, cream or oil spray, and no household remedy of any kind, on a
severe burn.

Taking them one at a time, because knowing why makes it stick:

- **Butter, oil, grease, lard.** A layer of fat over a burn holds heat
  against tissue you are trying to cool. It is not sterile. It has to be
  cleaned off before anyone can see the burn, and cleaning it off means
  scrubbing a wound that already hurts. The MedlinePlus sunburn guidance
  separately says to avoid oil-based products including petroleum jelly
  and butter.
- **Ice and iced water.** Covered above. Vasoconstriction, ischaemic
  necrosis, frostbite, hypothermia. The one that people are most
  confident about is the one most likely to deepen the injury.
- **Toothpaste.** A genuinely common practice: published surveys of burn
  first aid in several countries found people reaching for toothpaste,
  honey, oil, yogurt and aloe ahead of running water. Toothpaste is not
  sterile, it is mildly abrasive and alkaline, it traps heat, and it has
  to be removed. The research literature classes these as unsafe
  practices, and finds they cluster where formal first aid teaching has
  not reached.
- **Honey, egg white, yogurt, flour, mud.** Same reasoning. Unsterile
  material pressed into broken skin, which then has to be picked out.
- **Creams, ointments and sprays, including anaesthetic sprays.** Not on
  a severe burn. They obscure the wound, and MedlinePlus specifically
  advises against benzocaine and lidocaine products for sunburn. For a
  small superficial burn with unbroken skin, a plain moisturising lotion
  or aloe is fine; that is a different situation from a raw wound.
- **Adhesive dressings directly on the burn.** MedlinePlus advises
  against putting adhesive bandages on an electrical burn, and generally
  a dressing that sticks to a burn becomes part of the problem when it
  comes off.
- **Your own breath.** MedlinePlus says not to breathe, blow or cough on
  a burn. Blowing on it to cool it feels natural and puts mouth bacteria
  straight onto an open wound.
- **Anything by mouth, if the burn is severe.** No water, no food, no
  painkillers, because they may need surgery.

And one that is not a substance: **do not remove clothing that is stuck
to the skin.** Cut around it and leave the stuck part alone. Pulling it
takes skin with it.

## Blisters: leave them alone

The evidence here is weaker than most people assume, and the honest
summary is worth giving straight.

**What to do in the moment is not in doubt.** Leave blisters intact.
Military burn guidance says not to debride blisters until the patient has
reached a facility with emergency medical or surgical capability. The 2024
international consensus rates preservation of the intact blister during
early first aid as highly recommended. MedlinePlus says do not disturb
blistered or dead skin. Every source agrees: not at home, not by you.

**The reason is mechanical.** An intact blister is a sterile dressing
that fits perfectly and that your own body made. The consensus describes
it as a physical barrier that protects the wound and prevents bacterial
colonisation, provides pain relief, and maintains the moist environment
in which skin heals.

**What the evidence does not settle** is what a clinician should do with
a blister afterwards. The same 2024 consensus rates its own recommendation
to preserve blisters as based on low-quality evidence, and separately
allows removal, weakly and also on low-quality evidence, when a blister is
already damaged, or is large, thin-walled, likely to burst, or badly
contaminated. A randomised trial in *Medicine* comparing draining a
blister with a needle against cutting the roof off concluded that neither
is superior, with healing times around twelve days either way. A 2021
case report in the *Journal of Cutaneous and Aesthetic Surgery* states it
outright: "There is no consensus for burn blister management."

So the guidance to leave blisters alone is firm advice resting on modest
evidence, and the argument among specialists is about a decision you are
not the one making. Leave them intact, and let someone trained decide.

## Dressing it with what you have

After cooling, cover the burn. The purpose is to keep it clean and keep
the air off it, and that is all.

MedlinePlus: cover with a dry, sterile bandage or a clean dressing, keep
pressure and friction off it, and where the skin is broken an ointment is
preferred so the dressing does not stick. For a major burn, cover with a
dry sterile bandage if you have one or a clean cloth if you do not.
Military guidance says the same thing in field terms: cover burns with
loose, dry gauze or a clean sheet.

The word doing the work is **loose**. A burn swells. Anything snug now
will be a tourniquet in an hour.

Burned fingers and toes get separated from each other with non-stick
dressings so they do not heal stuck together. Elevate a burned limb above
the level of the heart if you can, which reduces the swelling.

A rule of thumb for having nothing: clean kitchen cling film, laid over
the burn in loose sheets rather than wrapped around a limb, is widely
taught as an improvised burn cover, because it is clean, it does not
stick, and a clinician can see through it. That is not something a US
federal source specifies, so treat it as a practical fallback. The
sourced instruction remains loose dry gauze or a clean sheet.

## Infection and tetanus

A burn is an open wound with its defences removed, and the infection risk
runs for days after the injury.

MedlinePlus lists the signs to watch for: drainage or pus from the burned
skin, fever, increased pain, red streaks spreading out from the burn, and
swollen lymph nodes. Red streaks in particular mean go now, not tomorrow.

**Tetanus.** The CDC lists burns among the wounds that carry dead tissue
and therefore tetanus risk, alongside crush injuries, fractures and
frostbite. Adults are recommended a tetanus booster every ten years. For
wound management the interval tightens: for a clean minor wound, a
booster is indicated if the last dose was ten or more years ago, and for
a dirty or major wound, including a burn, the trigger is five or more
years. So a burn is a reason to check the date of your last tetanus shot,
and if you cannot remember it, that itself is the answer. The CDC also
notes that antibiotics are not a substitute for this.

## Chemical burns are a different problem

A chemical burn keeps burning until the chemical is gone. Time under
water is not comfort, it is removal.

MedlinePlus gives the sequence:

1. Protect yourself first. Eye and skin protection. You are no help
   contaminated.
2. Get the person away from the source.
3. **Brush dry chemical powder off before any water touches it.** Brush
   it away from yourself and away from their eyes.
4. Remove contaminated clothing and jewellery.
5. Flush the skin with cool running water for 15 minutes or more.
6. Cover loosely with a sterile dressing or clean cloth.

**The exception that matters.** Do not flush with water if the exposure
involves dry lime, which is calcium oxide, or the elemental metals
sodium, potassium, magnesium, phosphorus or lithium. Those react with
water, and adding water makes the injury worse.

**Do not try to neutralise a chemical** with an acid or an alkali unless
a professional tells you to. The neutralisation reaction releases heat,
so you end up adding a thermal burn to a chemical one.

Call emergency services for difficulty breathing, seizures or loss of
consciousness. For a chemical that was swallowed or inhaled, in the
United States, Poison Control is **1-800-222-1222**, free, and staffed
around the clock. Every chemical burn should be seen by a burn service.

## Electrical burns are a different problem again

With electricity, the visible burn is often the least of it.

**Before you touch anyone.** MedlinePlus says not to get within 20 feet,
about 6 metres, of someone being electrocuted by high-voltage current
until the power is confirmed off. For household current, unplug it,
pull the fuse, or throw the breaker. If you cannot cut the power, push
them clear with something non-conducting and dry, a wooden broom handle
or a rubber mat, never metal and never anything wet.

**Then check breathing and pulse.** Electricity kills mainly by stopping
the heart. If they are unconscious with no pulse, start CPR.

**Why a small electrical burn is not a small injury.** Current enters at
one point and leaves at another, and it cooks tissue along the path in
between. Military burn guidance is explicit that with high voltage
injuries, above 1,000 volts, "skin contact points (cutaneous burns) of
limited extent can hide extensive soft-tissue damage." MedlinePlus puts
it plainly: some electrical burns look minor but there may be serious
internal damage, especially to the heart, muscles or brain.

One sign worth knowing, from the same military guidance: muscle destroyed
by current releases its contents into the blood, which damages the
kidneys, and the visible clue is **urine that is red or brown**. That is
an emergency finding.

Call emergency services for any electrical injury, including the ones
that look trivial. MedlinePlus adds: no ice, no ointments, no adhesive
bandages, do not break blisters or remove dead skin, and do not move the
person unless they are in ongoing danger.

## Pain

Ordinary over-the-counter painkillers are appropriate for minor burns.
MedlinePlus recommends them for minor burns and names ibuprofen for
sunburn. Follow the label, respect the dosing, and ask a pharmacist if
anything about the person makes you unsure.

Cool running water is itself a good analgesic, which is one more reason
the twenty minutes is worth doing properly.

Two limits. Do not give anything by mouth if the burn is severe, because
they may need an anaesthetic. And avoid anaesthetic sprays and creams
containing benzocaine or lidocaine, which MedlinePlus advises against.

If pain is poorly controlled despite sensible painkillers, that is itself
one of the American Burn Association's reasons to get a burn service
involved.

## What healing actually looks like

So you know what is normal and what is not.

**A superficial burn** is red, warm and sore, and it settles over a few
days. With sunburn, MedlinePlus notes the first signs may not appear for
a few hours and the full effect may take 24 hours or longer, pain is
worst somewhere between 6 and 48 hours, and peeling arrives several days
in. Peeling is the burn resolving, not an infection.

**A partial thickness burn** blisters, weeps, and is very painful for
the first days. The 2024 international consensus gives the healing times
by depth: a superficial second degree burn heals in under two weeks, a
shallower deep one in two to three weeks, and a deeper one mostly takes
longer than three weeks. Those weeks are the reason depth matters. A burn
still open after about three weeks is the kind that scars badly and is
usually better off surgically treated, which is why a burn that is not
clearly healing by then needs review.

**A full thickness burn** does not heal from the middle, because there is
nothing left in the middle to heal from. It closes from the edges, slowly
and with scar tissue, which is why these are grafted.

What to expect along the way: swelling that peaks in the first day or two,
weeping that gradually dries, itching as it heals, and skin that stays
pink, tight and sensitive for months and burns easily in the sun. Keep
new skin covered from sunlight.

What is not normal: increasing rather than decreasing pain after the
first day or two, spreading redness, red streaks, pus, a smell, fever, or
a burn that has not closed within about three weeks. Any of those, get it
seen.

## How you know you got it right

- You stopped the burning before you did anything else.
- Rings, watch and non-stuck clothing came off before the swelling did.
- Cool running water ran over it for twenty minutes, and nothing colder
  than a tap went near it.
- You covered the person up and kept them warm while you cooled the burn.
- You looked at where the burn is, not just how big it is, and you know
  that face, hands, feet, genitals, joints, anything circumferential,
  anything chemical or electrical, and any burn in a child or an older
  adult goes to a professional whatever its size.
- Nothing went on the burn except water and a loose clean covering.
- Blisters are intact.
- You asked when their last tetanus shot was.
- If someone was in a closed space with smoke, they were seen by a
  clinician even though their skin looked fine.

The one that carries the most weight is the fifth. Almost all of burn
first aid is a short, known procedure that anyone can do. The skill that
actually matters is recognising the burn that is past you, and handing it
over before you have made it worse trying to help.

### Health technology assessment

- Canadian Agency for Drugs and Technologies in Health. Cooling for
  Thermal Burns: Clinical Effectiveness and Guidelines, 2019 (at least
