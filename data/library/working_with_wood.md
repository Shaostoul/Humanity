# Working with Wood: Grain, Water, and Why Boards Move

Wood is the most available structural material there is. It grows back, it
cuts with hand tools, it is strong for its weight, and in most of the
world you can get some without asking anyone's permission. That is why
this guide exists before the one on steel.

The decision you are actually making, almost every time, is this: **which
piece of wood, for which job, and will it still be the right shape in a
year?** Everything below serves that question. Species, grain, moisture
and strength are not four separate topics; they are four ways of asking
whether this board will hold and whether it will stay put.

Where a number appears here, it comes from a government publication that
measured it. The main one is the USDA Forest Products Laboratory *Wood
Handbook*, which is the authority on anything made of wood and is in the
public domain because it is a federal work. Every number traces to
something in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a statement is a
working rule rather than a measured value, it says so in the text.

Two safety notes belong at the top rather than the bottom, because they
are the only two things here that can seriously hurt you. **Wood dust is
a potential occupational carcinogen and the exposure limit is far lower
than people assume.** And **the table saw is the tool that actually
injures people**, roughly twenty six thousand times a year in the United
States. Both have their own section at the end. Neither is a reason not
to work wood; both are reasons to set up properly before you start.

## The one fact that explains every surprise

Wood is not one material. It is three, depending on which way you push
it.

The *Wood Handbook* calls wood **orthotropic**: it has unique and
independent mechanical properties along three axes at right angles to
each other. The **longitudinal** axis runs parallel to the fibres, which
is what people mean by "with the grain". The **radial** axis runs across
the growth rings, from the centre of the tree outward. The **tangential**
axis runs across the grain but along the curve of the rings.

Think of a bundle of drinking straws glued together. Along the straws it
is enormously strong. Across them it is weak, and it wants to come apart
between the straws rather than through them. That is very nearly the
whole story.

Almost every beginner surprise is this one fact arriving unannounced:

- The board split when you drove a nail near the end. You loaded it
  across the straws, which is the direction with almost no strength.
- The shelf cupped into a shallow trough. The two across-grain
  directions shrink by different amounts.
- The joint failed even though the wood looked fine. The joint was
  carrying load in a weak direction, not the strong one.
- The drawer that fit in August will not close in February. Wood takes
  on and gives up water with the seasons, forever.

If you remember only one thing from this page, remember that wood is
strong in one direction and weak in the other two, and that it changes
size in the two weak directions.

## Along the grain versus across it

Here is the size of the difference, in real numbers. All of these are
from the *Wood Handbook*'s tests on clear, straight-grained samples.
Strengths are in pounds per square inch, written psi.

Take Douglas-fir at 12% moisture content. Squeeze it along the grain and
it takes **7,230 psi** before crushing. Squeeze it across the grain and
it gives way at **800 psi**. That is about **nine times** the strength in
one direction compared to the other, in the same piece of wood.

Pulling is even more lopsided. Compare like with like, using the
handbook's green-condition figures for both directions:

| Species | Pulled along the grain | Pulled across the grain | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|
| Western hemlock | 13,000 psi | 290 psi | about 45 to 1 |
| Western redcedar | 6,600 psi | 230 psi | about 29 to 1 |

Roughly **thirty to forty five times stronger along the grain than
across it**. No other common building material is anything like this
one-sided. Steel does not care which way you pull it.

This is why the across-grain numbers are the ones that decide your
design. Wood almost never fails by being crushed along the grain. It
fails by splitting, and splitting is tension across the grain, which is
the weakest thing wood does.

The other weak direction worth naming is **shear parallel to the
grain**: sliding the straws past each other lengthwise. Douglas-fir
manages 1,130 psi there, against 7,230 psi in compression along the
grain. A joint that relies on a short shoulder of wood resisting a
lengthwise sliding force is relying on one of the small numbers.

## Water: the other half of the subject

A living tree is mostly water. What happens to that water on the way to
your workbench governs strength, size, shape and rot.

Water sits in wood in two places: **free water** in the hollow cell
cavities, and **bound water** held inside the cell walls themselves.
They behave completely differently, and the boundary between them is the
single most useful idea in the topic.

### The fibre saturation point

When all the free water has gone but the cell walls are still fully
saturated, wood is at its **fibre saturation point**. The handbook is
careful to say this is easier to define than to measure, but that
practically it is the moisture content below which wood's physical and
mechanical properties start to change, and that this **averages about
30% moisture content**, varying by a few points between species and
individual pieces.

Above that point, drying does nothing except remove water. The board
does not shrink and it does not get stronger. Below that point,
everything starts happening at once: it shrinks, it stiffens, it gets
stronger, and it stops being food for fungi.

Moisture content, by the way, is expressed as a percentage of the wood's
*dry* weight, which is why it can exceed 100%. Green western redcedar
sapwood averages **249% moisture content**: two and a half times its own
dry weight in water.

### Green, air-dried, kiln-dried

| Condition | Roughly what moisture content | What it means for you |
|---|---|---|
| Green | Douglas-fir heartwood 37%, western hemlock heartwood 85%, red alder sapwood 97%, western redcedar sapwood 249% | Full size, weakest, will shrink a lot, will rot if it stays this way |
| Air-dried | Air drying is normally taken down to **20% to 25%** before anything else happens to the wood | Past the fibre saturation point, so most shrinkage has happened; below the rot threshold; still wetter than an indoor room |
| Kiln-dried | Driven to the target for the end use, commonly around 8% for interior work | Stable enough for furniture and trim; stronger; costs energy, which is why mills air dry first |

The green moisture figures above are species averages from the handbook,
and variation between trees, and even between boards from one tree, is
considerable.

The handbook's recommended moisture contents at the moment you install
the wood are worth knowing, because they are the target you are actually
aiming at:

| Use | Most of the United States | Damp coastal areas |
|---|---|---|
| Interior woodwork, flooring, furniture, trim | 8% average, 6% to 10% for individual pieces | 11% average, 8% to 13% |
| Exterior siding, trim, sheathing | 12% average, 9% to 14% | 12% average, 9% to 14% |

The handbook gives a third column for the dry southwestern states, where
the interior figure drops to 6%. If you are not in the damp coastal strip
or the desert southwest, the first column is yours.

### Equilibrium moisture content, and why wood never settles down

Dry wood does not stay at whatever number the kiln left it at. It moves
toward balance with the air around it. That balance point is the
**equilibrium moisture content**, and it depends on relative humidity
and temperature. At about 70 degrees Fahrenheit:

| Relative humidity | Wood settles at |
|---|---|
| 30% | 6.2% |
| 50% | 9.2% |
| 65% | 12.0% |
| 80% | 16.0% |
| 90% | 20.5% |

This is not a one-time adjustment. It is permanent, seasonal breathing.
The handbook's outdoor figures for Seattle run from **12.0% in July to
16.9% in December**; Portland, Oregon runs from **11.2% to 17.2%**.
Wood left outside in the Pacific Northwest swings about five percentage
points of moisture content every single year, and it changes size every
single year to match.

Finishes do not stop this. The handbook is explicit: moisture content
changes **can be retarded, but not prevented**, by protective coatings
such as varnish, lacquer or paint. A finish slows the swing and evens it
out. Nothing seals wood permanently. Design for movement instead of
trying to prevent it.

### Dry wood is stronger wood

Most of wood's mechanical properties improve as it dries below the fibre
saturation point. Douglas-fir again, green against 12%:

| Property | Green | At 12% moisture |
|---|---|---|
| Bending strength | 7,700 psi | 12,400 psi |
| Crushing strength along the grain | 3,780 psi | 7,230 psi |

Drying nearly doubles the crushing strength. This is a large effect and
it is the main reason lumber is dried at all, alongside stability and
rot resistance.

## Shrinkage, and why a board cups

Wood shrinks **most tangentially, about half as much radially, and only
slightly along the grain**. Those three numbers, and specifically the
ratio between the first two, explain warp.

Shrinkage from green to bone dry, as a percentage of the green
dimension:

| Species | Radial | Tangential | Tangential divided by radial |
|---|---|---|---|
| Western redcedar | 2.4% | 5.0% | 2.1 |
| Bigleaf maple | 3.7% | 7.1% | 1.9 |
| Western hemlock | 4.2% | 7.8% | 1.9 |
| Red alder | 4.4% | 7.3% | 1.7 |
| Douglas-fir (Coast) | 4.8% | 7.6% | 1.6 |

Along the grain, shrinkage is only **0.1% to 0.2%** for most species,
which is why a stud stays the length it was cut.

Now picture a flatsawn board, the ordinary kind with the growth rings
running roughly parallel to the face. The ring curvature means the face
nearer the bark is closer to tangential, and the face nearer the pith is
closer to radial. The tangential face shrinks nearly twice as much. The
board becomes a shallow trough. **That is cupping, and it is simply the
tangential-to-radial ratio made visible.** Nothing was done wrong.

The handbook names six kinds of warp: bow, crook, twist, oval, diamond
and cup. It traces all of them to one of two causes: **differences
between radial, tangential and longitudinal shrinkage**, or **growth
stresses** locked into the tree. Warp is made worse by irregular or
distorted grain, and by two kinds of abnormal wood covered further down.

Quartersawn boards, where the rings run roughly edge to edge through the
thickness, move mainly in thickness rather than width and cup far less.
That is the entire argument for quartersawn stock, and it costs more
because you get less of it from a log.

### Working out how much it will move

For the ordinary indoor range of 6% to 14% moisture content, the
handbook gives a **dimensional change coefficient** for each species and
direction. Multiply the starting dimension by the coefficient, then by
the change in moisture content in percentage points.

| Species | Radial coefficient | Tangential coefficient |
|---|---|---|
| Western redcedar | 0.00111 | 0.00234 |
| Bigleaf maple | 0.00126 | 0.00248 |
| Red alder | 0.00151 | 0.00256 |
| Western hemlock | 0.00144 | 0.00274 |
| Douglas-fir (Coast) | 0.00165 | 0.00267 |

Worked example. You have a 12 inch wide flatsawn Douglas-fir board at
12% moisture content, and it is going into a heated house that will pull
it down to 6%.

Tangential, so use 0.00267. Twelve inches, times 0.00267, times six
points of moisture change, gives **0.19 inches**, call it three
sixteenths of an inch. The board will lose nearly a quarter inch of
width.

The same board quartersawn uses the radial coefficient of 0.00165 and
loses **0.12 inches**, about an eighth. Same wood, same conditions,
nearly half the movement.

Three sixteenths of an inch is the difference between a tabletop that
looks fine and one that splits because you screwed it down at both
edges. This calculation takes thirty seconds and prevents a whole
category of failure.

## Softwood and hardwood are botany, not hardness

The names are misleading and the handbook says so directly. **Softwoods**
are woods from gymnosperms, mostly conifers: pine, spruce, fir, cedar,
hemlock, Douglas-fir. **Hardwoods** are woods from angiosperms, the
flowering broadleaf trees: maple, oak, alder, birch, poplar. That is the
entire distinction. It is about how the tree reproduces, not about how
hard the wood is.

The handbook's own counterexample is blunt: softwoods such as longleaf
pine and Douglas-fir are typically harder than the hardwoods basswood
and aspen. The measured hardness figures back it up:

| Species | Type | Side hardness at 12% moisture |
|---|---|---|
| Douglas-fir (Coast) | Softwood | 710 lb |
| American basswood | Hardwood | 410 lb |
| Quaking aspen | Hardwood | 350 lb |

Douglas-fir, a softwood, is twice as hard as quaking aspen and well over
one and a half times as hard as basswood, both of which are hardwoods.
Balsa, the lightest wood most people have ever handled, is also
botanically a hardwood.

So do not choose wood by the category name. Choose it by the numbers in
the next section.

## The species table

Everything here is from the *Wood Handbook* at 12% moisture content,
tested on clear, straight-grained samples. Specific gravity is the
handbook's density index, based on the wood's oven-dry weight and its
volume at 12% moisture; multiplying it by 62.4 gives a rough weight in
pounds per cubic foot, close enough to compare species by. Decay class is
for **heartwood only**, which matters a great deal and is explained in
the next section.

| Species | Specific gravity | Bending strength (psi) | Stiffness (million psi) | Crushing along grain (psi) | Hardness (lb) | Shrink radial / tangential | Heartwood decay class |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Douglas-fir** (Coast) | 0.48 | 12,400 | 1.95 | 7,230 | 710 | 4.8% / 7.6% | Moderately resistant |
| **Western hemlock** | 0.45 | 11,300 | 1.63 | 7,200 | 540 | 4.2% / 7.8% | Slightly or nonresistant |
| **Western redcedar** | 0.32 | 7,500 | 1.11 | 4,560 | 350 | 2.4% / 5.0% | Resistant |
| **Red alder** | 0.41 | 9,800 | 1.38 | 5,820 | 590 | 4.4% / 7.3% | Slightly or nonresistant |
| **Bigleaf maple** | 0.48 | 10,700 | 1.45 | 5,950 | 850 | 3.7% / 7.1% | Slightly or nonresistant |
| **Loblolly pine** (Southern Pine) | 0.51 | 12,800 | 1.79 | 7,130 | 690 | 4.8% / 7.4% | Slightly or nonresistant |

How to read that table for a real job:

- **Framing and anything structural.** Douglas-fir and Southern Pine are
  at the top for bending strength and stiffness, which is exactly why
  they are the framing lumber of the western and southern United States
  respectively. Western hemlock is close behind and often sold mixed
  with Douglas-fir.
- **Anything outdoors and unpainted.** Western redcedar, and it is not
  close. It is the weakest and softest wood in the table by a wide
  margin, and it is the only one whose heartwood is rated resistant. It
  also has by far the least shrinkage, so it stays flat. Fence boards,
  siding, shingles, raised beds.
- **Furniture and cabinets.** Bigleaf maple is the hardest here and takes
  wear well. Red alder is the classic Pacific Northwest cabinet wood:
  moderate in everything, easy to work, easy to finish, cheap locally.
- **Anything that will get wet and is not cedar.** Nothing in the
  slightly or nonresistant row belongs outdoors untreated. Hemlock,
  alder, maple and pine all need either preservative treatment or a
  design that keeps them dry.

## What actually rots wood

Rot is not caused by rain, or by age, or by wood being outdoors. It is
caused by **fungi eating the wood**, and fungi have requirements. Take
away any one of them and rot does not happen.

The *Wood Handbook* is precise about this, and the numbers are more
forgiving than most people expect:

- **Moisture.** Serious decay occurs only when moisture content is
  **above the fibre saturation point, average 30%**. Below that, the
  growth of decay fungi is substantially arrested.
- **Not from humid air alone.** By itself, water vapour in humid air will
  not wet wood enough to support significant decay. It takes liquid
  water: rain, condensation, a leak, or contact with wet ground. Humid
  air can still grow surface mould, which is a different and much less
  serious thing.
- **A working safety margin of 20%.** Fully air-dried wood usually sits
  at no more than 20% moisture content, and the handbook treats that as
  a reasonable margin against fungal damage. Twenty percent is the
  number used in field inspections as the maximum allowable level.
- **Temperature.** Decay is relatively slow below 50 degrees Fahrenheit
  and above 95, and essentially ceases at 35 degrees or at 100.
- **Air.** Wood can be too wet to rot. Water-soaked wood does not supply
  enough air to the interior for typical decay fungi, which is why
  foundation piles below the water table and logs stored under water
  sprays survive indefinitely.

Put together: **wood will not decay if it is kept air dry, and decay
already present will not progress.** That single sentence is the whole
of rot prevention. Detailing that sheds water and lets wood dry out
between wettings beats any wood species and any finish.

### Heartwood versus sapwood

**Heartwood** is the darker wood toward the centre of the trunk, no
longer conducting sap. **Sapwood** is the lighter band nearer the bark,
still alive and still moving water and sugars in the living tree.

Decay resistance lives in the heartwood, because that is where the tree
deposits the extractive chemicals that make certain species durable. The
handbook notes that for many species the **sapwood of wood products is
more susceptible to decay than the heartwood** is.

This has a hard practical consequence. **A cedar board with a pale stripe
of sapwood along one edge is not a decay-resistant board.** The rating in
the table above applies to the dark heartwood and to nothing else. When
you buy cedar for ground contact or for anything that stays wet, you are
buying heartwood, and you should be able to see that you got it.

The handbook's own guidance on what each class is good for:

- **Very resistant** heartwood, which includes black locust, Osage-orange,
  red mulberry and Pacific yew, generally performs satisfactorily even
  **in contact with the ground**.
- **Resistant** heartwood, which includes western redcedar, generally
  performs satisfactorily **above ground**.
- The **moderately resistant** and **slightly or nonresistant** classes
  will usually require some form of preservative treatment.

Note where Douglas-fir lands: moderately resistant, which means a
Douglas-fir post in the ground is a treated post or a temporary one.

Also note that old growth and second growth are not the same product.
The handbook rates baldcypress and redwood differently depending on
which they are, and says generally that wood from naturally resistant
species grown in old growth forests is often more resistant than the
same species from younger trees.

## Fasteners and joints, in terms of what the wood can carry

A fastener does not hold anything. The **wood around the fastener** holds
it, and it holds according to the rules above.

### Density does most of the work

The handbook's equation for how hard you must pull to withdraw a nail
driven into side grain is:

> maximum load (lb) = 7,850 times G to the power 2.5, times nail
> diameter, times depth of penetration, both in inches

where G is the specific gravity of the wood. The thing to notice is the
**exponent of 2.5 on density**. Holding power does not scale with
density, it scales much faster.

Work it through with the table above. Douglas-fir at 0.48 against
western redcedar at 0.32 is only a 1.5 times difference in density, but
raised to the 2.5 power it becomes **about 2.8 times the withdrawal
resistance** for the same nail driven to the same depth. This is
arithmetic from the handbook's own equation rather than a separate
measurement, but the equation is theirs.

That is the real reason cedar fence boards need more or larger fasteners
than the framing behind them, and why soft woods reward ring-shank or
screw-threaded fasteners that do not rely on friction alone.

### End grain barely holds at all

Driving into end grain means driving parallel to the straws, and the
fastener has nothing to bite between. The handbook's figures:

| Fastener and direction | Compared to side grain |
|---|---|
| Nails, resistance to sideways load, into end grain | About **two thirds**, with minimum results near 75% |
| Screws, withdrawal, into end grain | Averages **75%**, and is erratic |
| Lag screws into end grain | The handbook says plainly they **should not be used** there |

If a joint's strength depends on a fastener gripping end grain, redesign
the joint. This is why traditional joinery uses mortises, tenons,
dovetails and pegs: they transfer load into side grain and into
mechanical interlock, rather than asking end grain to hold a screw.

### Moisture cycling loosens everything

Nails driven into green wood that is then allowed to dry, or into dry
wood that is then cycled wet and dry, **lose a major part of their
holding power**. The handbook puts it as low as **25% of the value
measured just after driving**. Wood shrank away from the nail, and the
friction that was holding it is gone.

This is the mechanism behind every loose deck board and squeaky floor
you have ever met, and it is the practical reason to fasten wood at
roughly the moisture content it will live at.

### Splitting, holes and spacing

Splitting is tension across the grain, the weakest thing wood does, and
a fastener is a wedge. Three defences, all from the handbook:

- **Drill lead holes.** A hole about **90% of the nail shank diameter**
  gives somewhat *greater* withdrawal resistance than no hole at all,
  and prevents or reduces splitting, particularly in dense species. You
  lose nothing and gain twice.
- **Keep away from edges and ends.** As a general rule, drive nails no
  closer to the edge of the side member than half its thickness, and no
  closer to the end than the full thickness of the piece.
- **Use a smaller fastener.** Smaller nails can go closer to edges and
  ends precisely because they are less likely to split the wood.

### Metal choice

Where moisture is present, certain metals in contact with wood extractives
produce black staining, and certain preservative treatments corrode
fasteners. The handbook's answer for staining is **stainless steel,
aluminium or hot-dipped galvanised** fasteners. For wood treated with
copper-based preservatives, it reports that **copper, silicon bronze and
300 series stainless steels** have performed well.

## Picking a board at the pile

You now have everything needed to judge a board in about ten seconds.
Look at the end grain first, then sight down the length, then look at
the face.

**Read the end grain.** The curve of the growth rings tells you whether
the board is flatsawn, quartersawn, or in between, and therefore how much
it will move and which way it will cup. Rings running roughly parallel to
the wide face means flatsawn and maximum movement. Rings running roughly
perpendicular to the wide face means quartersawn and about half as much.

**Check the grain slope.** Grain that runs off the edge of a board rather
than along it costs real strength, and the handbook quantifies it as a
percentage of the same board with straight grain:

| Grain slope | Bending strength | Impact resistance | Crushing along grain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Straight | 100% | 100% | 100% |
| 1 in 20 | 93% | 90% | 100% |
| 1 in 15 | 89% | 81% | 100% |
| 1 in 10 | 81% | 62% | 99% |
| 1 in 5 | 55% | 36% | 93% |

A slope of 1 in 10, which is easy to miss and looks like nothing,
removes nearly a fifth of the bending strength and **more than a third
of the impact resistance**. Notice also that crushing strength along the
grain barely cares. Grain slope matters enormously for anything that
bends or takes a blow, and hardly at all for a post in compression.

**Judge knots by where they are, not just by size.** Knots lower most
mechanical properties for four reasons the handbook lists: clear wood is
displaced, the fibres around the knot are distorted into cross grain,
the discontinuity concentrates stress, and checks often form around
knots during drying.

Two things about knots surprise people:

- Knots have **a much greater effect in tension than in compression**,
  with bending in between. In a simply supported beam the loaded face is
  in compression and the underside is in tension, so **a knot on the
  underside matters far more than the same knot on top.** If a knotty
  board must be a beam, put the worst knot on the upper face.
- **Encased knots**, the dead loose ones that fall out and leave a hole,
  tend to come with *less* cross grain than tight intergrown knots, and
  the handbook rates them as generally **less problematic for most
  mechanical properties**. They look worse and are often structurally
  kinder. You may still reject one for appearance, or because a hole is a
  hole, but do not assume a tight knot is automatically the stronger
  board.

**Sight down the length for warp.** Bow, crook, twist and cup all show
up in a two second sight down the edge and the face. A twisted board is
the one to leave behind; the other three can often be worked around.

**Reject reaction wood and juvenile wood.** Normal wood shrinks 0.1% to
0.2% along its length. Reaction wood, which forms in leaning trunks and
under branches, and juvenile wood from near the centre of the tree,
**can shrink 2% lengthwise**, ten to twenty times as much. That is what
makes a stud crook weeks after it is nailed in. Look for wood from very
near the pith, unusually wide and uneven growth rings, and an abnormally
dense, dull-looking zone on one side of the board.

**Check for surface checks**, the fine splits that open on the face.
They form early in drying when the outer shell is stressed in tension
enough to fracture, and they are most common **on the face of flatsawn
boards**.

**Buy wood at the moisture content it will live at.** A kiln-dried board
that has spent three weeks on an open rack in a wet Pacific Northwest
winter is not a kiln-dried board any more. If it matters, bring the wood
inside and let it sit in the room it will live in for a couple of weeks
before you cut joinery. This is common trade practice rather than a
measured recommendation, but the equilibrium moisture content figures
above are exactly why it works.

## Safety

### Wood dust

This is the hazard people take least seriously and should take most
seriously, because it accumulates over years and there is no moment when
it hurts.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health classifies
**wood dust as a potential occupational carcinogen**, with the cancer
site given as **nasal cancer**. Its recommended exposure limit is a
time-weighted average of **1 milligram per cubic metre**. The OSHA
permissible limit is much higher, at 15 milligrams per cubic metre total
dust and 5 milligrams per cubic metre respirable, so the two disagree by
more than a factor of ten and the NIOSH figure is the health-based one.

The non-cancer effects listed are eye irritation, nosebleeds,
dermatitis, respiratory hypersensitivity, granulomatous pneumonitis,
asthma, cough, wheezing, sinusitis and prolonged colds. The target
organs are the eyes, skin and respiratory system, and the exposure
routes include skin and eye contact as well as breathing it.

One detail matters especially in the Pacific Northwest: **western red
cedar dust is the same NIOSH entry as wood dust**, sharing its
identifier. The wood most worth building with outdoors here is also on
the respiratory hazard list.

What this means in practice: dust collection at the tool, not a broom
afterwards; a properly fitted respirator rather than a paper nuisance
mask for sanding and routing; and not eating lunch in a shop full of
airborne dust. A sanding session generates far more respirable dust than
a sawing session, and produces almost no visible mess, which is exactly
backwards from how people judge risk.

### The tools that actually injure people

The table saw is the one. The Consumer Product Safety Commission
estimated that in 2017 there were **26,500 table saw blade-contact
injuries treated in United States emergency departments**. Of those,
**25,600, or 96.4%, involved a finger**. The breakdown by diagnosis was
**16,100 lacerations, 5,500 fractures, and 2,800 amputations**.

Two thousand eight hundred amputations in one year, from one tool.

A common belief is that kickback causes most of these. The Commission's
assessment of its own incident data is that **most blade-contact
injuries are not related to kickback**. Contact happens when a hand gets
too close feeding a small workpiece, when the operator is distracted,
when a glove catches the blade, when someone reaches to regain control
of a workpiece, and when someone brushes debris off the table while the
blade is still coasting after shutoff.

Federal workplace regulation requires three specific devices on a
hand-fed circular ripsaw, and they are worth naming because home saws
are frequently run without them:

1. **A hood** completely enclosing the portion of the saw above the table
   and above the material, self-adjusting to the thickness of the work.
2. **A spreader**, made of hard tempered steel and thinner than the saw
   kerf, to prevent material from squeezing the saw or being thrown back
   at the operator. This is the riving knife. The regulation permits its
   removal for grooving, dadoing and rabbeting, and requires that it be
   **immediately replaced** when those operations are finished.
3. **Nonkickback fingers or dogs**, positioned to oppose the saw's
   tendency to pick up the material and throw it back.

The same regulation requires a power control the operator can reach
**without leaving the point of operation**, which is worth arranging even
in a home shop.

Two other machines deserve a mention because they injure differently:

- **The jointer.** The regulation requires a cylindrical cutting head
  with the knife projecting **no more than one eighth of an inch** beyond
  the body of the head, an automatic guard covering the head on the
  working side of the fence, and a guard covering the head behind the
  fence. A jointer has no blade guard you can see working and the cutter
  is directly under your hands. Push blocks are not optional.
- **The portable circular saw.** Regulation requires both an upper and a
  lower guard covering the saw to the depth of the teeth, with the lower
  guard returning **automatically and instantly** to the covering
  position when the tool is withdrawn from the work, plus a
  constant-pressure switch that cuts power when released. A sticky lower
  guard on a well-used saw is a genuine defect, not a quirk.

None of this replaces learning the tools from someone who knows them.
It does tell you what "set up correctly" looks like, and it tells you
which guard someone removed.

## You own this when

- You can say which of the three directions a load is acting in, and
  whether that is the strong one or one of the weak ones.
- You can look at the end of a board and say whether it will cup, and
  roughly how much it will move across its width between summer and
  winter.
- You know that 30% moisture content is where shrinkage and rot both
  begin, and that 20% is the working margin below it.
- You buy heartwood when you buy for decay resistance, and you can see
  the difference in the board.
- You do not ask end grain to hold a screw.
- You have dust collection running before the tool starts, and the
  spreader is back on the saw before the next rip cut.

When those are true you can walk into any pile of lumber, in any
country, and choose correctly for the job. The underlying skill
transfers well beyond wood: knowing which direction a material is strong
in, knowing what environmental variable it responds to, and knowing
which failure mode is the one that actually hurts someone are the same
three questions behind every material you will ever build with.

### A note on what is not cited

Lumber retailers, tool manufacturers and woodworking publications
carry a great deal of wood advice, some of it good. None of it is an
authority on a measured property, and where their numbers disagree with
the *Wood Handbook*, the handbook measured it and they did not. Nothing
in this guide rests on one.
