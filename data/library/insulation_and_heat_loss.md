# Insulation and Heat Loss

A heated room costs money every hour it stays warm. Insulation is the
only thing you can buy once that keeps lowering that cost for the rest
of the building's life. Everything else in the heating bill is a
running expense: the fuel, the electricity, the filters, the service
calls. The shell of the building is the one part you pay for once.

That is why this comes before the guide on heating. If you size a
heater for a leaky, uninsulated room, you buy a bigger heater and then
feed it forever. Fix the room first and the heater gets smaller, the
fuel bill gets smaller, and the room stops having a cold side.

It also comes before the guide on ventilation, for a less obvious
reason. A tighter, better insulated building traps moisture as readily
as it traps heat, and trapped moisture rots timber and grows mould.
That is the caution in this topic, and it has its own section below.

Where a number matters here, it comes from a US government publication:
the EPA's ENERGY STAR programme, the Department of Energy's Building
America research, the Forest Products Laboratory, or the parts of
federal regulation that set what insulation has to do. Where a figure
is a rule of thumb rather than something an agency measured, it says so
in the text.

## The three ways heat moves, in things you can feel

You do not need equations for this. You need to recognise three
different sensations, because each one is stopped by a different thing.

**Conduction** is heat travelling through solid material. It is the
cold you feel when you put a bare hand on a window pane or a concrete
floor. The Forest Products Laboratory defines the property behind it
plainly: thermal conductivity is the rate of heat flow through one unit
thickness of a material with a temperature difference across it. Some
materials pass heat greedily and some grudgingly, and the spread is far
wider than most people expect. This is what insulation thickness fights.

**Convection** is heat carried by moving air. It is the draught across
your ankles, and it is also the reason an unheated upstairs somehow
stays warmer than a heated downstairs. ENERGY STAR describes the whole
building version of it: in cold weather, warm air rises in your house
just like it does in a chimney, escapes into the attic, and sucks cold
air in all around the house to replace it. Insulation does almost
nothing about this. Sealing does.

**Radiation** is heat crossing open space as invisible light, the way
you feel a wood stove across a room without touching it. It is the
weakest of the three in an ordinary heated room, but it is the reason
foil-faced products exist. The FTC's insulation rule is precise about
how they work: the test for reflective insulation measures the
emittance of the reflective surface, meaning its power to radiate heat,
and the resulting R-value depends on the emittance, the air space next
to it, and the direction the heat is flowing. A foil product with no
air space beside it is just a thin sheet.

Most of the money in an ordinary house is lost to the first two, and
most people spend their effort on the first one only.

## What R-value and U-value actually mean

R-value is resistance to heat flow. ENERGY STAR's definition is the
plain one: R-value is a measure of insulation's ability to resist heat
traveling through it, and the higher the R-value, the better the
thermal performance.

U-value, or U-factor, is the same fact stated backwards. It is the rate
of heat transmission, so lower is better. They are reciprocals of each
other, which is worth internalising because insulation is sold in
R-values and windows are sold in U-factors, and you cannot compare them
until you flip one of them over. A window rated U-0.25 is resisting
heat about as well as R-4 of anything else.

**R-values add.** Stack R-13 of batt and R-10 of exterior board and you
have R-23 through that path. This is the one piece of arithmetic in the
whole subject, and it is why ENERGY STAR's retrofit guidance is written
as "add R5 to R10 insulative wall sheathing beneath the new siding"
rather than as a replacement value. You are adding to what is there.

Two honest complications follow immediately.

The first is that the addition only holds along a single path through
the wall. It does not hold for the wall as a whole, because the wall
has studs in it. That is the thermal bridging section below, and it is
the single biggest reason a finished wall underperforms the number on
the bag.

The second is that R-value per inch is not a constant. The FTC actually
forbids advertising it. Under 16 CFR 460.20, a manufacturer may not
give the R-value for one inch or the R-value per inch of a product, and
in the narrow case where test results allow a range, the label must
carry the sentence: "The R-value per inch of this insulation varies
with thickness. The thicker the insulation, the lower the R-value per
inch." So treat every per-inch figure in this guide, including the ones
below, as a rough comparison between material families rather than a
number you can multiply out.

## Where a building actually loses heat

Here is the honest state of the evidence. There is no single federal
pie chart that says a typical house loses some exact percentage through
the roof, some through the walls, and some through the floor. The
proportions depend on the building's shape, its age, and where it is.
Anyone who quotes you crisp percentages for that breakdown is quoting
a rule of thumb, whatever it looks like.

What the agencies do say, and what actually decides where your money
goes, is this.

Space heating and cooling dominate the bill. The Energy Information
Administration reports that more than half, 52 percent in 2020, of a
household's annual energy consumption goes to just two end uses: space
heating and air conditioning.

The leaks that matter are not the ones you feel. ENERGY STAR is blunt
about it: people notice draughts around windows and doors and assume
those are the major source of wasted energy, but in most homes the most
significant air leaks are hidden in the attic and basement, and those
are the leaks that significantly raise your energy bill. They put the
total in an image worth keeping: if you added up all the leaks, holes
and gaps in a typical home's envelope, it would be the equivalent of
having a window open every day of the year.

Air leakage is not a rounding error next to insulation. You can put a
number on this using two parts of the same federal standard, HUD's
Manufactured Home Construction and Safety Standards. Take a single wide
home, 14 feet by 66 feet, as a worked example; the dimensions are mine,
the coefficients are HUD's.

- 24 CFR 3280.509(a) gives the default for air leakage: infiltration
  heat loss equals 0.7 multiplied by the temperature difference,
  multiplied by the feet of perimeter, in Btu per hour. The perimeter
  is 160 feet. On a night when it is 0 F outside and 70 F inside, that
  is 0.7 x 70 x 160, which is about 7,800 Btu per hour.
- 24 CFR 3280.506(b) caps the whole insulated shell in the coldest of
  its three zones at a heat transmission coefficient of 0.079 Btu per
  hour per square foot per degree F. The envelope of that home is about
  3,128 square feet, so the shell loses about 0.079 x 3,128 x 70, which
  is about 17,300 Btu per hour.

Leakage is therefore roughly 31 percent of the total loss, and close to
half as much as everything the insulation is doing. And note what the
regulation says about its own number: the shell coefficient explicitly
**excludes infiltration, ventilation, and condensation control**. The
code figure for the shell does not count the air leaking through it at
all. That is the whole argument for the next section.

## Seal first, insulate second

This order is not a preference. It is the order that gets you the most
comfort per dollar, and doing it backwards wastes both.

Insulation slows conduction. It does not stop air. Blown insulation
laid over an unsealed attic floor lets warm air keep pouring up through
the gaps, and now the gaps are buried where you cannot find them.
ENERGY STAR's guide tells you to look for dirty insulation in the
attic, because dirt is where air has been filtering through it. That
dirt is a photograph of the money leaving.

ENERGY STAR estimates that homeowners can save an average of 15 percent
on heating and cooling costs, or an average of 11 percent on total
energy costs, by air sealing their homes and adding insulation. Their
own methodology describes the target as sealing leaks to the attic
space, through the foundation, and around windows and doors to achieve
a 25 percent reduction in total air infiltration.

The sequence that follows from the chimney effect is attic, then
basement. ENERGY STAR describes the mechanism: warm air generated by
the furnace rises through the house and out through attic leaks, and
cold outside air gets drawn in through basement leaks to replace it.
Seal the top first, then seal the bottom to stop the draw.

Practical notes from the same guide:

- Plug the big holes first. Their words: do not worry about finding and
  sealing all the little holes; your biggest savings come from plugging
  the large ones. Look where inner and outer walls meet the attic
  floor, at dropped soffits, and behind attic kneewalls.
- Caulk handles gaps of 1/4 inch or less. Spray foam handles 1/4 inch
  up to about 3 inches.
- The rim joist, the framing where the floor joists land on top of the
  foundation, is a row of open cavities all the way around the
  building. Seal the top and bottom of each one.
- Weatherstrip the attic hatch. It is a hole in your ceiling.
- If ducts run through the attic, seal them with mastic or foil tape,
  never household duct tape. ENERGY STAR says sealing and insulating
  ducts can increase heating and cooling system efficiency by 20
  percent.

Three cautions that belong with the work, not after it:

- Keep all insulation 3 inches away from recessed can lights unless
  they are rated IC, meaning rated for insulation contact.
- Knob and tube wiring, found in houses built before about 1930, can be
  a fire hazard when it is in contact with insulation. If you find it,
  stop and get an electrician.
- Never cover soffit vents. Blocking airflow at the eaves is, in
  ENERGY STAR's words, the most common mistake homeowners make when
  installing insulation.
- After you tighten a house, have a technician check that the
  gas or oil furnace, water heater and dryer are still venting
  properly. A tighter house raises the chance of carbon monoxide
  building up indoors. This one is not optional.

## What insulation is made of, and the honest trade-offs

Start with why insulation works at all. The Forest Products Laboratory
publishes thermal conductivities for common building materials in the
same units, which makes the comparison honest. In US units, R-value per
inch is simply one divided by the conductivity, so the right hand
column below is arithmetic on the left hand column, not a separate
claim.

| Material | Conductivity, Btu in/(h ft2 F) | R per inch |
|---|---|---|
| Mineral wool | 0.25 | about 4 |
| Softwood lumber at 12 percent moisture | 0.7 to 1.0 | about 1.0 to 1.4 |
| Plaster | 5 | about 0.2 |
| Concrete | 6 | about 0.17 |
| Glass | 7 | about 0.14 |
| Steel | 310 | about 0.003 |
| Aluminium | 1,500 | about 0.0007 |

The Department of Energy's Building America programme gives the same
picture from the other direction, as a working range for designers:
insulation products run about R-3 to R-7 per inch, wood about R-1 per
inch, concrete about R-0.1 per inch, and steel about R-0.003 per inch.
Two independent federal sources agreeing on steel at R-0.003 per inch
is worth noticing, and we will come back to it.

For what you would actually buy for an attic, ENERGY STAR's figure is
the practical one: R-38 is about 10 to 14 inches deep, depending on
insulation type. That works out to somewhere around R-2.7 to R-3.8 per
inch for the ordinary batts and blown materials, which is the honest
middle of the Building America range.

Now the trade-offs, which are mostly about fire and water.

**Fire behaviour.** There is a real dividing line here, and federal
regulation draws it cleanly. Under HUD's standard at 24 CFR 3280.207,
ordinary thermal insulation must have a flame spread index of 25 or
less and a smoke developed index of 450 or less, and insulation left
exposed on an attic floor must additionally have a critical radiant
flux of not less than 0.12 watts per square centimetre. Fibrous
insulation meets that and can simply sit there.

Foam plastic cannot. Under 24 CFR 3280.208, foam plastic insulation
may not be used inside wall or ceiling cavities or left exposed to the
interior unless it is protected by an interior finish of 5/16 inch
gypsum board or equivalent, or used as sheathing no more than 3/8 inch
thick with a flame spread rating of 75 or less and separated from the
inside of the home by at least 2 inches of mineral fibre or an
equivalent thermal barrier. That is the trade you are making when you
buy the higher R per inch: foam needs a fire barrier between it and the
room, and fibrous insulation does not.

**Cellulose** has its own federal safety standard, 16 CFR 1209, and it
tells you exactly what the regulator was worried about. Cellulose must
have a critical radiant flux of at least 0.12 watts per square
centimetre, and in the smouldering combustion test it must show no
evidence of flaming combustion and lose 15 percent or less of its
initial weight. It must also pass a corrosiveness test, in which six
metal coupons are examined over a light bulb and must show no
perforations. A standard does not get a corrosion clause by accident:
treat contact between damp cellulose and metal as a known concern,
which in practice means keeping it dry.

**Moisture behaviour, and ageing.** Two things the FTC rule reveals
about products you will be sold:

- For polyurethane, polyisocyanurate, and extruded polystyrene, R-value
  tests must be run on samples that fully reflect the effect of ageing
  on the product's R-value. Those three foams lose R-value over their
  life, and the rule exists so the label accounts for it.
- For loose-fill cellulose, tests must be done at the settled density.
  It settles. Blow it deeper than the finished depth you want.

And one that matters more than any product choice: **compressed
insulation loses R-value, badly.** HUD publishes a table for exactly
this at 24 CFR 3280.509(c). Squash a batt uniformly to half its
intended thickness and it retains 61 percent of its rated R-value.
Blown insulation restricted to half its depth retains 72 percent. An
R-30 batt crammed into a space meant for R-19 is not R-30, and paying
for the higher number and then crushing it is one of the commonest
ways money is wasted in this trade.

## Thermal bridging, or why the wall is worse than the bag

Every stud in a wall is a path for heat that goes around the
insulation. Building America's definition is the useful one: thermal
bridging happens whenever framing members or attachment devices span
through an assembly from inside the conditioned space to outside the
thermal envelope.

How much of the wall is stud? HUD's default assumption, used when
nobody has measured the actual building, is that framing is 15 percent
of wall area excluding windows and doors, and 10 percent of ceiling and
floor area. So roughly a seventh of your wall is not insulated at all;
it is wood, at about R-1 per inch.

Now put steel in place of the wood. Steel conducts heat several hundred
times better than softwood does, on both federal sources above. A steel
stud is not a slightly worse stud. It is a metal fin running from the
warm side of the wall to the cold side, and it shorts out the
insulation between studs so effectively that a steel framed wall with
R-13 in the cavities performs nowhere near R-13 overall. This is why
you should never compare a steel framed building to a wood framed one
on cavity insulation alone.

Fasteners do it too, at a smaller scale that adds up. Building America
notes that cladding attachments can be a significant source of heat
transfer, and that metal Z-bar connectors through exterior insulation
can lose over 50 percent of the thermal resistance of that continuous
insulation layer.

The fix, in every case, is **continuous insulation**: a layer of board
on the outside of the framing, unbroken by studs, so every path through
the wall has to cross it. This is exactly what ENERGY STAR's retrofit
guidance is describing when it says to add R5 to R10 of insulative wall
sheathing beneath new siding in zones 4 through 8. The continuous layer
is worth more per R than the same R added inside the cavity, because it
is the only part of the wall the studs cannot bypass.

Federal practice takes this seriously enough to require it in the
arithmetic. Under 24 CFR 3280.508(c), the effect of framing must be
included in the heat loss calculation, and other low resistance paths,
which the regulation calls thermal shorts, must be explicitly accounted
for once they add up to more than 1 percent of the exterior surface
area. A thermal short is defined there as any path that separates
heated from unheated space and is insulated to less than half the level
of the material around it.

## The dew point, and how insulation rots a building

This is the caution in this topic, and it deserves your full attention
because the failure is invisible for years and then expensive.

Air holds water vapour, and warm air holds more of it than cold air.
Cool a parcel of air enough and it cannot hold what it has any more, so
the water comes out as liquid. The temperature where that happens is
the dew point. EPA's mould course states the everyday version: when
warm, humid air contacts a cold surface, condensation may form, the way
it forms on a cold bottle taken out of a refrigerator.

Here is why insulation is implicated. Insulation works by creating a
temperature difference across itself. That is its entire function. So
by definition, the far side of your insulation is colder than the room,
and the better the insulation, the colder that surface gets. If warm
indoor air can reach that cold surface, it will condense there, inside
the wall or the roof, where nothing dries and nobody looks.

What grows next is not in doubt. EPA is clear that mould grows on
virtually any organic material as long as moisture and oxygen are
present, and that wet building materials must be dried within 24 to 48
hours to avoid mould growth. A wall cavity that condenses every cold
night is never dry for 24 hours. EPA's recommendation for the air in
the room is to keep indoor relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally
between 30 and 50 percent.

The building science answer is to stop the moist air getting in, and to
let whatever does get in dry out in one direction. HUD's standard at
24 CFR 3280.504 is the clearest short statement of it in federal
regulation. Ceilings in its two colder zones must have a vapor retarder
of not more than 1 perm installed **on the living space side** of the
roof cavity. Exterior walls must use one of several methods: a vapor
barrier of not more than 1 perm on the living space side; or unvented
cavities with exterior covering and sheathing whose combined permeance
is **not less than** 5.0 perms, so the cavity can dry outward; or
cavities ventilated so condensation can dissipate.

Then read the part that catches people out. For homes built for humid
climates, the same regulation puts the vapor retarder on the
**exterior** side of the wall insulation instead, and requires the
interior finish to be at least 5.0 perms so the wall can dry inward.

The rule to take away is this: a vapor retarder goes on the warm side,
and which side is warm depends on your climate. A layer that is
correct in a cold climate is a moisture trap in a hot humid one. If you
are unsure which situation you are in, the answer is to ask someone
local who knows, not to guess, and not to install two of them on
opposite faces of the same wall, which guarantees that anything that
gets in can never leave.

Three practical signs that you have this problem already, from ENERGY
STAR's list of things to fix before you insulate anything:

- Wet or damp insulation, which means a leaking roof.
- Mouldy or rotted rafters or floor joists.
- Kitchen, bathroom or dryer vents exhausting moist air into the attic
  instead of outdoors. This is common, it is easy to check, and it is
  pumping a household's worth of water vapour into the coldest part of
  the building.

A history of ice dams belongs on that list too. ENERGY STAR treats it
as an indication of serious air leaks: heat escaping into the attic
melts snow on the roof, the water runs down and refreezes at the cold
eaves, and the resulting dam backs water up under the shingles. Their
counterintuitive but correct guidance is that a well insulated attic
should also be well ventilated, so it stays cold in winter.

## Windows

Windows are usually the worst surface in the room, and the reason is
not the frame or the seals. It is that glass is not an insulator.

The Forest Products Laboratory puts glass at a thermal conductivity of
7 Btu in/(h ft2 F). Invert that and a solid inch of glass is about
R-0.14. An ordinary pane is an eighth of an inch thick, so the glass in
a single glazed window contributes something in the region of R-0.02.
Effectively nothing. Compare that against an attic that ENERGY STAR
wants at R-49 to R-60 and you can see the scale of the mismatch.

Whatever a window does achieve comes from the air or gas trapped
between panes, the low emissivity coatings on the glass surfaces, and
the frame, not from the glass. That is why glazing count and coatings
matter so much and why the industry sells windows on U-factor. The
federal standard for manufactured homes simply requires it: under 24
CFR 3280.506(d), homes built for the coldest of its three zones must be
factory equipped with storm windows or insulating glass.

ENERGY STAR's practical guidance, for when you are buying:

- Certified windows, doors and skylights can shrink energy bills by an
  average of up to 13 percent on heating and cooling costs nationwide,
  compared with non certified products.
- For north facing windows, which get little direct sun, buy the lowest
  U-factor you can afford, because heat loss is all that is happening
  there.
- In a cold climate, a south facing window ideally has a higher solar
  heat gain coefficient, so it collects free sun, together with a low
  U-factor so it does not give the heat back at night.
- East and west windows should have a low solar heat gain coefficient
  or be shaded, especially west facing ones, which take the summer sun
  at the hottest part of the day.
- Even certified windows can suffer condensation in cold weather. If
  yours run with water, that is the dew point section above, showing up
  on the coldest surface in the room. It is information, not a defect.

If you cannot replace windows, and most people cannot, that is what the
next section is for.

## How much insulation you need depends on where you live

There is no universal right answer, and anyone who gives you one
without asking where you live is guessing. The United States is divided
into eight climate zones by temperature, each subdivided into three
moisture regimes labelled A for moist, B for dry and C for marine. That
map was built for the Department of Energy by Pacific Northwest
National Laboratory from analysis of 4,775 US weather sites, first
adopted in the 2004 IECC supplement, and adopted by ENERGY STAR for
Homes in 2006. The zones follow county boundaries specifically so that
you can look yours up by name.

**To find your zone**, use the Department of Energy's Building America
county guide listed in the sources below. It names every county in the
United States and gives its region. ENERGY STAR's R-value page carries
the same map with the recommended levels next to it.

ENERGY STAR's recommendations for retrofitting existing wood framed
buildings, which follow the 2021 IECC Residential Provisions Table
R402.1.3, run like this:

| Zone | Attic if uninsulated | Attic if 3 to 4 inches already | Floor |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | R30 | R25 | R13 |
| 2 | R49 | R38 | R13 |
| 3 | R49 | R38 | R19 |
| 4A and 4B | R60 | R49 | R19 |
| 4C, 5 and 6 | R60 | R49 | R30 |
| 7 and 8 | R60 | R49 | R38 |

**For the Pacific Northwest**, meaning western Washington and the
western side of Oregon, the zone is 4C, the marine zone. Building
America's county table puts King, Kitsap, Pierce, Snohomish, Thurston,
Whatcom, Skagit, Clallam, Jefferson, Mason, Grays Harbor, Pacific,
Lewis, Cowlitz, Wahkiakum and Clark counties in the marine region,
while the counties east of the Cascades are classed as cold. A marine
climate is defined there by a coldest month averaging between 27 F and
65 F, a warmest month averaging under 72 F, at least four months
averaging above 50 F, and a dry summer, specifically that the wettest
month of the cold season gets at least three times the rain of the
driest month of the rest of the year. If that sounds like where you
live, you are in 4C.

So for a house in western Washington, the targets are:

- **Attic:** R60 if it is currently uninsulated, R49 if it already has
  3 to 4 inches.
- **Floor:** R30.
- **Basement or crawlspace wall:** R15 of insulative wall sheathing, or
  an R19 batt.
- **Above grade walls, when the siding comes off anyway:** blow the
  empty cavity full, and add R5 to R10 of insulative sheathing beneath
  the new siding. If the wall is already an insulated 2x4 wall, add R10
  of sheathing.

Notice that the marine zone gets the same attic target as genuinely
cold zones 5 and 6 and the same floor target, even though its winters
are mild. Mild and long still adds up, and the wet part of a marine
climate is exactly the condition that makes the moisture section above
matter most.

## If you have very little money

In rough order of value returned per dollar. This ordering is a rule of
thumb built from what the sources below prioritise, not a measured
ranking.

1. **Find the big attic holes and plug them.** Cost is a few tubes of
   caulk and a couple of cans of foam. Chasing the large openings where
   walls meet the attic floor, at dropped soffits and behind kneewalls,
   is the highest return work in the entire subject.
2. **Weatherstrip the attic hatch and the exterior doors.** Hours, not
   days, and very little money.
3. **Seal the rim joist in the basement or crawlspace.** This stops the
   cold air being drawn in at the bottom to replace what left at the
   top.
4. **Check that the bathroom and dryer vents actually go outside.** Free
   to check. If they do not, fixing it protects the building itself,
   not just the bill.
5. **Plastic film over the worst windows for the winter.** ENERGY STAR
   publishes this as a do it yourself project for a reason: it is the
   cheapest thing that measurably helps the worst surface in the room,
   and the trapped air layer is doing the same job a second pane of
   glass would.
6. **Then add attic insulation,** once the air sealing is done. It is
   the easiest place in the house to add insulation and the one where
   depth is cheapest.
7. **Seal and insulate any ducts running through unheated space,**
   worth up to 20 percent of system efficiency on ENERGY STAR's figure.
8. **Walls and windows last,** because they cost the most per unit of
   improvement and, in the case of walls, are best done when the siding
   is coming off anyway for another reason.

Everything from 1 to 5 is achievable in a weekend for the price of a
tank of fuel. Nothing on that list requires a contractor, and nothing
on it can be undone by moving house, because by then it has already
paid you back.

## What can go wrong

- **You insulated and the bill barely moved.** Almost always air
  sealing that was never done, or done after the insulation buried the
  holes. Insulation slows conduction; it does not stop air.
- **One room is still cold.** Look for a bypass rather than thin
  insulation: a dropped ceiling, a kneewall with an open back, a
  cantilevered floor over outside air, or ducts running through an
  unheated attic.
- **The windows stream with water now.** You tightened the house, so
  the moisture that used to leak away is staying in. Manage it with
  ventilation and humidity, not by reopening the leaks. Keep indoor
  relative humidity below 60 percent, ideally 30 to 50, per EPA.
- **There is mould in a corner that was never mouldy before.** Same
  cause. Cold corners behind furniture are one of the places EPA
  specifically names, because the furniture blocks the room's warm air
  from reaching the wall and drops that surface below the dew point.
  Pull the furniture off the wall and fix the humidity.
- **Ice dams appeared or got worse.** Heat is still escaping into the
  attic and the attic is too warm. This is an air sealing problem
  showing up on the roof, and it can also mean your soffit vents got
  covered.
- **The new insulation looks dirty already.** Air is still moving
  through it. Find the hole.
- **You bought the highest R per inch product and the wall still tests
  poorly.** Check the studs. A continuous exterior layer usually beats
  a premium cavity material, because it is the only layer the framing
  cannot bypass.
- **The batts are bulging or crammed.** Compressed batts lose R-value
  on a published federal curve: half thickness means 61 percent of the
  rating. Cut them to fit rather than forcing them.
- **The furnace or water heater smells or the CO alarm goes off after
  the work.** Stop and call someone qualified now. A tighter house
  changes how combustion appliances vent, and this is the one failure
  mode in this guide that can kill you the same night.

## How you know it worked

- You can say which of the three heat paths you were addressing with
  each thing you did, and you did not try to fix air movement with
  insulation.
- The air sealing happened before the insulating, and you plugged the
  large openings rather than chasing every small one.
- You know your climate zone by name, you looked it up by county, and
  your targets came from the table for that zone rather than from a
  general recommendation.
- Nothing is compressed, nothing covers a soffit vent, and nothing sits
  against a non-IC recessed light.
- You know which side of your walls the vapor retarder belongs on and
  why, and there is not a second one on the opposite face.
- The bathroom and dryer vents discharge outdoors.
- Your combustion appliances have been checked since the house got
  tighter.
- The house is warmer at the same thermostat setting, and the cold
  side of the room is gone.

When those are all true you have not just lowered a bill. You have
changed what the building costs to run for the rest of its life, and
you understand the mechanism well enough to judge the next quote you
are given. Recognising a thermal short, knowing that a surface below
the dew point will wet itself, and understanding that a sealed envelope
needs deliberate ventilation are the same three ideas behind every
building science decision you will ever be asked to make.

### A note on what is not sourced here

No federal publication gives a single reliable percentage breakdown of
heat loss by building surface, so this guide does not quote one. The
ordering in the section on what to do with very little money is a rule
of thumb assembled from the priorities in the sources above, not a
measured ranking. The worked example comparing air leakage to shell
loss uses dimensions chosen for illustration; both coefficients in it
are HUD's.
