# Cold

Someone is cold and not acting right. Before you read another word:

1. **Get them out of the wind and out of the wet.** A building, a
   vehicle, a tent, the lee side of anything. Wind and wet are what turn
   survivable cold into hypothermia, and neither of them stops while you
   think.
2. **Get the wet clothes off and dry cover on.** CDC's order is: warm
   shelter, remove wet clothing, warm the centre of the body, which
   means chest, neck, head and groin. Not the hands. Not the feet.
3. **Get them off the ground.** The National Park Service says to
   "Insulate the body from the ground using any material available such
   as clothing, space blanket, etc." As a **rule of thumb**, ground
   takes heat out of a person faster than air does; the sourced part is
   the instruction, and what it means in practice is that a casualty
   lying on cold ground with blankets piled on top is still losing.
4. **Handle them gently, and do not let them walk.** This is the one
   that is not obvious and the one that kills. It is explained two
   sections down. Move them slowly, keep them flat, do not let them walk
   it off, do not rub them.
5. **Call for help.** MedlinePlus says to call 911 if the person is
   confused or has trouble thinking. CDC says a body temperature below
   95 F (35 C) needs medical attention immediately.

Everything after this is the explanation. Do those five things first.

One honest boundary. **This guide is not a substitute for medical care,
and nothing in it qualifies you to manage severe hypothermia on your
own.** Severe hypothermia is an intensive-care problem. What a person
without training can genuinely do is stop the heat loss, avoid the
handful of instinctive actions that make things worse, and get the
casualty to people who can rewarm them properly. That is not a small
job. Done right it is most of the outcome.

Two companions carry parts of this subject, so they are not repeated
here. Cold water is its own emergency on a much faster clock, and
[Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) covers cold shock, the gasping
reflex and the 1-10-1 rule in full. The cold dark house after a
windstorm, and the carbon monoxide deaths that come from trying to heat
it, are in
[The Hazards Your Place Actually Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live).

## What you can actually see

Hypothermia is defined by core temperature, and you will not have a core
temperature. You will have a person in front of you. So here it is by
what you can observe.

**The shivering stage.** NIOSH lists the early symptoms as shivering,
fatigue, loss of coordination, and confusion and disorientation. CDC's
list for adults is shivering, exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands,
memory loss, slurred speech and drowsiness. For infants, CDC says
"bright red, cold skin" and very low energy, which is worth knowing
because a baby does not shiver the way an adult does.

Shivering is the body working. It is a good sign in the sense that the
furnace is still lit. It is a bad sign in the sense that the furnace
should not have needed to light.

**The umbles.** The National Park Service teaches the four signs with a
mnemonic: "The four main signs of hypothermia can be easily remembered
using the mnemonic device 'umbles'. These signs include: stumbles,
mumbles, fumbles, and grumbles." The same guide notes that "These signs
indicate changes in motor coordination and levels of consciousness."

That is the most useful field test there is, because you do not need
equipment for it. Can they walk a straight line, speak clearly, work a
zip or a buckle, and hold a civil conversation? Losing any of those is a
neurological sign, and a neurological sign means the cold has reached
the brain.

**Denial belongs on the symptom list.** Point Reyes National Seashore
puts it plainly: "Victims of hypothermia often deny that they are cold
and often attempt to reassure others that they are 'Okay.'" Rocky
Mountain National Park adds that someone with hypothermia "is not aware
of their condition because symptoms begin gradually and their confused
state hinders self-awareness." So the patient's own report is the least
reliable evidence you have. Trust the umbles over the reassurance.

**The stage where shivering stops.** This is the one people misread.
NIOSH's late symptoms are: no shivering, blue skin, dilated pupils,
slowed pulse and breathing, loss of consciousness. MedlinePlus makes the
same point about shivering: it describes shivering that cannot be
controlled as a symptom, and notes that at extremely low body temperatures
the shivering may stop altogether.

Read that again with the person in front of you. They were shivering
violently. Now they are calm and still. Nothing improved. The body ran
out of the fuel and the nerve signalling that shivering needs, and it
gave up on the one defence it had. **Shivering stopping is a
deterioration, not a recovery**, and it is the moment the problem
becomes an emergency rather than a discomfort.

The published staging behind this, for context rather than for you to
apply: the Swiss system used in the clinical literature runs from HT I,
"Conscious, shivering," through HT II, "Impaired consciousness; may or
may not be shivering," to HT III, "Unconscious; vital signs present,"
and HT IV, "Apparent death; vital signs absent." Note that the staging
the professionals use is also built on what can be observed, because
they cannot measure a core temperature in a snowdrift either. StatPearls
places the loss of shivering at a core temperature of about 30 to 32 C.

**Paradoxical undressing.** At some point in deep hypothermia, some
people take their clothes off. It is real and it is common enough to
have been counted. A study of 207 fatal hypothermia cases in a
sub-arctic region found "Some degree of paradoxical undressing was
documented in 63 of 207 (30%) cases," most often shoes, then upper body
clothing, then lower. The authors attribute it to "a combination of
vasodilatation with consequent skin-warming, and diminished mental
capacity," reasoning that "a sudden opening of previously constricted
peripheral vessels could induce a sense of warmth, and, hence, a desire
to undress."

Two practical consequences. If you find someone cold and partly
undressed, do not assume assault or intoxication as the explanation and
do not let that assumption slow the rewarming. And if someone you are
with starts shedding layers in the cold and telling you they feel hot,
that is not them warming up. That is a late sign.

## Nobody is dead until they are warm and dead

This is the single most counterintuitive fact about cold, it is real
clinical guidance rather than folklore, and the federal sources say it
in plain words.

MedlinePlus is explicit on the point: **a person found lying motionless in
the cold must not be assumed to be dead.** CDC's winter weather guidance says the same
thing operationally: give CPR and keep giving it, because people who
appear dead from hypothermia have been revived.

The clinical reasoning is in the 2021 international update on accidental
hypothermia. In a hypothermic patient, "rigidity (apparent rigor mortis)
is not a reliable sign of death." And the list of findings that do
**not** rule out resuscitation is remarkable: "asystole, unwitnessed CA,
low core temperature, long no-flow or low-flow time, fixed, dilated
pupils, hypocapnia, old age, or trauma (even major trauma)." Every one
of those is something a bystander, and for that matter a paramedic,
would ordinarily read as death.

Cold slows the metabolism of the brain and heart enormously. That is the
same effect that surgeons deliberately induce for some operations. A
person cooled to the point of apparent death may have an intact brain
that is simply running very slowly, and that brain can come back.

What this means for you: **start CPR and keep going.** The decision that
someone cold is beyond help belongs to a clinician who has rewarmed
them, not to you at the scene and not to the first responder either. The
guidance does list genuine stopping conditions, such as a valid
do-not-resuscitate order or clear signs of irreversible death like fixed
dependent lividity, but those are deliberately narrow and none of them
is "they look dead."

## Why gentle handling matters

A severely hypothermic heart is electrically unstable. Move it roughly
and it can go into a fatal rhythm. The clinical term for this is rescue
collapse, and the 2021 update defines it as "Cardiocirculatory collapse
during extrication or transfer of hypothermic patients," attributed
"most frequently, by mechanical stimuli such as sudden movement."

The scale of it: **"rescue collapse appears to double the risk of death
in severely hypothermic patients."** The instruction that follows is
"Patients should be transported gently and horizontally to avoid
triggering hypothermic CA." A 2003 review in CMAJ gives the same
instruction from the arrhythmia angle: "Because of the risk of
arrhythmia, patients should have cardiac monitoring and should be
transferred gently." The National Park Service gives the plain-language
version: "Be gentle. Sudden movements can cause severe pain."

So: no shaking them awake. No hauling them upright. No slinging them
over a shoulder. No jogging them to the truck. Keep them flat. If they
cannot walk, "A patient who is unable to walk, should be placed in a
horizontal position on a stretcher."

And do not let them exercise their way warm. Walking generates heat, but
"Walking increases heat production but increases afterdrop." The 2021
guidance is that a hypothermic patient found lying down should not be
allowed to walk until they have taken calories and been allowed to
shiver for about 30 minutes.

## Rewarming: what helps and what harms

**Afterdrop** is the thing that makes rewarming counterintuitive. When a
body gets cold, it shuts blood out of the arms and legs to protect the
core, and those limbs get very cold. The 2021 update describes what
happens next: "After extrication from a cold environment, cooling
continues by redistribution of the heat from the warm core to the cold
periphery." The CMAJ review says it more briefly: an afterdrop "may
occur when blood supply to the cold periphery is recirculated to the
core."

In other words, the limbs are a reservoir of cold blood. Open them up
and that cold blood comes back to the heart, and the core gets colder
after the rescue than it was during it.

That single mechanism explains almost every rewarming error.

**A hot bath is wrong.** It dilates every peripheral vessel at once,
which is the fastest possible way to flush cold blood into an unstable
heart. MedlinePlus is direct about it, saying not to apply direct heat and
naming hot water, a heating pad and a heat lamp as the things to avoid. The Park Service guidance says
direct heat "can do extreme damage to the skin" and to use slow methods
instead.

**Rubbing the limbs is wrong**, for the same reason plus a second one.
It drives cold blood back to the core, and on frostbitten tissue it
physically destroys cells. The military guidance for cold injury is
explicit: "Rubbing the affected areas is to be avoided as this will
further traumatize the skin."

**Alcohol is wrong**, and the reason is worth knowing because the
folklore is so strong. The CMAJ review sets out three separate
mechanisms: "Alcohol and sedative drugs dull mental awareness of cold
and impair the judgement necessary to seek shelter or put on warm
clothing. In addition, alcohol inhibits shivering, perhaps by causing
hypoglycemia, and causes cutaneous vasodilatation, which counteracts the
thermoregulatory response." So it stops you noticing, stops you
shivering, and opens the vessels that were protecting your core. The
feeling of warmth is the heat leaving. CDC, NIOSH, MedlinePlus and the
Park Service all say the same thing in one line: do not give alcohol.

**What does help**, from CDC and NIOSH, in this order:

- Get them somewhere warm and dry.
- Remove wet clothing.
- Warm the centre of the body first: chest, neck, head and groin. An
  electric blanket if you have one, or skin-to-skin contact under loose
  dry layers of blankets, clothing, towels or sheets.
- If they are conscious and alert, warm drinks help. Not alcoholic, and
  nothing by mouth for someone who is not fully awake.
- Once they are warming, keep them dry and wrap the whole body including
  the head and neck.
- If there is no pulse, start CPR.

Notice that the warming is aimed at the trunk. The CMAJ review explains
why heat goes "to the trunk rather than the extremities" in exactly the
afterdrop terms above. Heat packs, where used, go on the trunk and
should not be put directly against skin.

The recurring beginner's instinct is to warm the parts that feel coldest
and complain loudest, which are the hands and feet. Those are the last
parts to warm, not the first.

## Wet feet, which is the injury that actually happens in a wet climate

In a mild marine climate, frostbite is rare and non-freezing cold injury
is not. It gets the most space here for that reason.

**Trench foot, also called immersion foot, does not require freezing and
barely requires cold.** NIOSH states that it "can occur at temperatures
as high as 60 F if the feet are constantly wet." Sixty Fahrenheit is
about 15.6 C. That is an ordinary autumn afternoon, and that is the
point.

The military guideline is blunt about the cause: it is "a syndrome
related to prolonged exposure to moisture. Classically it has been
associated with cold water, but can happen in all climates." The timing
matters: "The syndrome generally happens slower in warm water, taking
approximately 48 hrs, than cold water (earliest estimate 12 hours)."
Twelve hours of cold wet feet is one long day of work in the rain.

**What it looks like**, from the same guideline: it begins as "water
logging of the feet, most pronounced in the soles." Then, with
continuing exposure, "the foot becomes hyperemic, mottled, painful and
edematous, gradually progressing into blistering, hypoperfusion,
ulceration and gangrene." NIOSH's symptom list is the practical version:
reddening of the skin, numbness, leg cramps, swelling, tingling pain,
blisters or ulcers, bleeding under the skin, and gangrene, where "foot
may turn dark purple, blue, or gray."

**Treatment is not what you would guess.** For frostbite you immerse in
warm water. For immersion foot you do the opposite. The military
guideline: rewarming "is done by air drying at room temperature," and
its quality metric for the condition is that patients "have dry heat
administered." Do not soak these feet. NIOSH's field version: remove
shoes and boots and wet socks, dry the feet, and "Avoid walking on feet,
as this may cause tissue damage."

That last one is easy to get wrong because the person can usually still
walk. Walking on a foot in this state damages it further, and the damage
can be permanent.

**Chilblains** are the mild relative and they are worth naming so you do
not mistake them for something worse. NIOSH describes them as caused by
"repeated exposure of skin to temperatures just above freezing to as
high as 60 F," producing redness, itching, possible blistering,
inflammation, and in severe cases ulceration. Their first aid is: avoid
scratching, warm the skin slowly, keep any blisters clean and covered.

**Prevention is dry socks and a chance to dry out.** That is the entire
intervention and it is nearly free. Spare socks in a pocket. Boots that
actually keep water out. Feet out of wet boots at breaks. NIOSH's
prevention list for cold work includes waterproof insulated boots and
carrying extra socks, and that unglamorous line is the one that matters
most in a rainy climate.

## Frostbite

Frostbite is tissue freezing. It needs an air temperature well below
freezing, which is why it is the rarer problem in a mild wet climate and
the more likely problem on a clear cold night inland or at altitude.

**Check the core before the fingers.** The military guideline is
explicit that hypothermia comes first: "patients are likely hypothermic
and should be warmed expediently before focusing on cold injuries of the
extremities." MedlinePlus gives the same order. A warm-handed corpse is
not the goal.

**What it looks like, by depth.** The military guideline uses four
degrees:

- **First degree.** Superficial skin injury. Numbness, then pain on
  rewarming, redness, sometimes blue mottling, swelling, and peeling
  that starts around day 5.
- **Second degree.** Partial thickness. Everything above plus blistering
  surrounded by redness and swelling, appearing around day 2.
- **Third degree.** The full thickness of the skin and into the tissue
  beneath. Skin that is bluish to black and will not deform. Blood-filled
  blisters. Ulceration later.
- **Fourth degree.** As above but through to bone. The area may be cold
  to the touch and "may feel stiff or woody."

CDC's version for spotting it early: "Redness or pain in any skin area
may be the first sign of frostbite," followed by "a white or
grayish-yellow skin area," "skin that feels unusually firm or waxy," and
numbness. The parts that go first are the nose, ears, cheeks, chin,
fingers and toes.

**The grade is not knowable at the time.** The military guideline warns
that "The ultimate grade will not be truly known until treatment has
been attempted and a period of time has passed." Do not write anyone off
at the scene on the basis of how bad it looks.

**Rewarming, if and only if it can be finished.** The sourced number is
consistent across three of them. The military guideline: "Rapid active
re-warming is done in 104-108 F (40-42 C) water for 15- 30 minutes as
long as care can occur in an environment where there is no risk of
refreezing." MedlinePlus gives the same window, recommending water between
104 and 108 degrees F, which is 40 to 42.2 C, with the part soaked for 15
to 30 minutes. The CMAJ review:
"Rapid rewarming of a frostbitten extremity in a bath of water between
40 C and 42 C for 15 to 30 minutes may minimize tissue loss."

That range is narrow on purpose. The military guideline explains both
edges: "The temperature is important, as there is reduced effect with
cooler temperatures and higher temperatures will cause burns." Use a
thermometer. Water that feels comfortably hot to your hand is already
too hot for tissue that cannot feel it.

Expect it to hurt. The guideline notes the process "can be expected to
be very painful," and that the temptation to warm slowly because it is
better tolerated is a mistake, since "tissue survival is improved with
rapid re-warming."

**Do not thaw what may refreeze.** MedlinePlus lists among the things
not to do: thaw a frostbitten area if it cannot be kept thawed. The
military guideline is stronger: "It is imperative to prevent refreezing
of the impacted extremity as it results in more tissue damage," and
"refreezing after partial thawing will result in more severe injury." A
frozen foot walked out to a road is a bad outcome. A thawed foot that
refreezes on the way is a worse one.

**The rest of the do-not list**, from CDC and MedlinePlus:

- Do not rub or massage the area.
- Do not use a fireplace, heat lamp, radiator or stove.
- Do not use a heating pad or electric blanket on frostbite.
- Do not walk on frostbitten feet or toes unless you have no choice.
- Do not break the blisters.

The heating pad item looks like it contradicts the hypothermia advice,
where CDC does recommend an electric blanket. Both are right, and the
difference is what you are heating. An electric blanket on the trunk of
a hypothermic person warms a core that can still feel and complain.
Anything hot against frostbitten tissue is being applied to skin that
has lost sensation, and NIOSH gives the reason directly: affected areas
"are numb and can be easily burned." A numb patient cannot tell you that
you are burning them. If you want the other half of that problem, see
[Burns](/library#treating-burns).

## Wind chill, and what the chart actually says

Wind chill is one of the most misused numbers in weather reporting, so
it is worth being precise.

**What it is.** The National Weather Service defines it as "how cold
people and animals feel when outside," based on "the rate of heat loss
from exposed skin caused by wind and cold." It is a heat-loss index for
living skin, not an air temperature.

**What it is not.** From the NWS: "The only effect wind chill has on
inanimate objects, such as car radiators and water pipes, is to shorten
the amount of time for the object to cool. The inanimate object will not
cool below the actual air temperature." Wind makes things reach the air
temperature faster. It does not make them colder than the air. Your
pipes do not care about the wind chill number, and neither does a
thermometer.

**The formula**, published by the NWS and effective 1 November 2001, is:

```
Wind Chill (F) = 35.74 + 0.6215T - 35.75(V^0.16) + 0.4275T(V^0.16)
```

where T is air temperature in Fahrenheit and V is wind speed in mph. The
NWS notes it applies for temperatures at or below 50 F and wind speeds
above 3 mph.

**The frostbite bands.** The NWS chart shades three regions, labelled 30
minutes, 10 minutes and 5 minutes, meaning the approximate time in which
exposed skin can freeze. The NWS gives one worked example in words: "If
the temperature is 0 F and the wind is blowing at 15 mph, the wind chill
is -19 F. At this wind chill temperature, exposed skin can freeze in 30
minutes."

Reading the shading off the published chart, the coldest air temperature
at which each band begins, for a few representative wind speeds:

| Wind | 30 min band begins at | 10 min | 5 min |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 mph | -5 F | -35 F | not reached down to -40 F |
| 15 mph | 5 F | -15 F | -35 F |
| 30 mph | 10 F | -5 F | -20 F |
| 60 mph | 15 F | 5 F | -5 F |

The shape of that table is the useful part. **Wind buys the cold a great
deal of ground once you are already below freezing, and almost none
above it.** Going from 5 mph to 60 mph moves the 30 minute threshold
only from -5 F to 15 F, twenty degrees. But at a fixed -20 F, going from
5 mph to 30 mph moves you from skin freezing in 30 minutes to skin
freezing in 5. Same air, six times the hurry.

**And here is the number that matters in a mild wet climate.** Across
the entire published chart, at every wind speed up to 60 mph, no
frostbite band appears at any air temperature warmer than 15 F, about
-9 C. Silverdale's January mean low is 2.0 C, about 35.6 F. Its coldest
recorded temperature is -13.9 C, about 7 F, and its winter design
temperature is -6 C, about 21 F. Those and the rest of the local setting
are in
[Where You Are: Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa).

So in an ordinary local winter, **frostbite is essentially off the
table, and that is exactly why the wind chill number is dangerous here.**
A 35 F day with a 60 mph wind, which is well inside this area's
windstorm record, produces a wind chill of about 17 F by the NWS
formula. Damaging winter windstorms here are near-annual and the
recorded peak is 77 mph, on 14 December 2000, so this is an ordinary bad
day rather than an extreme. That is a genuinely alarming-sounding number
and skin still
cannot freeze in it. What the wind is doing at that temperature is
stripping heat out of a whole person at a rate their metabolism cannot
match. The injury on offer is hypothermia, not frostbite, and the chart
does not have a band for that.

## Who this actually happens to

**Most hypothermia deaths are not in the wilderness.** CDC recorded
1,024 deaths attributed to excessive cold or hypothermia in the United
States in 2023, with the majority in January and February and November
and December, January highest at 19.9 percent. In the earlier national
review covering 1999 to 2002, 4,607 death certificates carried
hypothermia-related diagnoses, 49 percent of the deceased were over 65,
67 percent were male, and only 22 percent were married against 52
percent of the general population. Of those deaths, "Most deaths were
not work related (63%); 23% of affected persons were at home when they
became hypothermic," and an Alabama study cited in the same report found
that "almost half of the hypothermia-related deaths occurred indoors."

Even in a genuinely cold and outdoors-heavy setting, the picture is less
like mountaineering than you would expect. In the sub-arctic series of
207 fatal cases, 93 percent were found outdoors, but "a large proportion
(81/207) of events occurred within close proximity to other people and
buildings," many within 500 metres of a structure. Ethanol was found in
43 percent of the victims. The mean age was 62.

The population this kills is old, often alone, frequently indoors in a
house that has lost its heat, and frequently within sight of help.

**Who is at higher risk**, from CDC and MedlinePlus:

- Older adults without adequate food, clothing or heating
- Infants sleeping in cold bedrooms
- People who stay outdoors for long periods, including people who are
  unhoused, and outdoor workers
- People drinking alcohol in excess or using drugs
- People with chronic illness, especially heart and circulation problems
- People who are malnourished or exhausted
- People taking certain medications

NIOSH adds a point that transfers directly to a mild climate: "In
regions unaccustomed to winter weather, near freezing temperatures are
considered factors for cold stress." A place that is never very cold is
a place where nobody owns the right coat.

## Prevention, which is where the real gains are

**Cold plus wet plus wind is a different problem from cold.** CDC states
it outright: "Hypothermia often occurs at very cold temperatures, but
can occur at cool temperatures (above 40 F), if a person is wet (from
rain, sweat or cold water) and becomes chilled." Forty Fahrenheit is
about 4.4 C. Silverdale's mean low is under 4.4 C for five months of the
year, and December and January together bring about 488 mm of rain out
of an annual 1,446 mm.

Read those two facts together. The local winter is a machine for
producing the exact combination CDC names: not very cold, very wet, and
regularly windy. That is why this guide spends more words on wet feet
than on frostbite.

**Cotton is the problem.** Rocky Mountain National Park: "Avoid clothing
that will absorb and hold water, like cotton and jeans." Cotton holds
water against you, loses its loft when wet, and then evaporates that
water using your body heat. A cotton shirt under a rain jacket, soaked
with your own sweat, is a cooling system you are wearing. Wool and
synthetics keep some insulating value wet; cotton keeps almost none.

**Layers, loose ones.** NIOSH: "Wear several layers of loose clothing
for insulation," and specifically that "Tight clothing reduces blood
circulation to the extremities." A tight boot is a cold foot. So the
extra pair of socks that does not fit in the boot is not extra
insulation, it is a circulation problem.

The rest of the NIOSH list is ordinary and complete: waterproof
insulated boots, a hat, protection for ears, face, hands and feet, warm
locations for breaks, limits on time outside, and a bag with spare
socks, gloves, hat, jacket and a thermos of something hot.

**Stay fed.** Shivering is muscular work and it runs on food. MedlinePlus
lists going without enough food, drink or rest in cold weather among the
causes of hypothermia, in the same breath as falling into cold water. The
2021 clinical guidance treats calories as part of the recovery, saying a
patient should have taken calories and shivered for about 30 minutes
before being allowed to walk.

**Stay hydrated.** MedlinePlus names inadequate fluids as a cause
alongside inadequate food. Cold suppresses thirst and cold air is dry,
so the mistake is easy.

**Keep the ground in mind.** Everything in the emergency section about
insulating from the ground applies before the emergency too. A bed roll
without a sleeping pad, a folding chair versus a rock, a stop on wet
grass versus a stop on a log: those are the same decision made in
advance and for free.

If you want the physics of where household heat goes and how to stop it
leaving, that is a separate subject and it has its own guide:
[Insulation and Heat Loss](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss).

## You own this when

- Your first five actions are out of the wind, out of the wet, off the
  ground, gently, and call, and you can do them without reading.
- You warm the chest, neck, head and groin, not the hands and feet, and
  you can say why.
- You know that shivering stopping is a deterioration.
- You would start CPR on someone cold who looks dead, and you know that
  the decision to stop is not yours.
- You know that a hot bath, rubbing the limbs and a drink are three
  versions of the same mistake, and that the mistake is called afterdrop.
- You would not let a hypothermic person walk to the vehicle.
- You treat immersion foot by drying it in air, and frostbite by
  immersing it in water at 104 to 108 F, and you do not confuse the two.
- You would not thaw a frozen foot you could not keep thawed.
- You read a wind chill number as a rate of heat loss from a person, not
  as a temperature anything reaches.
- You carry spare socks in the rain.

The one carrying the most weight is the sixth. Almost everything on this
list is knowledge. That one is a decision you will have to make while a
cold, confused adult is insisting they are fine and would rather just
walk, and it has to have been made in advance.

### Computed rather than quoted

- The wind chill value of about 17 F for 35 F air with a 60 mph wind was
  computed here from the National Weather Service formula above, not
  read from a published table. The formula is valid in that range, being
  at or below 50 F and above 3 mph.
- The frostbite band table was read off the shading of the published NWS
  wind chill chart rather than from a written list, because the NWS
  publishes the bands graphically. It reproduces the one example the NWS
  does state in words, 0 F with a 15 mph wind falling in the 30 minute
  band, which is the check that it was read correctly.

### Not sourced to any authority

- **The claim that ground conducts heat out of a person faster than air
  does.** It is the reason every source above tells you to insulate from
  the ground, but none of the federal sources consulted states the
  physical comparison, so treat the mechanism as a **rule of thumb** and
  the instruction, which is sourced to the National Park Service, as the
  part to act on.
- **The advice to carry spare socks specifically.** NIOSH's prevention
  list includes carrying extra socks, so the practice is sourced; the
  emphasis placed on it here for a wet marine climate is this guide's
  own judgement, drawn from the sourced fact that immersion foot needs
  only wet feet and about 12 hours.
