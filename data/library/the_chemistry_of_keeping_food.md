# The Chemistry of Keeping Food

Fresh food is mostly water, and that is the trouble with it. The USDA's
canning guide puts it in one line: "The high percentage of water in most
fresh foods makes them very perishable." It names four ways food goes
off: microorganisms (bacteria, moulds and yeasts) growing in it, the
food's own enzymes at work, reactions with oxygen, and loss of moisture.

Every way of keeping food works on one or more of those four. Salt and
sugar tie up the water. Acid makes the food too acid for the worst
bacteria. Drying takes the water away. Cold slows everything down. Heat
kills. Smoke does a little at the surface. And each of them has a gap,
something it cannot do, which is where people get hurt. This guide is
about both: the chemistry of why each method slows spoilage, and the
plain list of what each one cannot do.

**It contains no recipes, no processing times and no amounts.** Those
live in the tested guides, and the safety of a jar, a crock or a smoked
fish comes from following one of them exactly. The worked example is an
October harvest in Silverdale, Washington, where the simulation is set,
kept five different ways.

It is the reasoning behind several practical guides and does not repeat
them. [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) covers the
bacterium behind botulism, the pH 4.6 line and why a jar cannot be judged
by looking. [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) compares
the methods and [Cold Storage Without a
Fridge](/library#cold-storage-without-a-fridge) covers keeping food cold.
[Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning), [Pressure
Canning](/library#pressure-canning), [Drying Food](/library#drying-food), [Fermenting
Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables) and [Salting, Curing and
Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking) are the tested methods. [Microbes,
Good and Bad](/library#microbes-good-and-bad) introduces the organisms, and
[Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) the difference
between food that has gone off and food that will make you ill.

Its sources are the USDA's Complete Guide to Home Canning and its Food
Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS), the Food and Drug Administration
(FDA), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the US
Geological Survey (USGS), all US federal and in the public domain; and,
restated in our own words, a Pacific Northwest Extension guide to smoking
fish and a report the Institute of Food Technologists prepared for the
FDA. Where something is general practice or our own reading, the text
says so.

## First, what chemistry cannot do for you

- **It cannot replace a tested recipe.** The FDA's handbook of foodborne
  germs explains why: so many things in a food act on each other that
  predictions about whether a microbe will grow in it "can, in general,
  only be made through experimentation." A tested recipe is that
  experiment, already done. Change the salt, the acid, the jar size or
  the time and you are running a new one, on your family (our reading).
- **It cannot be measured in a kitchen.** The two numbers that decide
  most of this subject, water activity and the finished pH of a whole
  jar, are laboratory measurements. [Why Canning Has
  Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains why household pH strips are
  not a safety test.
- **It cannot make a boiling-water canner do a pressure canner's job.**
  The USDA: "Low-acid foods must be processed in a pressure canner to be
  free of botulism risks."
- **It cannot rescue food after the fact.** The FDA's note for consumers:
  "Cooking, freezing, and washing won't necessarily destroy toxins."
  [Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) lists the
  poisons that survive.

## Why fresh food spoils

The USDA's canning guide explains the four causes in a sentence each.
Microbes: "Microorganisms live and multiply quickly on the surfaces of
fresh food and on the inside of bruised, insect-damaged, and diseased
food." Enzymes and oxygen: "Oxygen and enzymes are present throughout
fresh food tissues." Its glossary defines enzymes as "Proteins in food
which accelerate many flavor, color, texture, and nutritional changes,
especially when food is cut, sliced, crushed, bruised, and exposed to
air." Moisture loss is the wilting lettuce and the shrivelled apple.

The four are not equally dangerous. Enzymes, oxygen and drying out
mostly spoil the look, taste and feel of food; microbes are the cause
that can also make people ill ([Telling Spoiled From
Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) separates them). So most of this
guide is about microbes, and about three numbers that decide whether
they can grow.

## The three numbers that decide what grows

The FDA's handbook names them: "Several factors encourage, prevent, or
limit growth of microorganisms in foods; the most important are aw, pH,
and temperature." The first, written aw, is water activity.

### Water activity: how much of the water is free

Water activity is not how wet a food is but how much of its water is
free for anything else to use. The FDA defines it by comparing the
vapour pressure of a food with that of pure water: "A water activity of
0.80 means the vapor pressure is 80 percent of that of pure water." Pure
water is 1.00, and the FDA says "Most foods have a water activity above
0.95 and that will provide sufficient moisture to support the growth of
bacteria, yeasts, and mold."

Dissolve something in the water, salt or sugar, and its molecules hold
on to the water around them. The FDA's handbook: "The microbial cell
must compete with solute molecules for free water molecules. Except for
Staphylococcus aureus, bacteria are rather poor competitors, whereas
molds are excellent competitors." That one sentence explains why salted
and sugared foods resist bacteria but still go mouldy.

The FDA's table of salt solutions shows how much salt it takes:

| Salt in the water | Water activity |
|---|---|
| 0.9 percent | 0.995 |
| 3.5 percent | 0.98 |
| 7 percent | 0.96 |
| 10 percent | 0.94 |
| 16 percent | 0.90 |
| 22 percent | 0.86 |

And its inspection guide gives some foods for comparison: liverwurst
0.96, a cheese spread 0.95, caviar 0.92, salami 0.82, soy sauce 0.80, and
peanut butter and dried milk 0.70.

The numbers that matter, all from the FDA:

- The bacterium behind botulism needs a water activity of about 0.93 to
  grow, and for some foods as high as 0.96 ([Why Canning Has
  Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains it).
- *Staphylococcus aureus* has grown at 0.83 under ideal conditions, and
  most of its strains are highly tolerant of salt and sugar.
- A commercial food controlled to 0.85 or less falls outside the federal
  rules for low-acid canned food. That is a regulatory line for
  factories, not a home target ([Why Canning Has
  Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).

Two things water activity does not do. First, it does not kill. The
FDA's handbook: below the limit for growth, "bacteria do not necessarily
die," and they "may remain dormant, but infectious." Second, it can make
bacteria harder to kill. The same handbook gives an example: at 60 C
(140 F), a tenfold drop in *Salmonella* took 0.18 minutes at a water
activity of 0.995, and 4.3 minutes at 0.94. That is the chemistry behind
the FSIS warning quoted in [Drying Food](/library#drying-food), that bacteria
become much more heat resistant once they are dried, and behind the rule
there that jerky meat is brought to a safe temperature by a tested
method, never by drying alone.

### pH: how acid it is

The USDA's glossary: pH is "A measure of acidity or alkalinity. Values
range from 0 to 14. A food is neutral when its pH is 7.0, lower values
are increasingly more acid; higher values are increasingly more
alkaline." And each step is large. The USGS: "Each number represents a
10-fold change in the acidity/basicness of the water. Water with a pH of
five is ten times more acidic than water having a pH of six." So a food at
pH 3.6 is about ten times as acid as one at 4.6, and about a hundred times
as acid as one at 5.6 (our arithmetic).

The FDA's handbook lists typical pH values for foods. A few:

| Food | pH |
|---|---|
| Lemons | 2.2 to 2.4 |
| Vinegar | 2.0 to 3.4 |
| Dill pickles | 3.2 to 3.5 |
| Jams and jellies | 3.1 to 3.5 |
| Sauerkraut | 3.4 to 3.6 |
| Apples (McIntosh) | 3.34 |
| Tomatoes, whole | 4.2 to 4.9 |
| Carrots | 4.9 to 5.2 |
| Cabbage | 5.2 to 6.0 |
| Salmon | 6.1 to 6.3 |
| Corn | 6.0 to 7.5 |

The FDA warns that the pH of foods "even those of similar types, varies
considerably," so read these as typical, never as a measurement of your
own food. Three things stand out (our reading of the table):

- **The cabbage and the sauerkraut are the same food.** Bacteria made the
  difference, by turning the cabbage's sugar into acid.
- **Tomatoes straddle the line.** The USDA draws it at pH 4.6, and some
  tomatoes sit above it, which is why the tested recipes add acid to every
  jar ([Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning)).
- **Nearly every vegetable, and all meat and fish, is low-acid.** The
  USDA lists red meats, seafood, poultry, milk and all fresh vegetables
  except most tomatoes as low-acid foods.

Every microbe has its own range. The FDA's handbook: "Moving away from
the pH optimum in either direction slows microbial growth." And the pH of
a food can change: "A food may start with a pH that precludes bacterial
growth, but as a result of the metabolism of other microbes (yeasts or
molds), pH shifts may occur and permit bacterial growth." That is why a
mouldy pickle or a scummy ferment is a problem beyond the mould itself
(our reading).

### Temperature: how fast everything happens

The FDA's handbook says each microbe has a minimum, a maximum and an
optimum temperature for growth. FSIS gives the range to fear: bacteria
grow most rapidly between 40 and 140 F, "doubling in number in as little
as 20 minutes." The FDA gives the two settings that matter at home: a
refrigerator at or below 40 F, and a freezer at 0 F.

### The three together

The numbers add up. The FDA's handbook describes a food at pH 5.0 and a
water activity of 0.935, each of which on its own would let the botulism
bacterium grow, that together may not: "Certain processed cheese spreads
take advantage of this fact and are therefore shelf-stable at room
temperature, even though each individual factor would permit the
outgrowth of C. botulinum." Most ways of keeping food stack more than one
barrier like this, and the tested recipe is what says the stack is high
enough (our reading).

## Each method: what it does and what it cannot do

### Salt

**What it does.** It ties up water, as above; the FDA's inspection guide
says water activity "is usually controlled by the use of salt or sugar."
In fish it also draws moisture out: the Pacific Northwest guide to
smoking fish says salt preserves smoked fish by reducing its moisture
(restated). In a ferment it picks the winners. The USDA: "In fermented
foods, salt favors the growth of desirable bacteria while inhibiting the
growth of others." And in a cure it works with nitrite. FSIS: "Nitrite and
salt inhibit the growth of Clostridium botulinum, a deadly microorganism
which can occur in foods under certain situations."

**What it cannot do.**

- Stop *Staphylococcus aureus* (FDA) or moulds. FSIS: "Molds also
  tolerate salt and sugar better than most other food invaders," which is
  why mould grows on ham, bacon and salami.
- Kill the worms in pork and wild game. The CDC: "Curing (salting),
  drying, smoking, or microwaving meat alone does not consistently kill
  the worms."
- Work where it has not reached. The Pacific Northwest guide says that
  without a laboratory test it is hard to be sure a fish has taken up
  enough salt, which is why proper cooking and cold storage are essential
  as well: the salt is still required, but it cannot be the only
  safeguard (restated).
- Make canned vegetables safe. In canning, the USDA says, salt only
  seasons the food. In fermenting it is the opposite, and the USDA warns
  against cutting it: "Caution: Do not attempt to make sauerkraut or
  fermented pickles by cutting back on the salt required."

### Sugar

**What it does.** It ties up water the same way salt does. The USDA:
"Sugar serves as a preserving agent, contributes flavor, and aids in
gelling."

**What it cannot do.**

- Stop moulds and yeasts. The USDA: "Even though sugar helps preserve
  jellies and jams, molds can grow on the surface of these products." It
  reports mycotoxins found in jars of jelly with surface mould, which is
  why jams are heat-processed and sealed and no longer covered with
  paraffin ([Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning)).
- Be reduced at will. The USDA: "Too little sugar prevents gelling and may
  allow yeasts and molds to grow." Reduced-sugar spreads have their own
  tested recipes, and the gelatin-based refrigerator spreads, it says,
  "spoil at room temperature, must be refrigerated, and should be eaten
  within 1 month."
- Make syrup a preservative. In canned fruit, as [Water-Bath
  Canning](/library#water-bath-canning) reports from the USDA's guide, syrup
  helps the fruit keep its flavour, colour and shape but does not prevent
  spoilage.
- Make honey safe for babies. Honey is mostly sugar, and still the CDC
  says: "Do not feed honey to a child who is younger than 1 year old."
  [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains why.

### Acid

**What it does.** It lowers the pH. The USDA: "The acidity level in foods
can be increased by adding lemon juice, citric acid, or vinegar." Or
bacteria make it, as in a ferment: "Native bacteria ferment natural
sugars to lactic acid, a major flavoring and preservative in sauerkraut
and in naturally fermented dills" (USDA). At pH 4.6 or below, the USDA
says, acid foods "contain enough acid to block their growth, or destroy
them more rapidly when heated," meaning the botulism bacteria. Its
glossary defines pickling as "The practice of adding enough vinegar or
lemon juice to a low-acid food to lower its pH to 4.6 or lower."

**What it cannot do.**

- Stop moulds and yeasts. FSIS says moulds "can thrive in high-acid foods
  like jams, jellies, pickles, fruit, and tomatoes," and the USDA says
  pickles spoil from yeasts and moulds as well as enzymes unless they are
  processed in a boiling-water canner (restated).
- Stop every germ. The FDA's handbook says *Shigella* tolerates low pH,
  and can survive and sometimes grow in acid fruits and vegetables.
- Work if it is uneven or short. The USDA: "There must be a minimum,
  uniform level of acid throughout the mixed product to prevent the growth
  of botulinum bacteria." And: "Do not alter vinegar, food, or water
  proportions in a recipe or use a vinegar with unknown acidity."
- Stay put. Yeasts and moulds can raise the pH, as above, so a ferment
  with scum on it is losing its acid ([Fermenting
  Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables)).

### Drying

**What it does.** It takes the water away, and the water activity falls
with it. The FDA: "The amount of available moisture can be reduced to a
point which will inhibit the growth of the organisms." The USDA's
glossary says low moisture controls the botulism bacterium in dried food.

**What it cannot do.**

- Kill. Bacteria in dried food can wait, "dormant, but infectious" (FDA),
  and, as above, survive heat better than in wet food.
- Stop moulds on food that was not dried enough or has taken up damp
  ([Drying Food](/library#drying-food)), because moulds compete for water better
  than bacteria (FDA).
- Make garlic or herbs safe to keep in oil. The CDC's rule for homemade
  flavoured oils draws no line between fresh and dried: "Refrigerate
  homemade oils made with garlic or herbs. Throw the oils away after 4
  days."

### Cold

**What it does.** It slows growth. The FDA: "Keeping foods chilled at
proper temperatures is one of the best ways to prevent or slow the growth
of these bacteria." And freezing stops it: "While freezing does not kill
most bacteria, it does stop bacteria from growing." The USDA's glossary
says freezer temperatures inhibit the botulism bacterium in frozen food.

**What it cannot do.**

- Kill most germs. Most of what went into the freezer comes out with
  the food. (One exception the CDC gives: freezing pork less than 6
  inches thick for 20 days at 5 F kills the worms that cause
  trichinellosis. Wild game is different, below.)
- Stop every microbe. The FDA's handbook names *Listeria* and *Yersinia*
  as bacteria that grow at refrigerator temperatures, and FSIS says moulds
  can grow there too.
- Undo what happened before. The FDA's note for consumers: proper
  refrigeration keeps most bacteria from growing to dangerous numbers, but
  food that already has high numbers when it goes in "could still cause
  illness." Nor, the same note says, does freezing necessarily destroy
  toxins.
- Kill the worms in wild game. The CDC: "Freezing wild game meats may not
  effectively kill all worms because some worms that infect wild game are
  freeze-resistant."
- Stop quality loss. The FDA says frozen food stays safe at 0 F but loses
  quality the longer it is kept.

### Heat

**What it does.** Enough heat for long enough kills. The USDA: "Pressure
does not destroy microorganisms, but high temperatures applied for an
adequate period of time do kill microorganisms." It also stops enzymes:
"Proper blanching or hot-packing practices destroy enzymes and improve
food quality" (USDA glossary). The glossary defines pasteurisation as
"Heating of a specific food enough to destroy the most heat-resistant
pathogenic or disease-causing microorganism known to be associated with
that food." And FSIS says moulds in high-acid foods are easily destroyed
in a boiling-water canner for the recommended time.

**What it cannot do.**

- Destroy botulism spores at boiling. The USDA: "Botulinum spores are very
  hard to destroy at boiling-water temperatures; the higher the canner
  temperature, the more easily they are destroyed." That is the whole
  reason for the pressure canner ([Pressure Canning](/library#pressure-canning)).
- Destroy every toxin: the FDA says cooking won't necessarily destroy them
  ([Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe)).
- Protect food once it cools. FSIS: "Bacteria can be reintroduced to food
  after it is safely cooked."
- Kill norovirus at modest temperatures. The CDC says noroviruses can
  survive 145 F, and that a quick steam will not kill them in shellfish.

In a jar of canned green beans there is no acid, no salt that matters and
no drying: heat is the only barrier, and the seal keeps new germs out
afterwards (our reading of the USDA's guide). That is why every number in
a pressure-canning recipe is followed exactly.

### Smoke

**What it does.** Less than people think. FSIS: "Smoking is slowly cooking
food indirectly over a fire." In hot smoking, the heat does the killing.
The smoke itself carries hundreds of compounds, among them phenols and
volatile acids, according to a review the Institute of Food Technologists
prepared for the FDA. That review found that short cold smoking lowered
*Listeria* on the surface of fish rather than raising it, though in one
study the bacteria grew inside the flesh during cold smoking; it noted,
citing an unpublished personal communication, that the smoke has much
less effect on *Listeria* once the fish's surface has dried into a skin
(a pellicle) before the smoke goes on; and it found that the effect of
liquid smoke on *Listeria* depended entirely on the product, from some
to none (restated).

**What it cannot do.** Preserve food on its own. The Pacific Northwest
guide to smoking fish says smoke itself is not an effective preservative
under most conditions, and that liquid smoke and nitrite are not to be
relied on for safety; what makes home-smoked fish safe is enough salt,
enough heat and storage at 38 F or below (restated). Cold-smoked fish is
never cooked, so it is raw food with a smoky taste ([Salting, Curing and
Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking)). And smoke does not kill the
worms in pork or wild game (CDC, above).

### Keeping air out

**What it does.** It slows the reactions with oxygen and keeps off the
moulds and yeasts that grow at the surface. The USDA lists removing
oxygen among the things proper canning does, and a ferment is kept under
its brine for the same reason ([Fermenting
Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables)). Against browning, the USDA's
glossary names ascorbic acid, the chemical name for vitamin C: "Lemon
juice contains large quantities of ascorbic acid and is commonly used to
prevent browning of peeled, light-colored fruits and vegetables."

**What it cannot do.** Stop the botulism bacterium, which grows only
without air (USDA). A moist, low-acid food sealed away from air at room
temperature is exactly the condition it needs: the CDC's examples are
garlic in oil and potatoes baked in foil, which it says to keep at 140 F
or hotter, or to refrigerate with the foil loosened. Vacuum packing is a
way to keep quality, not a way to keep food safe at room temperature
(our reading; [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) gives the
general test).

## How the barriers combine

Here is the same idea across the foods in the tested guides. The
"barriers" are this guide's summary of what the linked guide does.

| Food | What keeps it | What still needs care |
|---|---|---|
| Sauerkraut | Salt picks the bacteria, the bacteria make the acid, the brine keeps the air off; then cold, or a boiling-water canner, to store it ([Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables)) | The salt by weight; vegetables under the brine; a finished ferment is not shelf-stable on its own |
| Jam | Sugar, the fruit's acid, heat in a boiling-water canner and a seal ([Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning)) | Mould on the surface of an opened jar means it goes |
| Fresh-pack pickles | Vinegar's acid, heat and a seal ([Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning)) | The vinegar's strength and proportions, unaltered |
| Hot-smoked salmon | Salt, heat (150 F, preferably 160 F, held at least 30 minutes) and storage at 38 F or below ([Salting, Curing and Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking)) | The thermometer, not the smoke |
| Jerky | Heat to 160 F (165 F for poultry) by one of the tested ways, and dryness ([Drying Food](/library#drying-food)) | 165 F for venison and other wild game, and no jerky at all from bear, wild boar or meat-eating wild animals; the heating step, which drying does not replace |
| Canned green beans | Heat in a pressure canner, then a seal ([Pressure Canning](/library#pressure-canning)) | Everything, because nothing else is holding the line |
| Frozen peas | Blanching for the enzymes, then cold ([Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew)) | Freezing does not kill most germs; thaw in the refrigerator |

## Worked example: an October harvest in Silverdale, kept five ways

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on. A household near Silverdale has a glut in the same week:
apples, cabbages, prune plums, two coho salmon and a row of green beans.
For each, they ask three questions: which barrier does the work, why it
works, and what it cannot do.

1. **Apples into applesauce.** *Barrier:* heat in a boiling-water canner,
   and a seal. *Why it works:* apples are acid (the FDA's typical McIntosh
   is pH 3.34), so the botulism bacterium cannot grow, and the heat
   destroys the moulds and yeasts that could. *What it cannot do:* tell
   them the jar is safe by its seal or its look, which only the tested
   process can ([Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning) has this very
   example, step by step).
2. **Cabbage into sauerkraut.** *Barrier:* salt by weight, then the acid
   the bacteria make. *Why:* the salt favours the lactic acid bacteria,
   and they take the cabbage from around pH 5.2 to 6.0 to around 3.4 to
   3.6. *What it cannot do:* keep it at room temperature for good; when
   it is finished, it goes into the refrigerator or the canner ([Fermenting
   Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables)).
3. **Plums into prunes.** *Barrier:* drying. *Why:* with the water gone,
   bacteria cannot grow, and the fruit's sugar and acid add to it. *What
   it cannot do:* stop mould if the fruit is under-dried or the jar takes
   up damp, so the prunes are conditioned in jars and checked for beads of
   moisture before they are stored ([Drying Food](/library#drying-food)).
4. **Salmon into hot-smoked fish.** *Barrier:* salt, then heat, then cold.
   *Why:* the brine lowers the fish's water activity, the heat kills, and
   the cold slows whatever comes after. *What it cannot do:* the smoke
   adds flavour and a little surface protection, but it is not the
   preservative, so the fish goes into the refrigerator and is eaten
   within the time the guide gives ([Salting, Curing and
   Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking)).
5. **Green beans.** *Barrier:* cold, after blanching. *Why:* beans are
   low-acid, so a boiling-water canner cannot make them safe; the choices
   are the freezer, a pressure canner or drying by the tested method.
   They choose the freezer, and blanch the beans first so their enzymes
   stop working. *What it cannot do:* freezing does not kill most germs,
   so the beans are thawed in the refrigerator, not on the counter
   ([Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew), [Pressure
   Canning](/library#pressure-canning)).

Notice that every food here was kept by more than one barrier. Only
low-acid food in a sealed jar rests on heat alone, which is why a
pressure-canning recipe is followed to the minute (our reading).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Recipes.** Use tested ones and change nothing that affects safety:
  the salt, the acid, the sugar in traditional jams, the jar, the time,
  the temperature ([Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning),
  [Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables)).
- **Measurements.** Water activity and the finished pH of a product are
  laboratory work ([Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).
- **Commercial numbers.** Water activity 0.85 and the pH 4.7 for tomatoes
  in the federal canning rules are lines for factories, not home rules
  ([Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).
- **Curing salt.** Nitrite is measured to the recipe and stored labelled
  and away from children ([Salting, Curing and
  Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking)).
- **Selling** preserved food is regulated: ask your state's agriculture or
  health department first (general practice).

## Traps

- **"Salt kills germs."** It holds most bacteria back; *Staphylococcus
  aureus* and moulds tolerate it, and it does not kill the worms in pork.
- **"It's pickled, so it can't go off."** Moulds and yeasts like acid.
- **"Jam is all sugar; nothing can grow in it."** Mould can, and it may
  leave mycotoxins.
- **"Smoked food keeps."** The smoke is flavour; the salt, the heat and
  the cold do the keeping.
- **"Freezing sterilises."** It pauses. Most of what went in comes out.
- **"Dried means safe forever."** Damp brings mould, and bacteria in dried
  food are waiting, not dead.
- **"Boiling makes anything safe."** Not the spores in a low-acid jar, and
  not some poisons.
- **"Sealing out the air keeps food fresh."** For moist low-acid food at
  room temperature, it builds the conditions the botulism bacterium
  needs.
- **"A little more vinegar can't hurt, so a little less won't either."**
  Neither change is yours to make without a tested recipe.

## How the game models it

The game counts spoilage and the temperature where food is kept, and
leaves the chemistry out.

- **A clock, not chemistry.** Every food keeps for a set time
  (`spoilage_rate_hours` in `data/food_system.ron`), and the clock runs a
  twentieth as fast frozen (below -1 C), a quarter as fast in cold air (-1
  to 8 C), 0.6 times as fast in cool air (8 to 18 C), at the full rate at
  room temperature and two and a half times as fast above 30 C
  (`temperature_zones`). Preserved foods simply have longer clocks: ten
  years for canned food and for sugar, three years for field rations, two
  years for honey, 30 days for jerky, dried meat and dried fruit
  (`data/food_system.ron`, mapped to items in
  `data/food/item_profiles.ron`). Once spoiled, a food always gives you
  Food Poisoning when eaten (`src/systems/food.rs`).
- **The Freezer is the only cold store.** Food in the home's storage and
  vessels ages in the home's air, at room temperature, except in the
  Freezer, which keeps its food frozen (`keeps_zone` in
  `data/containers/types.csv`); [Cold Storage Without a
  Fridge](/library#cold-storage-without-a-fridge) has the detail.
- **Preserving recipes.** Dry Fruit, Dry Meat, Make Jerky and Can Food
  turn fresh ingredients into the longer-keeping foods (`data/recipes.csv`).
  In the Normal play mode, where the game starts (Settings > Gameplay >
  Play mode), a craft uses up its ingredients and wears any tool it needs,
  such as the Utility Knife for Can Food and Make Jerky
  (`src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`, `data/crafting/tools.ron`). None of
  them has a salt ratio, an acid, a temperature, a time or a seal to get
  right, and Can Food makes potatoes and carrots, low-acid vegetables,
  into canned food at a stove ([Pressure Canning](/library#pressure-canning)
  says what that leaves out).
- **The methods are only written down.** The food data lists eight
  preservation methods with how much longer each makes food keep:
  refrigeration 4 times, freezing 20, canning 100, drying 20, salting 15,
  smoking 10, vacuum sealing 5 and irradiation 50. The game loads that
  list but nothing uses it (`data/food_system.ron`,
  `src/systems/food.rs`), and its smoking
  row credits smoke with antimicrobial compounds and drying where the
  Pacific Northwest guide says smoke is no preservative on its own.
- **Under the default settings.** In Normal, only the Dev play mode's
  tools take you off the ship, so the food you carry ages at the ship's
  room temperature (`src/config.rs`, `src/engine/survival_env.rs`). The
  clock runs at the Realistic time speed, a game second to a real second,
  unless you change Settings > Gameplay > Time (`src/systems/time.rs`),
  and with "Start every session from the default home" off, as it is by
  default, your stores and their ages are kept between launches.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: water
activity, pH, salt and sugar concentrations, acid, smoke, nitrite and
air; the moulds that tolerate salt and acid; the bacteria that grow in
the cold; and the fact that a preserved food can fail. In the game it
fails only when its clock runs out.

## You own this when

- You can name the four causes of spoilage, and say which of them can
  make people ill.
- You can explain water activity, pH and temperature in your own words,
  and why each method works by changing one or more of them.
- You know that pH is a scale of tens, so a small change in the number is
  a large change in acidity.
- For salt, sugar, acid, drying, cold, heat, smoke and keeping air out,
  you can say what each does and at least one thing it cannot do.
- You can look at a preserved food and name the barriers keeping it, and
  the one that is doing most of the work.
- You use tested recipes, change nothing that affects their safety, and
  know that no kitchen measurement can stand in for one.
