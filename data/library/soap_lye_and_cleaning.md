# Soap, Lye and Cleaning

Soap is one of the oldest things people make at home, from two things a
farm has always had: fat from animals or plants, and an alkali. It is
also a household craft whose key ingredient can blind an eye and burn
skin. The danger is not in the soap. It is in
the lye, and in a handful of other cleaners, drain and oven cleaners
and bleach among them, that work by being harsh enough to hurt.

This guide is about what soap is and why it cleans, how to handle lye
without being hurt, the arithmetic of how much lye a batch of fat
needs, an outline of making a batch, and how to clean a home with the
cleaners you already have without making a poisonous gas. It also says
where your own work should stop.

It takes its definition of soap from the Food and Drug Administration
(FDA), its account of how soap cleans from the Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention (CDC), its hazard and first-aid information on
lye from the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health
(NIOSH) and the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's (NOAA)
CAMEO Chemicals database, its storage and poison advice from the Health
Resources and Services Administration's (HRSA) Poison Help service and
the Consumer Product Safety Commission's (CPSC) packaging rule, and its
cleaning advice from the CDC and the Environmental Protection Agency
(EPA). All are United States government publications, in the public
domain. Three sources are not public domain and are cited for facts we
restate in our own words: the Codex Alimentarius standard for animal
fats (published by the Food and Agriculture Organization of the United
Nations), Iowa State University Extension, and the MedlinePlus Medical
Encyclopedia. Where something is general practice rather than a
published rule, the text says so.

disease. [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns) has the full first aid for

## First: lye, and the other cleaners that burn

### What lye is, and where it already is in your house

"Lye" is the common name for two strong alkalis. NIOSH lists sodium
hydroxide under the names "Caustic soda, Lye [Sodium hydroxide], Soda
lye, Sodium hydrate" and potassium hydroxide under "Caustic potash, Lye
[Potassium hydroxide], Potassium hydrate". Sodium hydroxide, it says, is
a "Colorless to white, odorless solid (flakes, beads, granular form)."
It looks like coarse salt or sugar, which is part of what makes it
dangerous (general knowledge).

You probably have it already. CAMEO Chemicals says sodium hydroxide is
used in "cleaning compounds, drain cleaners", and MedlinePlus lists
drain cleaners, oven cleaners, hair straighteners and some metal
polishes among the household products that contain it. Everything in
this section applies to those products as much as to a tub of lye for
soap.

### What it does to a person

NIOSH lists the symptoms of sodium hydroxide as "irritation eyes, skin,
mucous membrane; pneumonitis; eye, skin burns; temporary loss of hair",
and its target organs as the eyes, skin and respiratory system.
Pneumonitis is inflammation of the lungs, from breathing its dust or
mist. CAMEO describes "Strong corrosive action on contacted tissues" and
says eye contact "produces severe damage."

In plain terms: lye on skin burns, lye in an eye can blind, lye
breathed in as dust or mist damages the airways, and lye swallowed
burns the mouth, throat and stomach. A lye burn may not hurt at first,
so do not wait for pain to tell you it is on you (general practice).

### Before you open the container

NIOSH's protective advice for both lyes is short: "Prevent skin
contact", "Prevent eye contact", and provide an eyewash and a quick
drench. At home (general practice, built on those rules):

- **Goggles, not glasses.** Wear chemical splash goggles that seal round
  the eyes. OSHA's rule on eye protection (29 CFR 1910.133) lists
  "acids or caustic liquids" among the hazards eye protection is for,
  and requires side protection where things can fly.
- **Gloves and long sleeves.** Chemical-resistant gloves, such as
  rubber or nitrile, long sleeves and closed shoes, and an apron if you
  have one.
- **Running water within reach before you start.** Work within a few
  steps of a running tap or a shower, close enough to reach with your
  eyes shut. The first aid below is 15 to 20 minutes of running water,
  which no jug can supply; a jug of clean water is only for the first
  seconds while you get to the tap.
- **Nobody else in the room.** Children and pets out, and nobody to
  bump you.
- **Air.** Work outdoors, or by an open window with a fan blowing out.

### Keep it in its own container

Lye sold for household use has to come in packaging that children find
hard to open. The CPSC's rule (16 CFR 1700.14(a)(5)) requires special
packaging for household substances in dry form, such as granules,
powder and flakes, containing 10 percent or more of sodium or potassium
hydroxide, and for all other household substances containing 2 percent
or more. That packaging only protects anyone while the lye stays in it.

HRSA's Poison Help prevention tips say to keep chemicals and household
products "in their original containers", not to use food containers
such as cups or bottles to store household cleaners and other
chemicals, and to keep them in locked cabinets or out of the reach of
children. It explains why the drink bottle is the worst place of all:
"Children and adults might think that products or other chemicals that
are stored in drink containers are safe to drink." It also says that
more than 90 percent of the time, poisonings happen in people's homes.

Lye pulls water out of the air. CAMEO says sodium hydroxide "readily
absorbs moisture from the air". Close the lid tightly every time (general
practice), or you will find a caustic slush.

### Mixing lye and water: the dangerous minute

Soap needs lye dissolved in water, and that is the step that needs the
most care (general practice). CAMEO says dissolving sodium hydroxide in
water can release enough heat to make it steam and spatter, and even to
set fire to combustible material beside it. NIOSH lists water among its
incompatibilities for that reason.

So (general practice, following that hazard):

1. Measure cold water into a heat-resistant container first.
2. Add the lye to the water, slowly, stirring, a little at a time.
   Never pour water onto a heap of lye: the first water to touch it
   boils.
3. Do it outdoors or by an open window, and do not lean over the
   container. It gives off fumes and gets very hot.
4. Let it cool where nobody will touch or knock it, and label it.

**Never in aluminium.** NIOSH lists "metals such as aluminum, tin &
zinc" among sodium hydroxide's incompatibilities, and CAMEO says damp
sodium hydroxide attacks aluminium and zinc "with the evolution of
flammable hydrogen gas." That rules out aluminium pans and spoons, tin,
and galvanised (zinc-coated) buckets. Use stainless steel, or a heavy
heat-resistant plastic sold for the purpose (general practice).

**Never with acid.** CAMEO says it "Reacts rapidly and exothermically
with acids." Keep vinegar, citric acid and acid cleaners away from it.

**Keep soap tools for soap.** A pot, spoon or jug that has held lye
does not go back to the kitchen. Label your soap tools and store them
with the lye (general practice).

### If lye gets on someone

The first seconds matter more than anything that follows (general
practice). HRSA's first steps in a poisoning emergency:

- **On the skin:** "Take off any clothing the poison touched. Rinse skin
  with running water for 15 to 20 minutes." Brush off any dry flakes or
  beads first, as [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns) describes.
- **In the eyes:** "Rinse eyes with running water for 15 to 20 minutes."
  NIOSH adds the detail that matters when someone is panicking: wash
  with large amounts of water, "occasionally lifting the lower and upper
  lids", and "Get medical attention immediately."
- **Breathed in:** "Get to fresh air right away."
- **Then call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222,** which connects you to your
  local poison centre, for every one of these.

**Swallowed lye** is an emergency: NIOSH's first aid is "Medical
attention immediately." MedlinePlus says not to make the person vomit
unless poison control or a health care provider tells you to, and to
give water or milk straight away unless a provider says otherwise, but
not if the person is vomiting, having convulsions or less alert, or
otherwise finds it hard to swallow. It suggests having ready the
person's age, weight and condition, the name of the product, and when
and how much was swallowed.

**Call 911 at once** if the person has trouble breathing, collapses,
has a seizure, or will not wake. MedlinePlus says that after an
exposure you can call either the local emergency number, such as 911,
or Poison Help; the signs that make it 911 are general practice, and so
is the reason: Poison Help gives advice by telephone and does not send
an ambulance.

**Do not put vinegar on a lye burn.** [Treating
Burns](/library#treating-burns) explains why not to try to neutralise a
chemical with an acid or an alkali unless a professional tells you to:
the reaction makes heat, and adds a heat burn to a chemical one.

### Bleach, ammonia and acids: never mix cleaners

The other way cleaning hurts people at home is a gas made in a bucket.
The CDC: "Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. This will
produce a poisonous gas." HRSA puts it more widely: "Never mix household
or chemical products together. Doing so can create a dangerous gas."
And on bleach in particular, "Bleach is especially toxic and should not
be mixed with anything other than water."

That includes two drain cleaners one after the other, a bleach spray
after a toilet cleaner, and vinegar added to bleach "to make it
stronger" (general practice, applying HRSA's rule). If a drain cleaner
has not cleared a blockage, the pipe is still full of it; tell anyone
who works on the drain, a plumber included, what you put down it
(general practice).

### Hot fat

Rendering fat for soap is cooking with a lot of hot fat, with a
kitchen fire's risks. The US Fire Administration: "Stand by your pan.
If you leave the kitchen, turn the burner off." "If you see any smoke or
the grease starts to boil, turn the burner off." And keep a pan lid or
baking sheet nearby to cover the pan if it catches fire: "This will put
out the fire." Never pour water on burning fat (general knowledge).

## What soap is, and why it cleans

The FDA, which has to define soap for its own rules, puts it simply:
"Ordinary soap is made by combining fats or oils and an alkali, such as
lye." The fats and oils "may be from animal, vegetable, or mineral
sources"; they are broken down into fatty acids, which combine with the
alkali to form soap. What you end up with is, in the FDA's words, the
"alkali salts of fatty acids". It adds two things worth knowing: "When
made properly, no lye remains in the finished product," and "In the
past, people commonly made their own soap using animal fats and lye
that had been extracted from wood ashes."

Why it cleans: the CDC's handwashing facts say "the surfactants in soap
lift soil and microbes from skin", and that lathering and scrubbing
make friction that helps lift dirt, grease and microbes. A surfactant
is a molecule with one end that mixes with water and one that mixes
with oil, which is how soap carries grease away in rinse water (our
explanation).

**Soap and detergent are different things.** The FDA says there are
very few true soaps on the market: "Most body cleansers, both liquid and
solid, are synthetic detergent products," popular "because they make
suds easily in water and don't form gummy deposits." That last point
is a weakness of soap: in hard water, soap forms the grey scum you see
on a bath (general knowledge), which detergents avoid.

### Hard soap and soft soap

Sodium hydroxide makes hard bar soap; potassium hydroxide makes soft or
liquid soap (general knowledge). The two are not interchangeable in a
recipe: they have different molecular weights, 40.0 for sodium
hydroxide and 56.1 for potassium hydroxide (standard chemistry; both
in `data/chemistry/compounds.csv`), so the same job needs a different
weight of each.

### Lye from wood ashes

The FDA says people used to extract lye from wood ashes, and that is
true: water poured through ashes carries away an alkali, mostly
potassium compounds (general knowledge). Iowa State University
Extension says wood ash contains roughly 5 to 8 percent potash
depending on the type of wood, and that wood ashes can be caustic, so
it advises gloves, long sleeves and trousers, eye protection and a mask
when handling them.

This guide does not give a method for making lye from ashes, and that
is deliberate (our reading). How strong the liquid comes out depends on
the wood, the ash and the water, so you cannot weigh out the right
amount the way you can with lye from a container, and a soap made with
too much is a soap that burns. If you want to make soap, buy lye sold as
100 percent sodium hydroxide, and never use drain cleaner, which may
contain other ingredients (general practice).

## How much lye a batch of fat needs

Every fat needs a particular amount of alkali to turn completely into
soap. That amount is its saponification value, and it is measured, not
guessed.

The Codex Alimentarius standard for named animal fats (Codex Standard
211-1999, published by the FAO and the World Health Organization) gives
it in milligrams of potassium hydroxide per gram of fat:

| Fat | Saponification value (mg potassium hydroxide per g of fat) |
|---|---|
| Edible tallow (beef or mutton fat) | 190 to 202 |
| Lard (pork fat) | 192 to 203 |

To use sodium hydroxide for a bar soap, convert by the ratio of the two
molecular weights, 40.0 / 56.1, which is about 0.713 (arithmetic):

- Tallow: 190 x 0.713 = 135 mg, and 202 x 0.713 = 144 mg, of sodium
  hydroxide per gram of fat.
- So **1 kg of tallow needs between about 135 g and 144 g of sodium
  hydroxide** to turn completely into soap (arithmetic). Lard comes out
  between about 137 g and 145 g.

Notice that the answer is a range, because no two batches of fat are
identical. That is why soapmakers deliberately use a few percent less
lye than the full amount, often called a superfat (general practice):
the shortfall leaves a little fat unchanged in the bar, which is
harmless and mild, instead of leaving lye unchanged in it, which is
not. With that range, work from the low end, and then take a few
percent off that.

**Worked example** (arithmetic, with the superfat as general practice):
you have 1.5 kg of rendered tallow. The full amount of sodium hydroxide
would be between 1,500 x 0.135 = 203 g and 1,500 x 0.144 = 216 g. Start
from the low end, 203 g, and take 5 percent off: 203 x 0.95 = about
193 g. Weigh it on a kitchen scale; a spoonful is not a measurement
([Ratios and Mixing](/library#ratios-and-mixing) explains why weight beats
volume).

A soapmaking calculator does this for any mix of fats, and tells you
how much water to dissolve the lye in (general practice). Use one, and
check its answer against the arithmetic above for any fat you can.

## Worked example: a batch of tallow soap, in outline

This is the cold-process method in outline. The order of the steps
and the habits in them are general practice; the safety rules are the
ones sourced above. Follow a tested recipe for the quantities, and read
the whole thing, the safety sections included, before you start.

1. **Plan and weigh.** Fat, lye and water, all weighed, from a
   calculator or a tested recipe. Set out the mould, lined with baking
   paper, and every tool, before the lye is open.
2. **Dress for it.** Goggles, gloves, long sleeves. Children and pets
   out. Window open or outdoors. A running tap within a few steps.
3. **Make the lye solution.** Lye into cold water, slowly, never the
   other way round, in a stainless steel or heat-resistant plastic
   container, without leaning over it. Set it somewhere safe to cool,
   and label it.
4. **Melt the fat** gently on a low burner, and stand by it. Let it cool
   back to warm.
5. **Combine.** Pour the cooled lye solution into the fat, slowly, and
   stir until the mixture thickens enough that a drizzle leaves a mark
   on the surface for a moment. This raw soap is still caustic: keep the
   gloves and goggles on.
6. **Mould.** Pour it into the mould, cover it, and leave it where
   nobody will touch it for a day or two until it is firm.
7. **Cut and cure.** Cut it into bars, still with gloves, and stand them
   apart on a rack somewhere airy for several weeks. Curing lets the
   last of the reaction finish and the water dry out; four to six weeks
   is a common figure.
8. **Clean up with the gloves on.** Wipe every tool of soap batter
   before washing it, and wash it separately from kitchen things.

**Before you use a bar,** check it (general practice). A bar that
stings or burns skin, has pockets of liquid in it, or feels slick and
wet long after it should have dried may contain unreacted lye: do not
use it on skin. Do not taste-test soap, whatever an old book says.
Ask your local waste service how to get rid of a failed batch.

## Cleaning, with what you have

Most cleaning at home needs only soap, water and scrubbing. The
stronger products have narrower jobs, and they are the ones that can
hurt you.

- **Clean first, then disinfect.** The EPA's six steps for using a
  disinfectant, a sheet written in April 2020 during the COVID-19
  pandemic, start with checking that the product carries an EPA
  registration number and is on the EPA's list of approved
  disinfectants, then reading the directions, and then: "Make sure to
  wash the surface with soap and water if the directions mention
  pre-cleaning or if the surface is visibly dirty." Dirt left on a
  surface shields what is under it (our explanation).
- **Leave it on long enough.** The EPA: "You can find the contact time in
  the directions. The surface should remain wet the whole time to ensure
  the product is effective." Spraying and wiping straight off is mostly
  cleaning, not disinfecting (our reading of the EPA's contact-time
  rule).
- **Gloves, and lock it up.** The EPA says to wear gloves and wash your
  hands after taking them off, and to "Keep lids tightly closed and
  store out of reach of children."
- **Bleach.** Follow the label for how much to use; the CDC says to
  follow manufacturers' instructions when you use bleach or any other
  cleaning product. For mould, the CDC's limit is no more than 1 cup of
  household laundry bleach in 1 gallon of water, with the windows and
  doors open and rubber boots, rubber gloves and goggles; the full
  procedure is in [Ventilation, Damp and
  Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould). Never mix it with anything but
  water.
- **Drain and oven cleaners.** Many contain lye: MedlinePlus lists both
  among the products that contain sodium hydroxide. Some drain openers
  are strong acids instead (general knowledge), and an acid cleaner
  meeting a lye cleaner in the same pipe is exactly the mixing HRSA
  warns against. Read the label, and treat either as you would lye:
  goggles and gloves, never mixed with another cleaner, never left where
  children can reach them.
- **Do not use cleaners on food or on skin.** HRSA's tips say never to
  use commercial cleaning products on food or food packaging, not to use
  bleach on food products, and to avoid using household cleaners and
  disinfectants on hands or skin improperly.

## If you sell or give away soap

The FDA's rules depend on what the product is and what it claims to do.
To count as "soap" under its regulatory definition, a product must be
made mainly of the alkali salts of fatty acids, those must be its only
cleaning ingredients, and it must be labelled and marketed only as
soap. A true soap that meets all three, the FDA says, is regulated by
the CPSC, not by the FDA. If it is sold to moisturise, scent or
deodorise, it is a cosmetic and the FDA regulates it, and the
Modernization of Cosmetics Regulation Act of 2022 requires registration
and listing of cosmetic product facilities and products with the FDA,
with some exemptions. If it claims to kill germs or treat a skin
condition, it is a drug. And "natural" or "organic" ingredients make no
difference: the FDA says the laws it enforces do not define those
words, and "It's important not to assume that using only ingredients
from plants will make your products safe."

Read the FDA's soap page and contact the agencies before you sell
anything (general practice).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Lye in an eye, swallowed lye, or a large burn.** Rinse, call Poison
  Help, and get medical care; call 911 for trouble breathing or
  collapse. [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns) says every chemical burn
  should be seen by a burn service.
- **Selling soap or cosmetics.** The FDA and the CPSC set the rules
  above.
- **Lye from wood ashes for anything that touches skin.** Its strength
  is unknown (our reading of Iowa State's varying potash figures).
- **Unknown chemicals.** If you find an unlabelled container, do not
  sniff it, mix it or pour it away; ask your local hazardous waste
  service (general practice).
- **A drain a cleaner did not clear.** A plumber, told what is in the
  pipe (general practice).

## Traps

- **Water onto lye.** It boils and spatters. Lye into water, slowly.
- **Aluminium pans and galvanised buckets.** Lye attacks them and gives
  off hydrogen.
- **Lye or cleaner decanted into a drink bottle or a food jar.** Keep it
  in its own labelled container.
- **Vinegar on a lye burn.** Water, a lot of it, for 15 to 20 minutes.
- **"Stronger" mixtures.** Two cleaners together can make a poisonous
  gas.
- **Using the soap too soon.** Raw and uncured soap is still caustic.
- **Measuring lye by the spoon.** Weigh it.

soap needs no curing, there is no drain cleaner, bleach or mixing of

water is the step that needs the most care.

## You own this when

- You know that many drain and oven cleaners are lye and some drain
  openers are acid, read the label, and treat both with lye's care.
- You wear sealed goggles, gloves and long sleeves before you open lye,
  and have running water within reach.
- You add lye to cold water, slowly, never water to lye, outdoors or by
  an open window, and never in aluminium, tin or galvanised metal.
- You keep lye and every cleaner in its own labelled container, never a
  food or drink container, locked up or out of children's reach.
- You can give the first aid: clothing off and 15 to 20 minutes of
  running water for skin or eyes, lids held open, Poison Help on
  1-800-222-1222, 911 for breathing trouble or collapse, no vomiting
  unless told, no vinegar.
- You never mix cleaners, and bleach only ever with water.
- You can work out how much sodium hydroxide a weight of tallow needs
  from its saponification value, and why you use a little less.
- You cure soap for weeks and check a bar before it touches skin.
- You clean before you disinfect, and leave a disinfectant wet for its
  contact time.
- You know which agency's rules apply before you sell soap.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, general knowledge, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- That lye looks like salt or sugar; that some drain openers are strong
  acids rather than lye.
- That a lye burn may not hurt at first; the protective kit (sealed
  goggles, rubber or nitrile gloves, long sleeves, a running tap or
  shower within a few steps and a jug only for the first seconds,
  nobody else in the room, outdoors or an open window).
- Closing the lid every time; the procedure for dissolving lye (cold
  water first, lye in slowly, never water onto lye, not leaning over it,
  labelled while it cools); stainless steel or heat-resistant plastic;
  keeping soap tools for soap.
- That the first seconds matter most; the signs that make it a 911 call
  (breathing trouble, collapse, seizure or unresponsiveness), and that
  Poison Help does not send an ambulance.
- Applying HRSA's never-mix rule to drain cleaners in sequence, bleach
  after toilet cleaner and vinegar in bleach; telling a plumber what is
  in the pipe.
- Never pouring water on burning fat.
- How a surfactant works; soap scum in hard water; sodium hydroxide for
  hard soap and potassium hydroxide for soft; what water carries out of
  ashes.
- Not giving a method for ash lye, buying lye sold as 100 percent sodium
  hydroxide, and not using drain cleaner.
- The conversion between the two lyes and every quantity in the worked
  example are arithmetic; the superfat and working from the low end are
  general practice; using a calculator.
- The order of the cold-process steps, the trace test, curing for four
  to six weeks, cleaning up with gloves on, checking a bar, not
  taste-testing, and asking the waste service about a failed batch.
- That dirt shields what is under it, and that spraying and wiping
  straight off is mostly cleaning.
- Reading the FDA's page and contacting the agencies before selling;
  not handling unknown chemicals; a plumber for an uncleared drain.
