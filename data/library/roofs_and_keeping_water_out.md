# Roofs and Keeping Water Out

Most of what a building does, it does by keeping water out. Walls hold
up the roof, the roof sheds the rain, and a great many slow, expensive
failures in a house start with water getting somewhere it should not:
rotten rafters, a mouldy ceiling, a sagging porch, a wet basement. The
Environmental Protection Agency's guide to moisture in buildings puts it
plainly: "In most climates, rain is the largest source of water in
buildings." The roof is the first and biggest thing standing in its way.

This guide is about how a roof keeps water out, where it fails, how to
look after one, and how to find a leak. It is also about the work's
main danger, which is not the water at all. Roof work is work at height,
and falls are the leading cause of death in construction.

It takes its safety rules from the Occupational Safety and Health
Administration (OSHA), its building knowledge from the Army's carpentry
manual, the EPA's moisture guidance and the Forest Service's Wood
Handbook, and its snow, ice and wind advice from the Federal Emergency
Management Agency (FEMA). Those are United States government
publications, in the public domain. It also draws facts, restated in our
own words, from two works written for the government by contractors: an
inspection guide written for the Department of Housing and Urban
Development (HUD) by a private institute in 2000, and a do-it-yourself
flashing guide from the Department of Energy's Building America Solution
Center, which the national laboratory that runs it publishes under a
contract notice. Where something is general practice or our own
reasoning rather than a published rule, the text says so.

Other guides cover the neighbouring ground. [Choosing Where to
Build](/library#choosing-where-to-build) decides where the water comes from
before the roof is built. [Insulation and Heat
Loss](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss) and [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould) deal with the attic under the roof,
where ice dams start. [Collecting Rainwater](/library#collecting-rainwater)
turns the roof into a water supply. [Repairing a
Building](/library#repairing-a-building) is what to do about the damage once
water has got in. And the ladder rules this guide leans on are in
[Keeping Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working).

## First: how roof work hurts people

### Falls

OSHA's fall prevention campaign opens with the fact that matters most
here: falls are the leading cause of death in construction, and in
2024 there were 389 fatal falls to a lower level out of 1,034
construction deaths, in data from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. Its
booklet for roofing contractors (*Protecting Roofing Workers*, 2015)
adds that falls from roofs were nearly 1,200, or 34 percent, of the
fall deaths in construction between 2003 and 2013.

Those numbers are about people who do this for a living, with
equipment and training. A homeowner on a roof once a year has neither.
OSHA's rule for residential construction (29 CFR 1926.501(b)(13))
requires fall protection for workers 6 feet (1.8 m) or more above a
lower level: a guardrail, a safety net or a personal fall arrest system,
which means a body harness, connectors and an anchorage on the building.
The rule is written for employers, not for you on your own house, but
the height at which a fall becomes dangerous is the same. Reading it
as a guide at home is our reading, and the honest conclusion is this:
**most roof work can and should be done from the ground or from a
ladder at the eaves, and work that needs you walking about on the roof
is work to hire out** unless you have been trained in fall protection
and have the gear (general practice).

What you can do safely, mostly from below:

- **Look from the ground.** HUD's inspection guide advises binoculars
  for roofs that cannot be reached or walked on. A good look from the
  yard, and from an upstairs window, finds most missing shingles,
  slipped flashing and clogged gutters (general practice).
- **Look from inside.** The underside of the roof, seen from the attic
  with a torch, shows where water has been (see the worked example
  below). Check what the attic insulation is first: if it might be
  vermiculite, look from the hatch only (see "Asbestos in old roofing and
  attic insulation" below).
- **Use a ladder properly.** The full ladder checklist is in [Keeping
  Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working). Two points belong here.
  The EPA's moisture guide says: do not rest ladders against gutters.
  And OSHA's roofing booklet recommends three points of contact going
  up and down (two hands and a foot, or two feet and a hand), with
  tools hauled up in a bucket on a rope rather than carried.
- **Stay off a wet, icy or windy roof.** OSHA's roofing booklet calls
  high winds, wet weather and ice especially hazardous, and names slate,
  tile, metal and some membranes as particularly slippery when wet.
  HUD's guide adds that slate, clay tile and asbestos cement shingles
  are brittle, break easily, and should not be walked on during an
  inspection.

### Skylights and holes

A skylight, or a roof hatch, or a hole left where a vent came out, is a
place a person can fall straight through. OSHA's rule (29 CFR
1926.501(b)(4)) requires protection from falling through holes,
skylights included, more than 6 feet above a lower level. When a cover
is used, its companion rule (1926.502(i)) says it must support at least
twice the weight that may be put on it, be fixed so the wind or a foot
cannot shift it, and be colour-coded or marked "HOLE" or "COVER". At
home: never step
on a skylight, and never lean on one (general practice). FEMA's snow
guide warns that skylights buried under snow are a high risk to anyone
on the roof, because they cannot be seen.

### Power lines

OSHA's roofing booklet says most electrocutions of roofers come from
contact with overhead power lines, and that the service drop (the line
from the pole to the house) is the most common. OSHA's rule for people
who are not trained electrical workers (29 CFR 1910.333(c)(3)) keeps
the person **and the longest conductive object they could touch** at
least 10 feet (305 cm) from an energised overhead line of 50,000 volts
or less to ground. That object is the ladder, the metal gutter section,
the length of flashing or the snow rake in your hands. What it is made
of does not let you off: OSHA's note to the rule says that at the
voltages of overhead power lines, "objects which do not have an
insulating rating for the voltage involved are considered to be
conductive." A wooden ladder or a plastic snow rake has no such rating,
so it counts. Look up before you lift anything long, and if a job brings
you near the line to the house, call the utility rather than working
around it (general practice).

### The structure under you

OSHA's rule (1926.501(a)(2)) says workers may stand only on surfaces
with the strength to support them, and its roofing booklet suggests
looking at an older roof from inside the building first. Rotten
sheathing gives way under a foot. A roof loaded with snow can fail
outright: the signs, and the rule to leave the building, are in "Snow"
below.

### Heat

A roof in summer is one of the hottest places a person can work. OSHA's
roofing booklet lists the signs of heat illness, among them throbbing
headache, dizziness, no sweating despite the heat, red hot dry skin,
nausea, confusion, seizures and unconsciousness, says heat stroke
needs immediate medical attention and can kill, and sums up the
prevention as water, rest and shade. On a roof, add the obvious: a
person who feels faint should come down before they fall down (general
practice).

### Asbestos in old roofing and attic insulation

The EPA lists roofing and siding shingles among the building materials
that may contain asbestos, and the Army's 1995 carpentry manual still
treats asbestos-cement roofing as one of the two most common coverings
for pitched roofs. You generally cannot tell by looking. The EPA's
advice for material that might contain asbestos: leave it alone if it is
in good condition; do not saw, sand, scrape or drill it; and leave
repair and removal to a trained and accredited professional. More is in
[What Not to Compost, Burn or Pour
Away](/library#what-not-to-compost-burn-or-pour-away) and [Repairing a
Building](/library#repairing-a-building).

**Check the attic insulation before you climb in.** This guide sends
you into the attic to look at the underside of the roof, and the same
EPA list includes attic and wall insulation containing vermiculite. The
EPA describes vermiculite insulation as a pebble-like, pour-in product,
usually grey-brown or silver-gold. If your attic has it, the EPA says to
leave it undisturbed, not to store boxes or other things in that attic,
not to let children play there, and not to try to remove it yourself;
renovation that would disturb it is for a professional asbestos
contractor. In such an attic, look from the hatch with a torch and do
not climb in, walk through the insulation or move it (our application
of the EPA's advice).

## How a roof keeps water out

### Three things water does

The EPA's moisture guide names the behaviour every roof is designed
around:

- **Water runs downhill.** Gravity is the roof's main tool.
- **Water wicks upward** through tiny cracks and pores, by capillary
  action. "When water is in tiny pores, gravity is not the most important
  force acting on it."
- **Water runs along the bottom or sides of materials.** Water from a
  leak "may travel many feet along the bottom of a floor joist or roof
  truss before collecting in a drop big enough to fall."

The first is why roofs slope. The second is why laps must be wide (our
reasoning), and why the EPA lists overhangs and drip edges as capillary
breaks at a roof's edge. The third is why a stain on the ceiling is so
often nowhere near the hole in the roof.

### Overlap: every piece sheds onto the one below

A shingled roof keeps water out the way feathers on a bird do: each
piece overlaps the one below it, so water running down always lands on
top of the next piece and never on a joint. The Army's carpentry manual
(FM 5-426) gives the numbers for a common 1-foot by 3-foot asphalt strip
shingle: laid 5 inches "to the weather", meaning 7 inches of each course
is covered by the course above, and the nails go in that covered part,
so the next course hides every nail head. The felt underlay beneath the shingles is laid with a 2-inch top
lap and a 4-inch side lap. The manual adds that no roofing should go on
unless the sheathing boards are absolutely dry.

On where the nails go, the manual gives two figures that do not agree:
about 6 1/2 inches up from the bottom edge in its section on laying
shingles, and, in its section on reroofing, about 3/4 inch above the
cutouts, through the thick part of the shingle, because practically all
the trouble the Army had with asphalt shingles came from nailing them
too high. On a common modern three-tab shingle the first figure lands
higher than the second, above the line most makers print (general
knowledge). So do not nail by the manual's numbers: shingles sold today
mark their own nailing line, and that line and the maker's instructions
are what govern (general practice).

Flashing works the same way. The Building America Solution Center's
do-it-yourself guide to flashing says the flashing around a pipe or a
vent should overlap and work into the roofing as if it were a shingle
itself, under the shingles above and over the shingles below. Its
rule for the step flashing where a roof meets a wall is the same idea
again: upper pieces over lower pieces, and the flashing under the
siding, never over it. **Upper over lower, everywhere** is the whole
principle, and almost every leak is a place where it was broken (our
summary of the sources above).

Where pieces are not overlapped, they are sealed. The EPA's table of
roof joints lists both: shingling or sealing provides the continuity.
A sealed joint depends on its sealant, which ages; that is why the
Building America guide tells owners of vents that rely only on caulk to
look at the caulk every two years or so.

### Slope

The steeper a roof, the faster water leaves it and the less time it has
to find a gap. OSHA defines a low-slope roof as one with a slope of 4 in
12 or less (4 inches of rise for every 12 inches across) and a steep
roof as anything steeper. How rise and run make an angle is in [Square,
Level and Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb).

Each covering has a least slope it can be trusted on. HUD's 2000
inspection guide gives these, which are worth knowing even though
codes and products have moved on since (check your manufacturer's
instructions and local code for current numbers):

| Covering | Least slope in the HUD guide |
|---|---|
| Asphalt shingles | 3 in 12 (4 in 12 and up is "normal") |
| Wood shingles | 3 in 12 |
| Wood shakes | 4 in 12 |
| Slate, clay tile, asbestos cement | 4 in 12 |
| Metal | from 1/2 inch per foot |

Below those slopes, roofs need coverings made for low slopes. The Army
manual says built-up roofing is used mainly on flat or nearly flat
roofs; HUD's guide lists built-up roofing, single-ply membranes, roll
roofing and metal as the low-slope coverings, says no roof should be
dead level, it must slope at least a little to drain, and says leaks in
low-slope roofs are harder to trace than in pitched ones.

### How long coverings last

HUD's guide gave these service lives in 2000, and they are useful as a
rough sense of scale rather than a promise (the product's own warranty
says what its maker expects):

- **Asphalt shingles:** about 20 years for the first layer and about 15
  for a second laid over it. Replace them when they lose their granules
  and start to curl. No more than two layers should normally be on a
  roof at once.
- **Wood shingles and shakes:** 25 to 30 years in climates that are not
  too hot and humid; they dry, crack, curl and, in damp places, rot.
  Replace them when more than a third show deterioration.
- **Metal:** 50 years or more if kept painted.
- **Slate, clay tile and asbestos cement:** possibly the life of the
  building, if good and maintained.
- **Built-up and single-ply membranes:** about 20 years.

A simple sign of a worn asphalt roof from the EPA's guide: look in the
gutters for the mineral granules that wash off the shingles.

## Where roofs leak

The Army manual names the weak places: "the lines along which adjoining
roof surfaces intersect" (ridges, hips and valleys) and the lines where
the roof meets the walls of dormers, chimneys and skylights. Ridges and
hips shed water, so they are only moderately at risk; valleys gather
water, so they leak more. The Forest Service's Wood Handbook says the
same from the point of view of rot: the design, workmanship and
maintenance of roofs are critical "particularly at roof edges and
points where roofs interface with walls." HUD's guide adds simply that
leaking skylights are common.

The Building America flashing guide gives a homeowner's checklist of
what should be there, most of it visible from the ground or a ladder:

- **Drip edge** along the roof's edges: an L-shaped metal strip between
  the roofing and the fascia board that keeps water out of the crevice
  behind. Where there is a gutter, the drip edge should come down
  **into** the gutter, not behind it.
- **Step flashing** where a roof runs up beside a wall or chimney: small
  L-shaped pieces laid like shingles, each upper one over the one below,
  tucked under the siding. About an inch of it should show below the
  bottom of the siding.
- **Kick-out flashing** at the bottom of a roof-to-wall line, which turns
  the water running down beside the wall into the gutter. Without it,
  the guide says, the flow overruns the gutter end and pours down the
  wall, where it is likely in time to rot the window trim below and get
  inside the wall. Home-made kick-outs bent from scrap are often far too
  small for the water.
- **Boots or collars** around every pipe and vent through the roof,
  worked into the shingles.
- **Valley flashing** where two roof slopes meet. Only part of it can be
  seen. An open metal valley, a wide, flattened W of metal down the
  valley, shows from below; the guide also calls for a self-adhering
  membrane in the valley under the underlayment and roofing, and that
  cannot be checked without lifting the roofing.

The guide's advice is the right one for a beginner: if any of these
are missing or need attention, hire a contractor.

## Gutters, downspouts and where the water goes

A roof does not finish its job at the edge. The Wood Handbook, in its
advice on keeping a building's wood dry, calls a fairly wide overhang,
about 2 feet (0.6 m), with gutters and downspouts kept clear of debris,
desirable. Then the water has to be taken away from the foundation, or the
roof has only moved the problem to the basement.

**How much water.** One inch of rain on one square foot of roof is 144
cubic inches, about 0.62 gallons (arithmetic). [Collecting
Rainwater](/library#collecting-rainwater) works it through: a 1,200 square
foot roof sheds about 750 gallons in a one-inch rain. In a wet place
that adds up fast. The [Silverdale locale page](/library#silverdale-wa)
gives about 1,446 mm (57 inches) of rain a year there, which on that
same roof is about 42,600 gallons a year (arithmetic), every gallon
of it to be put somewhere other than against the house.

**Gutters.** From HUD's guide: gutters should slope evenly to the
downspouts with no low spots, joints soldered or sealed, hangers no more
than 3 feet apart (18 inches where snow and ice last), and at least one
downspout for every 40 feet of gutter. If a screen or guard covers the
gutter, check that water can actually get in past it. The Building
America guide says to clean gutters regularly, twice a year where there
are many trees, and to trim branches back so they do not overhang the
roof.

**Downspouts.** HUD's guide says a downspout should not empty onto a
walk, a drive or steps, and that the force of the water can scour the
ground, so it needs a splash block or a paved channel. The Building
America guide warns that downspout extensions are often taken off to
mow and never put back, leaving the whole roof's water dumped against
the foundation. For new building, the EPA's moisture guidance asks for
downspouts to run into sloped pipes that carry the water at least 10
feet from the foundation, at a slope of at least 5 percent (6 inches in
10 feet), or farther where local code says so.

**The ground.** HUD's installation rule for manufactured homes (24 CFR
3285.203) requires the ground to slope away at least half an inch per
foot for the first ten feet. It is written for manufactured homes, but
it is a good, concrete target for any house (our reading).

## Ice, snow and wind

### Ice dams

FEMA's snow load guide explains the mechanism. Heat escaping through the
roof melts the snow on it, even in freezing weather. The melt runs down
to the eaves, which overhang the cold outdoors, and freezes there. The
ridge of ice grows, the meltwater behind it has nowhere to go, and it
backs up under the shingles and into the building. FEMA notes a second
problem: the ice keeps snow from sliding off and piles weight at the
eaves. HUD's guide says ice dams form in climates with long freezes,
especially where the average January temperature is 30 F (-1 C) or
below, and that electric de-icing cables in the gutters may be a sign
a house has had them.

The cure is not on the roof but under it: keeping the attic cold, so
the snow does not melt in the first place, by stopping heat escaping
into it and ventilating it. How is covered in [Insulation and Heat
Loss](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss) and [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould). Two rules from the sources while
the ice is there: the EPA's guide says not to try to open an
ice-blocked roof drain by chipping or breaking the ice, and FEMA warns
that icicles falling even a short way can injure.

### Snow

FEMA's *Snow Load Safety Guide* (2013), written partly for homeowners,
gives the weights. A foot of fresh snow weighs from 3 pounds per
square foot, light and dry, to 21 pounds per square foot, wet and
heavy. An inch of ice weighs a little under 5 pounds per square foot,
and a foot of ice about 57. On a 1,000 square foot roof, a foot of wet
snow is about 21,000 pounds (arithmetic).

**Its warning signs of a roof under too much load** include sagging
ceilings or roof boards, popping, cracking and creaking noises, bowing
roof framing, doors and windows that can no longer be opened or closed,
cracked or split wood, cracks in walls or masonry, and severe leaks.
**If you see any of them, FEMA says to get everyone out of the building
promptly and have a qualified design professional, such as a
professional engineer, inspect it.**

FEMA recommends that snow be removed by a licensed, insured roofing
contractor experienced in it. For what a homeowner can do, its advice
is to work from the ground with a non-metallic snow rake, which removes
the risk of being on the roof; to stand well back from the eave, because
roof snow can slide at any moment; to have someone keep people away
from where snow and ice will fall; to leave at least 2 inches of snow
on the roof rather than scraping it bare, which can damage the
covering and cause leaks; and never to use picks or other sharp tools.
FEMA says metal rakes can damage roofing. A non-metallic rake is not
safe near the service line either: any rake without an insulating
rating counts as conductive under OSHA's rule above, the non-metallic
one FEMA recommends included, so keep the whole rake, handle and all,
10 feet from the line (our application of the two sources).

### Wind

FEMA's *Wind Retrofit Guide for Residential Buildings* (first edition,
December 2010) says its teams find three parts of a house typically fail
in high winds: the roof and wall coverings, the openings (windows and
doors), and the connections that carry the load from roof to walls to
foundation. Once wind or debris breaks into the shell, wind-driven rain
follows. Its case from Hurricane Ike is a house whose roof structure was
destroyed by winds estimated at only 50 mph, because its connections
could not carry the loads on the roof down to the foundation. HUD's
guide advises having a structural engineer check the load path of a
house in a high-wind region, and looking in the attic for roof
sheathing nails that miss the rafters and for hurricane clips at the
wall tops. FEMA published a
second edition of the retrofit guide in April 2023; its site refused to
serve the file to our reader, so this paragraph rests on the first
edition.

## Looking after a roof through the year

- **Twice a year, and after storms.** The EPA's moisture guide says to
  inspect roofs semi-annually, or as the manufacturer requires, and as
  soon as possible after heavy winds or rains. Spring and autumn suit
  most places (our suggestion).
- **From the ground and from inside.** Binoculars outside (HUD); a
  torch in the attic, looking at the underside of the roof deck (HUD's
  guide, for ice dams, names the deck above the outside walls), and at
  ceilings and walls for stains (EPA). In an attic with vermiculite
  insulation, from the hatch only (EPA, above).
- **The gutters.** Clean, sloping to the downspouts, hangers tight,
  downspout extensions in place (HUD, Building America).
- **Moss.** In its advice for asphalt shingle roofs, the EPA's guide
  says moss and lichen keep the roofing damp, and that they can be
  removed with a water hose and nozzle, sprayed down the slope, never up
  under the shingles. That advice is for asphalt shingles only. Use a
  garden hose, not a pressure washer, which can strip the granules and
  force water under the shingles (general practice). Do it from a ladder
  at the eave or from the ground; a wet roof is exactly the slippery
  surface OSHA warns about (general practice). On any other covering,
  and above all on asbestos cement, which you must not scrape (EPA) and
  which is too brittle to walk on (HUD), leave moss to a roofer (general
  practice).
- **Caulked joints.** Look at sealant around vents and flashing every
  two years or so (Building America).
- **Write it down.** The EPA recommends a roof logbook and a simple map
  of the roof, with each inspection's findings, photographs and repairs.
  [Keeping Records](/library#keeping-records) gives the shape of a good log.

## Worked example: a brown stain on the bedroom ceiling

It rained hard on Tuesday, and on Wednesday there is a brown ring on the
bedroom ceiling.

1. **Make it safe.** If water is dripping near a light fitting or
   running down a wall past an outlet, keep away from it. Ready.gov's
   flood advice: do not touch electrical equipment if it is wet or if
   you are standing in water, and turn off the electricity only if it
   is safe to do so. Turning off that circuit at the panel, from a dry
   floor, is usually the safe way (general practice). If the ceiling is
   sagging with water, keep people out from under it (general
   practice).
2. **Catch the water** with a bucket, and move what can be moved.
3. **Look in the attic, during or soon after rain,** with a good torch,
   stepping only on the framing, never between it (general practice).
   First look at the insulation from the hatch: if it is loose, pebbly
   and grey-brown or silver-gold, it may be vermiculite, and the EPA
   says to leave it undisturbed, so stay at the hatch and call a
   professional if the trail needs following further. Remember the EPA's third rule: water runs along the underside of a
   rafter or truss for feet before it drips, so the drip above the
   stain is the end of the trail, not the start. Follow the wet wood
   uphill.
4. **Check the usual suspects** at the top of the trail: a chimney, a
   vent or pipe boot, a valley, a roof-to-wall line, a skylight, a nail
   that has worked loose.
5. **Rule out the other sources.** Not every ceiling stain is the roof.
   HUD's guide says plumbing stacks must continue through the roof and
   exhaust ducts must not end in the attic; a bathroom fan blowing moist
   air into the attic can drip like a leak. Frost on the underside of
   the roof deck that melts on a mild day does the same; that is a
   ventilation problem ([Ventilation, Damp and
   Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould)).
6. **Fix the cause, then the damage.** The roof repair itself may be a
   job for a roofer. Drying and repairing the ceiling, within the EPA's
   24 to 48 hours to prevent mould, is in [Repairing a
   Building](/library#repairing-a-building).
7. **Write down** the date, what you found and what was done.

## Know where your own work stops

Plenty of roof care is yours: looking, cleaning gutters from a ladder,
keeping records, clearing snow from the ground. The sources draw the
line in several places:

- **Anything that needs you walking on the roof** without training and
  fall protection (general practice, from OSHA's figures above).
- **Snow removal on the roof itself:** a licensed, insured roofing
  contractor (FEMA).
- **Slate, clay tile and asbestos cement roofs:** repaired or replaced
  by a qualified roofer (HUD).
- **Missing or failed flashing:** a contractor (Building America).
- **Possible asbestos:** a trained and accredited professional (EPA);
  for vermiculite attic insulation that work would disturb, a
  professional asbestos contractor (EPA).
- **Signs the structure is overloaded or failing:** leave the building
  and call an engineer (FEMA).
- **Work near the service line:** the electric utility (general
  practice).

## Traps

- **Caulk as a roof.** Sealant over a failed flashing buys a season; the
  water is still being asked to run uphill under it.
- **Chasing the stain.** Patching the roof directly above a ceiling
  stain often misses, because water travels before it drips.
- **Gutters that empty against the house.** A perfect roof with a
  disconnected downspout can still flood the basement.
- **Going up "just for a minute".** Most of what a roof needs can be
  seen from the ground or the eaves.
- **A long tool near the house line.** The ladder, the rake and the
  gutter section all count toward OSHA's 10 feet, whether they are made
  of metal, wood or plastic.

## How the game models it

The game has roofs, and it models the most important thing a roof does
for the person under it.

- **The Wood Roof.** From the Crafting page you can build a Wood Roof:
  a flat panel 4 m by 4 m and 0.2 m thick, made from 10 Wood Planks,
  which rests on top of walls (`data/blueprints/basic.ron`). Building a
  piece uses up its materials in every play mode, Dev included. On board
  the ship it takes them from your pack first, then from the home's
  storage; on a planet only what you carry counts, because the home's
  storage is in orbit. Finishing a piece gives experience in the
  Shelter Building skill. A roof is never built over a campfire,
  burning, gone out or still going up, because under a roof its smoke
  would fill the shelter ([Making and Controlling
  Fire](/library#making-and-controlling-fire)).
- **A roof keeps the rain and snow off you.** On a planet with weather,
  standing under a finished roof stops rain and snow from landing on
  you, so your clothes stop getting wetter. In the game's body heat
  model, soaked clothes keep only about 70 percent of their insulation,
  and drying them cools you, so this matters in cold rain. A roof also
  hides the cold night sky and shades you by day.
- **Walls keep the wind off, if they face it.** Under a roof, a wall on
  the side the wind comes from stops it, and an open side facing the
  wind lets it in. A roof on three walls with its open side turned away
  from the wind counts as a shelter. The Shelter line on the heads-up
  display says which: "Out of the rain" with how much of the wind gets
  in, "Out of the rain and the wind", or "Sheltered".
- **Taking a piece down.** While holding a piece to place, the Swing
  tool key (F by default) takes down the finished piece you are looking
  at, within 8 m, and every material comes back: into your pack, and
  what does not fit there goes back to storage, with a message saying
  so. A campfire gives back only its stones and the whole logs it has
  not burned.

**One setting changes what you keep.** Settings > Gameplay > "Start
every session from the default home" is off by default, so a roof you
built is kept between launches. With the setting on, only your
character (name, look and outfit) carries between launches, and a roof
you built starts the next session gone.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: its
roof is a flat slab with no slope, no shingles, no flashing, no
gutters and no drip edge, and it never leaks, wears, rots, gathers snow
or blows off. Nothing in the game damages a built piece, and nothing in
it models a fall from a height. In real life a roof is a large part of a
building's maintenance, and the danger in looking after one is the
height.

The shelter test is in `src/systems/construction/uses.rs`
(`shelter_at`, `ShelterCheck`), the weather on the body in
`src/engine/survival_env.rs` and `src/systems/body_heat.rs`, building in
`src/systems/construction/mod.rs` and taking down in
`src/engine/build_place.rs`.

## You own this when

- You do roof checks from the ground, from a ladder at the eaves and from
  the attic, and you leave walking on the roof to people trained and
  equipped for it.
- You keep yourself and anything long you hold, whatever it is made of,
  at least 10 feet from the power line to the house.
- You know whether your attic insulation could be vermiculite, and if it
  could, you leave it undisturbed and look from the hatch.
- You never step on a skylight, and you stay off wet, icy or windy
  roofs.
- You can explain why every piece of a roof laps over the one below,
  and point out the drip edge, step flashing, kick-out flashing and
  pipe boots on your own house.
- You know that a ceiling stain is the end of a trail, and you can
  follow it in the attic.
- You keep gutters clean and sloping, downspout extensions connected,
  and the ground falling away from the walls.
- You know the warning signs of a snow-overloaded roof and that the
  answer is to leave and call an engineer.
- You clear snow, if at all, from the ground with a non-metallic rake,
  well back from the eave, leaving a couple of inches on the roof.
- You inspect twice a year and after big storms, and you write down what
  you find.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- Treating OSHA's 6-foot fall protection threshold as guidance for a
  homeowner, and hiring out work that needs walking on the roof, are our
  reading and general practice; OSHA's rules bind employers.
- A look from the yard and an upstairs window finding most problems;
  the nailing line printed on modern shingles and following the
  maker's instructions; laps having to be wide because of capillary
  action (our reasoning).
- That the manual's 6 1/2-inch figure lands above the printed nail line
  on a common modern three-tab shingle is general knowledge, not from a
  cited source.
- Never stepping on or leaning on a skylight; calling the utility about
  work near the service line; coming down at the first sign of heat
  illness; turning off a circuit from a dry floor; staying out from
  under a sagging wet ceiling; stepping only on the framing in an attic.
- "Upper over lower, everywhere" is our summary of the Army manual's
  shingle laps and the Building America flashing guide.
- That a snow rake of any material, FEMA's non-metallic one included,
  falls under the 10-foot rule is our application of OSHA's note on
  conductive objects to FEMA's advice.
- Looking from the hatch only, and not climbing into an attic that may
  have vermiculite insulation, is our application of the EPA's advice to
  leave it undisturbed.
- Applying HUD's manufactured-home drainage slope to any house is our
  reading.
- Spring and autumn as the two inspection times, and doing moss removal
  from a ladder or the ground, are our suggestions beside the EPA's
  semi-annual inspection and hose method. Not using a pressure washer,
  and leaving moss on other coverings, asbestos cement above all, to a
  roofer, are general practice.
- The gallons per inch of rain, the yearly volume on a 1,200 square foot
  roof at Silverdale's rainfall, and the weight of wet snow on a 1,000
  square foot roof are arithmetic.
