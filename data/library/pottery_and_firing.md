# Pottery and Firing

Clay is one of the few materials a person can dig out of the ground and
turn into something permanent with nothing more than hands, water and
fire. Wet, it takes any shape. Dried, it holds that shape but falls
apart again in the rain. Fired hot enough for long enough, it changes
into a different material altogether: a pot that holds water, a tile
that sheds it, a brick that outlasts the person who made it.

On a homestead that means flowerpots, storage jars, bowls, crocks,
tiles, oven parts and bricks. It also means a kiln or a fire hot enough
to burn you badly, dust that damages lungs, and glazes that can poison
the food served from them for years without anyone noticing. This guide
is about what happens to clay in the fire, how to make and fire simple
pieces, how to keep pottery that touches food safe, and where your own
work should stop and a professional's begin.

It takes its account of what clay is and what firing does from the
Environmental Protection Agency's (EPA) descriptions of how bricks and
ceramics are made, and the National Park Service's teaching material on
Ancestral Pueblo pottery. Its safety rules come from the Consumer
Product Safety Commission's (CPSC) art and craft safety guide, the Food
and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Occupational Safety and Health
Administration (OSHA). All are United States government publications,
in the public domain. Where something is general practice rather than
a published rule, the text says so.

Several other guides sit next to this one. [Stone, Clay and
Earth](/library#stone-clay-and-earth) covers finding clay, digging it safely,
and building with unfired earth. [What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is) gives
the ribbon test for telling clay from silt and sand.
[Firewood](/library#firewood) covers burn bans, carbon monoxide and keeping a
fire under control. [Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables)
covers crocks for food. [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns) is what to
do when the fire wins.

**What depends on where you live.** The clay in your ground, whether you
may burn outdoors on a given day, and the rules for installing a kiln
all change from place to place. This guide says where to ask.

## First: how pottery hurts people

Five things hurt potters: dust, lead and other metals in glazes, the
heat and fumes of the kiln, open fires, and pots that burst. This
section takes them in turn, with the habit that prevents each.

### Dust from dry clay and glaze powder

Wet clay makes little dust. Dry clay turns to dust, and the dust is the
problem.
The CPSC's Art and Craft Safety Guide lists what clay can contain:
hydrated aluminium silicates with crystalline silica, talc, vermiculite,
kaolin, alumina, diatomaceous earth and sand, and, as a contaminant in
some talc and vermiculite, asbestos. It says long-term exposure to clay
dust or powdered mix can cause skin irritation, lung diseases such as
asbestosis and silicosis, and cancer.

OSHA names pottery and ceramics among the products made with
crystalline silica, and making ceramic products among the work that
exposes people to it. It calls silicosis "an incurable lung disease
that can lead to disability and death".

The CPSC's guide gives the habits that keep dust down, and they are the
whole of the defence at home:

- **Use clay that comes already mixed,** where you can. The CPSC
  suggests premixed clays "to minimize exposure to large amounts of clay
  dust."
- **Do the finishing while the piece is still damp.** The guide says not
  to pulverise dry clay or sand "green ware", meaning unfired pottery,
  and to finish it while it is damp or wet. Trimming leather-hard clay
  with a tool makes shavings; sanding dry clay makes dust.
- **Never sweep.** "When cleaning do not sweep dust. Use a wet mop,
  rags, and/or a vacuum with a HEPA filter system." A dry broom puts the
  finest, worst dust back into the air you breathe.
- **Wear a respirator for powders.** The guide calls for a respirator
  approved by the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health
  (NIOSH) when working with powdered glazes, and for avoiding dust
  inhalation generally. A cloth or paper nuisance mask is not one
  (general practice).
- **Keep the dust out of the house.** Wet-clean the work area and the
  floor often, keep a set of clothes for clay work and wash them
  separately, and keep food, drink and children out of the workspace
  (general practice; the CPSC's guide also says to keep food and drinks
  out of the art area).

### Lead and other metals in glazes

A glaze is a thin layer of glass fused onto the clay. The CPSC's guide
lists what glazes may contain: arsenic, uranium, lead, chromium VI,
lithium, beryllium, cobalt, antimony, cadmium, nickel, barium, vanadium
and others, and says they "May be toxic by inhalation, ingestion, and
skin contact." The dust of a raw glaze is a breathing hazard; the
finished glaze can be an eating hazard.

The FDA explains how a pot poisons food. Lead helps glaze particles
melt, and when a pot is fired at the proper temperature for the proper
time, essentially all the lead is bound into the glaze. Fired wrongly,
"the lead may not fuse to the earthenware and may contaminate food when
the pottery is used with food." It adds that potters who switched to
lead-free glazes but kept using old kilns once used for lead glazes can
contaminate their "lead free" pottery with lead left in the kiln.

The effects are slow and silent. The FDA says a child with lead
poisoning may not look or act sick, and that lead poisoning in children
has been linked to learning disabilities, developmental delays and
lower IQ scores. And it is permanent in the pot: "no amount of washing,
boiling, or other process can remove lead from pottery."

The rules that follow:

- **Do not use lead glazes, or glazes whose contents you do not know,
  on anything that may touch food or drink.** The CPSC suggests
  lead-free glazes, or those with sodium, potassium, calcium or
  magnesium fluxes. The simplest household rule is to keep lead glazes
  out of the house altogether (general practice).
- **Read what "food safe" actually means.** The CPSC: "A glaze labeled
  'food safe' does not mean that it is lead-free," only that if it is
  fired and applied properly it will not leach lead or cadmium above
  the levels the FDA allows. Fire it exactly as its label says, at its
  stated temperature, on a clay that matches it (general practice for
  the clay match).
- **Do not mix glazes.** "Do not mix different glazes together because
  this disrupts the balance of ingredients and could make a 'food safe'
  glaze into an unsafe product."
- **Label anything that is not for food.** The CPSC suggests marking
  lead- or cadmium-containing pieces "Contains Lead, Not for Food Use"
  or "Contains Lead, For Decoration Only", and even putting holes in
  useful-looking shapes so nobody fills them.
- **Test what will hold food.** The CPSC says that if there is even a
  slight chance your pottery could be used for food, you should have it
  tested to meet FDA or state standards, "if you sell it, but also if
  you just give pieces to family and friends." The FDA says lead-testing
  kits with swabs are sold in hardware stores and online, and that if a
  test finds leachable lead it strongly advises against using the piece
  for cooking, serving or storing food or drink.

### Kiln heat, fumes and light

A kiln is an oven built to hold the temperatures that turn clay into
ceramic, for hours at a time. The CPSC's guide covers the hazards in one
paragraph:

- **Installation is not a do-it-yourself decision.** "Contact local or
  state authorities for information on proper installation and safe
  operation of all kilns."
- **Fumes.** Indoor use of a kiln, electric or fuel-fired, "requires
  mechanical ventilation to the outdoors." Common kiln emissions, it
  says, include "chlorine, fluorine, carbon monoxide, metallic vapors,
  and ozone". The EPA's account of brick kilns explains one of them:
  clay holds a little fluorine, and as the pieces reach 500 to 600
  degrees C (930 to 1,110 degrees F) it is released, much of it as
  hydrogen fluoride.
- **Burns** from touching hot ware and the kiln itself. Wear the
  protective clothing and gloves the guide calls for.
- **Light.** The guide notes that "heating ceramic materials to glowing
  emits infrared radiation." Look into a glowing kiln only through
  infrared goggles or a welding shield. OSHA's rule on eye protection
  (29 CFR 1910.133) lists "potentially injurious light radiation"
  alongside flying particles and molten metal as hazards eye protection
  is for.
- **Electricity.** The guide says to use outlets protected by a ground
  fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) for electrical equipment. An
  electric kiln is a large electrical load; wiring a circuit for one is
  an electrician's job (general practice).

Keep a kiln clear of anything that burns, on the floor and the wall
behind it, by the distance its maker's instructions give, and do not
leave a firing unattended in its first hours or when it is near its
top temperature (general practice). Carbon monoxide is on the CPSC's
list of kiln emissions, so a kiln indoors also needs a carbon monoxide
alarm in the building (general practice); the full reasons are in
[Firewood](/library#firewood).

### Open fires: pit firing, bonfires and raku

People fired pottery in open fires long before kilns. The Park
Service's lesson on Ancestral Pueblo pottery defines a "fire cloud" as
a dark smudge on a pot that was fired outdoors, where a piece of
burning fuel touched it, and "smothering" as covering the pots with
powdered fuel, usually manure, during firing. Open firing is still
practised, and it brings a fire's hazards with it.

The CPSC's advice on raku firing, which it describes as adding sawdust
or other materials to heated ceramic ware, is the template:

- **Outdoors only.** To avoid carbon monoxide and smoke, "only perform
  raku firing outdoors away from open windows and air intakes."
- **Burns from tongs.** It calls burns from handling the pottery with
  tongs the major hazard with raku.
- **No treated wood.** Avoid sawdust from wood treated with
  preservatives or pesticides, such as chromated copper arsenate.

For any outdoor firing (general practice): check your local burn rules
and any burn ban first, as [Firewood](/library#firewood) describes; build it
well clear of buildings, fences and overhanging branches; keep water
and a shovel at hand; wear cotton or wool, not synthetics, with sturdy
boots and leather gloves; keep children and pets well back; and never
carry hot coals or a smoking pot into a garage or shed.

### Pots that burst

A pot that still holds water when it goes into the fire can crack or
burst as the water turns to steam, and throw hot fragments (general
practice). The EPA's description of ceramic manufacture explains why
factories heat slowly: before the real firing, ware is often held at
temperatures well below firing temperature to finish drying and to
remove residual and chemically bound water, "by gradually raising and
holding the temperature in several stages."

At home that means three habits (general practice): dry pieces fully,
which for a thick piece can take a week or more; warm them slowly at
the start of a firing, whether that is a kiln's slow first hours or
pots set near an open fire before they go in it; and stand clear of an
open firing in its early stages. Thick, uneven walls and closed hollow
shapes are the most likely to burst.

### Machines

The CPSC's guide covers the machines too: pug mills, which mix clay, can
cause crushing and amputation injuries, so their guards stay on; slab
rollers are not used with ties or loose clothing; extruders are fastened
tight to the bench; and throwing wheels and kick wheels can injure skin
and shins. Rest your wrists often against repetitive strain.

## What clay becomes in the fire

### Clay

The EPA defines clay as a natural, earthy, fine-grained material made
largely of a group of crystalline hydrous silicate minerals, the clay
minerals, which are composed mainly of silica, alumina and water and
form from the mechanical and chemical breakdown of rocks. The water is
part of the mineral, not just wet in it, which matters in the fire.

It sorts clays into six groups, four of them about pottery:

| Clay | What the EPA says about it | What that means for a potter |
|---|---|---|
| Common clay | A plastic clay or claylike material with a vitrification point below 1,100 degrees C (2,000 degrees F); the EPA treats making bricks from it in its brick section | The clay most people dig up. It fires at lower temperatures and melts or slumps sooner (our reading of "vitrification point") |
| Ball clay | A plastic, white-firing clay, used for bonding in dinnerware, tile, pottery and sanitaryware | Added to make bodies workable |
| Fire clay | Withstands 1,500 degrees C (2,700 degrees F) or higher; used for refractories | Kiln bricks, oven linings, and stoneware bodies |
| Kaolin | China clay, a white clay mainly of the mineral kaolinite | Porcelain and white wares |

The right-hand column is our reading. A clay you dig yourself is usually
some kind of common clay with sand, silt and organic matter in it
(general practice). Its firing
range is something you find out by testing, not something you can look
up.

### Unfired clay never sets for good

Dried clay is not ceramic. The Park Service's brief on adobe buildings
puts it plainly: "Because adobe bricks are not fired in a kiln as are
clay bricks, they do not permanently harden," but shrink and swell with
their water content, and are weaker the wetter they are. A sun-dried
pot is the same: soak it and it turns back into mud. Only firing
changes that.

### What the fire does, step by step

The EPA's description of brick kilns gives the sequence: in all kilns,
firing takes place in six steps, "evaporation of free water,
dehydration, oxidation, vitrification, flashing, and cooling." In plain
terms (the plain-language glosses are our explanation):

1. **Drying.** The water that made the clay soft leaves. The clay
   shrinks as it goes. The EPA says drying must be controlled to avoid
   "differential shrinkage, warping, and distortion": if one part dries
   faster than another, the piece pulls itself out of shape or cracks.
2. **Dehydration.** The water that is part of the clay minerals leaves.
   This is the step that turns clay into something that will not slake
   back to mud. The EPA's "bisque firing", a first low firing, exists to
   do this and to burn out organic matter before the high firing.
3. **Oxidation.** Organic matter and other impurities burn out.
4. **Vitrification.** The particles fuse. The EPA calls firing the
   process by which ceramics are "thermally consolidated into a dense,
   cohesive body", which factories do by heating to approximately
   two-thirds of the material's melting point and holding it there for
   a set time.
5. **Flashing**, an optional step that colours the surface by starving
   the fire of air.
6. **Cooling.** Slowly, so the piece does not crack (general practice;
   the EPA notes bricks are cooled to near room temperature before they
   leave the kiln).

Two numbers give a sense of scale. The EPA says a brick tunnel kiln's
firing zone is typically held at a maximum of about 2,000 degrees F
(1,090 degrees C), and that for most bricks the whole drying, firing and
cooling process takes between 20 and 50 hours. Pottery is not a quick
job.

Time matters as well as heat. The EPA says a short firing time gives a
product that is "porous and has a low density". An underfired pot is
weak and soaks up water, even if it got hot.

### Earthenware, stoneware, porcelain

The EPA's definitions describe how far vitrification has gone:

- **Earthenware** is "nonvitreous (porous)" ware, glazed or unglazed:
  artware, kitchenware, ovenware, tableware and tile.
- **Stoneware** is "vitreous or semivitreous" ware of fine texture,
  used for cookware, kitchenware, tableware, drainpipe and tile.
- **Chinaware** is vitreous ware "of zero or low absorption after
  firing", and **porcelain** is vitreous ware used mainly for technical
  purposes, such as insulators and chemicalware, as well as tableware.

"Vitreous" means glassy and close to watertight. The FDA draws the
practical consequence for earthenware: it is "a porous form of clay
which must be glazed in order for the pottery to hold food or liquid."
Glazing, it says, fuses a thin, glass-like coating onto the surface to
seal its pores.

### Glaze

The EPA says glazes consist mainly of oxides, made from raw materials
such as quartz, feldspars, carbonates, borates and zircon, and that
depending on their contents they mature at 600 to 1,500 degrees C
(1,110 to 2,730 degrees F). That range is why a glaze and a clay have to
be chosen together: a glaze that matures far above the clay's range
cannot be fired on it, and one that matures far below it will run or
blister (general practice).

## Making a pot by hand

These are the two oldest ways of making a pot without a wheel, and the
ones to learn first. The steps are general practice except where a
source is named.

### Preparing clay you dug yourself

Find your clay as [Stone, Clay and Earth](/library#stone-clay-and-earth)
describes, below the topsoil, and with its digging rules. Then (general
practice):

1. Break it up and let it dry completely, then soak it in a bucket of
   water until it falls apart into a slurry. Dry clay slakes far more
   evenly than wet lumps.
2. Pour the slurry through a kitchen sieve or window screen to take out
   stones, roots and grit. Stones left in clay can crack a pot in the
   fire.
3. Let the slurry settle, pour off the clear water, and dry the rest on
   plaster, cloth or wood until it is soft and no longer sticky.
4. Knead it on a board until it is even all through, to push out air
   and even out the water.

This is all wet work, which is also why it makes little dust; the dry
lumps in step 1 are the dusty stage, so handle them outdoors or damp
them first.

**Temper.** The Park Service's lesson defines temper as "gritty material
added to clay to prevent a piece from shrinking or breaking as it dries
or is fired," and paste as the mixture of clay and temper. A sticky
local clay that cracks as it dries usually wants clean sand or grog
(crushed fired clay) worked in, a little at a time (general practice).

### Worked example: a pinch pot

Roll a ball of clay about the size of an orange. Push your thumb into
the middle to about a finger's width from the bottom, then turn the ball
in one hand while you pinch the wall between thumb and fingers, working
from the bottom up and round and round, until the wall is an even
thickness of about a finger (general practice). Even thickness matters
more than any other single thing: thick spots dry slower and are the
places that crack or burst.

### Worked example: a coil pot

The Park Service's lesson describes the Ancestral Pueblo method,
"coiling and scraping": a clay snake is coiled round in a spiral to
make the base, more coils are added on to build the walls, and the
coils are then scraped together "erasing all signs of the individual
coils", with shaped pieces of wood or gourd as scrapers and smooth
stones to polish the surface. Its classroom version adds the rule that
makes joins hold: everything added "MUST be scored and slipped", that
is, roughened with a tool and painted with slip, a liquid clay, before
it is pressed on.

So (general practice for the details): roll even coils about as thick
as a finger; score and slip each one before you lay it; pinch it down
onto the one below on the inside and outside; and every few coils,
scrape and smooth the wall so it is one piece of clay, not a stack.

### Drying

Dry slowly and evenly (general practice). Cover the piece loosely with
plastic for the first day or two so the rim does not dry ahead of the
base, then uncover it, turn it now and then, and keep it out of sun and
wind. The Park Service's lesson calls the stage at which an unfired pot
can no longer be reshaped "leather hard"; that is the time to trim and
smooth it, while it still makes shavings rather than dust.

A pot is dry enough to fire when it no longer feels cool against your
cheek or the back of your hand, and has stopped getting lighter from
one day to the next (general practice). Cool means water is still
evaporating out of it.

### Worked example: test tiles before a whole batch

A clay you dug, or a clay and glaze you have never fired together,
should be tested before you trust a real piece to it (general
practice). Roll out a slab and cut six tiles. On each, while wet, press
a line exactly 10 cm long. Dry them, then fire them, if you have a kiln,
at a spread of temperatures.

Afterwards, measure the line. If it now reads 9 cm, the clay shrank by
10 percent from wet to fired (arithmetic), and a pot that must come out
20 cm across has to be made about 22 cm across. Drop each fired tile
into water for a day and weigh it before and after: the more it gains,
the more porous it is. A tile that has warped, bloated or partly melted
was fired past what that clay can take. Write down which tile was which
in your records, as [Keeping Records](/library#keeping-records) describes.

### Firing

There are two ways most people start (general practice):

- **In a kiln.** An electric kiln run by a controller is the
  predictable way. A first firing to a low temperature, the bisque,
  then a glaze firing to the temperature the glaze and clay are made
  for. Follow the kiln maker's and the clay and glaze makers'
  instructions for the schedule; the slow start at the bottom of every
  schedule is the part that keeps pots from bursting. A community
  pottery studio, a school or a ceramics supplier that fires other
  people's work is a good way to learn on a kiln that someone else
  installed and maintains.
- **In an open pit or bonfire,** outdoors, with all the precautions
  above. Pre-warm dry pots near the fire for an hour or more, turning
  them, then build the fire up around them gradually and keep it
  burning for hours, and let everything cool in place before you dig
  the pots out. An open firing usually does not get hot enough to
  vitrify clay, so the result is porous earthenware: good for
  flowerpots, dry storage and decoration, not for holding water unless
  it is glazed or sealed.

## Pottery that touches food

Most of the serious harm from pottery reaches people through their
food, so this is worth its own list.

- **Unglazed earthenware is porous.** The FDA says earthenware must be
  glazed to hold food or liquid. A porous pot soaks up what you put in
  it and is hard to clean (general practice).
- **Your own glazed pottery for food:** only commercial glazes sold as
  lead-free and food safe at your firing temperature, fired exactly as
  labelled, never mixed, and tested before you use it, as the lead rules
  above set out.
- **Old and bought pottery.** The FDA lists the "problem types" to
  watch for: pottery that is handmade with a crude appearance or
  irregular shape; antique; damaged or excessively worn; bought from
  flea markets or street vendors or from a maker you cannot judge; or
  brightly decorated in orange, red or yellow, "as lead is often used
  with these pigments to increase their intensity." If you cannot test
  it or establish where it came from, the FDA says, consider not using
  it for cooking, serving or storing food or drinks.
- **A warning stamp means it.** The FDA says pottery made only for
  decoration may have a warning stamped on the bottom, such as "Not for
  Food Use", and that items with such a warning should not be used for
  food or drink.
- **If a child has been eating or drinking from a problem piece,** the
  FDA says to stop using it and to talk to your healthcare professional
  about testing your child's blood for lead: "It's a simple test."
- **Heat and cold.** A pot can crack if you take it from cold to a hot
  stove or oven, or pour cold water into a hot one. Use only ware sold as
  ovenproof or flameproof for cooking, warm it with its contents, and
  never set an empty pot on a burner (general practice).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Installing a kiln.** The CPSC says to contact local or state
  authorities about installation and safe operation, and an indoor kiln
  needs mechanical ventilation to the outdoors. The electrical circuit
  for an electric kiln is an electrician's job, and the gas supply for a
  gas kiln a gas fitter's (general practice).
- **Salt glazing.** The CPSC says to check local building codes to
  ensure salt kilns are allowed at all; with ordinary salt the process
  makes hydrogen chloride gas.
- **Mixing glazes from raw chemicals.** The list of what glazes can
  contain is the reason to buy them ready-made (our reading of the
  CPSC's list).
- **Proving ware is safe for food.** A home swab test is a screen. For
  anything you sell, the CPSC says to have it tested to meet FDA or state
  standards; a laboratory does that (general practice for the
  laboratory).
- **Ovens, fireplaces and chimneys built of clay or brick that will be
  part of a house.** Those are building work with codes behind them;
  [Firewood](/library#firewood) and [Heating Water](/library#heating-water) cover
  the fire side (general practice).

## Traps

- **Firing a pot that is not dry.** It can burst. Wait, and warm slowly.
- **Sweeping the studio.** The worst dust goes back into the air. Wet
  mop or HEPA vacuum.
- **Trusting "food safe" on its own.** It means safe if fired and applied
  as directed, not lead-free.
- **Using grandmother's crock for sauerkraut** without knowing what its
  glaze is. Lead does not wash out.
- **Overfiring dug clay.** Common clay vitrifies below 1,100 degrees C;
  past its range, it slumps or bloats (our reading of the EPA's
  definition). Test tiles first.
- **Looking into a glowing kiln with bare eyes.** Infrared goggles or a
  welding shield.

## How the game models it

The game has clay, a kiln, and a handful of things fired in it.

- **Clay and where it comes from.** Raw Clay is an item in
  `data/items.csv`. Outside the home, the game places clay deposits you
  can collect from, as [Stone, Clay and Earth](/library#stone-clay-and-earth)
  describes.
- **The kiln.** The default family home already has a kiln in its forge
  room, beside the forge and the anvil station
  (`data/machines/home.ron`); the one-person home has none. You can also
  build one: Build Kiln takes 12 Stone Bricks and 4 Raw Clay and needs
  Shelter Building level 3 (`data/recipes.csv`).
- **What the kiln makes** (`data/recipes.csv`): Fire Brick turns 8 Raw
  Clay and 1 charcoal into 4 Stone Bricks, leaving wood ash, and trains
  Metalworking; Shape Planter turns 4 Raw Clay and 1 Paint into 2
  Planters ("Fire and glaze ornamental plant pots"); Cast Sink takes 5
  Raw Clay, and Cast Toilet 8 Raw Clay and 1 Purified Water, both
  training Woodworking; Lay Floor Tiles takes 4 Raw Clay and 1 Cement
  and gives 6 Stone Bricks; and Fire Ceramic Composite makes Concrete
  Blocks from clay, sand and carbon fibre. The kiln also makes charcoal.
  One recipe is worth knowing about so it does not mislead you: Fire
  Porcelain, from 2 Raw Clay, 1 Sand and 1 charcoal, produces a Glass
  Pane, not a piece of porcelain.
- **Food and glaze.** The game's container data counts glazed ceramic as
  food-grade and non-absorbent, with the note "Crocks for fermenting and
  storage while the glaze is lead-free and intact", and its Fermenting
  Crock is described as lead-free glazed stoneware
  (`data/containers/materials.csv`, `data/containers/types.csv`). The
  game has no lead glazes, so the condition in that note is never
  tested.
- **Time.** Fire Brick takes 15 seconds in the game (`craft_time_sec`).
  The EPA's real figure for drying, firing and cooling bricks is 20 to
  50 hours.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, crafting
  uses up no materials. The Crafting page still asks for the materials
  to be in your backpack and for a kiln to be where you are before it
  lets you press Craft, and a recipe that needs a skill level still
  needs it. To make clay something you spend, switch Play mode to
  Normal.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character's name, appearance and
  clothes carry between launches, so the clay you collected, what you
  fired and the skill levels you earned start the next session as they
  were in the default home. Turn the setting off to keep them.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: you
cannot make a pot or a bowl to store or serve food; nothing is shaped,
dried or bisque-fired; nothing shrinks, cracks, warps or bursts; there
is no glaze chemistry and no lead; only some kiln recipes burn fuel;
and the kiln makes no dust, fumes, heat or light that can hurt you. In
real life those are where both the skill and the injuries are.

## You own this when

- You keep clay dust down: premixed clay where you can, finishing while
  damp, wet mopping or a HEPA vacuum, never a broom, and a NIOSH-approved
  respirator for powders.
- You never put food in your own glazed ware unless the glaze is a
  commercial lead-free, food-safe glaze, fired as labelled, unmixed, and
  tested.
- You can name the FDA's problem types of pottery and you do not use them
  for food.
- You know that "food safe" on a glaze label is not the same as
  lead-free.
- You fire only dry pots, warm them slowly, and stand clear early in an
  open firing.
- You run a kiln only where it is ventilated to the outdoors, look into
  it only through infrared goggles or a welding shield, and let the
  authorities and the trades decide how it is installed.
- You do open firings outdoors only, within your local burn rules, away
  from windows and air intakes, with water and a shovel at hand.
- You can explain the difference between earthenware and stoneware, and
  why an unglazed earthenware pot seeps.
- You test a new clay with tiles, and can work out its shrinkage from a
  line you measured.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- Keeping a set of clothes for clay work, wet-cleaning often and keeping
  children out of the workspace, beside the CPSC's rules.
- Keeping lead glazes out of the house altogether, and matching a glaze
  to the clay as well as to its temperature.
- An electrician for a kiln circuit and a gas fitter for a gas kiln;
  clearances from the kiln maker's instructions; not leaving a firing
  unattended; a carbon monoxide alarm where a kiln is used indoors.
- The precautions for outdoor firing: burn rules, clearances, water and
  a shovel, natural-fibre clothing, keeping children back, never
  carrying coals indoors.
- That a pot holding water can burst in the fire, the habits that
  prevent it, and how to tell when a pot is dry.
- That dug clay is usually a common clay with impurities, and how to
  slake, sieve and knead it; adding sand or grog as temper.
- The pinch pot and coil pot details, and drying under plastic.
- Test tiles and the shrinkage and absorption tests; the shrinkage
  figures are arithmetic.
- The firing schedules in outline, pre-warming for open firing, and that
  an open firing usually gives porous earthenware.
- That a porous pot soaks up food and is hard to clean; thermal shock
  in cookware and never heating an empty pot.
- That a glaze maturing far from the clay's range runs, blisters or
  cannot be fired on it, and that overfired common clay slumps or bloats
  (our reading of the EPA's vitrification point).
- That buying glazes ready-made is the answer to their contents (our
  reading of the CPSC's list), and a laboratory for proving ware safe.
