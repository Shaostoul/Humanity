# Force, Levers and Mechanical Advantage

Sooner or later every household has to move something heavier than
anyone in it can lift: a boulder in the way of a fence line, a stump, a
generator that has to go up into a truck, a shed that has to come off
the ground for a new block, a nut rusted onto a bolt. People have done
this for thousands of years without being stronger than you. They used
machines, and nearly all of those machines are a lever or a ramp in
some form.

The Army's rigging manual starts where this guide starts: "A force is a
push or pull." Every machine in this guide does one thing with a push or
a pull. It trades distance for force. You move your end a long way with
a modest effort, and the load moves a short way with a large one. No
machine gives you more work out than you put in. Once that idea is
clear, a crowbar, a wheelbarrow, a block and tackle, a ramp and a car
jack are all the same machine.

This guide takes its physics from the Navy's training course *Basic
Machines* (NAVEDTRA 14037, 1994), which teaches levers, pulleys, ramps
and screws from the beginning with worked numbers, and from the Army's
*Rigging Techniques, Procedures, and Applications* (FM 5-125, 1995). Its
safety rules come from the armed services' joint hand tool manual (TM
9-243), the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) and the
Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC); its units from the National
Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST); and the gravity of the
Moon from NASA. All are United States government publications, in the
public domain. Where something is general practice, arithmetic or our
own example rather than a published rule, the text says so.

Some neighbouring guides: [Units and Converting
Them](/library#units-and-converting-them) has pounds, kilograms and newtons;
[Keeping Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working) has the full rules
for jacks; [Nails, Screws and Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts) covers
how tight a fastener should be; [Metals and
Alloys](/library#metals-and-alloys) explains why a bar bends or
snaps; and [Firewood](/library#firewood) covers splitting wedges, felling and
the chainsaw.

## First: where force hurts people

Machines multiply force, and so they multiply mistakes. These are the
ways moving heavy things injures people, and what the sources say to do.

- **Your back is a lever, and it is the one you cannot replace.** OSHA's
  page on heavy lifting (part of its online guide for electrical
  contractors, but written about lifting in general) calls lifting heavy
  items "one of the leading causes of injury in the workplace", and says
  "Lifting loads heavier than about 50 pounds will increase the risk of
  injury." It explains
  why bending is the problem in this guide's own terms: "Bending moves
  the load away from the body and allows leverage to significantly
  increase the effective load on the back." Its advice starts with your
  spine: "Maintain neutral and straight spine alignment whenever
  possible. Usually, bending at the knees, not the waist, helps maintain
  proper spine alignment." Move the item close and "use your legs when
  lifting an item from a low location"; keep the load as close to your
  body as possible; avoid twisting, and "Turn by moving the feet rather
  than twisting the torso"; lift between mid-thigh and shoulder height
  where you can; use two or more people for loads over 50 pounds (about
  23 kg); and use hand trucks, pallet jacks, and "ramps or lift gates to
  load machinery into trucks rather than lifting it." OSHA makes one
  exception, for large, bulky loads, where it may be better to bend at
  the waist "in order to keep the load closer to your body." It adds
  that cold muscles are less flexible and more easily pulled. Wear
  gloves and sturdy boots for heavy work, and keep your feet out from
  under anything that could drop (general practice).
- **A bar that slips.** The hand tool manual's safety rules for pry bars:
  bars are heavy, and "care must be taken to keep them from falling and
  striking someone"; "make sure the bar does not slip and cause personal
  injury"; and do not use bars for extra heavy work, "since they will
  bend and may cause injury." A bar under load stores energy. When the
  tip slips, the long end you are leaning on drops or springs back.
  Stand so that if it goes, your face, chest and feet are not where it
  goes, and push down rather than pulling up towards yourself (general
  practice).
- **A cheater bar.** It is tempting to slide a length of pipe over a
  wrench handle to shift a stuck nut. The hand tool manual says no: "Do
  not attempt to extend the handle in any way to increase the leverage
  on a wrench. Increased leverage may damage the wrench or the work." A
  wrench or a bolt that breaks under that much force lets go all at
  once, and the person pulling goes with it (our explanation). For a
  rusted nut, the same manual's advice is penetrating oil, and time for
  it to soak in.
- **Anything raised.** Never put any part of yourself under a load held
  up only by a jack or a lever. The hand tool manual's chapter on jacks
  says never to get under a load supported only by a jack, because any
  jack can fail. The full rules (a jack rated for the load, on a firm
  base, the load blocked as soon as it is up) are in [Keeping Things
  Working](/library#keeping-things-working). The same goes for a load you have
  levered up: block it before your hands go anywhere near the gap
  (general practice).
- **Ropes, chains and slings.** OSHA's rule for slings used to lift loads
  (29 CFR 1910.184) is written for workplaces, and every line of it is
  good sense at home. Slings "shall not be loaded in excess of their
  rated capacities"; a damaged one is not used; it is not shortened with
  knots; "Shock loading is prohibited", meaning no jerking or dropping a
  load onto a slack line; hands and fingers stay out from between the
  sling and the load while it is tightened; and "All employees shall be
  kept clear of loads about to be lifted and of suspended loads." The
  Army's rigging manual adds that a rope's breaking strength is not its
  working load, that knots, sharp bends and shock loading can cut a
  rope's strength "as much as 50 percent", and that dead or rotten trees
  and stumps make bad anchors "because they are likely to snap suddenly
  when a strain is placed on the line." A rope, chain or cable that
  breaks under load whips back along its own line, so stand to one side
  of a line under strain, never in line with it (general practice).
- **Loads that roll or slide.** OSHA's lifting guide warns against
  rolling heavy spools: "Once they are in motion, it is difficult to stop
  them." The rigging manual says to keep the angle of skids low "to
  prevent the load from drifting or getting out of control", and warns
  that greasing them can make a load slide sideways without warning.
  Keep people out from below a load on a ramp or a slope, and if you are
  the one moving it from below, plan where you will step aside; never try
  to stop a heavy load that has started to run back (general practice).
- **Things that tip over.** A tall piece of furniture with a child
  climbing its open drawers is a lever, with the front edge of its base
  as the fulcrum and the child's weight far out in front of it (our
  explanation). The CPSC's advice: "Anchor TVs and furniture, such
  as bookcases and dressers, securely to the wall", "store heavier items
  on lower shelves", and keep toys and remotes off the top, where
  children may climb for them. How to fix anchors to a wall is in [Nails,
  Screws and Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts).

If you remember one line from this section: a machine lets you move
something you could never hold, so plan what happens if it lets go
before you start, and make sure nobody's body is in that plan.

## Force, weight and mass

A force is a push or a pull. Weight is a force too: the Navy course
calls it "a measurement of the force, or pull of gravity, on an object."

That creates a word problem worth getting straight. NIST, which keeps
the national standards of measurement, says that in science and
technology the weight of a body is a force, measured in newtons (N), and
gives the example of a 10 kg copper ball, whose weight on the Earth's
surface is about 98 N. But in everyday use, NIST notes, "weight is
usually used as a synonym for mass," measured in kilograms, so that in
its own example, "the briefcase weighs 6 kg", the briefcase has a mass
of 6 kg. Both uses are normal. NIST's advice is that whenever the word
weight is used, it should be made clear which meaning is intended.

The difference matters off the Earth. Mass is how much stuff there is;
it is the same anywhere. Weight depends on the local gravity. NASA's
fact sheets put gravity at the Moon's surface at 1.62 metres per second
per second, 0.165 of the Earth's, and give the standard value for the
Earth as 9.80665 (exact). So a sack with a mass of 50 kg presses down
with about 490 N on Earth and about 81 N on the Moon (arithmetic).

The US customary unit of force is the pound-force. NIST gives one
pound-force as 4.448 222 newtons, so 100 pounds-force is about 445 N. The
Navy course, written for American sailors, gives every force in pounds,
and so does most of this guide; "a 100-pound push" means 100
pounds-force, roughly the push it takes to hold up a 45 kg mass
(arithmetic). Converting between them is covered in [Units and
Converting Them](/library#units-and-converting-them).

You can measure a force with a spring scale, which the Navy course
credits to Hooke: twice the weight stretches the spring twice as far. It
also warns that an old or overloaded spring gives inaccurate readings.
A balance, the other kind of scale, is a lever with equal arms.

## Work: the rule no machine breaks

The Navy course defines work this way: work "is done when a resistance
is overcome by a force acting through a measurable distance." Work is
force times distance. Lift a 90-pound bag 5 feet and you have done 450
foot-pounds of work; in metric units, force in newtons times distance in
metres gives joules (our conversion).

Two things follow, both from the same chapter:

- **Holding is not work.** A sailor holding a suitcase for fifteen
  minutes gets tired, but in this sense does no work, because nothing
  moves. Holding a load still achieves nothing and tires you all the
  same, so put it down or support it rather than stand holding it
  (general practice).
- **The resistance is not always the weight.** Pulling a 900-pound
  handcart along a level dock, you work against the rolling friction of
  the cart, which the course says "may be as little as 90 pounds", not
  against the 900. Lifting fights gravity; rolling fights friction. That is the
  whole case for wheels.

And then the rule that every machine below obeys. Ignoring friction,
"work put in = work put out." A lever that lets you lift 300 pounds with
a 100-pound push makes you push through three times the distance. In
the course's words, the advantage of a lever "is not that it makes any
less work for you, but it allows you to do the job with the force at
your command."

**Mechanical advantage** is how many times a machine multiplies your
force: the load divided by the effort. The course gives this as the rule
that "applies to all machines". If a machine has a mechanical advantage
of 4, your end moves four times as far as the load (arithmetic, from the
work rule).

**Friction** takes its share in every real machine. The course: "no
machine is 100 percent efficient." Efficiency is the work out divided by
the work in. Its examples, used below, run from 75 percent for a block
and tackle down to 20 percent for a screw jack.

## Levers

The Navy course calls the lever "the simplest machine". Every lever has
three parts: the **fulcrum**, the point it pivots on; the **effort**, your
push or pull; and the **resistance**, the load. The distances from the
fulcrum to the effort and to the load are the two **arms**.

The rule for all levers is a balance:

> length of effort arm x effort = length of load arm x load

so the mechanical advantage is the effort arm divided by the load arm.
The course is careful about one thing: use the same units on both sides,
feet with feet and pounds with pounds.

### The three classes

Where the fulcrum sits decides the class.

1. **First class: the fulcrum between the effort and the load.** A
   seesaw, an oar in its oarlock, and, in the course's list, "Crowbars,
   shears, and pliers". You push down; the load goes up.
2. **Second class: the load between the fulcrum and the effort.** The
   course's example is the wheelbarrow. The wheel is the fulcrum. With
   the handles 4 feet from the axle and the centre of the load 1 foot
   from it, a 50-pound lift on the handles raises 200 pounds of sand: a
   mechanical advantage of 4. Load the barrow so the heavy end is over
   the wheel and it gets easier, because the load arm shrinks (the
   formula's answer to the course's own question).
3. **Third class: the effort between the fulcrum and the load.** Your
   forearm is one. The biceps attaches about an inch below the elbow and
   the hand is about 18 inches out, so, by the course's arithmetic,
   holding a 10-pound weight takes a 180-pound pull from the muscle.
   Third-class levers give speed, not force: "you don't use third-class
   levers to do heavy jobs; you use them to gain speed." The course
   suggests trying to close a door by pushing on it 3 or 4 inches from
   the hinges.

Levers do not have to be straight. For a bent or curved handle, the arm
is the straight-line distance from the fulcrum, measured at right angles
to the direction the force acts, not the length around the curve (the
course's method for a pump handle).

### Worked examples from the Navy course

- **A 300-pound crate and a 9-foot bar.** You can push with 100 pounds.
  Put a wooden block under the bar as a fulcrum, 2 feet from the centre
  of the crate, so 6 feet of bar is on your side. Six is three times
  two, so your 100 pounds lifts 300.
- **A paint tin lid.** A 6-inch scraper rests on the rim, 1 inch from the
  edge of the lid, leaving 5 inches to your hand. A lid held down with
  50 pounds needs only 10 pounds at the handle.
- **A wrecking bar.** The course works out a mechanical advantage of 5
  for the curved claw of a wrecking bar pulling a crate apart.

### Using a pry bar well

The hand tool manual describes the crowbar as for "heavy prying and for
moving heavy timbers and other large objects short distances." Its two
instructions for using one are exactly the lever rule: use it "in a
position where the weight of the user's body is exerted downward on the
long section of the lever", and "When possible use a block or other
object as a fulcrum behind the bar, near the spot where the bar's point
is wedged under the object to be moved." A fulcrum close to the load
makes the load arm short and the advantage large.

**Worked example: a rock in a garden bed** (our example, arithmetic). A
bar 1.5 m long, its tip under the edge of a rock, a block 15 cm back from
the tip as the fulcrum. The load arm is 0.15 m and the effort arm 1.35 m,
a mechanical advantage of 9. If lifting that edge of the rock takes 900
N (about 200 pounds-force), you push down with about 100 N, roughly the
effort of holding up a 10 kg bag. But the work rule still applies: push
your end down 30 cm and the edge rises about 3.3 cm.

That is how heavy things are moved with a bar: a little at a time. The
rigging manual describes the same method for jacks, which it calls
jacking in stages: raise the load as far as the tool allows, put
cribbing (blocks of timber laid in alternate directions) directly under
it, lower it onto the cribbing, reset the tool, and repeat. Lever, block,
reset, lever again. Your hands never go under the rock; the block does.

### Your own back, as a lever

Go back to OSHA's sentence about bending. When you bend at the waist to
lift, your lower back is the fulcrum. The weight of your upper body and
the load act a long way out in front of it, and the muscles that hold
you up attach close to the spine, on a very short arm. Like the forearm
above, that is a lever working against you: the muscle force on the back
is many times the load in your hands (our explanation of OSHA's point,
using the course's lever rule). OSHA's two main rules both shorten that
lever. Keeping your back straight and bending at the knees, not the
waist, keeps your upper body over your hips instead of out in front of
them, and holding the load close to your body shortens the load's arm
(our explanation). Only for large, bulky loads does OSHA say bending at
the waist may be better, and then only to keep the load closer.

## Turning force: torque, wrenches and wheels

A force that turns something about an axis has a turning effect that
depends on how hard you push and how far from the axis you push. The
Navy course calls this the moment of force, or torque, measured in
foot-pounds: force times distance from the centre. NIST gives one
pound-force foot as 1.355 818 newton metres, the metric unit.

**A wrench is a wheel and axle.** The course compares the handle to one
spoke of a wheel and the nut to the axle, and says what every mechanic
knows: "the longer the handle, the tighter you can turn a nut." It also
warns that "you can turn a nut too tight and strip the threads or cause
internal parts to seize," which is why a torque wrench exists, and why
"The servicing or repair manual on an engine or piece of machinery tells
you what the torque" on each nut or bolt should be. [Nails, Screws and
Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts) has more on how tight is tight. This is
also the reason behind the hand tool manual's rule against cheater bars:
a longer handle multiplies torque past what the wrench and the bolt
were made to carry.

**Other wheels and axles** in the course: a doorknob; a brace, where a
10-pound push on a handle swinging through a 5-inch radius turns a
screwdriver blade with a quarter-inch radius against 200 pounds; and the
old well windlass, with an 8-inch crank and a 2-inch drum, a mechanical
advantage of 4. The bicycle runs the other way, using a small sprocket
and a large wheel to gain speed.

**Balancing moments.** When something is at rest, the turning effects
on each side of the pivot are equal. The course works this out for two
sailors carrying a 300-pound crate slung from a pole between their
shoulders, 8 feet apart, with the crate 3 feet from Sam and 5 feet from
Slim. Sam, nearer the load, carries 187.5 pounds; Slim carries 112.5. The
person nearer a slung load carries more of it. If two people of
different strength carry something on a pole, put the load nearer the
stronger one (our application).

## Pulleys and the block and tackle

A block is a frame holding one or more grooved wheels, called sheaves;
a tackle is blocks and rope together. The Navy course's rules, which it
says will give you the advantage of most tackles:

- **A single fixed block** changes only the direction of the pull. Its
  mechanical advantage is 1: to lift 1 pound you pull 1 pound. That is
  still worth having, because you can pull from where it suits you; the
  course's example is a sailor hauling flags up to a yardarm by pulling
  down, instead of climbing up with them.
- **A single movable block**, hooked to the load, has a mechanical
  advantage of 2. Each half of the rope carries half the load, so a
  200-pound cask needs a 100-pound pull, and your end of the rope moves
  2 feet for every foot the load rises.
- **Count the parts of rope going to and from the movable block.** That
  number is the mechanical advantage, "at a glance". Tying the dead end
  of the rope to the movable block adds 1.
- **Stacked tackles multiply, never add.** A tackle with an advantage of
  3 pulling on one of 4 gives 12. The course: "Don't make the mistake of
  adding mechanical advantages. Always multiply them."

**Worked example from the course.** A tackle with five parts of rope at
the movable block raises a 600-pound load 20 feet. Without friction the
pull is 600 divided by 5, 120 pounds, and 100 feet of rope passes
through your hands. In practice the course reckons on about 160 pounds,
which makes the tackle 75 percent efficient.

**Friction in blocks.** The Army's rigging manual gives a rule of thumb:
"Each sheave in the tackle system can be expected to create a resistance
equal to about 10 percent of the weight of the load." Its example: a
5,000-pound load, a tackle with a mechanical advantage of 4 and rope
running over four sheaves. Friction adds 40 percent of the load, 2,000
pounds, so the pull is 5,000 plus 2,000, divided by 4: 1,750 pounds, not
1,250.

**Worked example: a sack into a loft** (our example, arithmetic). A 25
kg sack weighs about 245 N. Hung from a single movable block, with the
rope then led through a fixed block at the top so you pull downwards,
the advantage is 2. Two sheaves add about 20 percent of the load, about
49 N, by the rigging manual's rule, so the pull is (245 + 49) / 2, about
147 N: roughly what it takes to hold up a 15 kg mass. You pull twice the
height of the loft in rope, and you stand to one side of the drop, not
under it.

### The rope is the weak link

The Navy course puts it bluntly: "the strength of your fall--rope--is a
limiting factor in any tackle." Its example is a big tackle on a rope
too small for the load: "The winch could take it, but the rope
couldn't."

The Army manual explains how to size a rope. The **breaking strength**
is the load at which it parts. The **safe working capacity** is the
breaking strength divided by a factor of safety, "a minimum standard FS
of 4." Its example: a new 1-inch Number 1 Manila rope breaks at 9,000
pounds, so it is used for no more than 2,250. Always use a factor of
safety, the manual says, because a rope weakens with use and weather,
and because "shock loading, knots, sharp bends, and other stresses" can
reduce its strength as much as 50 percent. For fibre rope without a
table, its rule of thumb is a safe working capacity in tons equal to the
square of the diameter in inches: a half-inch rope, a quarter of a ton.
For modern synthetic rope, use the working load on the maker's label
(general practice).

Every part of a lift has to carry the load, not just the rope. OSHA's
rule for chain slings says the hooks, rings and links "shall have a rated
capacity at least equal to that of the alloy steel chain with which they
are used" or the sling must not be used beyond "the rated capacity of
the weakest component." The anchor, the hook, the block, the rope and
the knot: the weakest of them is the strength of the whole system (our
summary of the same rule).

## Ramps, wedges and screws

The Navy course lists six simple machines, but notes that physicists
"recognize only two basic principles in machines: those of the lever and
the inclined plane." The wheel and axle, the block and tackle and gears
"may be considered levers"; the wedge and the screw "use the principle
of the inclined plane."

### The ramp

Rolling a 300-pound barrel straight up into a truck bed 3 feet high would
take a 300-pound lift. Up a 9-foot plank it takes, by the course's
arithmetic, 100 pounds, because the plank is three times as long as the
rise. The theoretical mechanical advantage of a ramp is its length
divided by its height.

Friction changes the real number, and it works both ways. The course's
second example: a 400-pound crate pushed up a 12-foot plank rising 3 feet
should need 100 pounds, but it may take 150, the extra 50 going to
friction between crate and plank. The same friction "helps you hold back
the crate from sliding down the inclined ramp."

**Worked example: a generator into a pickup** (our example,
arithmetic). A 150 kg machine on wheels weighs about 1,470 N. The truck
bed is 0.8 m up. A 2.4 m ramp gives an advantage of 3, so pushing it up
takes about 490 N plus rolling friction: about what it takes to hold up
a 50 kg mass. A 4.8 m ramp halves that. Even so, it is a two-person job.
Before you move it, shut the generator off and let it cool, close its
fuel valve if it has one, and keep it upright so fuel and oil cannot
spill onto a hot engine or into the truck bed. Use ramps rated for the
load and hooked or strapped to the tailgate, chock the truck's wheels,
keep everyone else to the side, and if it starts to roll back, step
aside and let it go (all general practice).
OSHA's lifting guide recommends exactly this, ramps or lift gates
instead of lifting.

### The wedge

A wedge is two inclined planes back to back. The course: "Long, slim
wedges give high mechanical advantage," and the greatest value of the
wedge is that it works where nothing else will, as in splitting a log.
Axes, knives and chisels are wedges. Splitting wood safely is in
[Firewood](/library#firewood).

### The screw

A screw, the course explains, "is actually an inclined plane wrapped
around a cylinder." One turn of the handle moves the load by one
**pitch**, the distance between threads. Its example is a jack screw with
a 24-inch handle and a quarter-inch pitch: the handle travels about 150
inches for every quarter inch of lift, a theoretical advantage of about
600, so a 50-pound pull could in theory lift "about 30,000 pounds".

In practice most of that is lost to friction, and on purpose. The
course says jack threads are cut so that more force goes into
overcoming friction than into useful work, because otherwise "the weight
of the load would cause the jack to spin right back down to the bottom as
soon as you released the handle." Its estimate for one jack is 20
percent efficient. Vises, clamps and turnbuckles hold for the same
reason. The friction that wastes your effort is what keeps the load
from running back.

## Pressure: the same force over more area

The Navy course: "Pressure is the amount of force within a specific
area": force divided by area, in pounds per square inch (psi) or newtons
per square metre. Its example is a 160-pound man on snow. Spread over
the roughly 60 square inches of his shoe soles, his weight presses at
about 2.6 psi and he breaks through the crust. On snowshoes of about 900
square inches it is about 0.18 psi, and the snow holds him. Same weight;
far less pressure.

The same arithmetic explains three things you will meet:

- **A jack on soft ground.** All the load goes through a small base, and
  the base sinks or tips. OSHA's rule for jacks requires the base to be
  blocked where there is no firm foundation, and the rigging manual says
  blocking used as a foundation for jacks "should be sound and large
  enough to carry the load", with the timbers "dry, free from grease, and
  placed firmly on the ground so that the pressure is evenly distributed."
- **Skids under a heavy load.** The rigging manual puts timber skids under
  heavy loads to "Distribute the weight over a greater area" and to give
  a smooth surface for moving.
- **A sharp edge.** A knife, an axe or a chisel puts a modest force on a
  very small area, which is why it cuts (general physics).

## Friction: the force that helps and hinders

The course defines friction as "the resistance that one surface offers
to its movement over another surface," and says it depends on the two
surfaces and the force pressing them together.

Friction is often the thing you want. It is what your boots stand on,
the reason sand or grit on an icy path helps, and the reason, in the
course's words, that "Nails hold structures together". It is what holds
a load on a ramp and a jack at its height.

When you want something to move, cut friction. Lubricate what slides,
and turn sliding into rolling, because "rolling friction is always less
than sliding friction." That is what wheels, bearings and rollers are
for. The rigging manual's method for moving very heavy loads on rollers:
hardwood or pipe rollers, smooth and long enough to pass completely
under the load, running on timber skids, usually four to six of them;
roll the load slowly forward, and as rollers come out behind, carry them
round to the front. Keep fingers and toes out from in front of a roller
that is about to take the load (general practice).

## Know where your own work stops

The arithmetic in this guide is the same arithmetic a rigger uses. The
judgement about when a job is too big comes from experience, and these
are the places to hand over (general practice unless a source is named):

- **Anything lifted over people, or high enough to kill if it falls,**
  and anything moved by crane, hoist or vehicle that you have not been
  trained to rig. OSHA's sling rule asks for slings to be inspected each
  day by "a competent person designated by the employer"; at home, that
  means somebody who actually knows how.
- **Taking out a wall, a post or a beam,** or jacking a building. The load
  it carried does not disappear; it moves somewhere else. That is a job
  for a builder or an engineer.
- **Felling trees and working on hung-up trees,** where huge forces are
  stored in wood that is under load. See [Firewood](/library#firewood).
- **Vehicle recovery with winches,** where cables are under enormous
  tension, and any job needing a load on stands that you cannot block
  properly.
- **Anything where the failure would hurt somebody other than you.**

## How the game models it

The game models gravity and mass, and not much else in this guide.

- **Every world has its own gravity.** Each world you can walk on
  carries a surface gravity in its data file (`data/planets/`: Earth
  9.81 metres per second per second, Mars 3.72, the Moon 1.62, Pluto
  0.62), and walking, jumping and falling on its surface use it. The
  Moon's 1.62 is the value NASA gives. The Cosmos page shows each body's
  surface gravity in metres per second per second and in g, and the F2
  performance overlay shows the gravity acting on you while you are on a
  surface.
- **The game counts mass, not weight.** Every item has a mass in
  kilograms (the `weight_kg` column of `data/items.csv`), and the
  Inventory page's Weight tile adds them up against 50 kg. That is
  weight in NIST's everyday sense, which is mass. The game's carrying
  limit is 50 kg. Wearing a small or large backpack raises that limit
  inside the game by 10 or 25 kg (`data/equipment.csv`), but the Weight
  tile does not know about it: it still shows your load out of 50 kg
  with a backpack on. The limit is the same on every world: on the
  Moon you can carry 50 kg, as on Earth, though in reality that load
  would press on your shoulders with about a sixth of the force. Going
  over the limit, with or without a backpack, does not slow you down
  or do anything else yet.
- **Levers and wheels are items, not machines.** You can forge a crowbar
  ("Leverage prying bar") and build a wheelbarrow, but nothing in the
  game uses either to multiply a force, and there are no pulleys, ramps
  or jacks that change how hard anything is to move.
- **Buildings are not checked for load.** The code has a prototype
  structural check, in which load runs down through framing to the
  foundation and an overloaded member breaks and passes its load to its
  neighbours, but it is not connected to building yet. Nothing you build
  stands or falls because of the forces in it.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there
are no lifting injuries, no bars that slip, no ropes that snap and no
loads that roll away. In the game, nothing heavy can hurt you by being
heavy. Do not carry that habit outside.

## You own this when

- You keep your back straight and bend at the knees, keep a load close
  to your body, turn with your feet, and get help or a machine for
  anything over about 50 pounds (23 kg).
- You can name the fulcrum, the effort and the load on any lever, and
  work out its mechanical advantage from the two arms.
- You know that every machine trades distance for force, and that no
  machine gives you more work than you put in.
- You use a pry bar with a block close to the load, your weight on the
  long end, and your body out of the way if it slips.
- You never extend a wrench handle with a pipe, and you soak a rusted nut
  in penetrating oil instead.
- You raise heavy things in stages and block them, and you never put a
  hand or foot under a load held only by a jack or a bar.
- You can count the parts of rope at the movable block and know the
  advantage of a tackle, allow for friction, and size the rope with a
  factor of safety of at least 4.
- You stand clear of suspended loads and out of line with a rope under
  strain, and you never tie to a dead or rotten tree.
- You can work out the push needed on a ramp from its length and height,
  you keep everyone else out from below the load, and you would let a
  load that runs back go rather than try to stop it.
- You know weight from mass, and pounds-force from newtons.
- You anchor tall furniture and keep the heavy things on the low
  shelves.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- Gloves and sturdy boots for heavy work, and feet out from under
  anything that could drop.
- Standing out of the path of a bar that might slip, pushing down rather
  than pulling up, and blocking a levered load before hands go near it.
- That a broken wrench or bolt lets go all at once (our explanation of
  the hand tool manual's rule).
- That a rope, chain or cable that breaks whips back along its line, and
  standing to one side of a line under strain.
- Keeping people out from below a load on a ramp or slope, stepping
  aside rather than trying to stop a load that runs back; rated ramps
  fixed to the tailgate; chocking the truck; two people for the
  generator; shutting a generator off, letting it cool, closing its fuel
  valve and keeping it upright before moving it.
- Putting a held load down rather than standing holding it.
- How a straight back, bent knees and a close load each shorten the
  lever on your spine (our explanation of OSHA's rules).
- Furniture tipping as a lever, and the back as a lever (our explanations
  of the CPSC's and OSHA's advice, using the Navy course's lever rule).
- The metric conversions and every worked example marked as ours: the
  rock and the 1.5 m bar, the sack into the loft, the generator up the
  ramp, the 50 kg sack on Earth and the Moon, and the joules.
- Putting a slung load nearer the stronger carrier.
- Using the maker's working load for synthetic rope.
- The weakest component as the strength of the whole system (our
  summary of OSHA's chain sling rule).
- That a sharp edge cuts by concentrating force on a small area, and
  keeping fingers and toes out from in front of rollers.
- The list of jobs to hand over, beyond OSHA's competent person.
