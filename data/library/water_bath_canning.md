# Water-Bath Canning

A boiling-water canner is a deep pot with a rack and a lid, and it is
where most people start canning: applesauce, peaches, jam, pickles,
tomatoes. With the right food and a tested recipe it is safe, ordinary
kitchen work. With the wrong food, or with a recipe nobody tested, it is
one of the few kitchen jobs that can kill the people you feed. The pot is
the same in both cases. What differs is what goes into it and how long it
stays there.

This guide is about that pot and the foods that belong in it: which foods
can go in a boiling-water canner and why the line falls where it does,
where tested recipes come from, how to follow one step by step (jars,
headspace, processing time, altitude, checking the seals), and what to do
with a jar that did not seal or looks wrong. The worked example is a batch
of applesauce in Silverdale, Washington, where the simulation is set.

It is the practical half of a pair. [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) is the reasoning: the organism behind
botulism, the pH 4.6 line, why a jar cannot be judged by looking at it,
and why altitude changes the numbers. Read it first; this guide does not
repeat it. [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) has the acid
amounts for tomatoes, a complete tested procedure for crushed tomatoes,
and the step-by-step disposal of a jar that has gone bad. Everything that
cannot go in a water bath is in [Pressure Canning](/library#pressure-canning).
The tested canning times for sauerkraut and fermented pickles are in
[Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables), and [Telling Spoiled
From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) covers spoiled food in general.

Its sources are the US Department of Agriculture's Complete Guide to Home
Canning and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), both US
federal and public domain, and, restated in our own words, the National
Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) at the University of Georgia
and Kansas State University's extension service. Where something is
general practice or our reading, the text says so.

## First, where water-bath canning can hurt you

### The wrong food in the pot

A boiling-water canner is for acid foods only. The USDA guide says it in
one line: "Low-acid foods must be processed in a pressure canner to be
free of botulism risks." Green beans, corn, potatoes, carrots, meat, fish
and soup in a water bath are not a shortcut. The CDC's warning is
specific: "Do not use a boiling water canner for low-acid foods because it
will not protect against botulism." The next section says exactly which
foods qualify, and [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)
explains why boiling for longer does not make the others safe.

### A recipe nobody tested

The safety of a jar comes from a process somebody measured: this food,
prepared this way, in this size of jar, for this long, at this altitude.
The CDC is blunt about where recipes come from: "Use only recipes and
cookbooks that follow the steps in the USDA guide." And in the next
breath: "Do not use other recipes, even if you got them from a trusted
friend or family member."

### Boiling water, steam and hot glass

A canner holds gallons of boiling water, and its jars are full of boiling
food. The USDA guide has you move jars with a jar lifter, gripped below
the neck and kept upright, and cool them on racks or towels. Lift the
canner's lid so the steam rises away from your face, keep children and
pets out of the kitchen while it is on, and never set a hot jar on a cold
or wet surface (general practice). Check every jar for chips and cracks
before you fill it (general practice): the USDA guide, writing about
reused mayonnaise jars, warns that scratches in glass that look
insignificant can crack a jar in the canner.

### A jar that has gone wrong

The USDA: "Do not taste food from a jar with an unsealed lid or food that
shows signs of spoilage." A spoiled jar is dangerous to handle as well as
to eat, so it is not opened, emptied into the sink or rinsed out; the
USDA guide has you wear rubber or heavy plastic gloves for it. The full
disposal procedure, which depends on whether the jar is still sealed, is
in [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew), under "When a jar
goes wrong". Follow it as written.

## Which foods can go in a boiling-water canner

The USDA draws the line at pH 4.6, a measure of acidity in which lower
numbers are more acid. [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)
explains the line; here is what it means in the kitchen. In the USDA's
words: "Acid foods have a pH of 4.6 or lower. They include fruits,
pickles, sauerkraut, jams, jellies, marmalades, and fruit butters."

You do not measure the pH yourself. A tested recipe has already put the
food on the right side of the line, with acid added where it is needed,
and it does so only as written. [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains why household pH strips are not
a substitute.

**Into the water bath, by a tested recipe:**

- most fruits: applesauce, apple slices, berries, cherries, peaches,
  pears, plums and the rest of the USDA's fruit guide;
- fruit juices, jams, jellies, marmalades and fruit butters;
- pickles and relishes made by a tested recipe with vinegar of 5 percent
  acidity;
- sauerkraut and fermented pickles, whose times are in [Fermenting
  Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables);
- tomatoes, with acid added to every jar.

**Only with acid added to every jar.** Three foods that seem to belong in
the list above are not reliably acid on their own. The USDA guide says
some tomatoes are now known to sit slightly above pH 4.6, and figs too,
so both must be acidified with lemon juice or citric acid before they go
in a water bath. Its fruit guide says the same of Asian pears, which it
says "must be acidified before canning in a boiling water canner". The
tested recipe gives the amount for each jar size: USDA's fig and Asian
pear recipes measure it as bottled lemon juice (or, for figs, citric
acid), and the amounts for tomatoes are in [Keeping What You
Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew).

**Mixtures, only by a tested recipe.** Salsa, chutney and relish mix
acid fruit or tomatoes with low-acid foods such as onions and peppers.
The USDA guide warns that most mixtures of low-acid and acid foods sit
above pH 4.6 unless the recipe adds enough lemon juice, citric acid or
vinegar to make them acid foods. That is why a salsa from a tested recipe
can be canned and the same salsa with an extra onion is an untested one
(our reading).

**Not at all, because nobody has tested a process.** The USDA's fruit
guide covers fruit purees, but not purees of bananas, Asian pears, figs,
tomatoes, cantaloupe and other melons, papaya, ripe mango or coconut:
"There are no home canning recommendations available for purees of these
products." Freeze or dry those instead (general practice; see [Keeping
What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) and [Drying
Food](/library#drying-food)).

**Never in a water bath.** Vegetables, unless a tested recipe has made
them acid (acidified tomatoes, pickles, relishes and salsas) or the
USDA's fruit guide lists them, as it lists stewed rhubarb; all meat,
poultry, fish, seafood and milk; and soups and stews made from them.
Those are low-acid foods. They go in a pressure canner by a tested
process, or they are not canned at all ([Pressure
Canning](/library#pressure-canning)).

### Why the line falls where it does

[Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) has the whole argument.
In short, the bacterium that causes botulism forms spores that the USDA
guide calls very hard to destroy at boiling-water temperatures, and in a
sealed jar of moist, low-acid food they can grow. Acid stops them. In the USDA's words, acid foods "contain
enough acid to block their growth, or destroy them more rapidly when
heated." So the job a water bath has to do in an acid food is a smaller
one, and boiling water can do it: the USDA puts processing times for acid
foods in a boiling-water canner at 5 to 85 minutes, against 7 to 11 hours
for low-acid foods. That second figure is why low-acid foods go in a
pressure canner instead.

Acid foods still need the full process. The USDA guide lists what proper
canning does: it removes oxygen, destroys the food's enzymes, prevents
the growth of undesirable bacteria, yeasts and moulds, and helps form the
high vacuum that seals the lid. Mould does grow in acid food that was not
processed properly: the USDA guide reports mycotoxins found in jars of
jelly with mould on the surface, and it no longer recommends paraffin or
wax seals for any sweet spread.

## Where tested recipes come from

- **The USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning** (Agriculture Information
  Bulletin No. 539, December 2009, revised 2015) is the authority, and it
  is free. The National Center for Home Food Preservation hosts it as
  eight files, an introduction and seven guides. For a water bath you
  want Guide 2 (fruit), Guide 3 (tomatoes), Guide 6 (fermented foods and
  pickled vegetables) and Guide 7 (jams and jellies), with Guide 1
  (principles) for the rules that apply to all of them.
- **The National Center for Home Food Preservation** publishes the
  USDA's tested processes on its website, with later updates.
- **Your county extension office.** Around Silverdale that is WSU
  Extension Kitsap County. The USDA guide sends you to your county
  extension agent if you do not know your altitude, and extension offices
  answer canning questions (general practice).

The CDC adds a warning about old recipes: "If your recipe is old, be sure
to use current processing times and pressures." The USDA's own preface
says research continues, and: "Make sure your food preservation
information is always current with up-to-date tested guidelines."

### What a tested recipe tells you

Each USDA recipe gives the same things, and every one of them is part of
the process (our reading of the guides):

- how much fresh food fills a canner load;
- what quality of food to choose;
- how to prepare it, including any acid to add;
- the pack: hot (the food heated before it goes into the jar) or raw (the
  food packed unheated, with hot liquid poured over);
- the jar sizes it was tested in;
- the headspace, the gap left between the top of the food and the lid;
- a table of processing times with one column for each range of
  altitude.

The USDA's general headspace is 1/4 inch for jams and jellies and 1/2
inch for fruits and tomatoes in a water bath; where a recipe gives a
different figure, use the recipe's. Processing times for half-pint and
pint jars are the same, as are times for 1-1/2 pint and quart jars, and
the USDA guide keeps half-gallon jars for very acid juices. A recipe
tested in pints is not a quart recipe (our reading).

### What you must not change

- **The acid, and anything that changes it.** No less lemon juice,
  citric acid or vinegar, no vinegar of unknown strength, and no extra
  low-acid ingredient in a mixture (the USDA's pickling cautions; [Why
  Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).
- **The proportions in pickles.** The USDA's caution reads: "Do not alter
  vinegar, food, or water proportions in a recipe or use a vinegar with
  unknown acidity."
- **The jar size, the pack and the time.**
- **The size of a batch of jam or jelly.** The USDA guide says to make one
  batch at a time, according to the recipe, and warns that increasing the
  quantities often results in soft gels.
- **The sugar in traditional jams and jellies.** The USDA guide says too
  little sugar prevents gelling and may let yeasts and moulds grow.
  Reduced-sugar spreads have their own tested recipes, made with modified
  pectins.

What you may change: salt in canned tomatoes and in fresh-pack pickles,
where it is seasoning, but never in fermented sauerkraut or fermented
pickles, where it is part of the safety ([Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)). And the syrup in canned
fruit: the USDA guide says syrup helps fruit keep its flavour, colour and
shape but does not prevent spoilage, and that fruit can be canned in
water or unsweetened juice instead.

## What you need

- **A boiling-water canner.** In the USDA's description it is made of
  aluminium or porcelain-covered steel, with a removable rack and a fitted
  lid, and it is deep enough that at least 1 inch of briskly boiling water
  will be over the tops of the jars during processing. On an electric
  range it must have a flat bottom, and it should be no more than 4
  inches wider than the element. Kansas State University's guide adds
  that a flat-bottomed stockpot with a lid and a rack in the bottom will
  do the job. On a smooth glass cooktop, follow the cooktop maker's advice:
  Kansas State warns that some cooktops switch a burner off when it gets
  too hot, which can leave the food under-processed. It also says that on
  these cooktops the canner's bottom must be completely flat and no more
  than an inch wider than the burner, and that some water-bath canners are
  not recommended on them at all, because their bottoms are not flat.
- **Home-canning jars.** Regular or wide-mouth Mason-type jars are the
  USDA's best choice, and with care they can be used again and again
  with a new lid each time. Commercial mayonnaise or salad dressing jars
  can be used for acid foods with new two-piece lids, but the USDA guide
  warns you to expect more failed seals and broken jars. Jars with wire
  bails and glass caps, and zinc caps, are not recommended for canning at
  all.
- **New flat lids, and screw bands.** A flat lid seals once; the bands
  can be used many times. The USDA guide says the sealing compound in
  unused lids works for at least 5 years from manufacture, so buy only a
  year's worth, prepare them as the lid maker directs, and do not use old,
  dented or deformed lids.
- **A jar lifter and a plastic spatula** (the USDA says plastic, not
  metal, for freeing air bubbles), clean paper towels to wipe the rims, a
  timer, and a towel or a rack for the jars to cool on.
- **Whatever the recipe calls for**: bottled lemon juice or citric acid,
  vinegar of 5 percent acidity, canning or pickling salt (USDA).

Not on the list, from the USDA guide: open-kettle canning, and processing
jars in ovens, microwaves or dishwashers, which do not prevent all risks
of spoilage; canning powders, which it calls useless; and paraffin wax.
The 2015 USDA guide did not recommend steam canners, because their
processing times were still being researched. Kansas State's 2026 guide
reports University of Wisconsin research published in 2015 and says an
atmospheric steam canner may be used for acid foods as long as strict
conditions are met, among them a tested boiling-water recipe whose time,
after the altitude adjustment, is 45 minutes or less. This guide covers
the boiling-water canner only. If you own a steam canner, use it by your
extension service's current guidance (our reading). An electric
multicooker with a canning button is not a canner of either kind
([Pressure Canning](/library#pressure-canning)).

## Step by step

This is the USDA's boiling-water procedure from Guide 1, in order. The
recipe you follow adds its own preparation, headspace and time.

1. **Choose the recipe and read all of it.** Find your jar size, your
   pack and the time in your altitude column before you start.
2. **Check and wash the jars.** Look and feel for chips and cracks on the
   rims (general practice). Wash the jars in hot water with detergent and
   rinse them well, or run them through a dishwasher (USDA).
3. **Keep the jars hot.** Stand them in water in the canner or a large
   stockpot, bring it to a simmer (180 F) and leave them there until you
   fill them (USDA). Washing and heating do not sterilise jars, and they
   need not be sterile when the processing time is 10 minutes or more.
   For a recipe processed for less than 10 minutes, which includes some
   jams, jellies and pickles, sterilise the empty jars first: boil them
   for 10 minutes below 1,000 feet, with 1 more minute for each additional
   1,000 feet (USDA).
4. **Prepare the lids** as the lid maker directs (USDA).
5. **Start the canner.** Fill it halfway with clean water, which is about
   right for a load of pints; for other loads, adjust it so the water will
   end 1 to 2 inches over the filled jars. Preheat the water to 140 F for
   raw-packed food or 180 F for hot-packed food (USDA).
6. **Prepare the food exactly as the recipe says.** Hot packing is the
   USDA's preferred style for a water bath, because it drives the most
   air out of the food.
7. **Fill the jars.** Add any acid the recipe calls for, then fill,
   leaving the recipe's headspace.
8. **Free the air bubbles** by sliding the plastic spatula down between
   the food and the glass and moving it up and down as you turn the jar,
   then check the headspace again and top up if needed. Jams, jellies and
   juices do not need this step (USDA).
9. **Wipe the rim** with a clean, damp paper towel, because food left on
   the sealing surface can stop the seal. Put on the lid and screw the band
   over it, tightened as the lid maker directs (USDA). The USDA guide gives
   both ways this goes wrong: too loose, and liquid escapes and seals fail;
   too tight, and air cannot get out, food discolours in storage, and lids
   can buckle and jars break.
10. **Load the canner.** Lower each jar onto the rack with the jar lifter,
    upright. Add boiling water until it stands at least 1 inch above the
    tops of the jars, or at least 2 inches when the processing time is
    over 30 minutes (USDA).
11. **Bring it to a full boil.** Lid on, heat on high, until the water
    boils vigorously. Only then set the timer, for the full time in your
    altitude column (USDA).
12. **Keep it boiling.** You may turn the heat down a little as long as a
    complete boil holds. The USDA: "If the water stops boiling at any time
    during the process, bring the water back to a vigorous boil and begin
    the timing of the process over, from the beginning." Add boiling
    water as needed to keep the water above the jars.
13. **Finish.** When the time is up, turn off the heat and take off the
    lid. Wait 5 minutes, then lift the jars out with the jar lifter,
    upright, onto a towel, with at least an inch between them (USDA).
14. **Leave them alone** for 12 to 24 hours at room temperature. The
    USDA: "Do not retighten lids after processing jars." Do not cool the
    jars in cold water either: the USDA guide lists that among the things
    that can make the food spoil. If a jar lost a lot of liquid in the
    canner, leave it closed. In the USDA's words: "If a jar loses
    excessive liquid during processing, do not open it to add more
    liquid."
15. **Test the seals, then store the jars** (below).

### Adjusting for altitude

Water boils at a lower temperature the higher you are, so a jar in a
boiling-water canner gets less heat for every minute it spends there. The
USDA guide says that using the sea-level processing time may result in
spoilage at altitudes of 1,000 feet or more, and every recipe table has a
column for each range of altitude. Use the column for the place where you
are canning, every time. The same correction applies to the other boiling
times in this guide: sterilising empty jars and the general sweet-spread
process (below) each add 1 minute for each 1,000 feet (USDA).

If you do not know your altitude, the USDA guide says to ask your county
extension agent. For Silverdale the answer is in the locale data: the
area runs from sea level to about 178 metres, which is about 584 feet, so
every jar canned there uses the first column, 0 to 1,000 feet
([Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa), from USGS
elevation data). Anyone canning outside that area looks up their own
elevation first (general practice).

### Testing the seals

After the jars have cooled for 12 to 24 hours, take off the screw bands
and test each lid in one of the USDA's three ways:

1. Press the middle of the lid with a finger or thumb. If it springs up
   when you let go, the lid is not sealed.
2. Tap the lid with the bottom of a teaspoon. A sealed lid gives a
   ringing, high-pitched sound. A dull sound means it is not sealed, or
   that food is touching the underside of the lid.
3. Hold the jar at eye level and look across the lid. It should be
   concave, curved down slightly in the centre. A flat or bulging lid may
   not be sealed.

A good seal shows that the lid held. It does not show that the food was
processed enough; only the tested process does that ([Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).

### A jar that did not seal

This means a jar that failed to seal on canning day, which happens to
everyone. It is not spoiled food. It is food that has not yet been
canned, and the USDA gives three choices for it:

- **Process it again within 24 hours.** Take off the lid, check the
  jar's sealing surface for tiny nicks, change the jar if it has one, put
  on a new, properly prepared lid, and process it again for the same
  time.
- **Refrigerate it**, and eat it within several days.
- **Freeze it** instead, after adjusting the headspace to 1-1/2 inches.

A lid that comes unsealed later, in storage, is a different matter. The
USDA guide explains that spoilage bacteria and yeast make gas that swells
lids and breaks seals, so a jar found unsealed on the shelf is treated as
spoiled: it is not tasted and it does not go back in the canner. It goes
to the disposal procedure ([Telling Spoiled From
Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe), [Keeping What You
Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew)).

### Storing the jars

The USDA's directions: take the screw bands off the sealed jars, wash the
lid and jar to remove any food, rinse and dry them, label and date them,
and store them in a clean, cool, dark, dry place. The best temperature is
between 50 and 70 F. Do not store them above 95 F, or near hot pipes, a
range or a furnace, under a sink, in an uninsulated attic, or in direct
sunlight; damp can corrode the lids and break the seals. Can no more than
you will use within a year (USDA).

Storing jars without their bands also helps you. The USDA guide notes
that some spoilage is easier to see in jars stored without screw bands
(our reading of why: a band can hold down a lid that has lifted).

### Before you eat from a jar

The USDA's check, as each jar is taken for use: the lid tight and
concave; no streaks of dried food running down the outside from the top;
no rising bubbles or unnatural colour in the food; and, while you open
it, no unnatural smell, no spurting liquid and no cotton-like mould on
the food or under the lid. The CDC's list of the signs of contamination
is shorter:

- the container is leaking, bulging or swollen;
- the container looks damaged, cracked or abnormal;
- the container spurts liquid or foam when opened;
- the food is discoloured, mouldy or smells bad.

**Throw it out, without tasting it,** when:

- the jar shows any of those signs (CDC, USDA);
- its lid came unsealed in storage (USDA);
- you cannot vouch for how it was canned. The CDC: "Do not eat food if
  you do not know whether safe canning guidelines were followed." That
  covers jars made by an untested recipe, and jars somebody else canned
  by a process you do not know (our reading);
- it is a low-acid food that went through a water bath. That food is
  thrown out, not boiled ([Why Canning Has
  Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).

Never taste a jar to check it. The CDC's rule: "If in doubt, throw it out!
Never taste food to see if it's safe." Follow the disposal procedure in
[Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew), wearing gloves,
whichever way the jar went wrong.

**Tomatoes get one more step.** The CDC advises boiling home-canned
tomatoes, and foods that contain them, in a saucepan before eating: 10
minutes below 1,000 feet, with 1 more minute for each additional 1,000
feet. That boil is a habit for jars that pass every check, not a way to
rescue a doubtful one ([Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).
And once any jar is open, it goes in the refrigerator (CDC).

## Jams, jellies and pickles

These are the foods most people start with, and they have a few rules of
their own (USDA, Guide 1).

- **Sweet spreads.** The USDA's general direction for jams, jellies and
  other sweet spreads is to fill them hot into sterile Mason jars with
  1/4-inch headspace, seal them with self-sealing lids and process them
  for 5 minutes in a boiling-water canner, adding 1 minute for each 1,000
  feet above sea level; if the jars were not sterilised, process them for
  10 minutes. Where a recipe gives its own time, use the recipe's (our
  reading).
- **No paraffin.** The USDA guide no longer recommends paraffin or wax
  seals for any sweet spread, because of the mould that can grow under
  them. A jar of jam with mould in it is thrown out, not scraped
  ([Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) gives the
  reason).
- **Sugar and batch size.** Keep the sugar in traditional recipes and
  make one batch at a time (above).
- **Pickles.** Use canning or pickling salt, white distilled or cider
  vinegar of 5 percent acidity, and a tested recipe, unaltered. Cut a
  1/16-inch slice off the blossom end of fresh cucumbers, which the USDA
  guide says may hold an enzyme that softens pickles. Fermented pickles
  and sauerkraut are in [Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables).

## Worked example: applesauce in Silverdale

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on. The recipe is the USDA's applesauce (Guide 2) and the canner
procedure is the USDA's (Guide 1).

It is an October weekend. A neighbour has given you a box of apples,
about 14 pounds: the USDA puts a canner load of 9 pints at an average of
13-1/2 pounds of apples, and 7 quarts at 21 pounds. You have a
boiling-water canner, nine pint jars with sound rims, new lids, and your
altitude: Silverdale is below 1,000 feet.

1. **Look up the time first.** Applesauce is a hot pack. In the 0 to
   1,000 feet column, pints take 15 minutes and quarts 20. (Higher up,
   pints and quarts take 20 and 25 minutes from 1,001 to 3,000 feet, 20
   and 30 from 3,001 to 6,000 feet, and 25 and 35 above 6,000 feet.)
   Because 15 minutes is more than 10, the jars need washing and keeping
   hot, not sterilising.
2. **Jars and canner.** Wash the jars and keep them hot in simmering
   water in a stockpot. Prepare the lids as their box says. Fill the
   canner halfway with water and heat it to 180 F, the temperature for a
   hot pack.
3. **The apples.** Wash, peel and core them. To stop them browning, the
   recipe lets you slice them into an ascorbic acid solution; the USDA's
   is 1 level teaspoon of pure ascorbic acid powder, or six crushed
   500-milligram vitamin C tablets, in a gallon of cold water. Drain the
   slices into an 8 to 10 quart pot, add 1/2 cup of water, and heat them
   quickly until tender, stirring now and then so they do not burn: 5 to
   20 minutes, depending on the apples.
4. **The sauce.** Press them through a sieve or food mill, or leave them
   chunky. Sugar is optional; the recipe suggests 1/8 cup per quart of
   sauce if you want it. Bring the sauce back to a rolling boil.
5. **Fill.** Take one hot jar at a time, fill it with hot sauce leaving
   1/2-inch headspace, free the air bubbles with the plastic spatula,
   check the headspace again, wipe the rim, and put on the lid and band.
   Lower it onto the canner rack with the jar lifter.
6. **Process.** With all nine jars in, add boiling water to at least an
   inch over the lids, put the canner lid on and turn the heat to high.
   When the water is boiling hard, set the timer for 15 minutes. It keeps
   boiling the whole time; had it stopped, you would bring it back to a
   boil and start the 15 minutes again.
7. **Out.** Heat off, lid off, wait 5 minutes, then lift the jars out
   upright onto a towel, an inch apart, and leave them overnight.
8. **Next morning.** You test each lid. Eight are concave and ring when
   tapped. The ninth springs back when pressed, so it goes in the
   refrigerator to be eaten this week. (It could instead have gone back
   into the canner within 24 hours with a new lid, for another 15
   minutes.)
9. **Store.** Bands off, jars washed, labelled "Applesauce" with the
   month and year, into a cool, dark cupboard away from the stove and the
   furnace, to be eaten within a year.

Notice who made the decisions. The time, the jar size, the pack and the
headspace were the recipe's. The only choices that were yours were the
sugar and whether to sieve.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Low-acid food is not water-bath work at all.** It goes to [Pressure
  Canning](/library#pressure-canning), or into the freezer or the dehydrator.
- **A recipe you invented, adapted or inherited** is not something to can
  until it matches a tested recipe exactly. If you want to preserve
  something no tested recipe covers, ask your extension office first
  (general practice).
- **A steam canner or an electric canner** is used only by tested
  instructions and your extension service's current advice (our reading
  of the extension material above).
- **Selling preserved food** is regulated. Ask your state's agriculture
  or health department before you sell any (general practice).

## Traps

- **"My grandmother water-bathed her beans."** Beans are a low-acid food.
  Nobody getting ill was luck, not a method ([Why Canning Has
  Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).
- **"Tomatoes are acid."** Some are not. Add the acid to every jar.
- **"It sealed, so it's safe."** The seal shows the lid held, not that
  the food was processed enough.
- **"I'll make a double batch of jam."** One batch at a time.
- **"Fifteen minutes is fifteen minutes."** Only in the right altitude
  column, and only counted from a full boil.
- **"It only stopped boiling for a minute."** Start the timer again.
- **"I'll top it up."** Never open a processed jar to add liquid.
- **"It smells fine."** The signs of spoilage catch some bad jars, not
  all of them.
- **Paraffin on the jam, or jars processed in the oven or dishwasher.**
  Neither is a canning method.

## How the game models it

The game does not model water-bath canning.

- **There is no canner.** No item in the game is a boiling-water canner,
  a canning jar, a canning lid or a jar lifter (`data/items.csv`), and no
  recipe has a headspace, a pack, an added acid or a processing time that
  changes with altitude (`data/recipes.csv`).
- **No recipe cans an acid food.** Apples and tomatoes are in the game
  (`data/items.csv`), but the recipes that use apples make dried fruit,
  juice and pie, the ones that use tomatoes make pizza and salad, and
  none makes applesauce, jam, jelly, pickles, or canned fruit or tomatoes
  (`data/recipes.csv`).
- **Its canning is the low-acid kind.** The recipe called Can Food turns
  potatoes and carrots into Canned Food at the home's Stove. In real life
  those are low-acid vegetables for a pressure canner, and [Pressure
  Canning](/library#pressure-canning) describes what the game does with them.
  The food data also has a general canning row, making food keep a
  hundred times as long, that draws no line between acid and low-acid
  food; no code acts on it yet (`data/food_system.ron`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the
difference between acid and low-acid food, every step in this guide, and
the fact that the food in a jar can look perfect and still be unsafe.

## You own this when

- You can say which foods may go in a boiling-water canner, which need
  acid added to every jar, and which never go in at all.
- You can say why a water bath is enough for an acid food and never
  enough for a low-acid one.
- You use only tested recipes, from the USDA guide or a source that
  follows it, and you change nothing that affects their safety.
- You know your altitude and read the right column every time.
- You can run the canner: preheat, load, cover the jars by an inch or two,
  time from a full boil, restart the timer if the boil stops, wait 5
  minutes, cool the jars for 12 to 24 hours.
- You can test a seal three ways, and you know the three choices for a
  jar that did not seal.
- You store jars without their bands, labelled, somewhere cool and dark,
  and use them within a year.
- You will not taste a doubtful jar, and you know where the disposal
  procedure is.
