# Making an Agreement That Holds

A homestead runs on agreements. You lend a neighbour your trailer for
the weekend. Two houses share one well. A friend keeps bees on the far
end of your field in exchange for honey. Three families split the cost
of a log splitter. A teenager mows the orchard every Saturday for pay.
A household agrees who cooks, who cleans and who feeds the animals.

Most of these never go wrong, and the ones that do rarely go wrong
because somebody was dishonest. They go wrong because the two people
remembered different things, because something happened that nobody
had thought about (the trailer came back with a flat tyre, the pump
failed in August, one family moved away), or because a word like
"soon" or "a fair share" meant one thing to one person and another to
the other.

An agreement that holds is one written clearly enough that when you
do disagree later, you can settle it by reading what you agreed,
instead of by arguing about what each of you remembers. That is the
whole skill. This guide is about how to get there, and about the few
agreements that can cost you far more than a trailer if you make them
casually.

The ideas about writing terms that cannot be misread come from NASA's
guidance on how to write a requirement, and from the federal
government's plain language guidance, which is published by the
General Services Administration on Digital.gov. The warnings come from
the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the federal rule on co-signing a
loan and the law that sets which lenders that rule reaches. The rest comes from USAGov, the US Department of Agriculture
(USDA) and the Training Within Industry programme that the US
government ran during the Second World War. All are United States
government publications, in the public domain. Where something is
general practice or our own reasoning, the text says so.

**This guide is not legal advice.** Contract law in the United States
is mostly state law, and it differs again in other countries (general
knowledge). What follows are habits that make agreements clearer and
safer anywhere. Whether a court would enforce a particular agreement,
and in what form it has to be written, is a question for a lawyer
where you live.

It sits beside [Trading Fairly](/library#trading-fairly), which covers swaps
of goods and work; this guide is about every other kind of promise.
[Keeping Records](/library#keeping-records) is how you keep what you agreed,
and the Humanity Accord's approach to disputes is in [Conflict
Resolution](/library#conflict-resolution).

## First, the agreements that can cost you your home

Most agreements between neighbours risk a few hours or a few dollars.
A handful can risk your savings, your credit or your land, and the
person asking may well be someone you love, in a hurry. Learn these
before anything else.

### Co-signing a loan

When a friend or family member cannot get a loan on their own, the
lender may ask for a co-signer. The FTC puts what that means plainly:
"When you cosign a loan, you agree to be responsible for someone else's
debt." If the borrower misses payments or stops paying, you must pay.

The federal Credit Practices Rule (16 CFR 444.3) requires lenders and
retail installment sellers "within the jurisdiction of the Federal
Trade Commission" (16 CFR 444.1) to give a co-signer a separate Notice
to Cosigner before the co-signer is bound, and it fixes the wording.
The Federal Trade Commission Act leaves banks, savings and loan
institutions and federal credit unions outside that jurisdiction (15
U.S.C. 45(a)(2)), so a loan from one of them is not covered by this
rule. **Do not count on receiving the notice**: weigh the risk the
same way whether it arrives or not.

Where it is given, the notice begins: "You are being asked to
guarantee this debt. Think carefully before you do. If the borrower
doesn't pay the debt, you will have to. Be sure you can afford to pay
if you have to, and that you want to accept this responsibility." It
goes on to say that you may have to pay up to the full amount of the
debt, plus late fees or collection costs, that "The creditor can
collect this debt from you without first trying to collect from the
borrower", that the creditor can use the same collection methods
against you, such as suing you or garnishing your wages, and that a
default may become part of your credit record.

The FTC's page on co-signing adds what people often do not expect:

- **You get no ownership.** Co-signing does not give you any title,
  ownership or other rights to whatever the loan buys. Your only role
  is to repay if the borrower does not.
- **You can lose what you pledge.** If you offer your own car,
  furniture or other property to secure the loan and the borrower
  defaults, you could lose it.
- **It counts against you even if they pay.** Lenders will treat the
  loan as your obligation, which may stop you getting credit yourself.
- **Getting out is hard.** The lender and the borrower must both agree
  to release you, and the FTC says the lender is not likely to.
- **No notice for some mortgages.** You may not get a Notice to
  Cosigner for some mortgage loans, because federal law does not
  require it for real estate purchases. The risk is the same.
- **State law can add protections.** In some states, the FTC says, a
  creditor must try to collect from the borrower before the co-signer,
  and may strike that sentence from the notice. Your state banking
  agency or attorney general can tell you what applies.

The rule of thumb that follows (our reading of the notice): **co-sign
only for an amount you could afford to pay in full yourself, and that
you would be willing to pay.** If you do co-sign, the FTC suggests
asking the lender for the total you might owe, adding: "The lender
doesn't have to do this, but might, if you ask." It also suggests
asking the lender to send you the monthly statements, or to agree in
writing to tell you of a missed payment; getting copies of the loan
papers ("The lender doesn't have to give you these papers, so you
might have to get copies from the borrower."); and checking your
credit reports.

### Your land, your deed and your water

The FTC's advice on home improvement scams contains a sentence worth
learning by heart: "Never transfer your deed to anyone without
consulting an attorney, a knowledgeable family member, or someone else
you trust."

The same caution applies to every agreement about land: a shared well,
a shared driveway, a right to cross someone's field, a boundary fence,
water drawn from a spring on a neighbour's side. An agreement about
land should be written by a lawyer and recorded with the county,
because a handshake between two owners may mean nothing to whoever
buys either property next (general practice). For a sense of what a
careful version contains, USDA's training for lenders in its
guaranteed home loan programme lists its rules for a house on a shared
well: shared wells are limited to four households, a recorded
agreement or easement is required to allow for maintenance and access,
and each home needs its own shut-off valve. That is one federal loan
programme's rule, not a law for everyone, but it shows what a lender
thinks a shared well needs before it will lend against the house.

### Signing what you have not read

From the same FTC page: "Don't sign a document you haven't read, is in
a language you don't understand, or that has blank spaces." And:
"Don't let anyone pressure you into signing any document." A blank
space can be filled in after you sign. Fill every blank or strike it
through, and initial each page (general practice).

Treat pressure as a warning sign in every kind of agreement: a fair
agreement will still be fair tomorrow, after you have read it twice
and slept on it (general practice, beside the FTC's rule).

### Lending what you cannot afford to lose

Lend money, tools or animals only if you could cope with never getting
them back, and write the terms down anyway (general practice). In our
view a written loan between friends protects the friendship more than
the money: it settles in advance the conversation neither of you wants
to have later.

### Agreeing to work that is not safe or not legal

An agreement does not make unsafe work safe. Wiring, gas and plumbing
connected to the water supply or the sewer may need a licensed
tradesperson and a permit where you live, whatever two neighbours
agree between themselves; [Trading Fairly](/library#trading-fairly) covers
this. If one side of the agreement is a job that can hurt someone, the
agreement should say who is trained to do it and what safety gear they
will use (general practice).

### When to get help, and where

If the agreement involves land, water rights, a business you will run
together, a large sum, or anything you would lose your home over, pay
for an hour with a lawyer before you sign (general practice). If
money is the obstacle, USAGov lists programmes that give free legal
advice and may help you find a free or low-cost lawyer, including the
Legal Services Corporation, through which you can find legal aid in
your community for people with low incomes. Some of these programmes,
it notes, limit their services to people with low incomes.

## What an agreement needs

Every agreement that lasts longer than a handshake should answer the
same questions. Most arguments later turn out to be about a question
nobody answered at the start (our observation, not a published
finding).

1. **Who.** The full names of everyone bound by it, and how to reach
   each of them. The FTC's checklist for a contract starts the same
   way: name, address and phone number.
2. **What, exactly.** In units, not in words like "some" or "a load"
   (see [Trading Fairly](/library#trading-fairly) on units). "Mow the
   orchard" becomes "mow the orchard below the barn, about half an
   acre, to about 3 inches".
3. **How you will both know it is done.** A test you could both apply.
   This is the heart of it, and the next section explains why.
4. **When.** Start date, finish date, how often. The FTC lists an
   estimated start and completion date among the things a contract
   should include.
5. **What each side gives.** Money, goods, work, the use of something,
   and when it changes hands.
6. **Who pays for what.** Fuel, parts, repairs, damage, breakage,
   testing, insurance.
7. **What if.** What happens if it is late, if it breaks, if someone is
   ill, if someone moves away, sells the land or dies.
8. **How it can change.** Usually: only in writing, signed by
   everyone.
9. **How a disagreement will be settled.** Who you will both ask if
   you cannot agree, before anyone thinks about a court.
10. **How it ends.** A date, or how much notice either side must give.

Items 5 to 10 are general practice. One more comes straight from the
FTC: put in writing "any promises made during conversations or calls".
A promise that was said but not written is the one most easily
forgotten, or remembered differently (our reasoning).

## Words that can be checked

Engineers face the same problem when they write down what a machine
must do, and NASA's systems engineering handbook has a checklist for
writing such a requirement so that it is clear, unambiguous and can be
checked. In our reading, the checklist works just as well for a promise
between neighbours.

**Can it be checked?** NASA asks whether a requirement can be "tested,
demonstrated, inspected, or analyzed" to show it was met. Ask the same
of each term: if we disagree later about whether this was done, what
would we look at? "Return the trailer in good condition" cannot be
checked; "return the trailer with both tyres holding air, the lights
working and the bed swept, as it was when we looked at it together"
can.

**Leave out the escape words.** NASA lists terms that make a
requirement ambiguous, among them "as appropriate," "etc.," "and/or,"
"but not limited to". It also lists words that cannot be verified,
including flexible, easy, sufficient, safe, adequate, when required, if
required, appropriate, fast, small, large, quickly and easily, and adds
"other 'ly' words". Around a homestead the same family of words
appears as "reasonable", "as needed", "in good shape", "soon", "a fair
share" and "help out". Each of them is a disagreement waiting for a
date.

| Instead of | Write |
| --- | --- |
| "Help with the hay when needed" | "Two people for one day of baling, on a dry day in July that we agree at least two days ahead" |
| "Split the well costs fairly" | "Split the pump's electricity, the annual water test and every repair 50/50" |
| "Keep the bees away from the house" | "Hives at least as far from the house as the old fence line, which is about 150 feet" |
| "Pay me back soon" | "$50 on the first of each month from May, until $600 is repaid" |

The figures in that table are our examples; the method is NASA's.

**Say who does what, in the active voice.** NASA's rule for a
requirement on people is that it takes the form "responsible party
shall perform such and such": it names who acts and what they do. The
federal plain language guidance says the same in everyday terms: "Use
the active voice." "The fence will be repaired" hides who repairs it.
"Sam repairs the fence by 1 June" does not.

**Use one word for a promise, every time.** NASA reserves "shall" for a
requirement, "will" for facts or a declaration of purpose, and
"should" for a goal. The plain language guidance recommends the short
word "must" in place of longer phrases like "be responsible for". The
principle behind both is that a promise and a hope are different, and a
reader must be able to tell which is which. Pick one word for a promise
("must" is the plainest) and keep "should" for things that are nice
but not promised. That combination is our reading of both sources.

**One promise per sentence.** NASA asks whether each requirement
expresses "only one thought". A sentence that carries three promises
makes it hard to say which one was broken.

**State it positively.** NASA's checklist prefers a requirement stated
positively over one stated as "shall not", and the plain language
guidance warns against double negatives and against exceptions to
exceptions. Its example: "You have to arrive by 5:00 if you want me to
pick you up" is clearer than the same rule written with two negatives.

**Use the same word for the same thing.** NASA checks that
terminology is used consistently. If the first paragraph says "the
pump house" and a later one says "the well shed", someone will one day
argue that they are two buildings.

**Give an example.** The plain language guidance: "Good examples can
substitute for long explanations." If "clean" matters, describe what
clean looks like, or agree to take a photo of it.

**Write down why, and what you assumed.** NASA wants each requirement
to come with a rationale, "including any assumptions". "We split the
water test because both houses drink from the well" tells a future
reader what the term was for. "We assume the well gives enough for both
houses in August" names the thing that, if it turns out false, means
the agreement needs revisiting.

**Do not leave a blank as "to be decided".** NASA advises minimising
"To Be Determined" values and, instead, writing a best estimate marked
"To Be Resolved" with who is responsible for settling it and by when.
"Fuel costs: about $20 a month, to be settled by Alice from the first
three receipts, by 1 August" keeps the gap honest and gives it an
owner.

**Decide what is worth writing down.** NASA's test for whether a
requirement is needed is to ask "What is the worst that could happen
if the requirement was not included?" And for a number: "What is the
worst thing that could happen if the tolerance was doubled or
tripled?" If the answer is "nothing much", leave it out or leave it
loose. An agreement that tries to cover everything is too long to
read, and an agreement nobody reads does not hold either (our
reasoning).

## Write it down, sign it, keep copies

The FTC's advice for contracts with a builder applies to every
agreement worth making: "Even if your state doesn't require a written
agreement, ask for one." Between neighbours that can be one page,
handwritten, dated, with both names at the bottom and a copy each
(general practice). Writing it down is not a sign of distrust. It is
how two people who trust each other find out, before they start, that
they had understood different things.

A few practical points, all general practice:

- **Date it, and sign it.** Everyone bound by it signs. Each keeps a
  copy, and one copy goes in your records ([Keeping
  Records](/library#keeping-records)).
- **Number the terms.** Then a later conversation can be about "term
  6" rather than about "the bit about the pump".
- **Read it aloud together before signing,** and have each person say
  in their own words what they have promised. Two people who can each
  explain the other's side have the same agreement.
- **Walk through what could go wrong.** Pick three things (it breaks,
  it is late, somebody moves) and ask what the page says happens. If
  it says nothing, add a line.
- **A witness or a notary proves who signed, not that the deal is
  fair.** A notary's stamp says the signer is who they claim to be; it
  says nothing about the terms.
- **Whether a court would enforce it depends on where you live.** Some
  kinds of agreement, especially about land, may have to be in writing
  and signed to be enforced at all. A lawyer can tell you.

## Agreements that last: review and change

Agreements about shared things run for years, and years change them:
prices rise, a pump is replaced, children grow up, a house is sold.
Put a review date in the agreement, once a year for anything ongoing,
and use it to ask whether each term still describes what you actually
do (general practice). When something changes, write the change down
and sign it the same way as the original, rather than letting the
paper and the practice drift apart.

The Training Within Industry programme, which taught supervisors in
American factories during the Second World War how to work with
people, put one of its rules this way: "Tell people in advance about
changes that will affect them." It added: "Tell them WHY if possible."
The rule was written for a foreman and his crew; in our reading it fits
a shared well just as well.

## Agreements in a group

When more than two people are bound by an agreement (a tool shared by
five families, a community garden, a household), it needs three more
things, all general practice:

- **How decisions will be made from now on.** By everyone, by a
  majority, or by one person for some things. Decide before the first
  disagreement, not during it.
- **Who keeps the master copy,** and how a change is recorded.
- **How someone joins and how someone leaves,** including what happens
  to what they put in.

The Humanity Accord sets out the rights and responsibilities every
person keeps in any group in [Rights and
Responsibilities](/library#rights-and-responsibilities), and
the ways a group can make decisions in [Governance
Models](/library#governance-models).

## When it goes wrong

Even good agreements meet events nobody imagined. The FTC's sequence
for a dispute with a contractor is a sensible order for any agreement:

1. **Try to settle it with the other person first.** The FTC notes:
   "Many disputes can be resolved at this level."
2. **Put it in writing.** The FTC suggests following a phone
   conversation with a letter sent by certified mail with a return
   receipt, so you can prove it arrived, and says: "Keep notes and
   copies of letters and documents for your files."
3. **If that fails, get outside help.** For a dispute with a business,
   the FTC lists your state attorney general or local consumer
   protection office and dispute resolution programmes. Between
   neighbours, a trusted third person you both respect can do the same
   job, and many areas have a community mediation service (general
   practice). For small sums of money there are small claims courts,
   with dollar limits and rules that vary by state (general practice).

The Training Within Industry programme taught a four-step way to
handle a problem between people that still reads well:

- **Get the facts.** Review the record, find out what rules apply,
  talk with the people concerned, get their opinions and feelings, and
  be sure you have the whole story.
- **Weigh and decide.** Fit the facts together, consider the possible
  actions, and "Don't jump at conclusions."
- **Take action.** Decide whether you can handle it yourself or need
  help, and watch the timing.
- **Check results.** Decide when you will follow up, and whether what
  you did helped.

The first step is where a written agreement pays for itself. "Review
the record" is easy when the record is a signed page; without one, a
dispute is two memories against each other.

## Worked examples

The names, figures and terms in these examples are ours, for
illustration.

### Lending a trailer for a weekend

Before: walk round the trailer together and note anything wrong with
it (a cracked light lens, a soft tyre). Then, on one page:

1. Pat lends Sam the 6 by 10 foot utility trailer from Friday 6 pm to
   Sunday 6 pm.
2. Sam uses it to move firewood only, never loads it beyond what the
   trailer is rated to carry, and tows it only with a vehicle and hitch
   rated for the loaded weight.
3. Sam returns it with the bed swept and both tyres holding air.
4. Sam pays for any damage beyond the cracked left light lens already
   noted.
5. If Sam is late, Sam phones Pat before 6 pm Sunday.

Signed by both, dated, one copy each. Five lines, ten minutes, and
the most likely arguments (the tyre, the light, the load, the time)
are already settled. The load limits in term 2 are general practice:
the trailer, the hitch and the towing vehicle each have a rated
capacity, and the lowest of them is the limit.

### Two houses, one well

This one is about land, so the right version is written with a lawyer
and recorded with the county (see above). What the two households
should agree before they see the lawyer:

- **Who owns the well and who has the right to reach it** for repairs,
  and along what route.
- **How costs split:** the pump's electricity, routine maintenance,
  water testing (see [Testing Water](/library#testing-water)), repairs, and
  eventually a new pump or a new well.
- **How the water is shared** in a dry spell, and who decides.
- **Shut-off valves** so either house can be isolated (one of USDA's
  rules for a shared well, above).
- **What happens when either house is sold:** the agreement should
  bind the next owner, which is the reason for recording it.
- **How a disagreement is settled,** and a yearly review date.

### A loan to a friend

Your friend needs $600 for a car repair. Before you agree, ask
yourself whether you could manage if it were never repaid. If not,
lend less, or help another way (general practice). Then write: the
amount; that it is a loan, not a gift; the repayments ("$50 on the
first of each month from 1 May"); what happens if a payment will be
late ("tell me before the first"); both signatures; the date.

Compare that with co-signing a $6,000 car loan for the same friend.
Your own $600 loan risks $600. The co-signed loan, by the federal
notice's own words, means "If the borrower doesn't pay the debt, you
will have to", plus fees, and unless your state requires otherwise the
lender may come to you first. They are not the same size of favour.

### A household's chores

The same method works inside a house. "Everyone helps keep the kitchen
clean" fails for the same reason "help with the hay" fails. "After
dinner: whoever cooked does not wash up; the other two wash, dry and
wipe the counters; the rota is on the fridge; we look at it again in a
month" can be checked by anyone who walks into the kitchen.

## How HumanityOS and the game model it

**In the game, trades with other players are a small, strict
agreement.** When you are connected to a server, the Trade page lets
you send another player a trade request, with an optional short
message. Once they accept, each of you lists items from your own
backpack. Any change to either side's offer clears both confirmations,
so nobody can agree to one offer and receive another; you can confirm
only while you are carrying what you offered; and the trade completes
only when both sides have confirmed the same offer. That is this
guide's rule in miniature: both sides agree to exactly the same terms,
and a change to the terms means agreeing again. [Trading
Fairly](/library#trading-fairly) covers the rest of how trading works.

**Outside the game, HumanityOS's Governance page is how a group writes
down a decision.** On a server you are connected to, a proposal has a
title and a full text, a kind (a local rule, a change to one of the
server's settings, or an amendment to the Humanity Accord), and a
voting window you set between 1 and 30 days. Votes are signed with
your identity, and the page says beside the voting buttons that votes
are final and cannot be changed. Each kind of proposal carries its own
bar for how many must vote and how large a majority it needs, kept as
data in
`data/governance/proposal_types.ron`: a local rule, for example, needs
at least 10 percent of the server's members to vote and 66 percent of
the weighted yes and no votes in favour, while an Accord amendment
needs 25 percent turnout and 75 percent in favour. An abstention
counts towards the turnout but not towards the majority. Deciding the
decision rule before the first disagreement is this guide's advice for
groups, and the page builds it in.

**One setting changes what you keep.** While Settings > Gameplay >
"Start every session from the default home" is on, which is the
default during development, only your character's name, look and
outfit carry between launches; goods you traded start over, and the
game remembers which trades it has already settled so that none is
handed over twice.

What the game does not model: there are no loans, no shared ownership,
no written contracts between players beyond the Trade page's offer,
and no way for an agreement to be broken and disputed. The Trading and
Leadership skills, which this topic belongs to, appear in the skill
list, but nothing in the game levels either of them yet.

## You own this when

- You never co-sign for more than you could afford to pay yourself,
  and you know that co-signing gives you the debt but not the thing,
  whether or not anyone hands you the federal notice.
- You never transfer a deed, or make an agreement about land or water,
  without a lawyer, and you record land agreements with the county.
- You never sign anything unread, blank, in a language you do not
  understand, or under pressure.
- You lend only what you could cope with losing, and you write it down
  anyway.
- Your agreements name who, what, how you will know it is done, when,
  what each side gives, who pays for what, what if, how it changes,
  how disputes are settled and how it ends.
- You can rewrite "soon", "fair", "as needed" and "in good shape" as
  something two people could check.
- You use one word for a promise, one promise per sentence, and the
  same name for the same thing throughout.
- You write down why each term is there, and what you assumed.
- Both of you can explain the agreement in your own words before you
  sign it.
- Shared agreements have a review date, and changes are written and
  signed.
- When something goes wrong, you talk first, put it in writing, get
  the facts before deciding, and know where to find a mediator, small
  claims court or free legal help.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our own reasoning, not sourced

- That most agreements go wrong through unanswered questions rather
  than dishonesty, and that most arguments are about a question nobody
  answered, are our observation; that contract law in the United States
  is mostly state law is general knowledge; that NASA's requirement
  checklist suits promises between neighbours is our reading.
- Co-signing only for what you could afford and would be willing to
  pay is our reading of the federal Notice to Cosigner; weighing the
  risk the same way whether or not the notice is given is our reasoning
  from the rule's limited reach.
- Having a lawyer write and record agreements about land, and that a
  handshake may not bind the next owner, are general practice.
- Filling or striking through blanks and initialling each page,
  lending only what you could cope with losing, and asking who is
  trained for any dangerous work in an agreement are general practice.
- Paying for an hour with a lawyer before a large or land-related
  agreement is general practice.
- Items 5 to 10 of the list of what an agreement needs are general
  practice.
- The homestead words that behave like NASA's unverifiable terms, and
  the examples in the table, are ours; recommending "must" for a
  promise combines NASA's and the plain language guidance's advice; the
  point that an agreement nobody reads does not hold is our reasoning.
- One page, dated and signed, with a copy each; numbering the terms;
  reading it aloud and explaining it back; walking through three things
  that could go wrong; that a notary proves identity, not fairness; and
  that some agreements about land may have to be in writing to be
  enforced are general practice.
- Review dates for ongoing agreements, and writing and signing changes,
  are general practice; applying the Training Within Industry rule on
  changes to agreements between neighbours is our extension.
- Deciding the decision rule, the master copy, joining and leaving in
  group agreements are general practice.
- A trusted third person, community mediation and small claims courts
  as outside help between neighbours are general practice.
- Towing only with a vehicle and hitch rated for the loaded weight,
  and the lowest rating being the limit, are general practice.
- The names, figures and terms in the worked examples are illustrations.
