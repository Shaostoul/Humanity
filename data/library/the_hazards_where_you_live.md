# The Hazards Your Place Actually Has

Most preparedness advice is written for nobody in particular, which
means it is written for nowhere in particular. It tells you to buy a
kit. It does not tell you that the kit a person needs on a floodplain
and the kit a person needs on a subduction zone are different kits, and
that the difference is not a detail.

That matters more than it sounds, because attention and money are
finite. Every hour and every dollar spent on the wrong hazard is an hour
and a dollar not spent on the one that will actually come. Generic
advice is not neutral. It has a cost, and the cost is paid on the day
something happens.

So this guide is not a list of things to buy. It is a method for
answering one question about your own address: what does this place
actually do, and how much notice does it give? Everything else follows
from that answer. The worked example at the end is Silverdale,
Washington, because that is the place this simulation is built on, and
because a real answer is more instructive than a general one. If you
live somewhere else, the method is the part that transfers.

One honest boundary before anything else. This guide cannot tell you
whether your house will stand up, whether your ground will liquefy, or
whether the slope behind you is stable. Those are site-specific
engineering questions, and the honest answer to all three is that
somebody has to look at your particular ground. Ready.gov says the same
thing about slopes: seek advice from a geotechnical expert for
evaluating landslide hazards. What this guide can do is tell you which
questions are worth paying someone to answer.

## Warning time is the organising idea

Hazards are usually sorted by how frightening they are. That is the
wrong axis. Sort them by how much notice they give, because notice
decides which skill is useful.

A hazard that gives you days of notice is a **plan**. You can watch a
forecast, fill containers, charge things, move the car, call the person
who lives alone up the road. Planning works because there is time to
plan in.

A hazard that gives you seconds is a **reflex**. There is no time to
decide anything, so whatever you do in those seconds is whatever you
already knew how to do without thinking. You cannot improve a reflex
during the event. You can only have built it beforehand.

A hazard that gives you minutes is the hardest case, because it looks
like there is time to think and there is not. Minutes are enough to walk
to high ground and not enough to gather your things first. The decision
has to be pre-made, and the pre-made decision is the whole preparation.

| Notice | What that makes it | What the preparation actually is |
|---|---|---|
| Seconds | A reflex | One trained response, practised until it needs no thought |
| Minutes | A pre-made decision | Knowing your route and your high ground before the day |
| Hours | A short plan | A checklist you can run without shopping |
| Days | A full plan | Supplies, fuel, medication, and other people |
| No notice, but slow onset | A siting decision | Answered before you buy, rent, or build |

Notice this cuts across the usual categories. Two earthquakes in the
same region can sit in different rows if one is offshore and one is
under your feet. A tsunami can be a five-minute hazard or a
three-hour hazard depending on which fault made it. The warning time,
not the name, is what tells you what to do.

## How to find your own hazards

This is the transferable part. Six questions, and a specific public body
that answers each one. Every one of these is free and most are federal,
which means you can check the answer rather than believe it.

**Will the ground here shake, how hard, and how often?**
The USGS National Seismic Hazard Model. It integrates what is known
about "earthquake sources, crustal deformation, active faulting, and
ground shaking" into maps of expected shaking, and it is the basis for
the seismic provisions in building codes. It is expressed as
probability: the 2023 maps show peak ground accelerations "having a 2
percent probability of being exceeded in 50 years, for a firm rock
site." You can query it for your own coordinates through the USGS
Earthquake Hazard Toolbox and the Unified Hazard Tool.

What it does not answer: whether *your* soil amplifies that shaking. The
model assumes firm rock. Soft fill, tidal flats and stream sediment
behave differently, and that is a state or county question.

**Is my address in a mapped flood hazard area?**
The FEMA Flood Map Service Center, which describes itself as "the
official public source for flood hazard information produced in support
of the National Flood Insurance Program." Search by address. It returns
your flood zone and, in the detailed zones, a base flood elevation.

The vocabulary is worth learning because it is precise. A Special Flood
Hazard Area is "the area that will be inundated by the flood event
having a 1-percent chance of being equaled or exceeded in any given
year." Shaded Zone X is the band out to the 0.2-percent-annual-chance
flood. Unshaded Zone X is beyond that. "100-year flood" is the same
thing as the 1-percent zone and is a much worse name for it, because it
sounds like a schedule and it is a yearly probability.

Ready.gov adds the caveat that matters: flooding does not follow lines
on a map. Being outside the zone is not the same as being safe, it is
the same as being unmapped.

**What is the county-level picture across all hazards at once?**
The FEMA National Risk Index, which "provides information for
communities most at risk to 18 different natural hazards" and "offers a
baseline risk measurement for expected annual loss, social vulnerability
and community resilience at the Census tract or county level." Useful as
a first sweep, because it will name hazards you had not thought of.
Coarse by design: a county is a large thing and your street is not the
county.

**What is the ground itself going to do?**
Two layers. Nationally, the USGS Landslide Hazards Program publishes a
high-resolution map of landslide susceptibility for the entire United
States and Puerto Rico, alongside the US Landslide Inventory.

Locally, the deeper answer is usually your **state geological survey**,
because the fine-grained mapping of landslides, liquefaction and tsunami
inundation is state work. In Washington that is the Washington Geologic
Information Portal, which carries landslide hazards, tsunami hazards and
evacuation routes, earthquake ground response including "liquefaction,
seismic site class, seismic design categories," and mapped faults. Every
state has an equivalent body; what it has mapped varies a lot.

**What has actually happened here, as opposed to what could?**
Two NOAA sources. The National Centers for Environmental Information
Storm Events Database is the logged record of severe weather events by
state and county zone, with the damage, injuries and fatalities
attached. The National Weather Service publishes local climate records
for individual stations: average high, average temperature, average low,
precipitation and snowfall, alongside record events.

This is the step people skip, and it is the one that most often
overturns an assumption. A thirty-year event log will tell you plainly
that the thing your town actually deals with is not the thing your town
talks about.

**Who will tell me on the day, and where am I supposed to go?**
Your local emergency management office, which holds the evacuation
routes, the shelter locations, and the plan the county is actually
operating. Ready.gov's framing of the household plan starts here: "Know
what disasters and hazards could affect your area, how to get emergency
alerts, and where you would go if you and your family need to evacuate."

And make sure an alert can physically reach you. Wireless Emergency
Alerts are short messages sent to any WEA-enabled mobile phone in a
targeted area, with a distinctive sound and vibration repeated twice;
there is no subscription and no charge. NOAA Weather Radio All Hazards
is "a nationwide network of radio stations broadcasting continuous
weather information directly from the nearest National Weather Service
office," carrying non-weather emergencies too, from more than 1,000
transmitters. A battery radio keeps working when the tower, the mains
and the internet do not.

One thing none of these can do: predict. USGS is blunt about it.
"Neither the USGS nor any other scientists have ever predicted a major
earthquake," and what they can produce instead is "the probability that
a significant earthquake will occur in a specific area within a certain
number of years." Everything above tells you what is likely over years.
Nothing tells you what is happening on Thursday.

## The worked example: Silverdale, Washington

Here is what those six questions return for one real address at the head
of Dyes Inlet on the Kitsap Peninsula. The full data, with the sourcing
for every figure, is in
[Where You Are: Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa).
What follows is that list resorted by warning time, because that is the
sort that changes behaviour.

### Seconds, or none at all

**A shallow crustal earthquake on the Seattle Fault zone, M6.5 to 7.5.**
The worst shaking case for this location, because the fault is shallow
and passes immediately east and south. USGS puts the chance of a
magnitude 6.5 or greater crustal fault earthquake in the Puget Sound
region at 17 percent in the next 50 years. The recurrence interval for
large events is thousands of years and the last one was the winter of
923 to 924 CE, which lifted parts of Restoration Point on Bainbridge
Island 23 feet. Warning time is effectively zero: Silverdale is in the
near field, so an early-warning alert may arrive with the shaking rather
than before it.

**A deep intraslab earthquake, M6.5 or greater.** 85 percent in 50
years. This is the one most likely to happen in an ordinary lifetime
here. Three have already: 1949 Olympia, 1965 Seattle, 2001 Nisqually,
16 years apart and then 36, with 24 years elapsed since. Because the
source is tens of kilometres down, shaking is less intense than an
equivalent shallow event and these rarely make tsunamis. Warning: seconds
at most.

**Cascadia megathrust shaking, M9.** 10 to 15 percent in 50 years, full
margin ruptures approximately every 500 years, the last in the winter of
1699 to 1700. Silverdale sits well inland of the offshore rupture, so
the local problem is not violent shaking but shaking that lasts for
minutes: liquefaction of the fill and delta sediment at the waterfront
and the Clear Creek mouth, landslides on the bluffs, and weeks without
lifelines on a peninsula reached by two bridges and ferries.

**Seiche and landslide-generated waves.** Sloshing in an enclosed basin,
or a bluff collapsing into the water and displacing it. Dyes Inlet is
small, enclosed and steep-sided, which is exactly the geometry for both.
No warning at all, and a nasty detail: the 1949 earthquake triggered a
large bluff landslide at the Tacoma Narrows three days later, which
generated its own local wave. The bluffs stay dangerous after the
shaking ends.

### Minutes

**A tsunami from a large Seattle Fault earthquake.** The Washington
Geological Survey models the inundation reaching the head of Dyes Inlet,
with a maximum amplitude of 5 feet at the Silverdale point and an
arrival time of 5 minutes. Along the same shoreline it is worse: less
than 3 minutes at Bremerton, Point White and the Bainbridge ferry
terminal. Nearby modelled amplitudes run higher than Silverdale's:
Bremerton 9 feet, Agate Passage 11, Point White 16. And the tide is
doing whatever it is doing on top of that, across a mean range of about
11.7 feet.

Five minutes is not enough time for anyone to warn you. The earthquake
is the warning. Washington DNR states plainly that for people in this
state, the single biggest warning of a potential tsunami is a large
earthquake, and Ready.gov's instruction is to evacuate on the natural
sign without waiting: "DO NOT wait! Leave as soon as you see any natural
signs of a tsunami or receive an official tsunami warning."

### Hours

**A tsunami from the Cascadia megathrust.** Much smaller inside Puget
Sound than the local case, because the wave has to refract through the
Strait of Juan de Fuca and Admiralty Inlet: the nearest modelled point,
Bremerton, gives 2.5 feet, and the modelled arrival is about 2 hours 40
minutes. This is the one tsunami case here where an official warning and
an organised evacuation are realistic. The dominant Cascadia problem
locally is the shaking and the multi-week outage, not the wave.

**Coastal and small-stream flooding.** Mapped and regulated. The
Silverdale waterfront is Zone AE with a base flood elevation of 14 feet,
the Chico Creek mouth AE at 13 feet, the Barker Creek mouth AE floodway,
while the built-up upland is Zone X, minimal hazard. There is no large
river here, so this is localised rather than catastrophic: 18 flood and
3 coastal flood events logged since 1996, the coastal ones driven by
high astronomical tide plus low pressure plus onshore wind, which is a
combination the Weather Service can see coming.

### Days

**Winter windstorms.** The most frequent damaging hazard here, and the
one with real preparation time. In the NOAA record for the Kitsap
Peninsula zones from 1996 to 2025, 24 of 30 years had at least one high
or strong wind event, 80 in total, peaking at 77 mph in December 2000.
On 15 November 2006 about 8,000 people in Kitsap County lost power,
roads were blocked by downed trees and lines, and the Hood Canal Bridge
closed for hours.

Read the next sentence twice, because it is the single most useful fact
in this document. The December 2006 storm caused eight indirect deaths
from carbon monoxide poisoning, from barbecues and generators used
indoors during the outage. The wind did not kill those people. The
response to the outage did.

**Heavy snow and ice.** 48 heavy snow events, 19 winter weather, 4
winter storm and 3 ice storm events logged since 1996, with heavy snow
in 18 of the 30 years. Wet lowland snow near freezing loads trees, brings
down limbs and lines, and isolates the steep bluff-top roads. Same
generator and carbon monoxide risk as the windstorms.

Worth being exact about the ranking, because it is a good lesson in
reading a record honestly: heavy snow events alone (48) outnumber high
wind events alone (45), but wind events of all kinds (80) outnumber
winter events of all kinds (74). Which hazard "wins" depends on how you
group the categories. The useful conclusion is not that one beats the
other, it is that both are near-annual and both produce the same
problem, which is a cold dark house.

**Wildfire.** Low by Washington standards and not zero, and the hazard
most likely to be underestimated here. No modern mapped fire perimeter
within about 15 km; the only perimeters intersecting that area are three
very large pre-settlement events in 1308, 1508 and 1701, roughly 200 and
193 years apart, with nothing mapped since. The westside Douglas-fir
regime is infrequent and stand-replacing. Fire weather gives days of
notice through Red Flag Warnings, but an actual fire run gives minutes
to hours, and the binding constraint here is not fire behaviour, it is
getting off a peninsula.

### The one with two different warning times at once

**Landslides on coastal bluffs.** This is the everyday geologic hazard,
and the one people forget while thinking about the megathrust. USGS
mapped 231 landslides in Kitsap County from lidar, and 67 percent of the
mapped landslide area lies within 500 metres of the coastline. Two sit
directly on the Dyes Inlet shoreline near Silverdale with possible
evidence of recent activity.

The mechanism is a perched water table: permeable sand over
low-permeability clay, so pore pressure builds through a sustained wet
spell and the bluff face lets go. In the winter of 1996 to 1997 coastal
landslides in Kitsap County destroyed millions of dollars of property
and killed a family of four on Bainbridge Island. The NOAA storm record
for these zones holds 10 debris-flow events since 1996, including a 2007
fatality.

The two warning times are the point. The **rain** that drives it is
forecast days ahead. The **failure** gives nothing. Ready.gov is direct
about the fast kind: "the most dangerous, life-threatening and deadliest
landslides are the ones that occur quickly, often with little notice,"
and "by the time you are sure a debris flow is coming, it will be too
late to get away safely." So the preparation is not a reaction at all.
It is knowing, before the winter, whether you live on or under a bluff.

## Why the everyday hazard usually matters more than the famous one

Put the numbers side by side and the ranking inverts.

A magnitude 9 on Cascadia is 10 to 15 percent in 50 years. A damaging
deep earthquake is 85 percent in the same 50 years, which makes it
between five and nine times as likely, depending on which end of the
Cascadia range you take. A damaging windstorm has shown
up in 24 of the last 30 years. Heavy snow in 18 of 30. A
landslide-producing winter recurs on a scale of years.

So in an ordinary lifetime at this address, the overwhelmingly probable
emergency is not the one with a documentary about it. It is a week in
February with no power, a road closed by a fallen tree, and a fridge
full of food going over.

Here is the part that makes this good news rather than a scolding, and
it is reasoning from the figures above rather than a claim anyone has
published. The preparation for the common case is most of the
preparation for the rare one. Stored water, a way to stay warm without
the mains, light, a battery radio, a month of medication and food that
does not need a freezer, these are the same items whether the cause is a
windstorm on Tuesday or the megathrust in fifty years. The megathrust
adds duration and it adds the shaking reflex. It does not replace the
list. Which means the honest advice is to prepare for the boring thing,
and accept that you have substantially prepared for the frightening one
as a side effect.

## What preparation looks like when the list drives it

In this order. The ordering is a rule of thumb, not a published
standard: it puts first the things that kill fastest and are hardest to
improvise.

**Water first.** The CDC says to store at least 1 gallon of water per
person per day for 3 days, covering "drinking, cooking, brushing teeth,
and other uses," and to try for a 2-week supply if possible. Ready.gov
gives the same gallon "for several days," and notes that in very hot
temperatures water needs can double, and that children, nursing mothers
and sick people may need more. Washington's own emergency management
division campaigns on "Be 2 Weeks Ready," two weeks rather than the
federal three days, which is a reasonable thing for a state that expects
lifelines to stay down.

Everything about containers, sanitising, labelling, rotation and what to
do about water you are unsure of is covered properly in two companion
guides, and there is no point repeating it here:
[Storing Water Safely](/library#storing-water-safely) and
[Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink).

**Then warmth, and this is where people die.** Not from cold. From what
they burn to fix the cold. CDC reports that more than 400 Americans die
each year from unintentional carbon monoxide poisoning not linked to
fires, and the symptoms read like ordinary winter illness: "headache,
dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain, and
confusion." The rules are absolute. Generators and fuel are used
outdoors only, at least 20 feet from windows, doors, vents and attached
garages. Never a generator inside a home or garage, even with the doors
and windows open. Never charcoal indoors, never a portable gas camp
stove indoors, never a gas oven as a heater. Install working carbon
monoxide alarms on every level of the home.

Eight people died that way in this region after one windstorm. That is
the highest-value paragraph in this document and it costs about thirty
dollars to act on.

The rest of warmth is unglamorous: layers, blankets, sleeping bags,
closing off one room. Know the signs of hypothermia (shivering,
exhaustion, confusion, fumbling hands, memory loss, slurred speech,
drowsiness) and warm the core first, which means chest, neck, head and
groin.

**Then light.** A flashlight for every household member, and spare
batteries. Not candles, in a house that may have just been shaken.

**Then information.** A battery or hand-crank radio, ideally a NOAA
Weather Radio with tone alert, because it works when the network does
not. A charged phone and a backup battery, because Wireless Emergency
Alerts need no subscription and reach the phone directly. Local paper
maps, for when the mapping app has nothing to talk to.

**Then medicine.** Prescriptions, a first aid kit, and a real plan for
anything that needs mains power or refrigeration; Ready.gov's advice is
to talk to your medical provider about that plan before you need it, not
during. On food in an outage, the numbers are specific: a refrigerator
holds temperature about four hours, a full freezer about 48, and food
held at 40 degrees Fahrenheit or above for two hours or more gets thrown
out.

**And the two-minute items that are not about disasters at all.** In any
given year you are far more likely to have a house fire than an
earthquake. The US Fire Administration's numbers: smoke alarms in every
bedroom, outside each separate sleeping area, and on every level
including the basement, interconnected so that when one sounds they all
sound; test monthly; batteries at least once a year; replace the whole
alarm 10 years from its manufacture date. Two ways out of every room, an
outside meeting place in front of the house, and a drill that everyone
in the household has actually walked. The reason is the number: you
could have less than two minutes to get out once the alarm sounds.

## Drop, cover and hold on, done correctly

For a seconds-of-warning hazard there is only the reflex. Here is the
whole of it, from Ready.gov.

**Drop** where you are, onto hands and knees. **Cover** your head and
neck with one arm and hand. **Hold** until the shaking stops. For the
cover step: "If a sturdy table or desk is nearby, crawl underneath it
for shelter. If no shelter is nearby, crawl next to an interior wall
(away from windows)." Stay on your knees and bend over to protect your
vital organs.

In bed: turn face down and cover your head and neck with a pillow. Do
not get up and cross a dark room full of broken glass.

In a vehicle: pull over and stop, and set the parking brake.

Outdoors: move to an open area away from buildings, trees, streetlights
and power lines.

Using a cane: drop, cover and hold on, or sit on a chair or bed and
cover your head and neck with both hands. Using a walker or wheelchair:
lock your wheels, then get as low as possible, bend over, cover your
head and neck, and hold on.

**The error that injures people is running outside.** Ready.gov states
it flatly: "If you are inside, stay and do not run outside; avoid
doorways." Two reasons. Getting out means crossing a floor that is
moving, on feet, past things that are falling, and then standing next to
the outside of a building, which is where the glass, the cladding and
the parapet land. And the doorway advice is a leftover from a different
kind of building; in an ordinary modern house a doorway is not stronger
than the rest of the structure and it will not protect your head.

If you get a ShakeAlert, it is a trigger for that same reflex, nothing
more. It gives "seconds before strong shaking arrives," and close to the
epicentre it gives little or none. It is not a prediction; the
earthquake has already started. USGS separates these carefully: early
warning is seconds to tens of seconds after an earthquake begins,
probabilities describe long-term chances over years, and prediction (a
date, a place and a magnitude) is not yet possible.

Then, and this is the part that belongs to shorelines: **when the
shaking stops and you are near the water, go up and inland immediately,
on foot, without waiting for anyone to tell you to.** Ready.gov's
generic instruction is to shelter at least 100 feet above sea level or
one mile inland, but the honest local instruction is simpler, because at
Silverdale the modelled wave is 5 feet and arrives in about 5 minutes.
Get off the low ground at the waterfront, Old Town and the Clear Creek
mouth, get uphill, and stay there until somebody with authority says the
sequence is over. You will feel foolish most times you do this. Do it
anyway.

## The honest limits

There are things a household cannot prepare for, and pretending
otherwise is its own kind of failure.

**You cannot store a bridge.** The Kitsap Peninsula is reached by two
bridges and ferries. A Cascadia event means multi-week loss of lifelines
across that whole peninsula. No quantity of tinned food fixes a road
network. That problem is solved, to the extent it is solved at all, at
the county and state level, and your part in it is to be one of the
households that does not need rescuing in the first week.

**You cannot prepare your way off bad ground.** If your house sits on
liquefiable fill, or on top of an actively creeping bluff, or below one,
no kit changes that. That is a siting question and it is answered before
you sign anything: look up the address on the state hazard portal and
FEMA's flood map, and pay a geotechnical engineer if the answer is
ambiguous. Many people do not get this choice at all. Renters, people
housed where housing exists, people who inherited the house. If that is
you, then the thing you can control is knowing exactly what your ground
does and exactly where you are going, which costs nothing.

**You cannot stockpile your way out of dependence on other people.**
Anyone on dialysis, oxygen, insulin or powered mobility equipment has a
hard limit measured in days, and that limit is a medical plan, not a
shopping list.

Which brings the answer round to where it actually lives. After the
frequent, boring, week-long version of every hazard on this list, what
determines how a street comes through it is not equipment. It is whether
anyone knows who lives alone, who has a generator and who knows how to
use it safely outdoors, who has the chainsaw, who cannot get down their
own steps, and which neighbour has the medical training. Washington's
emergency management division organises its preparedness guidance around
neighbourhoods for that reason, alongside families and pets. Ready.gov's
household plan asks who you will contact and where you will meet before
it asks what you will carry.

That knowledge is local, it is unwritten, and it cannot be bought in a
hurry. It is also, by a wide margin, the cheapest preparation on this
page.

## How you know it worked

- You can name the three hazards most likely to actually happen where
  you live, with a number attached to each, and say where the number
  came from.
- You have looked up your own address on a flood map and on your state's
  geologic hazard portal, and you know what they said.
- You can sort your hazards by warning time, and you know which of them
  give you seconds.
- Drop, cover and hold on is something your household has practised, not
  something you have read.
- If you are near a shoreline, every person in the house knows which way
  is uphill and goes there on the shaking, without discussion.
- There is stored water, a way to stay warm, light, a battery radio and
  a month of medication, and a carbon monoxide alarm on every level.
- You know the name of at least one neighbour who would notice if you
  did not come out for two days.

None of that makes you safe, and nothing on this page should be read as
a promise. What it does is move you from preparing for a hazard in
general to preparing for the ones your ground actually has, which is the
only kind of preparation that pays for itself.

### The locale data this guide's worked example rests on

Every Silverdale figure (probabilities, return periods, modelled tsunami
amplitudes and arrival times, storm event counts, mapped landslides,
flood zones and base flood elevations) comes from
`data/locales/silverdale_wa/hazards.json`, which records its own source
URL per hazard. Its provenance is USGS, FEMA, NOAA and the Washington
Geological Survey. The generated reading copy is
[Where You Are: Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa).

Two source notes for honesty. The Cascadia and Puget Sound probability
and recurrence figures trace to USGS Fact Sheet 2025-3050, whose PDF at
https://pubs.usgs.gov/fs/2025/3050/fs20253050.pdf returned HTTP 403 when
this guide was written, so those numbers are quoted through the locale
data file rather than from the fact sheet directly. The Seattle Fault
scenario and the tsunami model outputs are Washington Geological Survey
products, a state agency, restated here rather than copied.
