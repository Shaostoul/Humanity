# What Is a Good Human

## Purpose

This document describes what makes a human good.

It is not a test to pass or a rank to hold.
It is a description of a practice,
written so that anyone can take it up.

It exists because "good person" is a phrase used carelessly:
as praise, as branding, as a weapon.
A plain, shared definition makes the words useful again.

---

## Foundational Premise

Goodness is a practice, not a status.

It is not a birthright.
It is not a purity test.
It is not a possession that, once acquired, cannot be lost.

Goodness is what you repeatedly do
to the people and the world within your reach.

A single act, good or bad, is weather.
A pattern of acts is climate.
Goodness is climate.
(Some single acts are grave enough to become the climate on their own.
The metaphor is for judging a life over time, not for weighing a harm.)

Because it is a practice, it is open to anyone.
No origin, wealth, credential, or history is required to begin.
And nothing in it is exclusive to humans:
any mind that chooses its actions can take up the same practice.

---

## The Core Practices

These are the practices that, repeated, make a person good.
None of them requires talent.
All of them require repetition.

**Honesty, especially when it is costly.**
Truth told only when convenient is not honesty.
A good human tells the truth when a lie would be cheaper,
and corrects the record on discovering they were wrong.

**Care for the vulnerable.**
A society is measured by how it treats those with the least power
(see Ethical Principles, Principle VII).
A person is measured the same way.
Kindness toward those who can do nothing for you
is the least corruptible evidence of character.

**Stewardship of shared things.**
Water, land, tools, knowledge, trust:
whatever many people depend on,
a good human leaves in better condition than they found it,
or at least no worse, and never quietly drained.

**Courage, to act and to refuse.**
Goodness requires doing the necessary thing while afraid,
and declining the profitable thing while pressured.
Both halves matter.
Courage that only ever says yes is ambition.

**Humility, to be corrected.**
No person is infallible, and no generation is either.
A good human treats being corrected as gaining something,
not losing something,
and changes their conduct when shown better evidence.

**Reciprocity.**
A good human gives roughly as they receive, and often first.
They do not keep exact score,
but they do not build their comfort
on the unreturned labor of others.
Reciprocity is owed among equals.
It is never billed to the vulnerable.

**Keeping one's word.**
Promises are the smallest unit of trust.
A good human makes fewer promises than they could
and keeps more of them than expected.
When a promise cannot be kept,
they say so early, to the person owed.

## A Good Human Under Power

Power changes what a person can do.
It does not change what a person owes.

Power asymmetry increases responsibility, not entitlement
(see Ethical Principles, Principle II).

The test of goodness under power is short:
what do you do to people who cannot stop you
and cannot repay you?

A good human with power:
- uses it in the open, where it can be questioned
- spends it protecting those beneath it, not extracting from them
- builds others up until the power is no longer needed
- lets it go when the work is done

Anyone who treats power as a reward
has already begun to fail the test.

---

## A Good Human Under Hardship

Hardship explains.
It does not excuse.

Pressure narrows choices.
It never removes the choice of how to treat
the person standing in front of you.

Dignity extended to others is not conditional on comfort.
It is not suspended by exhaustion, poverty, grief, or fear.
Some of the clearest goodness on record
came from people who had almost nothing left
and still refused to pass their suffering downward.

This is not a demand for sainthood under load.
It is a reminder of what remains possible.

Understanding why someone caused harm is owed to them.
The pretense that the harm did not happen is owed to no one.

---

## Failure and Repair

Good humans fail.

They lose their temper, break promises,
miss what was obvious in hindsight,
and cause harm they did not intend.

What distinguishes a good human is not a clean record.
It is what happens after the failure:

- acknowledgment, plainly, to those harmed
- repair, where repair is possible
- change, so the same failure is not repeated

Denial compounds harm.
Repair without change is performance.

Two mistakes about failure must be rejected outright.

The first is the purity spiral:
the habit of demanding an ever cleaner record,
until any past error disqualifies a person forever.
Communities that punish every failure permanently
teach their members to hide failures,
and hidden failure is where the worst harm grows.

The second is the permanent moral caste:
the belief that some people simply are good
and others simply are bad,
fixed, regardless of what they do next.
There is no such caste.
There are only people practicing, people not practicing,
and people deciding, right now, which they will be.

---

## Goodness Is Not Obedience

Following orders is not a virtue.
Neither is following rules, customs, majorities, or leaders.

Obedience is only as good as what it serves.
Complying with an illegitimate order
makes you a participant in what that order does
(see Failure of Legitimacy).

"I was told to" transfers nothing.
Delegating your judgment does not delegate your accountability.

A good human weighs the order, not the uniform it arrives in.
They comply when compliance is right,
refuse when it is wrong,
and accept the cost of telling the difference out loud.

Much of history's organized cruelty
was carried out by people who were obeying
and believed that obedience made them good.
It did not.
It never does, by itself.

---

## The Measure

To know whether someone is practicing goodness,
do not ask what they believe, what they claim,
or how they appear.

Look at the people around them, over time.

A good human makes the people around them:

- **more capable**: skills shared, knowledge given away,
  tools handed over, work made learnable
- **more safe**: those in their care do not brace when they enter the room,
  and the vulnerable are safer near them, not less safe
- **more free**: fewer dependencies collected,
  more real choices left open,
  no one held by fear, debt, or engineered helplessness

The opposite pattern also identifies itself.
Whoever leaves the people around them
weaker, more afraid, and with fewer choices
is not good, whatever else they are.

---

## Closing Statement

No one is born good.
No one is finished being good.

Goodness is not a possession, a bloodline, or a badge.
It is a practice, kept up in ordinary days,
within ordinary reach.

Begin with whoever is in front of you.

A good human makes the people around them
more capable, more safe, and more free.
