# Salvage and Reuse

A pile of old fence boards, a stack of pallets behind a shop, a door
from a house being pulled down, a box of jars, a drum by the roadside:
to one person it is rubbish, to another it is stock. Salvage is the
skill of seeing the second thing, and the harder skill of knowing
which of it is safe to use, for what, and which should be left where it
is.

The Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) ranks ways of dealing with
waste from most to least preferred, and puts reducing and reusing at the
top, above recycling, and well above burning or burying. Reuse keeps the
work that went into a thing; recycling melts or shreds it back into raw
material. A salvaged board is a board. For a household on a small
budget, it is often the cheapest material there is.

Salvage has its own hazards, and they are not obvious. Old wood may
carry lead paint or arsenic-based preservative. A drum may have held
something that explodes when it is cut. A crib that looks sound may be
illegal to sell. This guide is about telling those apart.

It takes its rules and facts from the EPA, the Occupational Safety and
Health Administration (OSHA), the Consumer Product Safety Commission
(CPSC), Customs and Border Protection (CBP), the National Institute for
Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), the US Department of
Agriculture's canning guide and its Forest Products Laboratory. Those
are United States government publications, in the public domain. Where
something is general practice or our own reasoning rather than a
published rule, the text says so.

Other guides cover the materials themselves: [Working With
Wood](/library#working-with-wood), [Metals and
Alloys](/library#metals-and-alloys), [Stone, Clay and
Earth](/library#stone-clay-and-earth) and [Fibre, Cord and
Textiles](/library#fibre-cord-and-textiles). [What Not to Compost, Burn or
Pour Away](/library#what-not-to-compost-burn-or-pour-away) is the other half
of this one: what to do with the things you decide not to keep.

## First: the ways salvage hurts people

### Lead paint on old wood, doors, windows and furniture

The EPA says homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint, and that
the older the home, the more likely. Salvage from old buildings is
mostly painted wood: doors, windows, trim, siding, porch posts. The EPA
also lists refinishing old furniture among the jobs that can expose
people to lead.

Lead paint in good condition is usually not a hazard; the danger is the
dust and chips you make when you scrape, sand, cut or burn it. So, for
painted salvage of unknown age:

- **Assume it is lead paint.** The EPA's advice for a house whose age
  you cannot find out is to assume lead and work accordingly, and
  salvage of unknown age is the same question (our application). The
  EPA's test kit page explains why a home kit settles little: no kit has
  met both of its accuracy standards, and the recognised kits can show
  reliably only that lead paint is absent, and then only when a trained
  professional uses them.
- **Never burn painted wood, never torch it, and never dry-sand it.**
  The EPA's renovation rule forbids contractors to burn or torch painted
  surfaces, to power sand or grind them without a shroud and a HEPA
  vacuum, or to use a heat gun at 1,100 degrees Fahrenheit or more
  (40 CFR 745.85); the same practices are the ones to avoid with salvage
  at home (our reading).
- **Contain the work, mist it first, and clean up wet.** The
  EPA's lead-safe practices for do-it-yourselfers: mist surfaces before
  sanding, scraping, drilling or cutting; consider an N-100 respirator,
  coveralls, eye protection and gloves; wash your hands and face when
  you stop and your work clothes apart from the family's; keep children
  away; clean with a HEPA vacuum and wet wiping. The full list is in
  [Repairing a Building](/library#repairing-a-building).
- **Keep painted salvage away from children's things.** Painted cribs,
  toys and children's furniture are the worst place for old paint
  (general practice).

### Asbestos in old building materials

The EPA lists where asbestos may be found: vermiculite insulation, vinyl
floor tiles and sheet flooring backing, roofing and siding shingles,
textured paint and patching compounds, the paper, millboard or cement
sheets protecting walls and floors around wood stoves, pipes covered
with asbestos blanket or tape, and furnace and door gaskets. You generally
cannot tell by looking, and the EPA says not to saw, sand, scrape or
drill it, and to leave removal to a trained and accredited
professional. **None of those materials is worth salvaging**
(general practice): you cannot know it is safe, and taking it out is
the moment it releases fibres.

### Treated wood: CCA and creosote

Two old wood preservatives turn up in salvage again and again, and the
EPA has pages on both.

- **CCA (chromated copper arsenate),** a pressure-treated wood that is
  green-tinged when new. The EPA says it was used in homes for decks and play sets before
  2004, and that its makers stopped making it for homeowner uses in
  December 2003. The EPA's pesticide rules do not regulate reusing it,
  but the EPA says: it should not be reused as mulch; never burn it, or
  any other preservative-treated wood, at home, because of the smoke and
  ash; wear
  goggles and a dust mask when sawing it; and wash your hands after
  handling it.
- **Creosote,** the black, oily, tar-smelling treatment on old railway
  sleepers and utility poles. The EPA says creosoted sleepers are
  sometimes reused as landscape timbers, that reusing them is not
  regulated under pesticide law, and that the EPA did not find health
  risks of concern for the general public. That is not an all-clear for
  every use. Its warning is the same as for CCA: do not burn
  creosote-treated wood at home. Beyond that, wear gloves when you
  handle it and wash afterwards, and do not use creosoted sleepers for
  vegetable beds, indoors, or where children play (general practice, in
  line with the EPA's handling advice for CCA).

Colour is a poor guide. Newer copper-based treatments are green too,
and old CCA weathers to grey, so a grey board from an old deck, fence or
play set may still be CCA (general knowledge). Where you cannot tell
whether a board was treated, treat it as treated:
do not burn it, do not mulch it, wear a dust mask and wash afterwards
(general practice). How to read treated wood and its tags is in [Rust,
Rot and Decay](/library#rust-rot-and-decay).

### Containers that held something

A used drum, can or tank is only as safe as whatever was in it.

- **Pesticide containers.** The EPA says: "Never reuse empty pesticide
  containers. Pesticide residues can contaminate the new contents and
  cause serious harm."
- **Anything for water or food.** Use only containers that were made for
  food and held food. [Storing Water Safely](/library#storing-water-safely)
  and [Collecting Rainwater](/library#collecting-rainwater) give the rules,
  including never using a container that held a chemical or something
  you cannot identify.
- **Cutting, welding or heating a drum or tank.** This is the salvage job
  most likely to kill someone. OSHA's welding rule (29 CFR
  1910.252(a)(3)) forbids welding, cutting or other hot work on used
  drums, barrels, tanks or other containers "until they have been
  cleaned so thoroughly as to make absolutely certain that there are no
  flammable materials present" or any substance that heat could turn
  into flammable or toxic vapour, and requires hollow spaces to be
  vented first. OSHA's construction rule (29 CFR 1926.352(i) and (j))
  allows the alternative of filling the container with water, and
  requires a vent for any pressure before heat is applied. An angle
  grinder throws sparks too (general knowledge). **At home: never cut,
  grind, drill or weld a drum, fuel can, gas cylinder or tank that has
  held fuel, solvent or anything unknown** (general practice). A
  container a professional has made safe is a different thing from one
  that looks empty.

### Old fridges, freezers and chests

The CPSC's fact sheet on appliance entrapment reports children
suffocating inside old refrigerators, freezers, dryers, chests and
coolers whose doors cannot be pushed open from inside, most of them
four to seven years old, often playing hide and seek. Its advice for an
old appliance being stored or thrown out: the surest method is to take
the door off completely, usually a simple job with a screwdriver; and
it notes that many places make it unlawful to discard an old
refrigerator without removing the door. **If you salvage a fridge or
chest for any use, take the door off, or make sure it cannot latch,
the same day** (our application).

Two more reasons to leave a fridge's insides alone:

- **The refrigerant.** EPA rules under the Clean Air Act forbid
  intentionally venting refrigerant while disposing of equipment, with
  narrow exceptions, and the EPA's appliance disposal page says some
  municipalities will not collect a fridge until a professional, such
  as someone from an appliance repair shop, has removed its
  refrigerant. The same page says some newer appliances use propane
  (R-290), and the venting rule
  exempts isobutane (R-600a) in household fridges: both are flammable
  gases (general knowledge). Do not cut the pipes (general practice).
- **Other hazards inside.** The EPA says older appliances may also
  contain mercury in switches, contaminated oils and PCBs.

Microwave ovens and old tube televisions hold dangerous electrical
charge after they are unplugged; the reasons to leave them shut are in
[Working Out Why Something Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke).
Batteries and fluorescent tubes are in [What Not to Compost, Burn or
Pour Away](/library#what-not-to-compost-burn-or-pour-away).

### Galvanised steel and heat

Galvanised steel (zinc-coated: fence posts, pipe, roofing, buckets) is
excellent salvage cold. Heated by welding or torch cutting, the zinc
makes fumes. NIOSH lists metal fume fever,
with chills, aches, fever, cough and chest tightness, as the effect of
zinc oxide fume, and OSHA's welding rule requires local exhaust
ventilation for welding or cutting zinc-coated metal indoors (29 CFR
1910.252(c)). How to tell metals apart, and the welding rules, are in
[Metals and Alloys](/library#metals-and-alloys).

### Nails, splinters and sharp edges

Salvaged wood is full of nails, broken screws and staples, and a nail
through a boot or a hand is the classic salvage injury (general
practice). Pull or bend over every nail as you go, wear gloves and
stout boots, and never leave a board point-up on the ground. Old
windows add broken glass, so handle them with gloves and eye protection
and tape any cracked pane before you move it (general practice). A
puncture wound and the tetanus question are covered in [Bleeding and
Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds).

### Weight, and taking things down

Doors, radiators, cast-iron baths, beams and stacks of boards are heavy
and awkward; the lifting rules are in [Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](/library#force-levers-and-mechanical-advantage). Taking down a shed,
a fence or a wall to get at its materials is demolition: things fall,
walls lean, and wires and pipes run where you cannot see them. Have the
electricity, gas and water to it disconnected first (by the utility or a
licensed tradesperson where they are connected), work from the top down,
never stand where a section can fall on you, and get experienced help
for anything bigger than a small shed (general practice). Finding what
is behind a surface before you cut is in [Repairing a
Building](/library#repairing-a-building).

### Things whose whole job was to protect someone

Some things should not be salvaged for their original job: old ones may
not meet today's safety rules, or they may have been damaged in a way
you cannot see.

- **Cribs.** The CPSC's guide for resellers says new federal crib rules
  took effect on 28 June 2011, eliminating drop sides, and tells
  resellers to destroy any crib made before then unless there is
  written proof it meets the standard; no drop-side crib can meet it,
  even one fixed with new hardware. Between November 2007 and April 2010
  the CPSC had
  reports of 35 crib deaths from structural problems. The same guide's
  rule for nursery products: "When in doubt, throw it out!"
- **Recalled products.** The CPSC says it is illegal to sell any
  recalled product; check its recall list before you pass anything on.
  [Trading Fairly](/library#trading-fairly) has more.
- **Fall-arrest gear.** OSHA's rule (29 CFR 1926.502(d)(19)) says a
  harness or lanyard that has caught a fall must be removed from
  service and not used again until a competent person has inspected it
  and judged it undamaged. Second-hand gear with an unknown history has
  the same problem (our reading).
- **Helmets, child car seats and climbing rope** follow the same logic:
  they may have taken a hit you cannot see (general practice). Rope
  inspection is in [Fibre, Cord and Textiles](/library#fibre-cord-and-textiles).

## Seeing stock

With the hazards in mind, most salvage is ordinary, useful material.
The EPA's page on building materials lists what is commonly reused:
doors, hardware, appliances and fixtures; wood offcuts for small
framing pieces; brick, concrete and masonry as fill, a sub-base or
driveway bedding; leftover paint remixed for a primer coat; and
scrap wood chipped for mulch. The last one needs the caution above: only
clean wood, never painted or treated (our addition, from the EPA's CCA
page and lead guidance).

Good sources, all of them with permission (general practice):

- **Your own place.** Before anything goes to the tip, ask whether it is
  stock.
- **Building and renovation jobs.** Builders often pay to dispose of
  offcuts and old fittings, and may be glad to have them taken.
- **Deconstruction.** The EPA describes deconstruction as taking a
  building apart carefully so its parts can be reused, and lists its
  benefits, from saving materials to creating jobs.
- **Reuse centres and second-hand building supply shops.**
- **Neighbours and free listings.**

**Permission first.** Things left on someone else's land, in a skip or
at a transfer station still belong to somebody, and many tips forbid
taking things back out. Ask (general practice).

## Salvaged wood

Wood is the salvage most people start with, and the US Forest Service's
Forest Products Laboratory has tested it. Its 2008 study of Douglas fir
framing lumber taken from buildings being demolished (FPL-RP-650, by
Falk and colleagues) found:

- **It is weaker than new lumber of the same grade:** on average about
  25 percent lower in bending strength than test data for new lumber of
  the same species, though somewhat stiffer.
- **Nail holes matter where they sit.** Holes reduced strength when they
  were closely spaced, or had started splits, mainly along the edge that
  is stretched when the board bends. Its design advice is to put an edge
  full of nail holes on the side that is squeezed, not the side that is
  stretched.
- **You often do not know the species.** Without a grade stamp, the
  species cannot be guaranteed, and in the study's experience with
  lumber from the 1940s stamps were infrequent; of 1,078 pieces graded
  on site as Douglas fir, laboratory identification found 98 were
  something else.
- **Taking a building apart damages wood.** An earlier study it cites
  found that damage from dismantling lowered the lumber's quality by
  about one grade.
- **Its first recommendation is that reclaimed lumber be regraded before
  it is reused,** and it notes that reclaimed structural lumber and
  timber used in timber framing is, for the most part, regraded, with
  each design approved by a licensed engineer.

What that means at home (general practice, from the study):

- **Use salvaged wood without a grade stamp where it carries no
  important load:** shelves, fences, garden beds of untreated wood,
  compost bins, cladding, furniture, blocking.
- **Anything structural** (a joist, a rafter, a beam, a post that holds
  up a deck) needs a grade stamp, or a lumber grader or engineer to
  approve it. Building codes generally require graded lumber for
  structural use; your local building department can say what it will
  accept.
- **Look for rot,** with an awl as in [Repairing a
  Building](/library#repairing-a-building): the Wood Handbook says wood with
  visible decay should be assumed to have lost a great deal of its
  strength.
- **De-nail completely before cutting,** and run a magnet or a metal
  detector along a board before it goes near a saw or a planer; a hidden
  nail breaks blades and throws pieces.

**Storing it.** The Wood Handbook says wood will not decay if it is kept
air dry. For drying lumber it advises well-drained ground, debris and
weeds cleared, and piling that lets the wood dry fast and keeps the
wet off; where termites are a problem, stock held for a long time
should sit high enough to be inspected from beneath. For a salvage
pile: stack it off the ground on blocks, with thin sticks between
layers, under a roof or a tarp that leaves the
sides open (general practice, following the Handbook). A heap on wet
ground invites rot and termites (general practice).

## Pallets

Pallets are free, everywhere, and made of wood, which makes them a
common first salvage. Pallets used in international trade must
be treated against pests and stamped. CBP describes the stamp: the
International Plant Protection Convention symbol, a two-letter country
code, a number for the treatment facility, and a code for the
treatment:

- **HT**: heat treated, the wood's core held at 56 C (133 F) for at least
  30 minutes.
- **MB**: fumigated with methyl bromide.
- **DH**: dielectric heating, by microwave or radio frequency.

The mark tells you how the wood was treated against pests. It does not
tell you what the pallet carried or what was spilled on it. Leave
pallets that are stained, oily or smell of chemicals, whatever their
stamp. Many people prefer HT pallets for furniture and garden use; that
is a preference, not a rule we can cite. An unmarked pallet was most
likely made for domestic use and simply not stamped (our reading); it
tells you nothing either way. (General practice throughout, beside
CBP's description of the mark.)

Pallets make good compost bins, as in [Your First
Compost](/library#your-first-compost). Taking them apart without splitting
the boards is slow: cut the boards beside the blocks with a
reciprocating saw rather than prying (general practice).

## Jars and food containers

The USDA's canning guide (the *Complete Guide to Home Canning*, Guide 1)
is clear about which jars can be reused:

- **Mason jars** may be reused many times, with only new lids each time.
- **Commercial mayonnaise and salad dressing jars** may be used with new
  two-piece lids for canning acid foods, but expect more seal failures
  and breakage; they are not recommended for a pressure canner, because
  of excessive breakage. Other commercial jars
  that will not take two-piece canning lids are not recommended for home
  canning at all.
- **Metal cans can be used only once.**
- **Lids:** unused lids work well for at least five years from
  manufacture; old, dented or deformed lids should not be used.

Why canning has these rules is in [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules). For dry storage, any clean jar with a
sound lid is fine (general practice).

## Metal

Scrap metal is useful stock, and worth sorting: steel, aluminium,
copper and brass each have their uses and their scrap prices. The
magnet test and other ways of telling metals apart are in [Metals and
Alloys](/library#metals-and-alloys). Two cautions from above: never
heat or cut a container that held something, and do not heat galvanised
or painted metal indoors. Old pipe and wiring are usually better sold
as scrap than reused in a house; reusing them in plumbing or electrics
is a job for the licensed trades, if at all (general practice).

## Worked example: an old shed's worth of boards

A neighbour is pulling down a shed built about 1960, and offers you the
wood.

1. **Ask what it was used for.** A shed that stored chemicals or fuel is
   a reason to be pickier (general practice).
2. **Look at the roof and walls before anyone breaks anything.** Old
   roofing and siding shingles may contain asbestos (EPA); leave those
   to a professional and take nothing from them.
3. **Sort as it comes down:** painted (assume lead); green-tinged,
   tagged, or from anything that touched the ground, such as sills and
   posts (assume treated, whatever its colour); plain.
4. **De-nail as you go,** gloves and boots on, nails into a bucket.
5. **Plain boards:** shelves, a bench, a compost bin. Check each for
   rot with an awl.
6. **Painted boards:** reuse whole, painted side as it is, outdoors or
   away from children, or leave them. No sanding, scraping or burning.
7. **Treated boards:** reuse for something outdoors that is not a
   vegetable bed (general practice), never burned, never chipped for
   mulch (EPA).
8. **The framing:** good for non-structural work. For a new structure,
   only with a grade stamp or approval (FPL).
9. **Stack it** off the ground, stickered, under cover.

## Worked example: a 55-gallon drum

A free blue plastic drum, with a faded label.

1. **Read the label.** If it held food or drink ingredients, it can
   become a rain barrel ([Collecting Rainwater](/library#collecting-rainwater)).
2. **If it held a pesticide, never reuse it** (EPA). If it held a
   chemical or anything you cannot identify, do not use it for water,
   food, animals or the garden (general practice, as in the water
   guides).
3. **A steel drum that held fuel or solvent:** do not cut it, drill it,
   grind it or weld it. OSHA requires it to be cleaned so thoroughly
   that no flammable material is left, or filled with water, before any
   hot work, and vented (above). At home, leave it whole (general
   practice).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Asbestos-containing materials:** a trained and accredited
  professional (EPA).
- **Lead paint:** renovation, repair or painting in a pre-1978 home, a
  lead-safe certified contractor (recommended by the EPA, and required
  for paid work); painted salvage that would need stripping is often
  better left alone (general practice).
- **Refrigerant in an appliance:** a professional, such as an appliance
  repair shop (EPA).
- **Structural reuse of lumber:** a lumber grader, an engineer, or your
  building department (FPL, general practice).
- **Opening or cutting a container that held fuel or chemicals:** do
  not; if it must be done, a professional (OSHA, general practice).
- **Reusing pipe, fittings, wiring or breakers in a house:** the
  licensed trades (general practice).

## Traps

- **Burning the scrap.** Painted or treated wood in a stove or a bonfire
  puts lead, arsenic or creosote smoke and ash into the air and the
  garden.
- **"It is only a little dust."** Sanding old paint makes exactly the
  lead dust the EPA warns about, and children are the most at risk
  from it.
- **The empty drum.** The vapour left in an "empty" fuel drum is what
  explodes.
- **The door left on.** An old fridge in a garage or garden is a trap
  for a child until its door is off.
- **Salvage as structure.** Old framing looks strong; on average it is
  weaker than new lumber of the same grade, and you rarely know which
  wood it is.
- **The pile that never gets used.** Salvage stored on wet ground rots
  before it is needed. Keep what you will use, stack it dry, and pass
  on the rest.

## How the game models it

The game has a little of this, mostly in what crafting leaves behind.

- **Taking a piece down gives everything back.** While holding a piece
  to place from the Crafting page, the Swing tool key (F by default)
  takes down the finished piece you are looking at and returns every
  material to your pack, and what does not fit there to storage, with a
  message saying so. The code says plainly that this is a game
  choice, because nothing models what dismantling breaks; in real life,
  an earlier study cited by the FPL paper above found that taking
  buildings apart cost their lumber about a grade
  (`src/engine/build_place.rs`).
- **Byproducts are kept as stock.** Sawing logs into planks also gives
  Sawdust and Tree Bark, and at a sawmill Wood Slabs too. Sawdust goes
  into fertilizer at the Composter and serves as a garden mulch; bark
  is used to tan leather and as a mulch; slabs can be made into charcoal
  in a kiln. Smelting copper or iron leaves Slag, which can stand in for
  half the gravel in Slag Concrete, and many fires leave Wood Ash, which
  raises the pH of garden soil (`data/recipes.csv`, `data/items.csv`,
  `data/garden/weeds.ron`, `data/garden/soil_ph.ron`).
- **Scrap Metal has no use yet.** The item list has a salvage category
  (Scrap Metal, Lens, Crude Weapon and others). Some have a use: a Lens,
  for instance, goes into the prosthetic and cybernetic eyes in
  `data/medical.ron`, eye surgery needs one, and vendors sell it. But
  no recipe uses Scrap Metal, and the creatures whose loot includes it
  (the robots, and the goblin, in `data/creatures.csv`) never appear in
  the world by themselves; only the Dev page's spawn tool can place one.
- **No breaking down.** There is no way to take a crafted item apart
  into its materials, or to melt down scrap. A vendor refuses to buy
  defective goods, and its message suggests scrapping or recycling
  them, but the game has no way to do either yet.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** The game starts in the Dev play mode (Settings >
  Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, unless you turn off the Creative switch
  on the Inventory page, crafting does not use up what goes into it, so
  byproducts only matter as stock in Normal mode. Building a piece from
  the Crafting page uses up its materials in every mode: from your pack
  first, then the home's storage, on board the ship, and from your pack
  alone on a planet.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
  during development, only your character (name, look and outfit)
  carries between launches, so anything you kept starts the next
  session as the default home has it.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: nothing
in it is painted with lead, treated with arsenic, full of nails, or a
drum with vapour in it, and nothing salvaged is weaker than new. Real
salvage is mostly the judgement of which things are safe, and that is
the part the game cannot teach yet.

## You own this when

- You assume old paint is lead paint, and you never sand, scrape, burn
  or torch it without lead-safe precautions.
- You leave anything that might contain asbestos where it is.
- You treat any board that might be CCA or creosote-treated as treated,
  whatever its colour, and you never burn treated wood or chip it for
  mulch; you keep creosoted sleepers out of vegetable beds, the house
  and play areas.
- You never reuse a pesticide container, and you use only food-grade
  containers for water and food.
- You never cut, grind, drill or weld a drum or tank that held fuel,
  solvent or anything unknown.
- You take the door off any old fridge or chest the day it comes home.
- You do not salvage cribs made before June 2011, recalled products, or
  safety gear with an unknown past.
- You de-nail as you go, wear gloves and boots, and check wood for rot.
- You use ungraded salvage only where it carries no important load.
- You can read a pallet's stamp and know what it does and does not tell
  you.
- You reuse Mason jars with new lids, and know which other jars to keep
  out of the canner.
- You ask before you take, and you stack what you keep off the ground,
  dry and with air around it.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, general knowledge or our reading, not sourced

- Treating salvage of unknown age as lead-painted, and avoiding the
  contractor-prohibited lead practices at home, are our application of
  the EPA's advice for homes of unknown age and of 40 CFR 745.85.
- Keeping painted salvage away from children's things; not salvaging
  materials on the EPA's asbestos list; treating unknown wood as treated.
- How CCA wood (green-tinged when new) and creosoted wood (black, oily
  and tar-smelling) look, that newer copper treatments are green too and
  that old CCA weathers to grey, is general knowledge, not from the EPA
  pages.
- Gloves and washing when handling creosoted wood, and keeping creosoted
  sleepers out of vegetable beds, the house and play areas, are general
  practice beside the EPA's creosote page, which found no health risks
  of concern for the general public and warns only against burning.
- That building codes generally require graded lumber for structural use
  is general knowledge; ask the local building department what it
  accepts.
- That an angle grinder throws sparks, and that propane and isobutane
  are flammable, are general knowledge; never cutting, grinding,
  drilling or welding a used fuel or chemical container, and not
  cutting a fridge's pipes, are general practice.
- Taking a salvaged fridge's door off the same day is our application of
  the CPSC's advice for stored and discarded appliances.
- Applying the fall-arrest rule to second-hand safety gear is our
  reading; helmets, car seats and climbing rope are general practice.
- Nails as the classic salvage injury, de-nailing, gloves and boots,
  and checking boards with a magnet or metal detector before machining.
- Gloves, eye protection and tape for old window glass; disconnecting
  services before taking a structure down, working from the top down,
  staying clear of what can fall, and getting experienced help.
- Leaving painted salvage that would need stripping, and a heap on wet
  ground inviting rot and termites.
- Where to find salvage, asking permission, and tips that forbid taking
  things out.
- The household rules drawn from the FPL study (ungraded salvage only
  where it carries no important load; graded or approved lumber for
  structure; asking the building department), and the stacking method,
  which follows the Wood Handbook's air-drying advice.
- What a pallet stamp does not tell you, leaving stained pallets, the
  preference for HT pallets, the reading of unmarked pallets, and
  cutting pallets apart rather than prying.
- Clean jars for dry storage; sorting scrap metal; reusing old pipe and
  wiring only through the licensed trades.
