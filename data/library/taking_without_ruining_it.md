# Taking Without Ruining It

Kneel on the gravel at a minus tide, push a garden fork in past the
tines, and lever up. A clam comes out with the gravel, squirting, about
two inches across, oval, ribbed, a purple stain inside the shell at the
siphon end. You know what it is. It is a Manila clam. It is excellent
food. Nothing about it is poisonous today.

You may still be committing a crime by putting it in your bucket.

Not because you were wrong about the clam. Because of who owns the
ground under your knees, what month it is, whether you bought a piece
of paper in January, how big that clam is, how many are already in the
bucket, and whether the state health department has this particular
beach open for biotoxins today. Six questions, none of them answerable
by looking at the clam, all of them independent, and any one of them
can make the answer no.

That is what this guide is about. The Library already has a guide on
identifying what you find, and it opens by refusing to be a field
guide, which is the right refusal.
[Identifying Wild Plants](/library#identifying-wild-plants) covers the
question "what is this and will it hurt me". This one covers the other
half: **you were right, it is edible, and there are still reasons not
to take it.** Most of those reasons are not obvious, several of them
carry legal penalties, and a few of them are about damage you will
never see because it happens after you leave.

## What this guide will and will not do

It will not tell you the rule for the ground you are standing on. It
cannot. The rule depends on the parcel, and the parcel changes every
few hundred yards along a shoreline, and the number attached to it
changes by year and sometimes by emergency order mid-season.

What it will do is teach you the **shape** of the question, so that you
know how many separate answers you need and where each one lives. That
is a transferable skill. The specific numbers in here are worked
examples for one place, Silverdale, on Dyes Inlet in Kitsap County,
Washington, and every one of them was read on a real page on 15
September 2026. Some of them will be wrong by the time you read this.
The structure will not be.

Three honest warnings before anything else.

**This is not legal advice and nothing here is a permission.** Where a
number appears, it appears with the body that published it, so you can
go and check. Checking is the skill; reading this is not a substitute
for it.

**The consequences are not symmetric.** Mistaking something you may
take for something you may not is an offence, and in the case of a
federally listed species it is a serious one. Mistaking something you
may take for something you may not costs you a handful of berries.
When you cannot resolve the question, the cheap error is available and
you should take it.

**Moralising is not the point here.** There is a whole genre of writing
about foraging ethics that tells you to be respectful and gives you
nothing you can check. Being told to "take only what you need" does not
tell you that a geoduck limit is one clam and a horse clam limit is
seven, and it does not tell you which of those two is in your hole.
Where a checkable rule exists, this guide gives you the rule. Where
none exists, it says so in those words rather than inventing a
comforting one.

## The first question is whose land it is

Beginners skip this one. It is the question with legal teeth, and it is
the one that most often turns an innocent afternoon into a citation.

**Permission is the default requirement, not the exception.** In
Washington, entering or staying on someone else's property without
permission is criminal trespass: second degree on premises, a
misdemeanour, and first degree in a building, a gross misdemeanour.
Ignoring a posted sign or a request to leave is enough. The project's
own law layer carries this as rule `wa-criminal-trespass`, citing RCW
9A.52.070 and 9A.52.080. Note what that means for harvesting: the
trespass is complete when you are standing there. Taking something is a
separate matter on top of it.

There is no general right to forage on land you do not own. Some
countries have one. This one does not.

### The tideland trap

This is the one that catches people here, and it catches them because
the ground genuinely looks public.

Washington sold most of its tidelands. The Department of Ecology's page
on the public trust doctrine puts it plainly: the state sold 60 percent
of tidelands before 1971. That land was sold as property, and it is
still property. A stretch of gravel that is
underwater twice a day, that has no fence because a fence would not
survive, that you reached by walking along the water from a public
access point, can be somebody's parcel with a deed and a tax bill.

The Department of Fish and Wildlife says something about this that is
easy to misread. Its shellfish beaches page states that in Washington,
most state recreational harvest regulations do not extend to shellfish
harvest on private tidelands for personal use. Read carefully, that is
not a permission. It means the state's seasons and bag limits are not
the rule that governs a private beach. **The rule that governs a
private beach is the owner's.** The owner may harvest their own
shellfish under different constraints than you face on a public beach;
you may not harvest there at all without their permission.

And the public trust doctrine, which people reach for at this point,
does not rescue you. Ecology is candid that the doctrine protects
commerce, navigation and fisheries, and that the state Supreme Court
extended it to incidental recreational rights on the water. But on the
specific question of walking on a private beach, Ecology's own page
says no Washington court has ruled whether walking on the beach or
wading in the water on privately owned beaches, streambanks and
tidelands is a right under the doctrine. That is an unresolved question
of law, stated as unresolved by the state agency that would know.

**So: you can float over a private tideland in a boat. Whether you may
stand on it is not settled, and taking shellfish off it without
permission is not in doubt at all.** Use the state's public beach list
and confirm the beach is on it.

### The public land ladder, and why the rungs disagree

Once you are on public land, the next surprise is that "public land"
is not one thing. Five categories of public land near Silverdale have
five different harvest rules, and they do not vary in the direction you
would guess.

**A national park is the most restrictive by default.** The federal
regulation for the National Park System, 36 CFR 2.1, prohibits
possessing, destroying or removing plants or their parts. Gathering is
not a right you have and then lose; it is prohibited unless somebody
specifically turned it on. The exception reads: "The superintendent may
designate certain fruits, berries, nuts, or unoccupied seashells which
may be gathered by hand for personal use or consumption upon a written
determination that the gathering or consumption will not adversely
affect park wildlife, the reproductive potential of a plant species, or
otherwise adversely affect park resources."

Every clause in that sentence is doing work. **By hand**, so no tools.
**Fruits, berries, nuts, or unoccupied seashells**, so not roots, not
bark, not a live shell with an animal in it. **Upon a written
determination**, so the default is no and somebody has to have written
down a yes for that park.

Where a superintendent has written one, the numbers are specific.
Olympic National Park states that you can legally collect one quart per
person, per day of mushrooms. Mount Rainier National Park allows one
gallon of edible fungi per person per day for personal use or
consumption. Two national parks, same state, same agency, and a
four-fold difference. There is no national park rule. There is a rule
for the park you are standing in.

**A national forest is a different agency with different law, and it is
not the same thing as a national park.** This confuses people
constantly here because the two nearest units share a name. Olympic
National Forest states that no permit is required for incidental
gathering of mushrooms for personal use, and that you may collect up to
one gallon per species per year, and that you may not sell or trade
mushrooms. For berries the same forest allows one gallon per day, three
gallons per year, with commercial use prohibited. Fiddleheads are one
pound per person per day. Campers may collect downed dead wood for
immediate use without a permit, and a free personal use permit covers
up to six cords of firewood per calendar year.

Put those two side by side, because the comparison is the lesson.
Olympic National Park, the more protected designation, allows a quart
of mushrooms **per day**. Olympic National Forest, the working forest,
allows a gallon per species **per year**. A gallon is four quarts, so
**four days of legal picking in the park equals the forest's entire
annual allowance for one species.** Assume the park is the stricter of
the two, pick accordingly, and you are over the forest's limit in the
first week of the season.

**Washington state parks have their own rule and it is the most
generous of the lot.** WAC 352-28-030 allows recreational harvest,
possession or transport of edible plants and edible fruiting bodies
including, but not limited to, mushrooms, berries and nuts, up to two
gallons per person per day unless otherwise posted, and the two gallons
may be made up of one or more species. That is eight quarts a day,
eight times Olympic National Park's daily limit. It applies in
recreation, resource recreation, natural, natural forest and heritage
areas and in parks not yet classified. It does **not** apply in a
natural area preserve, where no harvest of edible plants or fruiting
bodies is allowed at all. Harvest techniques involving raking, or other
techniques with the potential to degrade park natural or cultural
resources, are prohibited. Commercial harvest of edibles is not allowed
on park lands. And the rule contains a clause worth noticing, which we
will come back to: it does not limit federally reserved tribal rights,
including treaty rights.

**Department of Natural Resources land has yet another set of
numbers.** DNR allows personal use harvest from some of its managed
lands: mushrooms limited to three gallons of a single species per day,
not to exceed nine gallons plus one mushroom per year; berries limited
to three gallons of a single species per day, not to exceed nine
gallons per year; brush and ferns limited to root ball removal of not
more than two plants per person per day and not more than four plants
per person per year. No reselling. Harvesting any forest products from
DNR campgrounds and picnic sites, natural area preserves or natural
resources conservation areas is prohibited unless the harvest is
specifically included in the site's management plan.

The "nine gallons plus one mushroom" is not a typo and it is not a
joke. It exists so that a single enormous mushroom that tips you over
nine gallons does not make you a criminal. Somebody thought about that.

Note the arithmetic in DNR's pair of numbers: three gallons a day
against nine gallons a year means **three good days exhaust your
year**, for that species. The daily limit is not the one that will
catch you. The annual one is, and nobody is counting it for you.

**County parks may prohibit it outright.** Kitsap County Code section
10.12.050 makes it unlawful to remove, destroy, mutilate or deface any
tree, shrub, flower or other plant, or any rock, cliff or other natural
feature, in a county park. "Park" is defined broadly at 10.12.020(1) to
include any park, playground, beach or recreation centre owned or used
by the county under the park director's supervision. A violation is a
misdemeanour under 10.12.190. So the default on county ground is
stricter than on state park ground, and a county **beach** is inside
that definition.

There is a carve-out, and I could not read it. Kitsap County Parks'
rules page refers to a separate document called "Mushroom Harvest
Policies", and a 2014 county resolution exists under that name, but
neither the resolution PDF nor the stewardship plan that quotes it
would render as readable text for me today. So I can tell you a county
mushroom policy exists and that the general prohibition in 10.12.050
would otherwise cover mushrooms, and I cannot tell you what the policy
says. Call Kitsap County Parks on 360.337.5350 before picking in a
county park. That is the number on their own rules page.

### The comparison, in one place

Mushrooms, because they are the one product with a number in every
jurisdiction:

| Land | Mushroom limit for personal use |
|---|---|
| Olympic National Park | 1 quart per person per day |
| Mount Rainier National Park | 1 gallon per person per day |
| Olympic National Forest | 1 gallon per species per calendar year, no permit |
| Washington state parks | part of 2 gallons per person per day of all edibles, none in a natural area preserve |
| Washington DNR land | 3 gallons of one species per day, max 9 gallons plus one per year |
| Kitsap County parks | general prohibition at KCC 10.12.050, separate mushroom policy I could not read |
| Private land | whatever the owner says |

Five different numeric limits, one flat prohibition, and no way to
derive any of them from any other. The spread between the tightest
daily figure and the loosest is twelvefold: Olympic National Park
allows a quart a day, DNR land allows three gallons of one species a
day, and three gallons is twelve quarts. The only safe generalisation
is that **there is no general rule**, and the question "how much may I
pick" has no answer until you have answered "where am I".

### A pass is not a permission

A Discover Pass costs 45 dollars a year or 10 dollars for a day and
covers parking at recreation lands managed by Washington State Parks,
the Department of Fish and Wildlife and the Department of Natural
Resources. Note what it is: **parking.** It buys you the right to leave
your car there. It carries no harvest right whatsoever, and the harvest
rules on those three agencies' lands differ from each other, as above.
A shellfish licence is a separate purchase. A specialized forest
products permit is a third. People conflate them because they are all
pieces of paper bought from the state.

## Tribal treaty rights, which are law and not courtesy

Silverdale sits inside the usual and accustomed areas of treaty tribes,
and the Suquamish Tribe is the local nation. This is not a cultural
footnote to a harvesting guide. It is a live legal framework that
determines who may take what from the beach you are standing on, and it
is older than the state of Washington.

**The source is a treaty, and a treaty is federal law.** The Treaty of
Point Elliott was signed on 22 January 1855 by the Dwamish, Suquamish
and some twenty other named tribes and bands. Because it is a federal
instrument it is public domain, and Article 5 is short enough to give
whole:

> The right of taking fish at usual and accustomed grounds and stations
> is further secured to said Indians in common with all citizens of the
> Territory, and of erecting temporary houses for the purpose of curing,
> together with the privilege of hunting and gathering roots and berries
> on open and unclaimed lands. Provided, however, That they shall not
> take shell-fish from any beds staked or cultivated by citizens.

Read the verbs. The right is **secured**, not granted: the tribes
reserved it, they did not receive it. It runs to **usual and accustomed
grounds and stations**, which are specific places, not a general
licence. And it is held **in common with all citizens**, three words
that the later litigation had to work out the meaning of.

**The courts quantified it.** In 1994 the federal district court took
up shellfish specifically, in the proceeding usually named for Judge
Rafeedie. As the Northwest Indian Fisheries Commission describes the
outcome, the tribes hold harvest rights to half of all shellfish from
all of the usual and accustomed places; all public and private
tidelands within the case area are subject to treaty harvest, with an
exception for artificially created beds and places specifically set
aside for non-tribal shellfish cultivation, which is the modern reading
of the treaty's "staked or cultivated" proviso; and tribal harvest on
private beaches is subject to extensive time, place and manner
restrictions. The Supreme Court declined to hear the case in 1999.

**Read that sentence about private tidelands again, because it is the
one that surprises people.** A private beach in Puget Sound is private
against you. It is not private against a treaty right that predates the
deed.

**Co-management is real and current, not historical.** The Suquamish
Tribe describes itself as one of 24 treaty tribes in the Puget Sound
area that co-manage shellfish resources with the Washington Department
of Fish and Wildlife, surveys beaches within its usual and accustomed
area, and manages half of the total allowable catch on private and
public beaches as the Treaty Share, with the state managing the other
half, excepting tidelands identified as staked and cultivated under the
Shellfish Growers Settlement. So when you read a WDFW beach season, you
are reading the state's management of the state's half of a jointly
managed resource. The number is not the whole of what the beach can
give; it is the part of it allocated to you.

Washington's own rules acknowledge this in their text. WAC 352-28-030,
the state parks harvest rule, carries an explicit clause that it does
not limit federally reserved tribal rights including treaty rights. A
state rule cannot reach them, and it says so.

**What this means for you, practically.** Three things, none of them
onerous.

Treaty harvest you may see happening on a beach, including one you
thought was closed or one that is privately owned, is very likely
lawful under a framework that does not run through the rules you were
reading. It is not poaching and it is not your business to police.

The Suquamish Tribe is a government with a fisheries department, not an
interest group. If a question is about shellfish management around Dyes
Inlet, they are one of the two co-managers holding the answer.

And the treaty's own words are worth carrying: hunting and gathering
roots and berries on **open and unclaimed lands.** In 1855 that phrase
described most of this peninsula. The reason so little of it is open
and unclaimed now is the same reason your own harvest question keeps
coming back to who owns the ground.

## What "protected" means, as a verb

"Protected" is a word people nod at without knowing what it prohibits.
It is worth knowing, because the prohibited acts are broader than most
people assume and they differ sharply between animals and plants.

The Library's companion guide,
[The Species Where You Live](/library#the-species-where-you-live), carries the
inventory: which species here are listed, what each listing is, and why
a blank field is not a clearance. That guide is the list. This section
is the verbs.

### The federal layer

**The Endangered Species Act defines "take" very broadly.** At 16
U.S.C. 1532(19), take means "to harass, harm, pursue, hunt, shoot,
wound, kill, trap, capture, or collect, or to attempt to engage in any
such conduct." Harass and harm are in there alongside kill. Attempting
counts. For a listed animal, you do not have to catch it or hurt it to
have taken it.

**But the Act treats plants completely differently, and almost nobody
knows this.** Section 1538(a)(1) prohibits taking listed fish or
wildlife within the United States. Section 1538(a)(2), the plant
provision, prohibits something much narrower: to "remove and reduce to
possession any such species from areas under Federal jurisdiction;
maliciously damage or destroy any such species on any such area; or
remove, cut, dig up, or damage or destroy any such species on any other
area in knowing violation of any law or regulation of any State or in
the course of any violation of a State criminal trespass law."

Parse the third clause. Off federal land, digging up a federally
endangered plant is a federal offence **only if** you were also breaking
a state law or trespassing. A listed animal on private land is
protected by the Act. A listed plant on private land, absent some other
violation, largely is not. That asymmetry is in the statute, it is
deliberate, and it is the single most surprising thing in federal
conservation law for somebody coming to it from the animal side.

**The Migratory Bird Treaty Act covers parts, nests and eggs, which is
why the feather on the trail is a problem.** 16 U.S.C. 703(a) makes it
unlawful, unless permitted by regulation, "at any time, by any means or
in any manner, to pursue, hunt, take, capture, kill, attempt to take,
capture, or kill, possess, offer for sale, sell..." and continues
through barter, purchase, ship, export, import, transport and carry.
The protection extends to "any part, nest, or egg of any such bird" and
to any product composed in whole or part of a bird, part, nest or egg.

**Possess** is on that list, and **any part** covers feathers. A
handsome flight feather picked up off the ground is a part of a
protected bird, and possessing it is inside the literal prohibition.
Whether anyone will ever bother you about one is a separate question
from whether it is lawful, and the honest answer to the first is
usually no and to the second is no as well. An empty nest is likewise
named in the statute. If you want a feather in your house, buy one from
a species the Act does not cover.

**Marine mammals are protected by a third statute with its own
definition.** Under the Marine Mammal Protection Act, take means to
harass, hunt, capture, or kill, or attempt any of those, and the Act
distinguishes Level A harassment, which injures, from Level B
harassment, which disturbs behaviour patterns including migration,
breathing, nursing, breeding, feeding or sheltering. NOAA Fisheries
administers it for whales, dolphins, porpoises, seals and sea lions;
the Fish and Wildlife Service for walrus, manatees, sea otters and
polar bears.

Level B harassment is the one that catches ordinary people. **Making an
animal change what it was doing is take.** The shipped species data
carries the local distances: NOAA guidance to observe seals and sea
lions from at least 50 yards and to limit observation to 30 minutes or
less (record `harbor_seal`); and for Southern Resident killer whales, a
federal prohibition on approaching within 200 yards or positioning a
vessel in their path within 400 yards, with Washington requiring
boaters to stay 1,000 yards away since 1 January 2025, a state rule
that applies to kayaks and paddleboards as much as to motorboats
(record `killer_whale`). Where two rules apply, the stricter binds.

A seal pup alone on a beach is not abandoned and is not yours to help.
Approaching it is the take.

### The state layer

Washington's protected wildlife rule, WAC 220-200-100, is titled with
its own operative sentence: wildlife classified as protected shall not
be hunted or fished. It has three buckets, threatened, sensitive and
other, and the "other" bucket is enormous. It sweeps in all birds not
classified as game birds, predatory birds or endangered species,
mammals of the orders Cetacea and Pinnipedia, all bats except those in
or near occupied buildings, and named species like the painted turtle
and the California mountain kingsnake.

Washington's predatory birds are only six species. Everything else with
feathers that is not a game bird is protected wildlife, with no season,
no licence and no bag limit, which means no lawful way to take one. The
companion species guide works this through with the Steller's jay and
the Douglas squirrel, and it is worth reading there rather than
repeating here.

**And here is the plant asymmetry again, at state level.** Washington's
rare plant list is maintained by the Natural Heritage Program inside
the Department of Natural Resources, which assigns plants a state
conservation status of Endangered, Threatened, Sensitive or Possibly
Extinct or Extirpated, and tracks nearly 400 plants and nonvascular
species with conservation status, twelve of which are also listed under
the federal Endangered Species Act. The same page states that legal
responsibility for managing fish and wildlife species in the state is
held by the Department of Fish and Wildlife.

Notice what that division implies and be careful about what it does
not prove. The agency that holds the enforcement power over wildlife is
not the agency that holds the rare plant list. I could not open a page
today that states in terms whether Washington generally prohibits
taking a rare plant, so **I am not going to tell you it does or that it
does not.** What I can tell you is that a state Endangered listing for
a plant and a state protected classification for an animal are issued
by different agencies under different authorities and should not be
assumed to carry the same consequences. If you are about to dig
something that appears on the Natural Heritage list, ask the Natural
Heritage Program what the status means before you do, rather than
guessing in either direction.

### Protection you would never guess from rarity

Two of Washington's harvest rules have nothing to do with a species
being scarce, and they bite ordinary people.

**Specialized forest products.** RCW 76.48 sets thresholds above which
picking becomes a regulated activity needing both a specialized forest
products permit and the landowner's permission. More than 20 pounds of
cut or picked evergreen foliage, which the statute defines to include
evergreen boughs, huckleberry foliage, salal, fern, Oregon grape,
rhododendron, mosses and bear grass. More than 5 pounds of cascara
bark. More than 5 gallons of wild edible mushrooms. More than 5
Christmas trees. More than 5 native ornamental trees or shrubs, meaning
ones lifted with the roots intact. Cones, berries and seeds are
excluded from the foliage definition.

None of those species is rare. The law exists because brush picking is
a real industry on this peninsula and the permit system is how
landowners and enforcement tell a picker from a thief. But it means a
cheerful afternoon cutting salal for a wreath business crosses a legal
line at 20 pounds, and it means the 5 gallon mushroom threshold sits
underneath every one of those agency limits in the table above as a
second, independent constraint.

**Forest practices.** Significant timber harvest is regulated under RCW
76.09 and generally needs an approved forest practices application;
cutting a few trees on a residential lot usually falls to county
clearing and critical areas rules instead. The project's law layer
carries this as `wa-commercial-forest-cutting-follows-forest-practices-rules`.
If what you want is a tree rather than what grows on one, this is the
regime you are in.

## Licences, seasons and limits, worked on shellfish

Shellfish is the live case here, because Dyes Inlet is on the doorstep
and because the rules stack in a way that teaches the general shape
better than anything else does.

**Three independent authorities must all say yes, and a fourth question
sits underneath them.** You need a licence. The Department of Fish and
Wildlife must have that specific beach open, within its size and bag
limits. And the Department of Health's biotoxin status for that beach
must be open on the day you go. WDFW puts the first two together
bluntly: to harvest shellfish for human consumption safely and legally,
both the health status and the WDFW season for a public beach must be
open. Underneath all three sits the ownership question from the top of
this guide, because most tideland here is private.

**The biotoxin question belongs to a different guide and you should go
and read it.** [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) covers it
properly, including why cooking and freezing do not help and why a
toxic clam looks, smells and tastes normal. The one thing to carry over
here is that the tide is what makes digging possible and has nothing
whatsoever to do with whether the animal is safe to eat. Two
independent questions. The hotline is 1-800-562-5632 and the answer
expires daily.

**The licence.** Washington requires a licence to fish or shellfish
regardless of residency, and everyone aged 16 and over needs one. A
resident shellfish and seaweed licence is 21.58 dollars; non-resident
47.39; resident senior aged 70 and over, 13.99. The annual combination
licence, which covers freshwater and saltwater fishing as well, is
74.37 for a resident aged 16 to 69, 28.83 for a resident senior, 12.89
for a resident with a disability, and 170.00 for a non-resident. WDFW
states that prices shown include all applicable fees and that buying
multiple items in one transaction may reduce the cost. The step from
shellfish-only to the full combination is 52.79 dollars.

### Why this guide will not restate the limits

The shipped species data at `data/locales/silverdale_wa/species.json`
already carries sourced WDFW limits for every shellfish here, with the
URL each one came from, in the `protected` and `season` fields. The
record ids are:

`manila_clam`, `pacific_littleneck_clam`, `butter_clam`,
`basket_cockle`, `varnish_clam`, `eastern_softshell_clam`,
`fat_gaper_horse_clam`, `geoduck`, `pacific_oyster`, `olympia_oyster`,
`bay_mussel`, `dungeness_crab`, `red_rock_crab`, `spot_shrimp`.

Go there for the numbers rather than trusting the ones in a document
like this, and here is the concrete reason why. The basket cockle's
minimum size was raised to 2.5 inches in 2026, larger than the 1.5 inch
minimum that applies to other clams, and WDFW gave its reasons as heat
sensitivity, harmful algal blooms and a newly identified bivalve
disease. That is a rule that changed inside the last year for
biological reasons that were not known a few years ago. **A limit is a
living number and a document is a fossil.** Any guide that hard-codes a
bag limit into prose is wrong on a schedule.

What is worth learning is the **shape** of the limits, because the shape
is stable even when the numbers move:

**Limits come in incompatible kinds.** A combined limit across many
species, with exclusions: no more than 40 clams and not more than 10
pounds in the shell, excluding horse clams and geoducks. A first-dug
limit, where you keep what comes up whether you like it or not: the
first 1 geoduck, the first 7 horse clams, regardless of size or
condition. A per-species count: 18 oysters. A sex-and-size limit: five
male Dungeness crab at 6.25 inches carapace width, everything soft,
female or undersized released. Four different mental models, all in
force on the same beach on the same afternoon.

**Two caps on the same limit means one of them binds first.** Forty
clams and ten pounds works out to four ounces a clam. If the clams on
your beach average more than four ounces in the shell, **you will hit
ten pounds well before forty clams**, and the count is not the number to
be watching. Work out which cap binds for the animals actually in front
of you rather than counting to the headline number.

**A first-dug limit removes your judgement on purpose.** You do not get
to dig ten geoducks and keep the best one. The first one is yours and
that is the end of the day's geoduck. The rule is written that way
precisely to stop the sorting behaviour, because sorting means digging
up and discarding nine animals that are unlikely to survive being dug.

**Some species have no minimum size, which is not a licence to take
small ones.** Softshell clams and varnish clams have no minimum. The
varnish clam is an established invasive, so the reasoning there is
obvious. The absence of a number is not an endorsement.

**And some limits are pure conservation and pure closure.** The Olympia
oyster, Washington's only native oyster, is not open for recreational
or commercial harvest of wild stock, and the record `olympia_oyster`
notes that by 2012 only about 5 percent of its historical bed habitat
remained. The 2.5 inch minimum for oysters does most of the practical
protecting, because almost no Olympia oyster ever reaches it. Which
gives you the one rule worth memorising for this beach: **if an oyster
is under 2.5 inches, leave it, whichever species you think it is.**

**Where the data admits it does not know, it says so, and so should
you.** The `bay_mussel` record states outright that the daily limit and
minimum size for mussels could not be confirmed from a citable WDFW
page, and tells you to look it up in the current sport fishing rules
rather than assume it matches the 40 clam limit. That is the correct
behaviour from a dataset and it is also the correct behaviour from you.

### The rules that are about the beach rather than the animal

Buried in the shellfish rules is a second category people miss
entirely, because they scan for numbers and these have none. They are
not bag limits. They are instructions about leaving the habitat
functional.

**Refill your holes.** WDFW requires clam harvesters to refill the
holes they dig, and advises pushing undersized or discarded clams into
the refilled hole to reduce mortality. A clam left lying on the surface
of a gravel flat does not walk back down. It dies, or a gull has it.
The hole itself is also a problem: it changes drainage and it buries
the neighbours.

**Shuck oysters on the beach and leave the shells.** This one has no
equivalent in the clam rules and people break it without realising. The
WDFW rule, as carried in the shipped record `pacific_oyster` with its
source URL, is that oysters must be shucked on the beach and the shells
left on the same tideland at the same tide height where they were
taken. The reason the same record gives is structural: the shell is the
hard surface the next generation settles on, so removing it removes the
reef. **The shell is the reef.** Carry a bucket of shells home and you have
not just taken the oysters, you have removed the surface the next
generation needed, from the exact tide height where it needed to be.
WDFW's March 2026 season release repeated it: leave oyster shells on
the beach and refill dig holes, to avoid damaging habitat and killing
clams.

**Oysters you eat on the beach count against your limit.** Obvious once
stated, routinely ignored.

**Each harvester needs their own container and must actually be
harvesting.** The point of that rule is to stop one person filling
several limits under other people's licences.

**Spread out.** WDFW's own advice is to visit less popular beaches to
spread harvest pressure, and separately not to park unsafely or
illegally along busy roadways, which is how a good beach becomes a
closed one.

## Harvest that does not destroy the population

Now the part where honesty is harder, because the law runs out and the
science is uneven. Some of what follows is well established. Some of it
is reasoning from mechanism. Some of it is custom wearing a lab coat,
and I will say which is which.

### What part you take changes everything

The single most useful distinction in harvesting is not which species
it is. It is **which part of the individual you are taking, and what
that part was for.**

**Fruit is the plant's reproductive surplus, and taking it is usually
cheap.** A berry exists to be eaten; that is the entire strategy. The
plant has already spent the energy, the seeds are already made, and
being eaten is how they travel. Taking fruit does not remove the
individual, does not remove next year's growing points, and in the
normal case costs the plant the dispersal it wanted rather than the
plant itself. This is why fruits, berries and nuts are the category
that even the national parks rule is willing to designate for
gathering: 36 CFR 2.1 names precisely "fruits, berries, nuts, or
unoccupied seashells" and nothing else.

That said, the fruit was not only for you. On this peninsula, berries
are a food supply for birds and mammals at the exact moment they need
to put on mass. Taking all of a small patch is not a plant conservation
problem; it is an ecosystem one, which
[How an Ecosystem Holds Together](/library#how-an-ecosystem-holds-together)
treats properly.

**A leaf is a cost the plant actually pays.** Leaves are the
photosynthetic machinery. Stripping them removes the plant's ability to
make the sugar it needs to refill its roots before winter, and the cost
compounds if you come back to the same individual repeatedly in a
season. A plant can lose some leaf area routinely, because being
browsed is normal; it cannot lose most of it repeatedly without
drawing down reserves it will need.

**A root or a bulb is the whole individual, and that is not a
metaphor.** This is where harvesting stops being a tax and becomes a
removal. The clearest sourced statement of it comes from the Fish and
Wildlife Service on American ginseng, whose harvest is regulated
precisely because of this: "The harvest of a ginseng root kills the
plant, that is why it's important never to harvest underage or
undersize plants."

Ginseng is worth understanding even though it does not grow here,
because it is the best-documented case in the country of a root harvest
that needed rules. It was placed in Appendix II of CITES in 1975, so an
export permit is required. All wild and wild-simulated roots for export
must come from plants five years of age or older, which is read off the
rhizome as four or more stem scars; most states that permit harvest
require five years and three compound leaves, called prongs. In all 19
states that allow it, the season starts in September, which is when
seeds are ripe and can be planted back. And the international shipment
must not be harmful to the survival of the species.

Look at what that rule set is doing. It is not limiting how many roots
you take. It is **requiring that every individual you kill has already
reproduced several times**, and timing the season so the seeds go back
in the ground. That is a fundamentally different design from a bag
limit, and it is the design that root harvests need. Locally, the same
logic applies to anything taken as a bulb or a tuber, common camas
being the obvious one here.

**Bark is often fatal, and the geometry is the whole story.** Bark
carries the living tissue that moves sugar from the leaves down to the
roots. Take a strip and the tree routes around it. Take a complete ring
and there is no route.

Be careful about what is cited here and what is not. I could not open a
primary federal page today stating that mechanism in terms; the Forest
Service documents on girdling that I tried are scanned or encoded and
would not render as text. **Treat the mechanism as reported rather than
cited by me.**

What I **can** cite is the other side of it. Forest Service research on
bark-peeled ponderosa pines in the Bitterroot Mountains dated the
peelings from the mid-1600s until the early 1900s, found the scars on
living as well as dead trees, and dated them by dendrochronology
precisely enough to read off who was there and when. The study
concludes those trees should be recognised as ecologically and
culturally valuable features of old pine forests.

Note carefully what that does and does not establish. It establishes
that partially peeled trees carry dateable scars and are found alive,
over a span of roughly 300 years of harvesting. It does not, by itself,
quantify how often a peeled tree dies, and the study I opened does not
address that. So the defensible statement is the narrow one: **partial
bark removal leaves trees that go on living, and the geometry is what
differs between taking a strip and taking a ring.** Washington has
already put a number on the commercial end of it: more than five pounds
of cascara bark makes you a specialized forest products harvester
needing a permit and the landowner's permission.

### Perennial and annual are not the same animal

An annual plant lives one season and reproduces once. Take it before it
seeds and you have removed it and its entire contribution to next
year's population in one act. Take it after it has seeded and you have
taken a corpse.

A perennial carries over. Its reserves are in a root, a rhizome, a bulb
or a woody frame, and the harvest question becomes whether you took a
part it can rebuild from stored energy or the store itself. That is why
the part-taken distinction above matters more for perennials, and why
the timing question matters more for annuals.

This is mechanism, not a cited finding, and it is the kind of reasoning
that should change what you do rather than being a fact you memorise.
Before taking anything, ask: does this individual get another season,
and did I just take the thing it needed to have one?

### Why the patch tells you nothing about the recovery

Here is the problem that makes foraging harder than it looks, and no
amount of care on the day solves it.

**A patch that regrows in one year and a patch that regrows in twenty
look identical on the day you harvest.** They are both green, both
abundant, both apparently fine. The difference is in growth rate and
reproductive age, which are invisible. You get exactly one observation,
at one moment, and the variable you need is a rate.

Which means the feedback loop that would normally teach you is broken.
If you overharvest a fast-recovering patch, next year looks fine and
you learn that you did no harm. If you overharvest a slow one, next
year also looks roughly fine, because slow decline over twenty years is
not visible year to year to a person who visits a few times a season.
**You will get the same signal in both cases, and the signal will be
reassuring in both cases.** The slow patch is the one you have to reason
about rather than observe, and reasoning means knowing how old the
individual has to be before it reproduces. Ginseng's five years is the
worked example: a rule that exists because the observation on the day
cannot tell you.

### Taking the biggest, and what I could not verify

A thing that gets repeated, correctly in spirit, is that consistently
taking the largest individuals selects against the population over
generations, because the largest are disproportionately the
reproducers and because size is heritable. The paper most often cited
for it in plants is titled "Human-induced dwarfing of Himalayan snow
lotus", which tells you its conclusion from the title alone.

**I could not open that paper today.** The journal returned 403, PubMed
returned a cookie wall, and the full text on PMC returned a security
check. I do not restate a source I could not read, so: the claim
exists, I could not verify it, and you should not take the numbers from
me.

What I **can** show you is that the regulators behave as though the
underlying concern is real. That is weaker evidence, but it is
checkable. Almost every harvest rule quoted in this guide is structured
to protect individuals that have not yet reproduced, rather than to cap
the total take:

- Shellfish minimum sizes, so a clam spawns before it is legal.
- Ginseng's five-year, four-stem-scar requirement for export.
- The oyster minimum of 2.5 inches, which incidentally shelters the
  entire Olympia oyster population.
- The cockle minimum raised to 2.5 inches in 2026 for stated
  sustainability reasons.

Notice the direction. Every one of those says take the **older and
larger** ones, which is the opposite of what selection pressure would
recommend and the same as what population maintenance requires: leave
the juveniles until they have bred. Both concerns point at the same
practical behaviour for a harvester, which is to take the middle and
leave both ends, and to leave the exceptional individual alone. I am
labelling that as reasoning and as what the rules imply, not as a
finding I sourced today.

### The rules of thumb, and which of them are just custom

Now the direct answer to the question this section exists for.

You will read, in foraging books, on blogs and in the mouths of
otherwise careful people, that you should take no more than one third
of what is present. You will also read one in ten. You will also read
five percent. Sometimes the third is explained as one for you, one for
the animals, one for the plant.

**I went looking for a published basis and did not find one.** The rule
appears in foraging guides and blog literature. I could not find it
published by any wildlife agency, land management agency or peer
reviewed source that I was able to open. So here is the honest label:
**the one-third rule is custom, not a finding.** Presenting it as though
it were an ecological law is the kind of error this project treats as a
defect, because a reader who goes looking for the study will not find
it and may throw out the good instinct along with the fake authority.

There are three structural reasons to be suspicious of it beyond the
missing citation, and they are worth understanding because they
generalise.

**The numbers do not agree with each other.** One third and one in ten
differ by a factor of three, and five percent by a factor of six from
the third. A genuine finding does not come in three mutually
incompatible versions that nobody reconciles.

**The denominator is never specified.** A third of what? Of the fruit on
one plant? Of the plants in the patch you can see? Of the population,
which you cannot see and could not count? Those are wildly different
quantities and the rule is silent on which one it means, which is a
reliable sign that it was never derived from anything.

**It ignores the part-taken distinction entirely.** A third of the
berries and a third of the bulbs are not remotely the same act. The
first costs a population some dispersal. The second kills a third of
the individuals. Any rule stated as a single fraction, applied
identically to fruit, leaf, root and bark, is guaranteed to be either
far too cautious for one of them or catastrophic for another.

**So what should you do instead?** In order:

1. **Use the published limit where one exists.** In the great majority
   of situations near Silverdale, one does: an agency limit for the
   land you are on, a bag limit for the species, or both. The published
   limit is the answer and your rule of thumb is not needed.
2. **Where no limit is published, say so out loud and act on the
   part-taken logic.** Fruit, generous. Leaf, moderate and not from the
   same individual repeatedly. Root, bulb or bark, treat every one as
   a death and ask whether that individual had already reproduced.
3. **Take the middle and leave the extremes.** Leave the small because
   they have not bred. Leave the exceptional because it is the best
   breeder present.
4. **Move.** The single most effective conservation act available to a
   forager is not taking a smaller fraction of one patch. It is walking
   further and spreading the take across many. This is the same advice
   WDFW gives shellfish harvesters, which is to visit less popular
   beaches to spread out the pressure.
5. **And keep the honest version of the custom, which is worth
   keeping.** As a brake on human appetite, "don't clean it out" is
   good and probably ancient. It is a discipline, not a measurement.
   Use it as one.

## Mushrooms are a special case

They are, but not quite for the reason people say.

**The popular claim, stated accurately.** The National Park Service's
page for Mount Rainier puts it this way: "Picking a mushroom is similar
to picking a berry or apple in that the larger organism is usually not
harmed."

That is the claim, from a federal source, and note the word the source
itself chose: **usually.** The reasoning behind it is sound and worth
understanding. The mushroom you pick is a fruiting body. The organism
is the mycelium, a network living in the soil or the wood, often
considerably older and larger than the visible mushroom and
unaffected by your knife. In that respect a chanterelle really is more
like an apple than like a carrot, and picking it is closer to harvesting
fruit than to killing an individual. On the four-part scheme above,
mushrooms are in the cheap category.

**What I could not establish.** The evidence usually cited for this is a
long-term Swiss study of mushroom harvesting. I could not open it
today: the journal page returned 403, the institutional repository
would not render, and the abstract services refused or failed. I do not
restate a paper I could not read, so I am not going to tell you what it
tested, how long it ran or what it found, and you should not take any
of that from me. What I have is the Park Service's sentence, which is
sourced, hedged by its own author, and enough to act on.

**What follows from the hedge.** "Usually not harmed" is not "never
harmed", and the practical reading is that the act of picking is cheap
while the **way** you go about it may not be. Two regulators at two
different levels of government both wrote that into their rules, and
neither of them capped the quantity to do it. The federal parks rule,
36 CFR 2.1, permits designated gathering only "by hand", which is a
prohibition on tools rather than on amounts. Washington's state parks
rule, WAC 352-28-030, prohibits harvest techniques involving raking or
anything else with the potential to degrade park resources, while
allowing a generous two gallons a day. **Both of them restricted the
method and left the quantity alone**, which tells you which of the two
they think does the damage.

So: pick or cut the mushroom, whichever you prefer, and **do not rake
the duff to find them.** The visible mushroom is cheap. The forest floor
it came out of is not, which brings us to the last section.

**And the national forest rules exist and are specific.** Personal use
mushroom harvest on Olympic National Forest needs no permit and is
capped at one gallon per species per year, with selling and trading
prohibited. Commercial permits are separate and priced by volume: 50
dollars for 50 gallons on a 14-day permit, 80 dollars for 80 gallons on
a 30-day permit, and 120 dollars for 120 gallons for a calendar year. A
commercial use permit is required for anyone intending to sell, or
harvesting more than the incidental use allowance.

Underneath all of that sits the state threshold: more than five US
gallons of wild edible mushrooms is a specialized forest product under
RCW 76.48 regardless of whose land it came from, requiring a permit and
the landowner's permission. On Olympic National Forest the federal
personal-use cap of one gallon per species per year is the binding
constraint long before the state threshold is reached; on DNR land,
where nine gallons a year is allowed, the two regimes are closer
together than they look. Check both.

## What you disturb besides the thing you take

The take is the part you notice. It is frequently not the part that
does the damage.

**Trampling and compaction.** Feet compress soil. Compacted soil takes
in less water, which means less moisture stored, fewer seeds
germinating and fewer seedlings surviving, and more of the rain running
off the surface instead, which erodes. The mechanism is slow, it is
cumulative, and it is invisible on any single visit.

What it looks like when it has run long enough is documented. The Park
Service's account of a half-mile visitor-created trail at Cuyahoga
Valley states that increased traffic there "has resulted in visible soil
compaction, trampling of vegetation, soil erosion, and potentially even
the spread of invasive plant species", and that "Large sections of the
streamside plant community are now devoid of vegetation and rare plants
have not been re-located."

Read that last clause again. **The rare plants are not there any more,
and nobody harvested them.** They were walked on. If you return to a
productive spot repeatedly, you are building a trail whether you
intended to or not, and your approach route will do more damage than
your basket.

The practical answers are dull and they work: vary your route in, do
not walk the same line twice, stay on the durable surface where one
exists, and accept that the fastest way to a patch is usually the way
that kills it.

**The intertidal, where everything you move is somebody's house.** A
rock on a gravel beach is a habitat with two sides, and the underside
is the good one: it is dark, damp at low tide, and sheltered. The
Olympic Coast National Marine Sanctuary's tidepooling guidance is
direct about all of it, and being federal it can be quoted:

> If you want to peek under a rock, put it back the way you found it.

> Never force an animal off its spot, you may tear off its feet, or
> squeeze its organs.

> Find footholds on bare rock, they are not as slippery and you will
> avoid stepping on the animals and plants.

> Intertidal animals should not be collected for bait.

> Always obey fish and wildlife laws with respect to seasons, bag
> limits, and sexes of animals taken for food.

"Put it back the way you found it" is the whole rule and it is
routinely half-done. A rock rolled aside and left with its underside up
kills the community that was underneath, by sun and by drying, and kills
the one that was on top, by burial. Turning it back over is not
tidiness; it is the difference between looking and destroying. And
"never force an animal off its spot" has a specific target: a limpet or
a chiton prised off a rock usually does not reattach.

The same logic drives the clam-hole rule from earlier. Refill the hole,
and put the undersized animals back into it rather than leaving them on
the surface.

**Invasive seed, which travels on you.** The Park Service is specific
about the mechanism: "Seeds can readily become lodged in our boots and
stuck to our clothing." Equipment "can also house myriad plant parts,
insect and aquatic species eggs and larvae, and microscopic critters."
Vehicles carry "seeds and plant fragments becoming attached to dirty
cars, boats, and trailers, or insects attaching their sticky egg masses
to their surfaces." And firewood carries "wood-boring insects, insect
eggs, and fungi".

This is the damage most foragers never account for, because it happens
at the next site rather than this one, and because a forager is
precisely the person most likely to walk from a disturbed roadside
verge into intact forest. **You are the vector, and you are going in
the worst possible direction.** Knock your boots off and brush the seeds
out before you leave a weedy site, not after you arrive at a clean one.
Wash the vehicle after driving unpaved roads. Do not move firewood.

Locally this connects to something the species guide raises: Himalayan
blackberry is a designated noxious weed here, which means stands along
roads, ditches and utility corridors may have been sprayed. Those same
corridors are where you pick up the seed you then carry inland.

**Litter and the beach you close behind you.** Washington's litter law
scales penalties by volume, from a civil infraction for a cubic foot or
less up to gross misdemeanour, with cleanup costs on top; the project's
law layer carries it as
`wa-littering-is-illegal-with-penalties-scaled-to-the-amount`. More
practically, WDFW's own advice about parking includes not parking
unsafely or illegally along busy roadways, and the reason that advice
exists is that access to good beaches gets withdrawn over parking and
rubbish far more often than over shellfish.

## What the shipped law data does and does not have

This guide's declared data layer is `data/laws/laws.json`. Read its own
disclaimer before you lean on it: it says in terms that it is a worked
example rather than the law, that entries may be incomplete, out of
date or simply wrong, that most have never been checked by a person,
and that you should never act on it without reading the cited source.
That is the right posture and it is also a limit you need to know
about.

**What it has that is relevant here.** Five entries carry real weight
for a harvester:

- `wa-criminal-trespass`, the permission-is-the-default rule, citing
  RCW 9A.52.070 and 9A.52.080.
- `usa-don-t-harm-protected-species-or-their-habitat`, the Endangered
  Species Act entry, which correctly notes the take prohibition
  includes harm, harassment and significant habitat degradation, and
  that it applies on private land.
- `wa-commercial-forest-cutting-follows-forest-practices-rules`, the
  RCW 76.09 entry, for anything at the scale of trees.
- `kitsap-kitsap-requires-buffers-around-wetlands-and-streams` and
  `wa-critical-areas-on-your-property-are-protected-wetlands-strea`,
  which between them tell you that the wet ground and stream margins
  where a lot of the good plants are carry clearing restrictions even
  on land you own.

Also relevant at the margins:
`wa-shoreline-work-needs-a-permit-shoreline-management-act`,
`kitsap-clearing-grading-and-filling-land-needs-a-county-permit`,
`usa-filling-wetlands-or-waters-needs-a-federal-clean-water-act-p`,
and `kitsap-gatherings-and-events-in-county-parks-need-permission`,
which is the only entry in the whole file that cites the Kitsap park
code.

**What it does not have, and this is a real gap between the
curriculum's promise and the data.** The topic `forage_ethics` promises
to teach the reader to "know what is protected or illegal to take", and
declares `data/laws/laws.json` as its data layer. Of the 170 rules in
that file, **not one covers harvesting.** Specifically, the file has
nothing on:

- **Hunting, fishing or shellfish licences.** There is no rule in the
  file about needing a licence to take anything wild. There is a rule
  about licensing your dog.
- **Seasons, bag limits or size limits** of any kind.
- **The Migratory Bird Treaty Act.** The single most commonly violated
  wildlife statute in the country, and the one that surprises people
  most because it covers feathers and nests, does not appear.
- **The Marine Mammal Protection Act**, on a county where marine
  mammals are visible from the shore most weeks of the year.
- **Tribal treaty rights.** Nothing. No treaty, no usual and accustomed
  areas, no co-management, no Rafeedie. This is the largest omission in
  the file for this county, because it is the framework that determines
  who may take shellfish from every beach in it.
- **Tideland ownership and the private-beach problem.** The file has
  fourteen entries under "Environment and land" and none of them
  mention tidelands.
- **State protected wildlife.** WAC 220-200-100, the rule that makes
  nearly every native bird untakeable, is absent, as is any WDFW
  listing concept.
- **RCW 76.48 specialized forest products**, the permit thresholds that
  turn brush picking into a regulated activity.
- **Public land access categories.** Nothing on national parks, national
  forests, state parks, DNR land, the Discover Pass, or the fact that
  the rules differ between them.

The category structure is not the problem; "Environment and land" is
the obvious home for most of it. The content is simply not there. I am
reporting this rather than fixing it, per the writing rules for this
Library, so that the fix gets its own commit and its own reasoning.

**A smaller observation about the species data**, which is much better
populated. In `data/locales/silverdale_wa/species.json`, every mammal,
fish and shellfish record carries a populated `protected` field, and 45
of the 66 plant records carry `null`. The companion species guide
already documents what that null means, correctly, as "no protection
was found, which is not the same as verified unprotected". So this is a
known and honestly labelled gap rather than a defect. It is worth
flagging here only because **a guide about what you may legally take is
exactly the reader who will hit it**, and because the asymmetry is real
and not merely an artefact of effort: as this guide's protection
section sets out, plants genuinely are regulated by different agencies
under different authorities than animals are, at both federal and state
level. The empty field is partly a reflection of a genuinely thinner
legal layer, which is itself the thing worth teaching.

## You own this when

- You ask "whose land is this" **before** you ask "what is this", and
  you can name the five public land categories near Silverdale and say
  that their harvest rules are different without needing to remember
  which is which.
- You know that most Washington tideland is private, that a private
  beach looks exactly like a public one, and that whether you may even
  stand on one is an unsettled question in Washington law.
- You can state what the Treaty of Point Elliott reserved, in 1855, and
  you understand that treaty shellfish harvest reaches private tidelands
  and that the state manages half a jointly managed resource.
- You know that "take" under the Endangered Species Act includes harm
  and harassment and attempts, that the Act protects listed plants far
  more narrowly than listed animals, and that the Migratory Bird Treaty
  Act reaches parts, nests and eggs, which means feathers.
- You look up a bag limit in the current rules rather than repeating one
  you read somewhere, and you can say why: the cockle minimum changed
  in 2026.
- You can say which cap binds first when a limit has two, and you
  understand what a first-dug limit is for.
- You refill the hole, put the small ones back in it, shuck the oysters
  on the beach and leave the shells at the same tide height.
- You can say, without hedging, that "leave a third" is custom rather
  than a published finding, and you can give the three structural
  reasons why a single fraction cannot be right for fruit, leaf, root
  and bark alike.
- You treat a root or a bulb as the death of an individual, and you ask
  whether that individual had already reproduced.
- You put the rock back the way you found it, every time, including the
  time when nobody is watching and you are already late.
- You knock the seeds off your boots at the weedy roadside rather than
  at the trailhead of somewhere intact.

### Could not be sourced, and what was done instead

- **The long-term Swiss mushroom harvesting study**, by Egli and
  colleagues. The publisher page returned 403, the institutional
  repository would not render, the abstract service returned 405 and the
  aggregator returned 403. Nothing about its design, duration or results
  is stated in this guide, because I could not read any of it. The guide
  rests instead on the National Park Service's own sentence, which is
  sourced and carries its own hedge.
- **Human-induced dwarfing of Himalayan snow lotus** (Law and Salick,
  reported in PNAS 2005), the paper most often cited for size-selective
  harvest driving change in a plant population. The journal returned
  403, PubMed returned a cookie wall and PMC returned a security check.
  Only its title is used in the text, and the text says so; no numbers
  or methods are given. The
  argument is carried instead by the observable structure of the
  regulations, which is checkable.
- **The mechanism of girdling.** The Forest Service publications on
  girdling that were opened are scanned or use embedded subset fonts and
  would not render as text. The claim that a complete ring of bark
  removal kills a tree is therefore presented in the text as reported
  rather than cited. The sourced fact used in its place is the Bitterroot
  bark-peel study, which dates peelings across roughly 300 years and
  finds the scars on living as well as dead trees. The text says
  explicitly that this establishes that partially peeled trees go on
  living and does not quantify how often one dies.
- **Kitsap County's Mushroom Harvest Policies.** The 2014 county
  resolution PDF and the stewardship plan that quotes it would not
  render as readable text. The existence of the policy is sourced to the
  county parks rules page, which references it; its contents are not
  stated here, and the guide gives the county parks telephone number
  instead.
- **Washington seaweed harvest rules.** The detailed seaweed rules that
  appear in search results trace to a WDFW pamphlet from 2017-18, whose
  PDF would not render as text, and the current shellfishing regulations
  page is an index that does not carry them. **No seaweed rule of any
  kind is stated in this guide as a result**, including the daily limit,
  the tool restrictions and the state park closures. Seaweed harvest
  needs the same shellfish and seaweed licence, which is sourced above;
  everything else about it you must look up in the current sport fishing
  rules.
- **The "leave a third" rule and its relatives.** Searched for
  deliberately and not found in any agency publication or peer-reviewed
  source that would open. Present in foraging guides and blog
  literature, in at least three mutually incompatible versions. Labelled
  in the text as custom rather than a finding, with the reasons.
