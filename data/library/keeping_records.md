# Keeping Records

Every spring, people who grow food stand in front of a bed and try to
remember what they planted there last year, which variety did well, when
the first frost came, and how many jars of tomatoes were left over. The
memory feels clear. Whether it is right in the details, nobody can
check, and the details are what the decision needs.

A record is how you stop asking your memory to do a notebook's job. It
does not have to be elaborate. It has to be written at the time, in
numbers rather than impressions, in a place you will find again, and it
has to be read back when the next decision comes round.

The US Army's land navigation manual makes the point about something as
small as counting your paces: do not try to remember the count; move a
pebble, tie a knot, or put a mark in a notebook. If a soldier walking a
compass bearing cannot trust memory for a count of a few hundred, none
of us can trust it for a growing season.

This guide takes its rules from places where records are taken
seriously because a lot rides on them: the federal produce safety and
organic rules for farms, the National Institutes of Health guidance for
research notebooks, the National Weather Service's instructions to its
volunteer weather observers, aviation maintenance records, and the IRS.
All of them are United States government publications and in the public
domain. None of them is written for a home garden, which is exactly why
they are useful: they show what a record needs when somebody else has to
be able to trust it, and that somebody is often you, a year from now.

## What a record is for

Decide this before you start, because it decides what you write.

- **To repeat what worked.** Which tomato, sown on what date, in which
  bed, gave the harvest you want again.
- **To stop repeating what did not.** The variety that split, the bed
  that waterlogged, the batch of sauerkraut that went soft.
- **To see slow change.** Your own first frost date, the level of a
  well, the yield of a bed over five years. Slow changes are invisible
  day to day and obvious in a column of numbers.
- **To find faults.** A record of what changed and when is often where
  a diagnosis starts; see [Working Out Why Something
  Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke).
- **To prove something.** A sale, a tax deduction, a claim that produce
  was grown a certain way.
- **To hand over.** If you are ill, away or gone, somebody else carries
  on from what you wrote down.

The National Institutes of Health puts the last one as the governing
principle for research records: another person should be able to
reproduce or reconstruct your work from your records, and if you leave
before the work is done, somebody else should be able to continue it by
reading them. It is a good test for any record. Could a neighbour run
your garden next spring from your notebook?

## What goes in an entry

Two federal sources list what an entry needs, and between them they
make a good checklist.

The **Produce Safety Rule** (21 CFR 112.161), written for farms that
grow fruit and vegetables for sale, says each required record must
include, as applicable:

- the name and location of the farm;
- the actual values and observations obtained during monitoring;
- an adequate description of the produce, such as the commodity name,
  the specific variety and any lot number;
- the location of the growing area, such as a specific field;
- the date and time of the activity.

It also says the record must be created at the time the activity is
performed or observed; be accurate, legible and indelible; and be dated,
and signed or initialed by the person who did the work.

The **NIH research guidelines** say a record should describe who did the
work, what was done, when, why, as part of what project, how, with what
materials, what was found, what it means, and what comes next.

The rule does not apply to a home garden: it excludes produce grown by
an individual for personal consumption. You are not obliged to keep any
of this. But the two lists boil down to an entry you can write in a
minute, and it is worth adopting whole.

**A garden entry, built from those lists:**

| Field | Example |
|---|---|
| Date and time | 2027-04-18, 7:30 am |
| Where | Bed 3, north end |
| What | Sowed lettuce, 'Buttercrunch', seed packet lot 4471, bought 2026 |
| How much | One 3 m row, seeds about 2 cm apart |
| Conditions | Soil damp; overnight low 4 C on the thermometer by the shed |
| Who | AB |
| Next | Check for emergence from 2027-04-25 |

Then, later, entries that close the loop: the date the first seedlings
showed, how many came up, the date of first harvest, what the harvest
weighed, and what went wrong.

## Write numbers, not adjectives

The Produce Safety Rule asks for "actual values and observations", and
the phrase is worth taking literally. "Watered well" is an opinion.
"Watered 20 litres, soil damp 10 cm down" is a record. "Good harvest" is
a mood. "3.4 kg" is something you can compare with next year.

Always write the unit. "Watered 20" could be litres, gallons or minutes,
and in a year you will not know which. [Units and Converting
Them](/library#units-and-converting-them) is about what happens when a number
loses its unit on the way from one person to another, and you a year
from now are another person.

If you estimated rather than measured, say so: "about 30 m, paced". An
estimate written down as if it were a measurement is a quiet lie to your
future self. [Estimating](/library#estimating) explains how to give the
estimate an honest range.

## Zero, trace and missing are three different things

The National Weather Service's network of volunteer weather observers,
the Cooperative Observer Program, was formally created in 1890, and its
instructions to observers contain the best single lesson in record
keeping there is. For daily rainfall, recorded to the hundredth of an
inch:

- **If no rain fell, write 0.00.**
- **If too little fell to measure, write T, for trace.**
- **If you could not take the reading, write M, for missing.** When you
  come back and the gauge holds several days' rain, enter the total on
  the day you read it and add a remark saying how many days it covers,
  such as "3-day precip total".

A blank cell could mean any of those three. A record full of blanks
cannot tell a dry week from a week you were away, and an average worked
out from it will be wrong without anyone noticing. So never leave a
reading blank: write the zero, the trace, or the M, and say why.

## Write it at the time

The Produce Safety Rule's requirement that a record be "created at the
time an activity is performed or observed" has a plain reason behind it:
an entry written later is written from memory, and memory is the thing a
record is there to replace. Write the entry while you are standing in
the garden, or within the same day at worst. A notebook in a coat
pocket, or a photo of the bed with a note, beats a careful write-up
three days later.

## Same time, same way

The Weather Service asks its observers to read their instruments at the
same time each day, seven days a week, for consistency. The reason
applies to anything you measure repeatedly: a reading taken at 7 am and
one taken at 3 pm are not measuring the same day, and the difference
between them is partly the clock.

So for anything you track:

- **Read it at the same time.** A rain gauge every morning, a water
  meter on the first of the month, the hens' eggs every evening.
- **Read it the same way.** The same gauge in the same spot, the same
  scale, the same method.
- **When you change how you measure, write it down.** "From 2027-06-01,
  rain gauge moved away from the hedge" explains a jump that would
  otherwise look like a change in the weather.

## Corrections: cross out, never erase

The NIH guidelines ask that records be kept so that new entries or
corrections can be identified and validated, and the Produce Safety Rule
asks for records that are indelible. Both point at the same habit: when
you find a mistake, draw a single line through it so the original can
still be read, write the correct value beside it, and initial and date
the change. That is our reading of the two rules rather than a
quotation, but the reason is plain: a record that can be silently
changed cannot be trusted, even by the person who keeps it.

This is also why ink beats pencil for anything that matters. If you are
working in the rain with a pencil, copy the entry over in ink the same
day.

**Spreadsheets.** The NIH guidelines warn that spreadsheets usually have
no way to show what was changed and when. Their suggestions, which work
at home too: name files with the version date and the author, keep the
old versions rather than overwriting them, use a change-tracking feature
if there is one, and lock a sheet once a season is finished.

## Write dates so they sort and cannot be misread

"03/04" is the 4th of March in the United States and the 3rd of April in
much of the rest of the world. Write dates year first, then month, then
day: 2027-03-04. Written that way a date cannot be misread, and a list
of dates, or of files named by date, sorts itself into time order. This
is the order the international date standard uses; the advice to adopt
it is general practice rather than a rule from any source here.

## Labels are records too

A label is a record that travels with the thing it describes.

- **Jars.** The USDA's Complete Guide to Home Canning says to label and
  date jars before storing them, and to can no more food than you will
  use within a year. A label with the contents, the date and the batch
  lets you eat the oldest first and find every jar from a batch if one
  goes wrong. If your notebook also records the recipe and process time
  for that batch, the label is the key to it.
- **Seeds.** [Saving Your Own Seeds](/library#saving-your-own-seeds) asks for
  the crop, the variety and the date on every container. Its tables show
  saved seed lasting from one or two years for some crops to five for
  others, so a jar with no date on it is a gamble.
- **Anything you mix.** A sprayer, a bucket of fertiliser solution, a
  jug of fuel mixed for a two-stroke engine: what is in it and when it
  was made.

## A maintenance log for every machine

Aviation has a federal rule for maintenance records, and it is short.
Federal aviation regulation 14 CFR 43.9, "Content, form, and disposition
of maintenance, preventive maintenance, rebuilding, and alteration
records", requires each maintenance entry to contain a description of
the work, the date it was completed, the name of whoever did it (if not
the approver), and the approver's signature with their certificate number
and kind of certificate.

That is the right shape for a homestead log too, kept for each pump,
generator, mower, chainsaw, vehicle and stove:

- the date and the machine's hours or mileage, if it has a meter;
- what was done, and what was found (a worn part, a leak, a loose
  belt);
- parts used, with their part numbers;
- who did it.

The US Navy's electronics technicians' handbook ends its troubleshooting
procedure with the same instruction: once a fault is fixed, record the
necessary information in a recordkeeping book for other maintenance
people in the future. A log like that turns the next breakdown from a
mystery into a lookup: "the last time it did this, it was the fuel
filter, at 410 hours". See [Working Out Why Something
Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke).

## Meter readings: turning a running total into information

A water meter, an electricity meter and a vehicle odometer each show a
running total. The total on its own tells you almost nothing. Two
readings and a subtraction tell you a great deal.

The EPA's WaterSense programme gives two worked uses:

- **A baseline.** Look at your water use in a cold month such as
  January or February, when nobody is watering the garden. The EPA says
  that if a family of four uses more than 12,000 gallons in such a
  month, there could be serious leaks. You can only make that
  comparison if you know what you used.
- **A test.** Read the meter, use no water for two hours, and read it
  again. If it changed at all, the EPA says you probably have a leak.

Read your meters on the same day each month, write the readings in a
column, and subtract. The differences are your usage, and a month that
stands out is a question worth asking.

## A list of medicines

One record can matter more than all the others in an emergency. The FDA
recommends that everyone keep a medication list covering prescription
and non-prescription medicines, vitamins and supplements. It says the
list should include emergency contacts and allergies, and for each
medicine its name, its strength, what it is taken for, and when, how and
how much is taken.

The FDA's further advice:

- review and update the list whenever something changes, such as a new
  prescription, a different dose, or a medicine stopped;
- keep a copy with you, for example as a photo on your phone or a paper
  copy with your health insurance card;
- share it with a trusted relative, friend or caregiver who might speak
  for you in an emergency.

The FDA notes the list can help paramedics and hospital staff if you
cannot speak for yourself. Write the strengths exactly as they are on
the label, units and all.

## How long to keep things

Some records have legal minimums. These are United States examples; if
you live elsewhere, your own rules will differ.

- **Taxes.** The IRS says to keep records that support an item on a tax
  return until the period of limitations for that return runs out:
  generally 3 years, 4 years for employment tax records, 6 years if
  income was under-reported by more than 25 percent, 7 years for a claim
  for a loss from worthless securities or a bad debt deduction, and
  indefinitely if no return was filed. Records about property are kept
  until the period of limitations runs out for the year you dispose of
  it.
- **Farms selling produce under the Produce Safety Rule** keep their
  required records for at least 2 years after they were made.
- **Certified organic operations** keep their records for not less than
  5 years. The organic rule also asks that records be adapted to the
  particular business and disclose all activities and transactions in
  enough detail to be readily understood and audited, which is as good a
  definition of a useful record as any.

Garden notebooks, maintenance logs and weather records have no legal
minimum, and they cost almost nothing to keep. Keep them for good. Their
value grows with every year you add, because the slow changes only
show up over many years. The Weather Service makes the point about its
own volunteers: partly because the cooperative network has run for many
decades with relatively stable methods (it also names its high station
density and its high share of rural locations), it is recognised as the
most definitive source on United States climate trends in temperature
and precipitation. Its page
on the programme also records that Thomas Jefferson kept an almost
unbroken record of the weather from 1776 to 1816. Nobody keeping a
weather notebook in 1776 knew what it would be used for, which is the
point.

## Keep a copy somewhere else

A record kept in one place is lost with that place. Ready.gov, the
federal government's preparedness site, advises keeping important
documents in a safe deposit box, on an external drive, or in the cloud,
so they can be reached during a disaster. The NIH lists "backed-up"
among the qualities every research record should have, and the
Produce Safety Rule allows a farm to keep its records offsite as long as
they can be brought back within 24 hours.

For a home, a simple pattern:

- photograph the notebook pages at the end of each month, and store the
  photos somewhere other than the house;
- keep digital records in two places, and check now and then that the
  second copy actually opens.

The Produce Safety Rule treats photographs and scans as true copies of
the originals, which is a useful reminder that a phone photo of a page
is a real record and not just a reminder of one.

**Keep more than one old copy.** If you only ever keep the latest
backup, a mistake made today gets copied over yesterday's good version
tonight. Keeping several dated versions lets you go back past the point
where something went wrong. The game's own save system is built this
way, for that reason (see below).

## Reading your records back

A record nobody reads is a diary. Set a time to read yours: the end of
each season, and before every big decision.

- **Compare the plan with what happened.** The USDA's canning worksheet
  (worked through in [Estimating](/library#estimating)) plans a year of jars.
  Count the jars left on the shelf when the next harvest comes in. That
  number is next year's correction.
- **Look for trends, not single years.** One bad tomato year may be the
  weather. Three in a row in the same bed, while other beds did well,
  points at the bed.
- **Check your estimates.** If you keep a log of your estimates beside
  the measured answers, as [Estimating](/library#estimating) suggests, it will
  tell you whether you lean high or low.
- **Write down what you decided and why.** Next year's notebook should
  start with last year's conclusions.

## A starter list for a homestead

Not everything, but the records that pay for themselves fastest:

- **Garden:** sowing and planting dates, varieties and seed source, bed,
  germination, first and last harvest dates, harvest weights, pests and
  problems and what you did about them.
- **Weather:** daily rain and the overnight low, read at the same time
  each day, with zero, trace and missing kept apart. Your own first and
  last frost dates. [The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar)
  explains frost dates as probabilities built from decades of other
  people's readings; your own readings tell you how your patch differs
  from the nearest station.
- **Water:** meter readings, tank levels, and test results with the date
  and the lab (see [Testing Water](/library#testing-water)).
- **Food stores:** what was canned, dried or fermented, the date, the
  recipe and its source, the process used, and when each batch was
  finished.
- **Machines:** a maintenance log for each one.
- **Money:** receipts for what you buy and records of what you sell.
- **Health:** the medication list for each person in the household.

## How the game models it

The game keeps two records of its own, and both are worth looking at
for what they teach.

- **A meter, not a diary.** In the default ship life support mode,
  Station-supplied, the Live power card on the Homes page shows how much
  electricity your home has drawn from the ship's reactor and returned
  to it, under "Metered from the ship". It is a running total. The game
  keeps it internally to the watt hour, but the card shows it in
  kilowatt hours to two decimal places, so what you read moves in steps
  of 10 watt hours. Like a real meter, it only becomes information when
  you write down a reading, come back later, and subtract.
- **Several dated copies, not one.** Settings > Data > Save snapshots
  lists earlier copies of your home. The game saves every 2 minutes and
  when you quit, and before it saves over your home it keeps the
  previous save as a snapshot: the newest 10 copies, at most one every
  15 minutes. Each copy is named by the moment it was taken, written
  year first so the names sort in time order, and a restore keeps the
  save it replaces, so it can be undone. The reason it keeps ten copies,
  spaced out in time, rather than one copy or ten made minutes apart, is
  the reason given above: a save spoiled by a mistake could otherwise
  push every good copy out before anyone noticed.

**One setting changes both.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start every
session from the default home" is on, which is the default during
development, only your character carries between launches. The ship
meter starts over each time the game starts, and the home save, and so
the snapshots taken of it, keeps your character on top of the last home
saved while the setting was off, rather than your home as you left it
this session. Turn the setting off if you want the meter and your home
to carry over.

What the game does not do: it keeps no garden diary, harvest log or
maintenance log for you. Those are yours to keep, in the game as in
life.

## You own this when

- You write entries at the time, with the date, the place, what you
  did, how much, and who did it.
- Your records hold numbers with units, and estimates are marked as
  estimates.
- You never leave a reading blank: zero, trace and missing are each
  written as what they are.
- You read your instruments at the same time and the same way, and note
  when that changes.
- You correct by crossing out, never by erasing or overwriting.
- Your dates are written year first.
- Every jar, seed container and mixed bucket has a label.
- Every machine you depend on has a maintenance log.
- Your household's medication lists are current, and someone else knows
  where they are.
- Your records exist in more than one place, in more than one version.
- You read them back before you decide.
