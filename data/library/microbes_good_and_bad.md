# Microbes, Good and Bad

A teaspoon of garden soil, a crock of sauerkraut, a rain barrel and the
inside of your own gut have one thing in common: each is crowded with
living things too small to see. Most of them do you no harm, and many do
work you could not do without. A few make people ill, and a handful of
those can kill. The CDC's own definitions put the balance in two lines:
pathogens are the germs that cause infections, and "Most germs are
harmless and even helpful to people."

This guide is about telling the useful ones from the harmful ones in the
only way that works. You cannot do it by looking, smelling or tasting.
You can do it by knowing what each kind is, where it comes from, what it
needs to grow, and what stops it: heat, cold, salt, acid, drying, air,
soap and time. The worked example is a week in a kitchen in Silverdale,
Washington, where the simulation is set.

It is the foundation for several other guides and does not repeat them.
[Life in Soil](/library#life-in-soil) covers the bacteria and fungi that make
soil fertile, and [Your First Compost](/library#your-first-compost) how to put
them to work. [Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables) is the
tested way to let bacteria preserve food. [Pests and
Disease](/library#pests-and-disease) covers the fungi, bacteria and viruses that
attack crops. [When Food or Water Makes You
Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick) covers the illnesses: how long
after eating they start, who is most at risk, keeping someone hydrated and
when to see a doctor. [The Chemistry of Keeping
Food](/library#the-chemistry-of-keeping-food) explains why salt, sugar, acid,
drying, cold, heat and smoke each slow microbes down. [Telling Spoiled
From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe), [Handwashing and
Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene) and [Making Water Safe to
Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) cover the three everyday defences.

Its sources are the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the
Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and its handbook of foodborne germs,
the Bad Bug Book, the National Institutes of Health (NIH), the US
Department of Agriculture (USDA) and its Food Safety and Inspection
Service (FSIS), and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), all US
federal and in the public domain. Where something is general practice or
our own reading, the text says so.

## First, five rules that keep people safe

1. **You cannot see, smell or taste most of what makes people ill.** The
   FDA: "Be aware that food can make you very sick even when it doesn't
   look, smell, or taste spoiled." The germs that spoil food and the
   germs that make people ill are mostly different ones, and [Telling
   Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) explains why. Never
   taste a food to find out.
2. **Some illnesses take only a few germs.** The CDC on the gut parasite
   *Giardia*: "swallowing just a few Giardia germs can make you sick." On
   hepatitis A, it says the virus spreads when someone swallows it "even
   in very small amounts." For these, keeping germs out of food and water
   matters as much as stopping them from growing (our reading).
3. **Warmth and time let many of them multiply fast.** FSIS says bacteria
   grow most rapidly between 40 and 140 F, "doubling in number in as
   little as 20 minutes." The CDC's rule is to refrigerate perishable food
   within 2 hours, or within 1 hour when it is above 90 F.
4. **Soap, water and clean hands break the commonest route.** Germs from
   people and animals reach food and mouths on hands, and [Handwashing and
   Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene) has the method. The CDC: "Hand
   sanitizer does not work well against norovirus."
5. **Antibiotics are for some bacterial infections, and a doctor decides.**
   The CDC: "Antibiotics DO NOT work on viruses." It also says not to share
   them, not to save them for later, and not to take antibiotics
   prescribed for someone else.

And one rule above all of them: an illness with the danger signs in [When
Food or Water Makes You Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick), such
as bloody diarrhoea, a high fever or signs of dehydration, is a doctor's
job, not this guide's.

## What a microbe is

The USDA's canning guide defines microorganisms as "Independent organisms
of microscopic size, including bacteria, yeast, and mold." The CDC uses
the everyday word, germs, for all of the very small living things,
counting parasites and viruses alongside bacteria and fungi. Here are the
five kinds you will meet in a house, a garden and a body.

| Kind | What it is | How big, where the CDC gives it | Everyday examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bacteria | Single cells; the USDA calls them "one-celled microorganisms widely distributed in nature" | *E. coli* about 0.5 by 2 to 8 micrometres | The bacteria that sour sauerkraut; *E. coli* in the gut; *Salmonella* |
| Yeasts | Fungi that can live as single cells, as baker's yeast does (EPA); in the USDA's words, "A group of microorganisms which reproduce by budding" | Not given | Baker's and brewer's yeast; the white film on a ferment |
| Moulds | FSIS: "microscopic fungi that live on plant or animal matter," made of many cells, with root threads and spores | Not given | Blue cheese moulds; the fuzz on old bread |
| Viruses | Not cells at all; the FDA says a virus "can replicate only within living host cells" | About 0.03 micrometres | Norovirus; hepatitis A |
| Parasites | Some are single cells, such as *Giardia*, which the FDA calls a single-celled parasite; some are worms, such as *Trichinella* | *Giardia* cysts 8 by 19 micrometres; *Cryptosporidium* oocysts 4 to 6 | *Giardia* in untreated stream water |

A micrometre is a thousandth of a millimetre. The sizes come from the
CDC's travel health handbook, which uses them to say which water filter
catches which germ; [Making Water Safe to
Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) puts them to that use. The
mushrooms you pick or grow are fungi too: FSIS puts them in the same
family as moulds, and [Growing Mushrooms](/library#growing-mushrooms) and
[Identifying Wild Plants](/library#identifying-wild-plants) cover them.

Three things follow from the table (our reading of it):

- **A virus cannot multiply in a sandwich or a jug of water.** It waits
  there until it reaches a living cell, so the refrigerator does nothing
  to it, and what matters is keeping it from getting there.
- **Bacteria and moulds can multiply in food.** That is why time and
  temperature matter for them.
- **You never see a germ on its own.** What you see as slime, film or
  fuzz is a colony that has already grown large (FSIS says a mould can
  sometimes be seen with the naked eye; that is the colony).

They are also everywhere food comes from. The USDA's canning guide:
"Microorganisms live and multiply quickly on the surfaces of fresh food
and on the inside of bruised, insect-damaged, and diseased food." And it
warns that washing fresh food reduces their numbers "only slightly";
peeling root crops does much more. That is not a reason to stop washing
produce, which the CDC recommends; it is a reason not to treat a rinse as
a cure.

## How fast they multiply

The USDA's glossary gives the speed: "When alive in a suitable
environment, they grow rapidly and may divide or reproduce every 10 to 30
minutes. Therefore, they reach high populations very quickly."

Work it through for one bacterium dividing every 20 minutes, the fastest
rate FSIS gives for the danger zone. After 1 hour there are 8. After 2
hours, 64. After 4 hours, 4,096. After 8 hours, about 16.8 million (our
arithmetic). Real growth slows as the food, the water or the room runs
out, so treat that as the shape of the danger, not a forecast. It is why
the food safety limits are counted in hours, and why the last hour of a
warm afternoon does far more harm than the first.

### Spores

Some bacteria have a way to wait out bad times. The FDA's Bad Bug Book,
in its note for consumers: "Some types of bacteria form spores that
aren't killed by cooking. Spores are a survival mode in which those
bacteria make an inactive form that can live without nutrition and that
develops very tough protection against the outside world." The danger
comes afterwards: "After cooking, the spores may change and grow into
bacteria, when the food cools down."

That one fact explains three rules you will meet elsewhere: cool cooked
food quickly, keep it cold, and never trust boiling alone to make a
low-acid jar safe ([Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)).
Moulds spread by spores too, of a different kind. FSIS says mould spores
can be carried by air, water or insects, and that dry spores "float
through the air and find suitable conditions where they can start the
growth cycle again."

## What they need, and what stops them

Every microbe has a range of temperature, acidity and moisture it can
grow in, with a best point somewhere inside. The FDA's Bad Bug Book says
it of acidity, and the same is true of temperature: "Moving away from the
pH optimum in either direction slows microbial growth." It names the three
factors that matter most in food: water activity (how much of a food's
water is free for microbes to use), pH (acidity) and temperature.

The useful thing to notice is that **every condition stops some microbes
and leaves others alone.** That is how preserving food works, and it is
also how it fails.

| Condition | What it does | What still grows or survives |
|---|---|---|
| Warmth, 40 to 140 F | The range where bacteria grow fastest (FSIS) | The ones FSIS names as growing to dangerous levels in food left out: *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Salmonella* Enteritidis, *E. coli* O157:H7 and *Campylobacter* |
| A refrigerator, 40 F or below | The FDA: "Proper refrigeration keeps most types of bacteria from growing to numbers that can cause illness" | *Listeria* and *Yersinia* grow at refrigerator temperatures (FDA); moulds can grow there too (FSIS) |
| A freezer, 0 F | The FDA: "While freezing does not kill most bacteria, it does stop bacteria from growing." | Most of what went in comes out again when it thaws |
| Cooking to a safe temperature | Kills most bacteria and parasites (FDA) | Spores (FDA); some poisons germs have already made (FDA); norovirus can survive 145 F (CDC) |
| Acid, pH 4.6 or below | Stops the bacterium behind botulism from growing (USDA) | Moulds can "thrive in high-acid foods like jams, jellies, pickles, fruit, and tomatoes" (FSIS); *Shigella* tolerates low pH (FDA) |
| Salt or sugar | Ties up the water; most bacteria compete badly for what is left (FDA) | The FDA: "Except for Staphylococcus aureus, bacteria are rather poor competitors, whereas molds are excellent competitors." |
| Drying | Leaves too little free water to grow | Bacteria below their limit "may remain dormant, but infectious" (FDA); moulds on food that was not dried enough ([Drying Food](/library#drying-food)) |
| Keeping air out | Keeps off the moulds and yeasts that grow where food meets air ([Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables)) | The botulism bacterium grows only without air (USDA) |

The salt line is the clearest example of choosing a winner. In the USDA's
words, from its rules for sauerkraut and fermented pickles: "In fermented
foods, salt favors the growth of desirable bacteria while inhibiting the
growth of others." But salt on its own does not decide the winner: the
FDA's line on competitors above makes *Staphylococcus aureus* the
exception, so the acid the right bacteria make, and the whole tested
recipe that gets them making it, matter as much as the salt (our reading).

And the factors work together. The FDA describes a cheese spread that
keeps at room temperature because its acidity and its water activity,
each of which on its own would let the botulism bacterium grow, together
stop it. It then draws the conclusion that matters for every home cook:
predictions about whether a microbe will grow in a particular food "can,
in general, only be made through experimentation." That is what a tested
recipe is. [The
Chemistry of Keeping Food](/library#the-chemistry-of-keeping-food) goes through
each condition in detail.

## The ones we rely on

### In your gut

The NIH's environmental health institute defines the microbiome as "the
collection of all microbes, such as bacteria, fungi, viruses, and their
genes, that naturally live on our bodies and inside us." It says "there
are as many microbes as there are human cells in the body," and lists
their work: "They protect us against pathogens, help our immune system
develop, and enable us to digest food to produce energy."

*E. coli* shows how one name can cover both sides. The CDC: "Most E. coli
are harmless and are part of a healthy intestinal tract. E. coli help us
digest food, produce vitamins, and protect us from harmful germs." A few
kinds of *E. coli* cause serious illness, as the next section says.

That is also why antibiotics are not harmless. The CDC: "Antibiotics and
antifungals kill some germs that cause infections, but they also kill
helpful germs that protect our body from infection." The NIH's digestive
diseases institute (NIDDK) adds that antibiotics can change the gut's
bacteria and raise the chance of an infection with *C. difficile*, a gut
bacterium that causes diarrhoea, and the CDC counts *C. diff* infection
among the more serious side effects of antibiotics. The CDC also says
that anyone who has been taking antibiotics or antifungals should tell
their doctor about three or more episodes of diarrhoea in 24 hours.

What about probiotics, the live bacteria sold as supplements? NIDDK says
researchers are still studying them for diarrhoea, that many doctors and
professional societies do not think the evidence supports using them to
treat or prevent it, and that you should talk with your doctor before
trying them (restated).

### In the kitchen

The USDA defines fermentation as "Changes in food caused by intentional
growth of bacteria, yeast, or mold." Its examples: "Native bacteria
ferment natural sugars to lactic acid, a major flavoring and preservative
in sauerkraut and in naturally fermented dills. Alcohol, vinegar, and
some dairy products are also fermented foods."

You can see the bacteria's work in the FDA's table of food acidity:
cabbage at pH 5.2 to 6.0 and sauerkraut at 3.4 to 3.6. The lower the
number, the more acid, and in sauerkraut the acid is what the bacteria
made from the cabbage's own sugar. (Dill pickles, at 3.2 to 3.5 against
5.1 to 5.7 for cucumbers, can be soured by bacteria or by vinegar: the
USDA says fresh-pack pickles are acidified quickly with vinegar. The
table does not say which kind it measured.) The FDA warns that the pH
of foods "even those of similar types,
varies considerably," so these are typical ranges, not targets to test
your own crock against ([Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)
explains why household pH strips are not a safety test).

**Yeast** raises bread. The EPA, assessing the species behind it: "Also
known as Baker's Yeast or Brewer's Yeast, this organism has been used for
centuries as leavening for bread and as a fermenter of alcoholic
beverages." It found the same yeast living on fruits and vegetables.

**Moulds** make some cheeses. FSIS: "The molds used to manufacture these
cheeses are safe to eat." The same page is just as plain about the other
kind: "Molds that are not a part of the manufacturing process can be
dangerous."

A ferment is only as safe as the process that runs it. A crock where the
wrong microbes won is not a slightly worse sauerkraut but a jar of
low-acid vegetables in brine ([Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)), which is why [Fermenting
Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables) teaches only tested recipes.

### In the soil and the compost heap

The EPA: "Composting is the managed, aerobic (oxygen-required) biological
decomposition of organic materials by microorganisms." And what those
microorganisms need: "They use carbon and nitrogen to grow and reproduce,
water to digest materials, and oxygen to breathe." That is the whole
reason behind the greens, browns, water and turning in [Your First
Compost](/library#your-first-compost). The EPA also lists what to keep out of a
backyard pile: pet waste and cat litter, and meat, fish, bones, dairy and
fats, which it says a backyard pile generally does not get hot enough to
break down fully.

In the ground, bacteria and fungi break down dead plants, hold nutrients
where roots can reach them and, on legume roots, take nitrogen from the
air. [Life in Soil](/library#life-in-soil) covers all of it, along with the few
soil organisms that make people ill.

### In the garden and the medicine cabinet

Even pest control can be a microbe. The EPA: "Bacillus thuringiensis, or
simply Bt, is a naturally occurring soil bacterium that, when sprayed on
plants, is toxic to certain pest insects." [Pests and
Disease](/library#pests-and-disease) explains how to use it and why selective
does not mean harmless.

And medicine owes microbes a great deal. The USDA's glossary notes that
microorganisms "are sometimes intentionally added to ferment foods, make
antibiotics, and for other reasons."

## The ones that make people sick

### A small minority, and the same kind can be both

Of all the microbes around you, only some cause disease, and the line
does not always run between kinds. The CDC: "Most kinds of E. coli are
harmless, but some can make you sick." One group of them, Shiga
toxin-producing *E. coli*, can trigger a kidney illness called hemolytic
uremic syndrome, which the CDC says can lead to kidney failure, lasting
health problems and death. So "E. coli" on a water test or in a news
story names a family, not a verdict ([Testing
Water](/library#testing-water) explains what the water test means; the
family point is our reading).

### Two ways a germ makes you ill

The FDA's Bad Bug Book gives, for each germ, an estimate of how many it
takes to make someone ill, and the estimates fall into two very different
groups. Read them with the FDA's own warning attached: the numbers are
rough, many come from outbreak investigations or from feeding studies on
healthy young adults, and young children, older adults and people with
weakened immune systems may fall ill from fewer.

**Germs that need only a few.** For these the FDA's estimates are as low
as 1 to 10 virus particles for norovirus, as low as one cell for
*Salmonella*, 10 to 100 cells for *E. coli* O157:H7, 10 to 200 cells for
*Shigella*, one or more cysts for *Giardia* and 10 to 100 for
*Cryptosporidium*. With doses that small, the defences that count are the
ones that keep germs out: clean hands, safe water, raw meat kept away
from food that will not be cooked, and thorough cooking (our reading).
The refrigerator does little, because these germs do not need to grow in
the food to make you ill. The FDA: "many microorganisms do not need to
multiply in food to cause disease."

**Germs that need to grow in the food first.** *Staphylococcus aureus*
makes people ill through a poison it produces in the food, a level the
FDA says is reached when its numbers pass 100,000 per gram. For
*Clostridium perfringens* the FDA gives more than a million cells, and
says it "replicates much more quickly than do most other bacteria"; the
CDC's list of its sources is meat, poultry, gravies and other foods cooked
in large batches and held at an unsafe temperature. For *Bacillus cereus*
the FDA gives 100,000 to 100 million cells, and says the harm comes from
poison already made in the food. For these, time and temperature are the
control, and reheating may come too late: the FDA's chapter on
*Staphylococcus aureus* says its poisons "are highly heat stable and can
remain biologically active" after the bacteria themselves are killed.
[Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) has the
details, including why reheated rice is a classic case.

### How they reach you

The commonest route is the mouth. The CDC's account of norovirus covers
the three ways in: close contact with someone who is ill, such as caring
for them, sharing their food or utensils or eating food they handled;
eating or drinking something contaminated; and touching a contaminated
surface and then putting unwashed fingers in your mouth. Its table of
foodborne germs lists where the common ones come from: raw or undercooked
poultry, meat and eggs; raw milk and juice; raw fruit, vegetables and
sprouts; contaminated water; and animals, with backyard poultry, reptiles,
amphibians, small mammals and pets including cats and dogs named
(restated).

People can carry and spread germs without being ill. The FDA reports
studies suggesting that 30 percent of people infected with norovirus have
no gut symptoms but still shed large amounts of virus. The CDC says
norovirus can still be spread for 2 weeks or more after someone feels
better, and that hepatitis A can spread up to 2 weeks before any symptoms.

The other routes have their own guides: germs breathed out and in
([Handwashing and Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene)), germs in soil
entering a wound or rising in dust ([Life in Soil](/library#life-in-soil),
[Bleeding and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds)), and germs carried by
insects and rats ([Rats, Flies, Mosquitoes and
Ticks](/library#rats-flies-mosquitoes-and-ticks)).

### Moulds and their poisons

FSIS: "Some molds cause allergic reactions and respiratory problems." A
few, in the right conditions, make poisons called mycotoxins, and the
mould's root threads can run deep into soft food where you cannot see
them. [Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) has the
FSIS table of which mouldy foods can be trimmed and which must go. Do not
sniff mouldy food to check it: FSIS says doing so "can cause respiratory
trouble."

## The right tool for the right microbe

- **Antibiotics** work only on bacteria, and only some infections need
  them. The CDC: "Antibiotics DO NOT work on viruses." When a doctor does
  prescribe them, the CDC says to take them exactly as prescribed, not to
  share them, not to save them for later and not to take anyone else's,
  and to get rid of leftovers through a drug take-back programme. Using
  them also breeds resistance: the CDC says it speeds up when these drugs
  pressure bacteria and fungi to adapt, and defines it plainly: "AR
  happens when germs like bacteria and fungi develop the ability to
  defeat the drugs designed to kill them."
- **No medicine for most stomach viruses.** The CDC says there is no
  specific medicine for norovirus and that antibiotics will not help,
  because they fight bacteria, not viruses. Fluids are the treatment
  ([When Food or Water Makes You
  Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick)).
- **Soap and water for hands, every time it matters.** Soap lifts germs
  off rather than killing them, which is why it handles the ones
  sanitiser misses ([Handwashing and Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene)).
- **Bleach for surfaces after vomit or diarrhoea.** The CDC's strength and
  method are in [When Food or Water Makes You
  Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick). The CDC: "Never mix bleach
  with ammonia or other cleaners. This will produce a poisonous gas."
- **A rolling boil for doubtful water.** It handles bacteria, viruses and
  parasites alike, though not chemicals ([Making Water Safe to
  Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink)).

## Worked example: a week of microbes in a Silverdale kitchen

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on. A household on a small lot outside Silverdale: two adults, a
three-year-old, four hens, a compost bin and an October garden. Each day,
notice which microbes the family is relying on and which it is keeping
out, and what decides it.

1. **Monday: sauerkraut.** They shred the last cabbages and salt them by
   weight, as [Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables) gives,
   pack them in a crock and weigh them down under the brine. The bacteria
   that will do the work are already on the cabbage. The salt favours
   them, the brine keeps air off the cabbage, and a room at 70 to 75 F
   suits them. Over the next weeks they will take the cabbage from around
   pH 5.2 to 6.0 to around 3.4 to 3.6 (FDA's typical figures). *Relying
   on them.*
2. **Tuesday: bread and compost.** The bread dough rises because of
   yeast. The cabbage cores and outer leaves go into the compost bin,
   buried under dry leaves so the microbes get nitrogen, carbon and air
   and the rats get nothing ([Your First Compost](/library#your-first-compost)).
   *Relying on them.*
3. **Wednesday: eggs.** Collecting eggs means handling hens, their feed
   and their droppings, and the CDC lists backyard poultry among the
   sources of *Salmonella*. Everyone washes their hands with soap
   afterwards, and the eggs go into the refrigerator ([Chickens and
   Eggs](/library#chickens-and-eggs)). *Keeping them out.*
4. **Thursday: chicken for dinner.** The chicken thawed in the
   refrigerator, not on the counter. It is not rinsed: the CDC says washing
   raw chicken can spread germs to other foods, the sink and the counter.
   It goes on its own board, is cooked to 165 F checked with a thermometer,
   and the leftovers are in shallow containers in the refrigerator within
   2 hours (the CDC's rules). *Keeping them out, and stopping them
   growing.*
5. **Friday: the three-year-old vomits.** Norovirus is going round the
   daycare. Nobody reaches for antibiotics, which would do nothing. The
   parents clean up with gloves and the CDC's bleach solution, wash their
   hands with soap and water rather than sanitiser, and keep the child
   out of the kitchen; the parent who is not nursing him does the cooking
   (general practice; the CDC's rule is that someone who is ill prepares
   no food for others until 2 days after their symptoms stop). They watch
   him for signs of dehydration, and [When Food or Water Makes You
   Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick) tells them when to call the
   doctor. *Keeping them out.*
6. **Saturday: a white film on the sauerkraut.** It is flat and white, not
   fuzzy and not in round patches: kahm yeast, which [Fermenting
   Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables) says to skim off. It grows where
   the brine meets air and feeds on the acid, so they add weight and top
   up the brine. *A microbe that is harmless in itself, but working
   against the one they want.*
7. **Sunday: fuzz on the jam.** An opened jar of plum jam at the back of
   the refrigerator has a spot of mould. FSIS says to discard mouldy jam,
   not scoop the mould out: "The mold could be producing a mycotoxin."
   Into the bin it goes, and the shelf gets wiped. *Keeping them out.*

Count the decisions. Not one of them needed a microscope. Salt, air,
warmth, cold, heat, soap and time decided every case.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Signs of serious illness**, from bloody diarrhoea to a baby who will
  not drink, go to a doctor: [When Food or Water Makes You
  Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick) has the list.
- **Antibiotics** are a healthcare professional's decision, never a
  leftover in a cupboard (CDC).
- **A ferment, cure or canning recipe nobody has tested** is not yours to
  invent: [Fermenting Vegetables](/library#fermenting-vegetables), [Salting,
  Curing and Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking) and [Why Canning Has
  Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules).
- **Whether your water carries germs** is a laboratory's question
  ([Testing Water](/library#testing-water)).
- **Large piles of bird or bat droppings** in a barn or attic are a job
  for professionals ([Life in Soil](/library#life-in-soil)).

## Traps

- **"It smells fine."** The germs that make people ill usually change
  nothing you can smell.
- **"The fridge kills germs."** It slows most of them, *Listeria* grows in
  it, and even freezing does not kill most bacteria (FDA).
- **"I cooked it, so it's safe."** Cooking kills most bacteria, but not
  spores, and not some poisons germs have already made.
- **"Antibiotics will sort out this stomach bug."** The commonest stomach
  bug, norovirus, is a virus, and antibiotics do not work on viruses.
- **"There are some antibiotics left over from last time."** The CDC says
  not to save them or take anyone else's.
- **"Sanitiser is as good as washing."** Not against norovirus.
- **"I feel fine, so I can't pass anything on."** People spread norovirus
  and hepatitis A without symptoms.
- **"It's natural, so it's safe."** A ferment is safe because a tested
  process made it acid, not because nobody added chemicals.
- **"All bacteria are bad."** Most are harmless, many are useful, and your
  own gut depends on them.

## How the game models it

The game models what some microbes do, but none of the microbes
themselves.

- **Food spoils on a clock, and nothing on screen shows it.** Every food
  has a time it keeps (`spoilage_rate_hours` in `data/food_system.ron`):
  18 hours for raw fish, a day for raw meat, three days for cooked meat, a
  week for fresh milk and for vegetables such as lettuce, cabbage and
  tomatoes, three weeks for eggs, ten years for canned food. The clock
  stands in for the work of spoilage microbes, and it runs faster or
  slower with the temperature where the food is kept: a twentieth as fast
  frozen, a quarter as fast in cold air, 0.6 times as fast in cool air and
  two and a half times as fast in heat (`temperature_zones`). Nothing on
  screen marks a food as spoiled, so in the game you cannot see it at
  all: the first sign is the illness.
- **Spoiled and raw food makes you ill.** Eating a spoiled food gives a
  quarter of its nourishment and always gives you Food Poisoning, and
  some foods eaten as they are carry a chance of it: raw meat 40 percent,
  raw fish 25, eggs 15, fresh milk 5 and raw vegetables 2
  (`src/systems/food.rs`, `data/food_system.ron`). [When Food or Water
  Makes You Sick](/library#when-food-or-water-makes-you-sick) says what Food
  Poisoning does to you in the game.
- **Under the default settings.** A new game starts in the Normal play
  mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), and only the Dev play mode's
  tools take you off the ship, so in Normal the food you carry ages in
  the ship's air, at room temperature (`src/config.rs`,
  `src/gui/pages/dev.rs`, `src/engine/survival_env.rs`,
  `src/systems/atmosphere.rs`). The clock runs at the Realistic time
  speed unless you change it (Settings > Gameplay > Time), so a game
  second is a real second and raw meat really does keep about a day
  (`src/systems/time.rs`). Settings > Gameplay > "Start every session
  from the default home" is off by default, so your food, with its age,
  is still there at the next launch.
- **Crop diseases spread in humid air.** Gray mold, powdery mildew and
  downy mildew can appear on the crops each one attacks, favoured by
  humid air and by their own temperature ranges
  (`data/garden/pests.ron`); [Pests and Disease](/library#pests-and-disease)
  covers the real ones. They run while Settings > Gameplay > "Garden
  pests, diseases and weeds" is not Off; its default is Gentle, which
  halves the damage. The garden's pest controls also include Bt Spray,
  which the game describes as a soil bacterium that hurts only the
  caterpillars that eat it (`data/items.csv`).
- **Compost without microbes.** The Inventory page's "Compost and draw off
  urine" button turns your character's Waste into Fertilizer at once, and
  the Composter's compost recipes take a 20-second craft that, in Normal,
  uses up the materials (`src/gui/pages/inventory.rs`,
  `src/systems/food.rs`, `data/recipes.csv`). Letting Waste build up gives
  the Unsanitary condition, which slows you a little, and composting
  clears it; no germs are involved.
- **Fermenting is only written down.** The food data has a "Fermented"
  cooking method that the game loads but never uses
  (`data/food_system.ron`, `src/systems/food.rs`). The Make
  Cheese recipe says it cultures the milk, but it takes only milk and
  salt, and Bake Bread takes flour, water and salt: there is no yeast or
  culture in the game (`data/recipes.csv`, `data/items.csv`).
- **Illnesses are only listed, and antibiotics treat only bacteria.**
  The game's data includes a cold, flu, an infection, parasites and
  plague (`data/status_effects.csv`), but nothing in the game gives them
  to you. The Antibiotics item ends only the conditions the data marks
  as caused by bacteria (the infection, an infected wound and plague),
  not the cold, the flu or Food Poisoning, so with nothing like that on
  you, its Use button keeps it in your pack and says why
  (`data/medical/treatments.ron`, `src/systems/treatment.rs`,
  `src/gui/pages/inventory.rs`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there are
no germs to wash off, nobody passes an illness to anyone else, no water
makes you ill, the *Listeria* that grows in a refrigerator and the spores
that survive cooking do not exist, and nothing in the game ferments or
rots by the rules this guide describes.

## You own this when

- You can say what bacteria, yeasts, moulds, viruses and parasites are,
  and roughly how big each is.
- You can name three microbes you rely on and three that make people
  ill, and explain how *E. coli* can be both.
- You know which conditions stop which microbes: why the refrigerator
  slows most bacteria but not *Listeria*, why acid stops the botulism
  bacterium but not moulds, and why salt and sugar stop most bacteria but
  not *Staphylococcus aureus*.
- You can explain why some illnesses take a few germs and others need
  large numbers, and which defence matters for each.
- You never use antibiotics for a virus, and never save, share or borrow
  them.
- You wash your hands with soap after the toilet, the animals and the
  soil, and before food.
- You can keep a ferment and a compost heap working because you know
  what their microbes need.
