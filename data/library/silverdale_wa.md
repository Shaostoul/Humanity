# Where You Are: Silverdale, Washington

This is the real place the simulation is built on. Every number below
comes from the same data the world itself is built from, so what you
read here and what you walk around in cannot drift apart.

**The setting.** Head of Dyes Inlet, an arm of Puget Sound, on the
Kitsap Peninsula.

**The shape of it.** Sea level to about 178 metres, averaging 64. Sea
level in Dyes Inlet to about 178 m on the upland plateau. The town
itself is low: the GNIS Silverdale point is 4.8 m (16 ft) and the Clear
Creek mouth 2.8 m (9 ft), while the CDP centroid uphill is 15.7 m (51
ft) and Bucklin Hill is 69 m (227 ft).

### Frost, and the mistake that costs a crop

Last spring frost: March 29 on average, as early as March 5 and as late
as April 24. First autumn frost: November 14 on average, as early as
October 26 and as late as December 1.

The average is the 50th percentile: in half of all years the last spring
frost falls AFTER it. Planting tender crops to the average kills them in
half of all years. Plant to late_90pct (April 24), the date past which
only one year in ten still delivers a frost, and have row cover ready by
early_10pct in autumn (October 26), not by the average. All thresholds
are 32 F. At the 36 F threshold, which is the honest one for seedlings
on a clear calm night, the 10-percent-risk spring date is May 13.

**Extremes on record.** 38.9 C at the highest, -13.9 C at the lowest. A
building here should be designed for about -6 C in winter.

## What happens when

The climate table above is the weather. This is the year as events: what
comes into leaf, what fruits, what runs up the creek, what arrives and
what leaves. Each entry says how confident it is, because a date from a
published record and a regional generalisation are not the same claim.

measured = computed here from raw observations or predictions, or taken
from a NOAA normal computed from observations. published_range = a named
source states this range, though usually for a region larger than
Silverdale. local_consensus = the existing species record and regional
sources agree, but no single publication gives the date. estimated = a
regional generalisation applied here with no local source, and the event
says so. Nothing in this file dresses an estimate as a measurement.
Where the honest answer is that the timing varies too much to pin down,
the event says that instead of giving a date.

### January

The coldest and one of the wettest months: 236 mm of rain, a mean high
of 8.1 C and only 8.9 hours of daylight. Nothing grows. The deep minus
tides of the month all fall near midnight. This is maple sap season on a
hard clear snap, and hazel catkins are already out on bare branches
while eagles are settling onto nests.

- **The wet half of the year**. October through March. These six months deliver 1,154 mm of the annual 1,446 mm, which is 79.8 percent of the year's water. December alone brings 252 mm, nearly as much as April through September put together (294 mm across six months). This is the half of the year to design drainage for, to expect saturated ground and landslide risk in, and to fill every cistern in. It is also the half in which soil work compacts rather than tills.
- **Winter minus tides run at night**. November through February, the deep lows falling between roughly 22:00 and 02:00. The tide table will show you tides as low as any in June, and they are real, but they are in the dark and in the cold. Digging them means a headlamp, a falling tide you cannot see the edge of, and a long walk back over ground that is filling behind you. Treat a winter minus tide as an expert trip, not a family one, and note that the biotoxin question is unchanged by the season.
- **King tides and the winter flood risk**. Late December and January, when the highest astronomical tides coincide with the storm season. The highest water ever observed at Bremerton, 4.74 m above MLLW on 27 December 2022, exceeded the highest astronomical tide by 0.45 m. That extra half metre was storm surge stacked on an already extreme tide. A shoreline structure has to survive the tide plus the surge plus the wind waves, not the tide alone, and the season for all three arriving together is the turn of the year.
- **Marine biotoxin closures, which have no season**. Any month. There is no safe season and no pattern you can plan around. This is the one thing on this calendar that a calendar cannot answer. Paralytic, diarrhetic and amnesic shellfish poisoning come from algal blooms that the Washington Department of Health tests for continuously, and a closure can appear in any month. The water looks clear during a toxic bloom. Cooking and freezing do not destroy the toxin, because it is in the meat. Check the Department of Health Shellfish Safety Map or the hotline on 1-800-562-5632 on the day you harvest, every time, and never infer safety from a good tide or from last week's status. A separate authority answers a separate question.
- **Shellfish beach seasons**. Beach by beach, set annually by WDFW. There is no statewide month you can rely on. Two independent gates must both be open before you may legally and safely harvest: the WDFW season for that specific beach, and the Department of Health biotoxin status for that specific beach. Neither implies the other, and a good tide implies neither. WDFW also publishes an annual best clam and oyster harvest tides chart, which is the tide half of the same planning problem.
- **The winter steelhead run**. Late autumn into spring. The local fish are winter run. Unlike the salmon, steelhead do not all die after spawning, and they run through the months when nothing else does. Summer run fish are mostly east of the Cascades, so a steelhead in a Kitsap creek is a winter fish. Seasons here are tightly restricted and frequently closed outright; read the current rules rather than assuming an open season exists.
- **Pacific herring spawn**. WDFW gives herring spawning in Puget Sound from January and into June. Herring spawn on nearshore vegetation and the event is visible from the beach: the water turns milky white or teal where a large spawn has happened. It is the hinge of the local food web in late winter. Wintering sea ducks switch from mussels and clams onto herring eggs during spring migration, which is what puts them in condition to fly north and lay, and the spawn draws gulls, eagles and predatory fish at the same time. If you want to know why the inlet suddenly gets busy in February, this is why.
- **Surf smelt spawn, which has no single season**. Year round. Different beaches and stocks spawn at different times, and WDFW surveys for spawn year round. Surf smelt lay eggs about a millimetre across in the upper intertidal on mixed sand and gravel beaches, and a given beach may be a spawning beach in a season you would not expect. The practical consequence is not a harvest date but a construction constraint: WDFW protects documented forage fish spawning beaches from shoreline development, so upper intertidal work on a mixed sand and gravel beach is a permitting question in any month.
- **Hazel catkins shed pollen**. January to March in the west coast states, with early March given for Oregon. Catkins expand 5 to 15 days before the leaves. Long yellow catkins on bare branches in the middle of winter, which is startling the first time you notice it and is the earliest flowering of any local shrub. It marks the shrub for you months before the leaves make it findable, so this is when to note where the hazels are if you want the nuts in September.
- **Poison hemlock rosette, the dangerous anonymous stage**. The first year rosette stands through autumn, winter and early spring. It bolts and flowers in late spring and early summer, then sets seed and dies. The rosette is when poison hemlock looks most like parsley, and it is green at the exact time of year when a garden or field edge has little else green in it. This overlaps precisely with the wild carrot rosette, which is the root digging stage of an edible plant, and with the cow parsnip rosette. The dead standing stems remain poisonous. Never dig a white taprooted umbellifer in the rosette stage on the strength of leaf shape.
- **Water hemlock root, dangerous in every month**. Shoots in spring, white umbels in summer, top dies back in autumn. The tuberous root is in the ground all twelve months. The most poisonous plant in North America grows in the same wet ground as water parsley, hemlock waterparsnip and cattail, all of which are gathered. In winter nothing above ground shows where it is, and the root is the most poisonous part. This is why the calendar cannot make wet ground digging safe at any time of year, and why the honest advice for winter root digging in a wet meadow is not to.
- **Poison oak drops its leaves and keeps its oil**. Leafless from late autumn through early spring. The Forest Service gives flowering March to June and fruit dispersal in summer and autumn. Losing the leaves removes the only identification character most people know, while the urushiol stays on the bare stems. A leafless, thornless vine in winter is not identified, and firewood gathering season is exactly when people handle unidentified bare stems. New spring leaves are often glossy and reddish.
- **Bigleaf maple seed disperses**. Seeds ripe August to October, dispersing October to January, with some remaining on the tree until March. The paired winged samaras spin down through the whole wet season rather than dropping at once. Practically this is a gutter and drain problem as much as a botanical event, and it is the reason the tree seeds itself into every disturbed corner.
- **Winter waterfowl arrive on the inlet**. WDFW records birds departing coastal moulting areas from late August through November and moving to wintering areas, primarily in Puget Sound. Most numerous here roughly October through April. The inlet changes character completely for half the year. Surf scoters are the most abundant of the diving ducks and feed on the mussels and clams of the same beaches people dig. They switch onto herring eggs during spring migration, so the waterfowl season and the herring spawn are the same story told twice. An empty looking Dyes Inlet in July is normal; an empty one in January means something is wrong.
- **Varied thrush comes down into town** (local consensus). In the Silverdale lowlands mainly October through April. It breeds upslope in denser wetter forest and moves down into town for the winter, so its arrival is an altitude signal rather than a latitude one. It becomes most obvious feeding on the ground under shrubs during cold snaps, which makes it a good indicator that the weather has turned hard enough to push birds off the hills.
- **Bald eagles at the nest** (local consensus). Birds at the nest from roughly January or February, with young fledging in mid summer. Eagles nest remarkably early here, starting in the depth of winter, which is only possible because the autumn salmon runs fed them first. Numbers rise through autumn and winter as birds concentrate on the runs and the carcasses afterward, and then those same birds are on eggs by February. The chum run and the eagle nesting season are the same food chain three months apart.
- **Bigleaf maple sap runs**. WSU Extension records that in the Pacific Northwest maples are tapped in the winter months, December to February, when freeze and thaw events cause sap flow, and that collection is generally limited to a three to four week window. The only harvest in the dead of winter, and the one that most surprises people who think sugaring is a New England thing. It needs nights below freezing and days above it, which here means a cold clear snap rather than a typical grey wet January week, so the usable window inside the season is short and you have to be ready to take it. Sap flow stops at bud break. Bigleaf maple sap runs much thinner than sugar maple, so expect to boil a great deal for very little.

sharply to 148 mm and daylight gains an hour and a half over January.

lights up the wet ground, herons start gathering at their colonies, and
herring are spawning in the inlet.

### March

Genuinely spring, with 11.9 hours of daylight by mid month. The average
last frost is 29 March, which means half of all years frost later than
that. Red alder flowers and sheds the season's dominant pollen, nettle
shoots reach cutting size, and salmonberry begins to bloom.

- **Red flowering currant bloom** (local consensus). March through June, opening as the leaves emerge. The showiest native bloom of early spring and a hummingbird plant. Nettle is the best wild green here and the window is genuinely short. Cut the top few inches while the plant is under about knee height. Once it flowers and sheds pollen in summer it is no longer food: older plants develop gritty mineral deposits that irritate the urinary tract. Cooking or drying destroys the sting completely. Note the honest limit on the dates: the Forest Service gives flowering from late May to October but does not publish a shoot emergence date, so the start of this window is a regional generalisation rather than a measured date.
- **Death camas leafs out early and blooms late spring**. Green leaves appear early in spring ahead of most other growth; creamy white flowers in late spring; the plant dies back by summer. The early leaves are the hazard, because they come up when people and livestock are looking for the first green growth of the year and there is nothing else to compare them to. The bulb stays in the ground and is dangerous in every month, including the months when there is no leaf to find it by.

- **Poison oak drops its leaves and keeps its oil**. Leafless from late autumn through early spring. The Forest Service gives flowering March to June and fruit dispersal in summer and autumn. Losing the leaves removes the only identification character most people know, while the urushiol stays on the bare stems. A leafless, thornless vine in winter is not identified, and firewood gathering season is exactly when people handle unidentified bare stems. New spring leaves are often glossy and reddish.
- **Winter waterfowl arrive on the inlet**. WDFW records birds departing coastal moulting areas from late August through November and moving to wintering areas, primarily in Puget Sound. Most numerous here roughly October through April. The inlet changes character completely for half the year. Surf scoters are the most abundant of the diving ducks and feed on the mussels and clams of the same beaches people dig. They switch onto herring eggs during spring migration, so the waterfowl season and the herring spawn are the same story told twice. An empty looking Dyes Inlet in July is normal; an empty one in January means something is wrong.
- **Varied thrush comes down into town** (local consensus). In the Silverdale lowlands mainly October through April. It breeds upslope in denser wetter forest and moves down into town for the winter, so its arrival is an altitude signal rather than a latitude one. It becomes most obvious feeding on the ground under shrubs during cold snaps, which makes it a good indicator that the weather has turned hard enough to push birds off the hills.
- **Great blue herons gather at nesting colonies** (local consensus). Birds gather at colonies from roughly February onward, and colonies are in use through spring and early summer. Herons are here all year but they concentrate at colonies in late winter, and that is the vulnerable moment. Disturbing an active colony can cause the whole colony to abandon, not just one nest, so this is the season to keep well back from a stand of big stick nests. Plan any noisy work near one outside February to June.
- **Bald eagles at the nest** (local consensus). Birds at the nest from roughly January or February, with young fledging in mid summer. Eagles nest remarkably early here, starting in the depth of winter, which is only possible because the autumn salmon runs fed them first. Numbers rise through autumn and winter as birds concentrate on the runs and the carcasses afterward, and then those same birds are on eggs by February. The chum run and the eagle nesting season are the same food chain three months apart.
- **Pileated woodpecker breeding season**. WDFW gives the breeding season as late March through early July. A year round resident whose drumming becomes constant in spring. It excavates the large cavities that a long list of other species later nest in, so a big dead tree left standing is worth more than the firewood in it.

### April

The month the canopy closes: alder buds burst on 3 April and the trees
are fully leafed by early May. Rain falls away to 98 mm. Ospreys return.
Past 24 April only one year in ten still delivers a frost, so this is
when tender crops can go out. The first daylight minus tides of the year
appear late in the month.

- **Last spring frost**. March 5 at the 10th percentile, March 29 on average, April 24 at the 90th percentile. All at the 32 F threshold. The average is the 50th percentile, which means that in half of all years the last frost falls AFTER March 29. Planting tender crops to the average kills them in one year out of two. April 24 is the date past which only one year in ten still delivers a freeze, and that is the honest plant-out date for tomatoes, beans, squash and basil. At the 36 F threshold, which is what actually damages seedlings on a clear calm night, the 10 percent risk date is May 13.
- **Frost free growing season**. A median of 227 frost free days, running roughly from the end of March to the middle of November. Long enough for two successions of most cool season crops and one of warm season crops. The limit here is not frost but summer water and autumn light: the season is generous at both ends and stingy in the middle.
- **The daylight minus tides**. Mid April through mid August. In 2026 every daylight low tide at or below -2.0 ft MLLW fell between April 18 and August 12; across 2026 to 2029 the earliest is April 11 and the latest August 12. This is the single most useful tide fact for anyone digging clams here, and it is not obvious. Dyes Inlet gets roughly the same number of deep minus tides every month of the year, but in the winter half they all happen in the middle of the night and in the summer half they all happen around midday. Between about mid April and mid August you can dig a deep low in daylight; outside it you cannot, whatever the tide table total says. There are only about 22 to 26 such daylight tides in a whole year, in clusters of four to six days around the new and full moon, so they are worth marking on a calendar in advance. A geoduck needs -2.0 ft or lower, which is why this window is effectively the geoduck season.
- **Marine biotoxin closures, which have no season**. Any month. There is no safe season and no pattern you can plan around. This is the one thing on this calendar that a calendar cannot answer. Paralytic, diarrhetic and amnesic shellfish poisoning come from algal blooms that the Washington Department of Health tests for continuously, and a closure can appear in any month. The water looks clear during a toxic bloom. Cooking and freezing do not destroy the toxin, because it is in the meat. Check the Department of Health Shellfish Safety Map or the hotline on 1-800-562-5632 on the day you harvest, every time, and never infer safety from a good tide or from last week's status. A separate authority answers a separate question.
- **Shellfish beach seasons**. Beach by beach, set annually by WDFW. There is no statewide month you can rely on. Two independent gates must both be open before you may legally and safely harvest: the WDFW season for that specific beach, and the Department of Health biotoxin status for that specific beach. Neither implies the other, and a good tide implies neither. WDFW also publishes an annual best clam and oyster harvest tides chart, which is the tide half of the same planning problem.
- **The winter steelhead run**. Late autumn into spring. The local fish are winter run. Unlike the salmon, steelhead do not all die after spawning, and they run through the months when nothing else does. Summer run fish are mostly east of the Cascades, so a steelhead in a Kitsap creek is a winter fish. Seasons here are tightly restricted and frequently closed outright; read the current rules rather than assuming an open season exists.
- **Pacific herring spawn**. WDFW gives herring spawning in Puget Sound from January and into June. Herring spawn on nearshore vegetation and the event is visible from the beach: the water turns milky white or teal where a large spawn has happened. It is the hinge of the local food web in late winter. Wintering sea ducks switch from mussels and clams onto herring eggs during spring migration, which is what puts them in condition to fly north and lay, and the spawn draws gulls, eagles and predatory fish at the same time. If you want to know why the inlet suddenly gets busy in February, this is why.
- **Surf smelt spawn, which has no single season**. Year round. Different beaches and stocks spawn at different times, and WDFW surveys for spawn year round. Surf smelt lay eggs about a millimetre across in the upper intertidal on mixed sand and gravel beaches, and a given beach may be a spawning beach in a season you would not expect. The practical consequence is not a harvest date but a construction constraint: WDFW protects documented forage fish spawning beaches from shoreline development, so upper intertidal work on a mixed sand and gravel beach is a permitting question in any month.
- **Indian plum bloom**. The Forest Service gives flowering from January to May across the range, varying with latitude and elevation, concurrent with leaf development. Here that lands in February through April. White flowers on drooping racemes, on a shrub that is otherwise easy to walk past. The plant is dioecious, so only some shrubs will set fruit, and male plants start flowering earlier and are more numerous. If you are marking shrubs to come back to for fruit in May, mark the ones flowering later.
- **Skunk cabbage lights up the wet ground**. Emerges early in the spring, generally before most other growth. The bright yellow spathe is the first strong colour of the year and it maps the wet ground for you better than any soil survey: where skunk cabbage grows, the water table is at or near the surface. Use it to site paths, drainage and anything you do not want standing in water. Note that this is also when green false hellebore and the year's first spring greens are coming up in the same wet ground, and those are not the same plant.
- **Salmonberry bloom**. The Forest Service gives March to June for western Oregon and southwestern Washington. Magenta flowers among the first of any shrub in these woods. The Forest Service notes the timing aligns with rufous hummingbird migration and that the birds may be important pollinators, so this bloom is also when the hummingbirds arrive. Leaf flush runs March to April, with first leaves recorded by 21 March in western Washington.
- **Red flowering currant bloom** (local consensus). March through June, opening as the leaves emerge. The showiest native bloom of early spring and a hummingbird plant. Worth knowing because it is the shrub most worth deliberately planting near a house here: it flowers when almost nothing else does and feeds the birds that arrive with it.
- **Red alder flowers**. Late February to early May across the region. In Snohomish County, a Puget Sound lowland county, flowering begins 29 March and flowers have all fallen by 12 April. Alder pollen is the dominant early spring allergen here and it arrives before most people think of hay fever season. The Snohomish County dates are close enough to Silverdale in latitude and elevation to use directly, which makes this one of the few precisely dated events on this calendar.
- **Red alder leafs out**. Leaf bud burst 3 April, completely leafed out 2 May, recorded in Snohomish County, Washington. Alder is the tree that closes the canopy over the creek bottoms, so this is the date the understory goes dark and the water starts being shaded. On the other end of the year, alder is one of the first deciduous trees to drop: the majority of leaves fall during October and virtually all are gone by the first week of November.
- **Bigleaf maple bloom and leaf out**. Late April to early May in the Pacific Northwest, with flowers appearing before or with the leaves. Hanging green flower racemes on a big tree, and one of the few large nectar sources of the spring. The tree resumes growth very early in the year, which is the same physiology that makes the winter sap run possible.
- **Stinging nettle shoots, a narrow window** (local consensus). Roughly March into May, while the shoots are young and before flowering. Nettle is the best wild green here and the window is genuinely short. Cut the top few inches while the plant is under about knee height. Once it flowers and sheds pollen in summer it is no longer food: older plants develop gritty mineral deposits that irritate the urinary tract. Cooking or drying destroys the sting completely. Note the honest limit on the dates: the Forest Service gives flowering from late May to October but does not publish a shoot emergence date, so the start of this window is a regional generalisation rather than a measured date.
- **Bracken croziers uncoil**. Fronds usually begin to emerge between March and early May. Read this one as a warning rather than a harvest. The Forest Service records that western brackenfern has been found to be mutagenic and carcinogenic in rats and mice, and that all parts of the plant including the spores are carcinogenic, to the point that face masks are recommended for people working in dense bracken. It is widely eaten as a fiddlehead and it should not be. Its real calendar value is as an indicator: croziers in the open are often killed by late spring frost, so a frosted bracken patch tells you that ground is a frost pocket.
- **Knotweed shoots** (local consensus). Spring only, as the new reddish shoots push up. One of the few cases where eating an invasive is genuinely useful, though it does nothing to control it. The important thing is disposal rather than harvest: knotweed regrows from tiny fragments of root or stem, so cuttings must never go in compost, in a green bin, or anywhere near water. Moving the plant is how it spreads.
- **Cattail shoots and pollen** (local consensus). Shoots in spring, flower stalks and pollen in early summer, rhizomes dug in autumn and winter. The most productive wild starch in this landscape and available in some form nearly all year, which is rare. The caution is the water, not the plant: cattails grow where runoff collects, so what is upstream matters more than what is in your hand.
- **Death camas leafs out early and blooms late spring**. Green leaves appear early in spring ahead of most other growth; creamy white flowers in late spring; the plant dies back by summer. The early leaves are the hazard, because they come up when people and livestock are looking for the first green growth of the year and there is nothing else to compare them to. The bulb stays in the ground and is dangerous in every month, including the months when there is no leaf to find it by.
- **Poison hemlock rosette, the dangerous anonymous stage**. The first year rosette stands through autumn, winter and early spring. It bolts and flowers in late spring and early summer, then sets seed and dies. The rosette is when poison hemlock looks most like parsley, and it is green at the exact time of year when a garden or field edge has little else green in it. This overlaps precisely with the wild carrot rosette, which is the root digging stage of an edible plant, and with the cow parsnip rosette. The dead standing stems remain poisonous. Never dig a white taprooted umbellifer in the rosette stage on the strength of leaf shape.

### June

the entire year fall around midday near the solstice, which is the best

The water looks clear during a toxic bloom. Cooking and freezing do not destroy the toxin, because it is in the meat. Check the Department of Health Shellfish Safety Map or the hotline on 1-800-562-5632 on the day you harvest, every time, and never infer safety from a good tide or from last week's status. A separate authority answers a separate question.
- **Shellfish beach seasons**. Beach by beach, set annually by WDFW. There is no statewide month you can rely on. Two independent gates must both be open before you may legally and safely harvest: the WDFW season for that specific beach, and the Department of Health biotoxin status for that specific beach. Neither implies the other, and a good tide implies neither. WDFW also publishes an annual best clam and oyster harvest tides chart, which is the tide half of the same planning problem.
- **Pacific herring spawn**. WDFW gives herring spawning in Puget Sound from January and into June. Herring spawn on nearshore vegetation and the event is visible from the beach: the water turns milky white or teal where a large spawn has happened. It is the hinge of the local food web in late winter. Wintering sea ducks switch from mussels and clams onto herring eggs during spring migration, which is what puts them in condition to fly north and lay, and the spawn draws gulls, eagles and predatory fish at the same time. If you want to know why the inlet suddenly gets busy in February, this is why.
- **Surf smelt spawn, which has no single season**. Year round. Different beaches and stocks spawn at different times, and WDFW surveys for spawn year round. Surf smelt lay eggs about a millimetre across in the upper intertidal on mixed sand and gravel beaches, and a given beach may be a spawning beach in a season you would not expect. The practical consequence is not a harvest date but a construction constraint: WDFW protects documented forage fish spawning beaches from shoreline development, so upper intertidal work on a mixed sand and gravel beach is a permitting question in any month.
- **Salmonberry bloom**. The Forest Service gives March to June for western Oregon and southwestern Washington. Magenta flowers among the first of any shrub in these woods. The Forest Service notes the timing aligns with rufous hummingbird migration and that the birds may be important pollinators, so this bloom is also when the hummingbirds arrive. Leaf flush runs March to April, with first leaves recorded by 21 March in western Washington.
- **Cattail shoots and pollen** (local consensus). Shoots in spring, flower stalks and pollen in early summer, rhizomes dug in autumn and winter. The most productive wild starch in this landscape and available in some form nearly all year, which is rare. The caution is the water, not the plant: cattails grow where runoff collects, so what is upstream matters more than what is in your hand.
- **Camas bloom, the only safe identification window**. May to July depending on elevation and snow cover. This is the single most important safety window on this calendar. Common camas and meadow death camas grow in the same ground and their bulbs are indistinguishable, and death camas is lethal. The blue flower of common camas against the creamy white of death camas is the only difference a beginner can reliably see, and it exists for a few weeks a year. Leaves then die back and seeds disperse from late May to August, after which nothing above ground marks the spot. Digging an unmarked bulb in autumn is how people are poisoned. If you intend to harvest camas, you mark the plants in bloom and you dig only what you marked.
- **Death camas leafs out early and blooms late spring**. Green leaves appear early in spring ahead of most other growth; creamy white flowers in late spring; the plant dies back by summer. The early leaves are the hazard, because they come up when people and livestock are looking for the first green growth of the year and there is nothing else to compare them to. The bulb stays in the ground and is dangerous in every month, including the months when there is no leaf to find it by.
- **Water hemlock root, dangerous in every month**. Shoots in spring, white umbels in summer, top dies back in autumn. The tuberous root is in the ground all twelve months. The most poisonous plant in North America grows in the same wet ground as water parsley, hemlock waterparsnip and cattail, all of which are gathered. In winter nothing above ground shows where it is, and the root is the most poisonous part. This is why the calendar cannot make wet ground digging safe at any time of year, and why the honest advice for winter root digging in a wet meadow is not to.
- **Salmonberry ripens, the first fruit of the year**. June into July around Puget Sound. The Forest Service gives fruit ripening June to July in the southern range and seed dispersal June through August. The first wild fruit of the year and the one that tells you the foraging season has opened. Ripens 30 to 36 days after pollination, and the fruiting period spreads over several weeks within a population, so a patch yields for longer than a single plant does. Colour is not ripeness: they run yellow through orange to deep red and all of those can be ripe, so go by how easily the fruit lifts off.
- **Indian plum fruit**. In the Pacific Northwest, dispersal may begin in May and be nearly finished in July. Ripens far earlier than most people expect and is gone before the main berry season starts. Fruit runs peach coloured, then reddish, then deep blue black under a whitish bloom when ripe. Only female plants fruit, and fewer than 60 percent of pistils set, so a good shrub is worth remembering. Birds strip them fast.
- **Trailing blackberry ripens**. June through August, earlier on warm open sites. The Forest Service gives fruit ripening June to August. The native blackberry, and the better one. Small, seedy and far more strongly flavoured than the Himalayan, ripening a full month or more earlier. It trails along the ground on thin wiry stems rather than forming the head high thickets, so it is easy to walk over without seeing. Worth deliberately looking down for in July.
- **Red elderberry ripens**. The Forest Service gives flowering April to July and fruit June to September in Washington, with seed dispersal June to July, and notes ripening may extend to December in a mild winter. Bright red clusters, very conspicuous, and conditional food at best: the seeds, bark, leaves and roots are toxic and the raw fruit causes vomiting. Treat it as something to know rather than something to pick. It is a useful marker of the calendar because it is the most visible fruit of midsummer.
- **Great blue herons gather at nesting colonies** (local consensus). Birds gather at colonies from roughly February onward, and colonies are in use through spring and early summer. Herons are here all year but they concentrate at colonies in late winter, and that is the vulnerable moment. Disturbing an active colony can cause the whole colony to abandon, not just one nest, so this is the season to keep well back from a stand of big stick nests. Plan any noisy work near one outside February to June.
- **Bald eagles at the nest** (local consensus). Birds at the nest from roughly January or February, with young fledging in mid summer. Eagles nest remarkably early here, starting in the depth of winter, which is only possible because the autumn salmon runs fed them first. Numbers rise through autumn and winter as birds concentrate on the runs and the carcasses afterward, and then those same birds are on eggs by February. The chum run and the eagle nesting season are the same food chain three months apart.
- **Pileated woodpecker breeding season**. WDFW gives the breeding season as late March through early July. A year round resident whose drumming becomes constant in spring. It excavates the large cavities that a long list of other species later nest in, so a big dead tree left standing is worth more than the firewood in it.
- **Band-tailed pigeons present** (local consensus). Present spring through early autumn, largely gone south by late autumn. A native forest pigeon that most people mistake for a feral rock dove. It is a migrant here and a heavy user of elderberry and cascara fruit, so its season tracks the berry season closely.
- **Harbor seal pupping**. NOAA records that females usually give birth during the spring and summer and that the pupping season varies by location, with Washington inland waters running roughly two months later than coastal populations. A pup resting alone on a beach has usually not been abandoned; its mother is feeding offshore and will return. Do not touch it, do not put it in the water, keep dogs and people well back, and call the NOAA Marine Mammal Stranding Network. This is the season when a well meaning rescue does the most harm.
- **Southern Resident killer whales in inland waters**. NOAA gives their range as including the inland waterways of Washington State during spring, summer and autumn. They follow the salmon, so their presence is a direct readout of how the runs are doing. Dyes Inlet has hosted them: the practical point is that this is a seasonal possibility rather than a reliable sighting, and that approach distances are legally regulated.
- **The window for cutting invasive shrubs** (local consensus). Scotch broom flowers roughly April into June and sets pods through summer. Cut before the pods ripen. Timing is what makes the difference between control and spread. Cutting broom in flower and before seed set prevents a year of seeding; cutting it once the pods have ripened scatters them. The same logic applies to tansy ragwort, whose second year plants flower June into September and carry their highest alkaloid load at flowering. Late summer is the wrong time for both.

### July

The driest month of the year at 21 mm, against a mean high of 24.7 C.
Irrigation is not optional. Berries run hard: trailing blackberry, red
huckleberry, red elderberry, Oregon grape. Chinook are in the marine
areas. Fire risk begins to matter in a landscape nobody thinks of as
flammable.

- **Frost free growing season**. A median of 227 frost free days, running roughly from the end of March to the middle of November. Long enough for two successions of most cool season crops and one of warm season crops. The limit here is not frost but summer water and autumn light: the season is generous at both ends and stingy in the middle.
- **The summer dry spell**. Late June through mid September, sharpest in July and August. July gets 21 mm and August 28 mm against a July mean high of 24.7 C. A garden here needs irrigation in summer despite the region's wet reputation, and that surprises people who move here. It is also when the fuels dry out: this is the fire window in a landscape everyone assumes is too wet to burn. Plan the summer water supply in the wet season, because there is no rain to catch when you need it.
- **The daylight minus tides**. Mid April through mid August. In 2026 every daylight low tide at or below -2.0 ft MLLW fell between April 18 and August 12; across 2026 to 2029 the earliest is April 11 and the latest August 12. This is the single most useful tide fact for anyone digging clams here, and it is not obvious. Dyes Inlet gets roughly the same number of deep minus tides every month of the year, but in the winter half they all happen in the middle of the night and in the summer half they all happen around midday. Between about mid April and mid August you can dig a deep low in daylight; outside it you cannot, whatever the tide table total says. There are only about 22 to 26 such daylight tides in a whole year, in clusters of four to six days around the new and full moon, so they are worth marking on a calendar in advance. ### August

Himalayan blackberry ripens in quantity, huckleberries and salal come

### September

The hinge of the year. Rain returns to 46 mm and daylight drops below 13
hours. Coho stage off the creek mouths waiting for rain, acorns and
hazelnuts fall, evergreen huckleberry and blue elderberry ripen, and the
first real soaking rain starts the mushroom season whenever it happens
to come.

- **Frost free growing season**. A median of 227 frost free days, running roughly from the end of March to the middle of November. Long enough for two successions of most cool season crops and one of warm season crops. The limit here is not frost but summer water and autumn light: the season is generous at both ends and stingy in the middle.
- **The summer dry spell**. Late June through mid September, sharpest in July and August. July gets 21 mm and August 28 mm against a July mean high of 24.7 C. A garden here needs irrigation in summer despite the region's wet reputation, and that surprises people who move here. It is also when the fuels dry out: this is the fire window in a landscape everyone assumes is too wet to burn. Plan the summer water supply in the wet season, because there is no rain to catch when you need it.
- **Marine biotoxin closures, which have no season**. Any month. There is no safe season and no pattern you can plan around. This is the one thing on this calendar that a calendar cannot answer. Paralytic, diarrhetic and amnesic shellfish poisoning come from algal blooms that the Washington Department of Health tests for continuously, and a closure can appear in any month. The water looks clear during a toxic bloom. Cooking and freezing do not destroy the toxin, because it is in the meat. Check the Department of Health Shellfish Safety Map or the hotline on 1-800-562-5632 on the day you harvest, every time, and never infer safety from a good tide or from last week's status. A separate authority answers a separate question.
- **Shellfish beach seasons**. Beach by beach, set annually by WDFW. There is no statewide month you can rely on. Two independent gates must both be open before you may legally and safely harvest: the WDFW season for that specific beach, and the Department of Health biotoxin status for that specific beach. Neither implies the other, and a good tide implies neither. WDFW also publishes an annual best clam and oyster harvest tides chart, which is the tide half of the same planning problem.
- **The coho run** (local consensus). September into December, later than the chum and pushing further upstream. Coho hold at the creek mouths and wait for the first heavy autumn rain to raise the water before running. That makes them the one salmon event you should watch the forecast for rather than the calendar: a dry October delays them, and a big early storm brings them in days. They spawn further up than chum, so they are the fish that reaches the smaller tributaries. Because juveniles then spend about a year and a half in fresh water, a creek only holds coho if it holds water all summer, which makes them the honest indicator of whether a stream is actually healthy.
- **The chinook run**. Summer into early autumn. NOAA gives chinook returning to spawn generally in summer or early fall. Puget Sound chinook are listed as threatened under the Endangered Species Act, and spring, summer and fall run stocks all exist in the Sound. For a person here this is mostly a marine fishery rather than a creek spectacle, and the rules are reset annually and amended mid season by emergency rule, so last year's dates are worthless. The runs also do not all behave alike, which is why the ESA listing covers a whole set of populations rather than one season.
- **The pink run, odd years only**. August through October, and only in odd numbered years in Puget Sound. Pink salmon live a strict two year cycle, so a Puget Sound run exists in odd years and is effectively absent in even ones. The last run was 2025 and the next is 2027. A simulation set in an even year should show none. One honest caveat: NOAA notes that pink salmon do spawn in even years in some Puget Sound rivers, so the odd year rule is a statement about the harvestable run here, not a biological absolute.
- **Salmon viewing season** (local consensus). WDFW gives peak viewing months as between July and November statewide. Around Dyes Inlet the reliable month is November. WDFW names seven Kitsap County viewing sites, including Chico Salmon Park, Clear Creek Trail, Poulsbo Fish Park and Salmon Haven at Dickerson Creek. WSU Extension runs the annual Kitsap Salmon Tours in early November, which is the local answer to when to go. WDFW's own advice if the creek looks empty is to try again in a few days or weeks, after rainfall, or at another location, which is a reminder that these runs are rain driven and not date driven. Approach slowly and stay out of the water: quick movement reads as a predator.
- **Surf smelt spawn, which has no single season**. Year round. Different beaches and stocks spawn at different times, and WDFW surveys for spawn year round. Surf smelt lay eggs about a millimetre across in the upper intertidal on mixed sand and gravel beaches, and a given beach may be a spawning beach in a season you would not expect. The practical consequence is not a harvest date but a construction constraint: WDFW protects documented forage fish spawning beaches from shoreline development, so upper intertidal work on a mixed sand and gravel beach is a permitting question in any month.
- **Water hemlock root, dangerous in every month**. Shoots in spring, white umbels in summer, top dies back in autumn. The tuberous root is in the ground all twelve months. The most poisonous plant in North America grows in the same wet ground as water parsley, hemlock waterparsnip and cattail, all of which are gathered. In winter nothing above ground shows where it is, and the root is the most poisonous part. This is why the calendar cannot make wet ground digging safe at any time of year, and why the honest advice for winter root digging in a wet meadow is not to.
- **Deadly mushrooms fruit with the same rain as the good ones** (estimated). With the autumn flush. The exact season for Kitsap County is not established from any public source. Funeral bell fruits alongside honey mushroom and on the same wood, and it contains the same toxin as the death cap. The death cap season for western Washington is not established from any public source either, so this calendar cannot tell you a month to be careful in. Be careful in all of them. No seasonal rule substitutes for identification, and the autumn flush that brings chanterelles brings these at the same time and in the same woods.
- **Himalayan blackberry ripens**. The Forest Service gives flowering June to August and fruit ripening August to September in Washington. The invasive one, and by volume the biggest wild food source in this landscape by a wide margin. It ripens about a month after the native trailing blackberry and bears far more heavily. Picking it is the one foraging activity with no conservation downside at all. The thickets themselves are the problem: they shade out everything and are miserable to clear, so harvest is not control.
- **Red huckleberry ripens**. July through September. The Forest Service seasonal table gives flowering April to June for the Northwest and fruit ripening August to September in Washington, July to August in Oregon. Translucent red berries on bright green twigged shrubs, very often growing straight out of a rotting stump or nurse log, which is the easiest way to find them. Sharp rather than sweet. Note the source disagreement honestly: the Washington row of the Forest Service table says August to September while the local record says July through September with a July and August peak, so late July is the early edge rather than a reliable start.
- **Evergreen huckleberry ripens, latest of the berries**. Fruit ripens from July to September across the range and often hangs into October. Flowering is recorded as May to June in Mason County, Washington, the county next door. The last berry of the year and the best keeper, small and dark and sweeter after a frost. Because it ripens well after everything else and holds on the evergreen shrub into October, it extends the foraging season by a month past the point most people stop looking.
- **Salal ripens**. The Forest Service gives fruit ripening August through October, with fruit persisting on the stem until December. Flowering is May to July in western Oregon and southwestern Washington. The most abundant understory berry here and the most underrated: mealy raw, excellent cooked or dried, and it hangs on the plant long after ripening so the harvest window is unusually forgiving. A Washington record notes fruiting starting in the third week of June, so the very earliest fruit predates the main window by some weeks.
- **Red elderberry ripens**. The Forest Service gives flowering April to July and fruit June to September in Washington, with seed dispersal June to July, and notes ripening may extend to December in a mild winter. Bright red clusters, very conspicuous, and conditional food at best: the seeds, bark, leaves and roots are toxic and the raw fruit causes vomiting. Treat it as something to know rather than something to pick. It is a useful marker of the calendar because it is the most visible fruit of midsummer.
- **Blue elderberry ripens**. Flowering May to July, fruit ripe from about late August. The Forest Service records fruits ripe 21 August and seed fall starting 18 September in Oregon. The elderberry actually worth picking, dusty blue under a waxy bloom, and cooked it is the best preserve in this landscape. The rule is the same as for the red: cook it, and take only the fruit, never the green parts or the stems.
- **Oregon grape ripens** (local consensus). Fruit ripens July to August on dull Oregon grape; tall Oregon grape ripens to a dusty blue black by early autumn. Intensely sour and full of seed, which is why almost nobody eats it raw and why it makes very good jelly. Both species are evergreen, so they are findable in every month, and marking a good stand in winter costs nothing.
- **Bitter cherry ripens** (local consensus). Flowers April to May, fruit ripens July through September. Named accurately. The value here is not food but timing and wildlife: it is one of the earliest tree blossoms in April and a heavy bird crop in late summer. The stones, like all Prunus, contain cyanogenic compounds.
- **Hazelnuts ripen**. Fruit ripening September to October in California and September to November on the west coast; August to September in British Columbia. Nuts abscise when fully ripe in autumn. The most calorific wild food in this landscape and the hardest to get, because squirrels and jays strip them within days of ripeness. The practical answer is to watch the husks from August and pick slightly early, then cure them, rather than waiting for a clean drop that will never come. California hazelnut seeds may hang on the shrub longer than beaked hazelnut and are sometimes winter persistent.
- **Oregon white oak acorns fall** (local consensus). Acorns ripen and fall in autumn, roughly September into October. The only oak here, and uncommon enough that a stand is worth knowing. Acorns need leaching to be food. Their bigger role is ecological: an oak woodland is the richest wildlife habitat in the Puget lowlands and the rarest, which is also the habitat Indian plum is most associated with.
- **The autumn mushroom flush, which rain triggers rather than the calendar**. There is no date. The National Park Service records for Olympic National Park, directly across Hood Canal, that while mushrooms can be found nearly year round, the peak season occurs after the start of fall rains up until the first major frosts. In practice that is the stretch between the September rains returning and the first hard freeze, which the frost normals put around the end of October at the earliest. The season ends at the first hard frost, which the climate normals put anywhere from 26 October to 1 December.

- **Surf smelt spawn, which has no single season**. Year round. Different beaches and stocks spawn at different times, and WDFW surveys for spawn year round. Surf smelt lay eggs about a millimetre across in the upper intertidal on mixed sand and gravel beaches, and a given beach may be a spawning beach in a season you would not expect. The practical consequence is not a harvest date but a construction constraint: WDFW protects documented forage fish spawning beaches from shoreline development, so upper intertidal work on a mixed sand and gravel beach is a permitting question in any month.
- **Poison hemlock rosette, the dangerous anonymous stage**. The first year rosette stands through autumn, winter and early spring. It bolts and flowers in late spring and early summer, then sets seed and dies. The rosette is when poison hemlock looks most like parsley, and it is green at the exact time of year when a garden or field edge has little else green in it. This overlaps precisely with the wild carrot rosette, which is the root digging stage of an edible plant, and with the cow parsnip rosette. The dead standing stems remain poisonous. Never dig a white taprooted umbellifer in the rosette stage on the strength of leaf shape.
- **Water hemlock root, dangerous in every month**. Shoots in spring, white umbels in summer, top dies back in autumn. The tuberous root is in the ground all twelve months. The most poisonous plant in North America grows in the same wet ground as water parsley, hemlock waterparsnip and cattail, all of which are gathered. In winter nothing above ground shows where it is, and the root is the most poisonous part. This is why the calendar cannot make wet ground digging safe at any time of year, and why the honest advice for winter root digging in a wet meadow is not to.
- **Poison oak drops its leaves and keeps its oil**. Leafless from late autumn through early spring. The Forest Service gives flowering March to June and fruit dispersal in summer and autumn. Losing the leaves removes the only identification character most people know, while the urushiol stays on the bare stems. A leafless, thornless vine in winter is not identified, and firewood gathering season is exactly when people handle unidentified bare stems. New spring leaves are often glossy and reddish.
- **Deadly mushrooms fruit with the same rain as the good ones** (estimated). With the autumn flush. The exact season for Kitsap County is not established from any public source. Funeral bell fruits alongside honey mushroom and on the same wood, and it contains the same toxin as the death cap. The death cap season for western Washington is not established from any public source either, so this calendar cannot tell you a month to be careful in. Be careful in all of them. No seasonal rule substitutes for identification, and the autumn flush that brings chanterelles brings these at the same time and in the same woods.
- **Madrone berries ripen**. Berries ripen from early autumn until December or January; seed collectors gather fruit from October to December. One of very few fruits still ripening as the year closes, which makes it disproportionately important to birds in early winter. Range wide dates rather than a Silverdale record, so treat the months as indicative.
- **Bigleaf maple seed disperses**. Seeds ripe August to October, dispersing October to January, with some remaining on the tree until March. The paired winged samaras spin down through the whole wet season rather than dropping at once. Practically this is a gutter and drain problem as much as a botanical event, and it is the reason the tree seeds itself into every disturbed corner.
- **The autumn mushroom flush, which rain triggers rather than the calendar**. There is no date. The National Park Service records for Olympic National Park, directly across Hood Canal, that while mushrooms can be found nearly year round, the peak season occurs after the start of fall rains up until the first major frosts. In practice that is the stretch between the September rains returning and the first hard freeze, which the frost normals put around the end of October at the earliest. This is the clearest case on the calendar of an event that a date would misrepresent. Fruiting here is triggered by the first sustained autumn rain after the summer drought, so in a year when the rain arrives in early September the season starts a month earlier than in a year when it holds off until October. Watch the forecast and the calendar together: count roughly from the first real soaking rain, not from a fixed day. The season ends at the first hard frost, which the climate normals put anywhere from 26 October to 1 December.
- **Winter waterfowl arrive on the inlet**. WDFW records birds departing coastal moulting areas from late August through November and moving to wintering areas, primarily in Puget Sound. Most numerous here roughly October through April. The inlet changes character completely for half the year. Surf scoters are the most abundant of the diving ducks and feed on the mussels and clams of the same beaches people dig. They switch onto herring eggs during spring migration, so the waterfowl season and the herring spawn are the same story told twice. An empty looking Dyes Inlet in July is normal; an empty one in January means something is wrong.
- **Varied thrush comes down into town** (local consensus). In the Silverdale lowlands mainly October through April. It breeds upslope in denser wetter forest and moves down into town for the winter, so its arrival is an altitude signal rather than a latitude one. It becomes most obvious feeding on the ground under shrubs during cold snaps, which makes it a good indicator that the weather has turned hard enough to push birds off the hills.
- **Black-tailed deer rut**. November and December locally. The Forest Service gives the rut for the species as beginning as early as September and ending as late as March depending on location, with one studied population conceiving 75 percent of fawns between 21 November and 1 December. Bucks move far more than usual and lose their caution, and vehicle collisions peak with it. Drive the wooded roads at dawn and dusk in November expecting a deer in the road. Fawns follow from late May to mid July in the northern part of the range, which is when a doe will be aggressive near cover and when a fawn lying alone is not abandoned.
- **Black bears feed hard before denning** (local consensus). Active roughly March through November in western Washington, with the heaviest feeding in late summer and autumn. Most bear conflict happens in the weeks before denning, when a bear needs enormous daily calories and stops being fussy about where they come from. That is exactly the season of ripe fruit, full compost heaps, and rubbish waiting for collection. They den through the coldest months rather than truly hibernating, so a mild winter can keep them up.

### December

The wettest and darkest month: 252 mm and 8.4 hours of daylight.
December alone brings nearly as much rain as April through September
combined. King tides plus storm surge produce the highest water of the
year, while the lowest tides fall near midnight. Maple sap can run on a
cold snap, and madrone berries are among the only fruit left.

- **First autumn frost**. October 26 at the 10th percentile, November 14 on average, December 1 at the 90th percentile. Have row cover ready by October 26, not by the average. One year in ten has already frozen by that date. This is also the end of the outdoor tomato and basil season and the point at which bracken and other soft growth dies back brown.
- **The wet half of the year**. October through March. These six months deliver 1,154 mm of the annual 1,446 mm, which is 79.8 percent of the year's water. December alone brings 252 mm, nearly as much as April through September put together (294 mm across six months). This is the half of the year to design drainage for, to expect saturated ground and landslide risk in, and to fill every cistern in. It is also the half in which soil work compacts rather than tills.
- **Winter minus tides run at night**. November through February, the deep lows falling between roughly 22:00 and 02:00. The tide table will show you tides as low as any in June, and they are real, but they are in the dark and in the cold. Digging them means a headlamp, a falling tide you cannot see the edge of, and a long walk back over ground that is filling behind you. Treat a winter minus tide as an expert trip, not a family one, and note that the biotoxin question is unchanged by the season.
- **King tides and the winter flood risk**. Late December and January, when the highest astronomical tides coincide with the storm season. The highest water ever observed at Bremerton, 4.74 m above MLLW on 27 December 2022, exceeded the highest astronomical tide by 0.45 m. That extra half metre was storm surge stacked on an already extreme tide. A shoreline structure has to survive the tide plus the surge plus the wind waves, not the tide alone, and the season for all three arriving together is the turn of the year.
- **Marine biotoxin closures, which have no season**. Any month. There is no safe season and no pattern you can plan around. This is the one thing on this calendar that a calendar cannot answer. Paralytic, diarrhetic and amnesic shellfish poisoning come from algal blooms that the Washington Department of Health tests for continuously, and a closure can appear in any month. The water looks clear during a toxic bloom. Cooking and freezing do not destroy the toxin, because it is in the meat. Check the Department of Health Shellfish Safety Map or the hotline on 1-800-562-5632 on the day you harvest, every time, and never infer safety from a good tide or from last week's status. A separate authority answers a separate question.
- **Shellfish beach seasons**. Beach by beach, set annually by WDFW. There is no statewide month you can rely on. Two independent gates must both be open before you may legally and safely harvest: the WDFW season for that specific beach, and the Department of Health biotoxin status for that specific beach. Neither implies the other, and a good tide implies neither. WDFW also publishes an annual best clam and oyster harvest tides chart, which is the tide half of the same planning problem.
- **The chum run** (local consensus). Late October into December in the local creeks. This is the biggest wildlife event of the year in central Kitsap and the easiest to see. Chum spawn in the lowest, most accessible reaches of the creeks, within a short walk of salt water, so Chico Creek and the other Dyes Inlet streams put spawning salmon within a few metres of a trail. NOAA records chum spawning from late summer to March with the peak in early winter when flows are high, and the local peak is November. The carcasses afterward are the year's biggest nutrient pulse into the creeks and draw eagles, gulls and bears.
- **The coho run** (local consensus). September into December, later than the chum and pushing further upstream. Coho hold at the creek mouths and wait for the first heavy autumn rain to raise the water before running. That makes them the one salmon event you should watch the forecast for rather than the calendar: a dry October delays them, and a big early storm brings them in days. They spawn further up than chum, so they are the fish that reaches the smaller tributaries. Because juveniles then spend about a year and a half in fresh water, a creek only holds coho if it holds water all summer, which makes them the honest indicator of whether a stream is actually healthy.
- **The winter steelhead run**. Late autumn into spring. The local fish are winter run. Unlike the salmon, steelhead do not all die after spawning, and they run through the months when nothing else does. Summer run fish are mostly east of the Cascades, so a steelhead in a Kitsap creek is a winter fish. Seasons here are tightly restricted and frequently closed outright; read the current rules rather than assuming an open season exists.
- **Surf smelt spawn, which has no single season**. Year round. Different beaches and stocks spawn at different times, and WDFW surveys for spawn year round. ### What this calendar deliberately leaves out

Three things a reader might expect and will not find. First, no
month-by-month biotoxin season: closures have no season and the calendar
must not imply the tide answers that question. Second, no mushroom
dates: fruiting here is triggered by rain, not by the calendar, so a
date would be a fabrication. Third, no fixed shellfish beach seasons:
those are set beach by beach and annually, and reprinting last year's
would be worse than silence.

Half the land around Silverdale (49.9 percent) is one soil: Alderwood, a
moderately well drained gravelly sandy loam sitting on dense glacial
till at roughly 50 to 100 cm. Add the other till soils (Kapowsin 5.3,
Harstine 2.8, McKenna 2.6, Shelton 2.1, Kapowsin variant 1.2) and about
64 percent of the area is a thin gravelly mantle over a hardpan. That
single geometry explains almost every practical problem here: water
perches on the till through the wet season (tables at 46 to 75 cm), then
the shallow mantle above it dries out completely in the Puget Sound
summer rain shadow, because Alderwood holds only about 8.7 cm of
plant-available water in a metre and a half and Shelton holds barely 5.
Winter-wet and summer-droughty on the same ground is the default
condition, not an anomaly.

## Water

**The watershed.** Kitsap (WRIA 15). WRIA 15 is named in RCW
90.94.030(2)(a) as one of eight watersheds with a Streamflow Restoration
committee. Ecology adopted the WRIA 15 watershed plan in December 2024.

**Where it goes.** Runoff at Silverdale reaches the open ocean through a
chain of tidal basins, not a river. Each step below is the USGS
Watershed Boundary Dataset 'tohuc' pointer, followed from the Silverdale
HUC12 outward.

**Surface water nearby.** Dyes Inlet, Clear Creek, West Fork Clear
Creek, Strawberry Creek, Barker Creek, Koch Creek, Chico Creek,
Dickerson Creek, Wildcat Creek, Mosher Creek.

**Groundwater.** Yes. Groundwater is the normal domestic water source on
the Kitsap Peninsula, and it is the only one: the peninsula is
surrounded by saltwater and, as USGS puts it, 'the hydrologic setting is
similar to that of an island'. There is no upstream river to divert
from.

## The tide

**Pattern.** Two highs and two lows per day, but the pair are markedly
unequal. The mean diurnal high water inequality at Bremerton is 0.273 m
and the mean diurnal LOW water inequality is 0.870 m, so it is the two
lows that differ most. One of the two daily low tides is much lower than
the other, which is why local low-tide beach access is so uneven day to
day.

**A caution about the numbers.** NOT a NOAA published datum. Derived
here by applying NOAA's own subordinate-station ratio method to the
Seattle datums. Use it for planning only; for anything you would wade or
anchor by, use the live predictions for station 9445901 or the published
Bremerton datums.

**Currents.** Peak currents through the Narrows reach about 4.5 knots on
the flood and 4 knots on the ebb, and the typical daily maximum is over
2 knots. That is faster than a person can swim. Slack water windows
exist between each reversal and are the only safe crossing times.

## What can actually hurt you here

Not a generic list. Somewhere on a subduction zone has a different
honest preparedness answer from somewhere on a floodplain, and preparing
for the wrong one is worse than not preparing at all. The column that
matters most is warning time, because it decides whether preparation or
reaction is the useful skill.

### Cascadia Subduction Zone megathrust earthquake (M~9)

The defining regional event. Cascadia's fault is more than 800 km (500
miles) long and shaking from these earthquakes 'can last for a few
minutes' (WA DNR). Silverdale sits well inland of the offshore rupture,
so it is a long-duration, moderate-to-strong shaking problem rather than
near-fault violent shaking: the hazards here are duration-driven -
liquefaction of the Clear Creek delta fill at the head of Dyes Inlet and
the waterfront, landslides on the Dyes Inlet bluffs, and multi-week loss
of lifelines across a peninsula reached by two bridges and ferrie.
Long-duration shaking; liquefaction risk concentrated on artificial
fill, tidal flats and stream sediments (the Silverdale waterfront and
Clear Creek mouth sit on exactly that); bluff landsliding around Dyes
Inlet; prolonged isolation of the Kitsap Peninsula.

**Likelihood:** 10-15 percent chance of an approximately magnitude 9
earthquake on the Cascadia Subduction Zone in the next 50 years (USGS,
2025). **Return period:** Full-margin ruptures approximately every 500
years; partial-margin ruptures approximately every 1,000 years (USGS
Fact Sheet 2025-3050, fig. 4). The last event was the winter of
1699-1700, about 325 years ago. NOTE: the '330-year' figure in that fact
sheet is for SOUTHERN Cascadia only and does not apply here. **Warning
time:** No warning of the earthquake itself beyond ShakeAlert: in the
Pacific Northwest warning times for people experiencing strong shaking
are typically less than 10 seconds, and 50-80 seconds only for users far
from the epicentre of an offshore megathrust. For the tsunami, see the
Cascadia tsunami entry - hours, not minutes, inside Puget Sound.

### Deep intraslab (Benioff zone) earthquake, M6.5+

Moderate but widespread. Because the source is deep (tens of km), peak
shaking intensity is lower than for an equivalent-magnitude shallow
crustal earthquake, and these events rarely generate tsunamis. The three
modern examples all damaged this region: 1949 M6.8 Olympia, 1965 M6.5
Seattle, 2001 M6.8 Nisqually. WA DNR notes the deep April 1949 Tacoma
earthquake caused damage from southern Oregon to British Columbia.
Shaking damage to unreinforced masonry and chimneys, liquefaction on
waterfront fill, and landslides on saturated bluffs. The 1949 event
triggered the Tacoma Narrows landslide, which generated a local wave.

**Likelihood:** 85 percent chance of a magnitude 6.5 or greater deep
(intraslab) earthquake in the Puget Sound region in the next 50 years
(USGS, 2025). This is by far the most likely damaging earthquake to
occur in a given lifetime here. **Return period:** Observed intervals in
the instrumental record: 1949 to 1965 is 16 years, 1965 to 2001 is 36
years (USGS Fact Sheet 2025-3050, fig. 3). 24 years have now elapsed
since 2001. **Warning time:** Seconds at most, via ShakeAlert (typically
under 10 seconds where shaking is strong). No other warning.

### Shallow crustal earthquake on the Seattle Fault zone (M6.5-7.5)

The worst-case shaking for this location, because it is shallow and
close. The state/FEMA scenario models a M7.2 rupture on the northernmost
strand from the Kitsap Peninsula to just east of Lake Sammamish (63 km /
40 miles). The 923-924 CE event lifted parts of Restoration Point on
Bainbridge Island 23 feet straight up, generated a tsunami in Puget
Sound, and triggered a large landslide into Lake Washington. Severe
shaking, possible surface rupture, liquefaction, coseismic landsliding
on the Dyes Inlet bluffs, and a locally generated tsunami arriving
within minutes (see tsunami entry).

**Likelihood:** 17 percent chance of a magnitude 6.5 or greater crustal
fault earthquake in the Puget Sound region in the next 50 years (USGS,
2025). The Seattle fault zone passes immediately east and south of
Silverdale; recent geologic mapping suggests it extends from the Olympic
Mountains on the west to the Cascades on the east, and trenching at
Waterman Point and Point Glover in Kitsap County found evidence of
surface-rupturing earthquakes about 1,100 years ago. **Return period:**
Uncertain and long. An 8,000-year investigation found evidence for
possibly one additional earthquake about 6,900 years ago, 'suggesting a
recurrence interval of thousands of years for large earthquakes'.
Trenching on the Bainbridge Island scarp revealed evidence for up to
three surface-rupturing earthquakes in the past 2,500 years. Last known
event: winter 923-924 CE. **Warning time:** Effectively none. Silverdale
is within the fault zone's near field, so ShakeAlert may deliver little
or no useful lead time; strong shaking can arrive with the alert.

### Tsunami from a large Seattle Fault earthquake (the near-field, minutes-not-hours case)

Modelled at the Silverdale point (47.64518, -122.68845): maximum
amplitude 5 ft. Nearby modelled points: Bremerton/Sinclair Inlet 9 ft,
Point White 16 ft, Liberty Bay 7 ft, Agate Passage 11 ft, Bangor 3 ft.
The scenario is described by WGS as 'modeled after a simulated large
magnitude Seattle fault earthquake, similar to that of the 923 CE
event'. A roughly 5 ft wave at the head of Dyes Inlet about 5 minutes
after shaking stops, on top of whatever the tide is doing (the GREAT
DIURNAL range here, highest high to lowest low, is about 11.7 ft, so
tide stage dominates the outcome). Low ground at the Silverdale
waterfront, Old Town, and the Clear Creek mouth is the exposed area.

**Likelihood:** Tied to a large Seattle Fault rupture (see above). The
Washington Geological Survey's modelled inundation extent for this
scenario does reach the head of Dyes Inlet at Silverdale. **Return
period:** Governed by Seattle Fault recurrence: thousands of years
between large earthquakes; last event 923-924 CE. **Warning time:** 5
minutes to the inundating wave at Silverdale (WGS modelled arrival
time). Elsewhere on this shoreline it is worse: 'less than 3 min' at
Bremerton/Sinclair Inlet, Point White and the Bainbridge ferry terminal.
There is no time for an official warning. The earthquake itself is the
warning - WA DNR states that for people in Washington, the single
biggest warning of a potential tsunami is a large earthquake. This is a
self-evacuation-on-shaking hazard, so preparation beats reaction.

### Tsunami from a Cascadia M9.0 megathrust earthquake (the far-field, hours case)

Much smaller inside Puget Sound than the local Seattle Fault case,
because the wave must refract through the Strait of Juan de Fuca and
Admiralty Inlet. The WGS L1 M9.0 model has no separate Silverdale point;
the nearest modelled point, Bremerton, gives a maximum amplitude of 2.5
ft. Bangor 9 ft, Lynch Cove 12 ft. Modest water-level surge and strong
currents in Dyes Inlet hours after the shaking; the dominant local
problem from Cascadia is the shaking and the multi-week infrastructure
outage, not the wave.

**Likelihood:** Accompanies the Cascadia megathrust event (10-15 percent
in 50 years). **Return period:** Approximately 500 years for full-margin
Cascadia ruptures. **Warning time:** About 2 hours 40 minutes to
Bremerton (WGS modelled inundating-wave arrival). Hours of warning
inside Puget Sound, unlike the outer coast. This is the one tsunami case
here where official warning and organised evacuation are realistic.

### Seiche and landslide-generated waves in Puget Sound

The concrete local precedent is the April 1949 deep M~7 earthquake,
which triggered a large landslide on 90 m (300 ft) high bluffs at the
Tacoma Narrows three days later; that slide caused a local tsunami. Dyes
Inlet is a small, enclosed, steep-sided basin with bluffs around it,
which is the geometry in which both effects occur. Sloshing and abrupt
local waves in Dyes Inlet independent of any open-ocean tsunami; bluff
collapse into the inlet displacing water.

**Likelihood:** Not quantified in any source located. WA DNR defines a
seiche as 'the sloshing of a closed body of water from earthquake
shaking' and lists seiches among the secondary effects of both
megathrust and shallow crustal earthquakes. Landslide-generated waves
are separately documented: WA DNR states numerous deposits from
landslide-generated tsunamis triggered by local earthquakes are found
throughout Puget Sound, and that delta failures and landslides caused by
shaking may create or amplify tsunamis. **Return period:** Not published
for Dyes Inlet specifically. **Warning time:** None. Both are triggered
by shaking and arrive within seconds to minutes; the delayed 1949
Narrows slide shows failures can also occur days after the earthquake,
so the bluffs stay dangerous after the shaking ends.

### Landslides on coastal bluffs (the everyday geologic hazard here)

Lethal and expensive. In the winter of 1996-1997 coastal landslides in
Kitsap County destroyed millions of dollars of public and private
property and killed a family of four on Bainbridge Island. NOAA's storm
record for the Kitsap Peninsula zones logs 10 debris-flow events since
1996, including a 2007 fatality (a Mason County man killed in bed by a
landslide) and a December 2010 episode causing about 1 million dollars
of damage. Bluff failure around the Dyes Inlet shoreline, taking out
shoreline houses, roads and the ground beneath them; road offsets at
headscarps. Triggers are precipitation, coastal erosion at the bluff
toe, and seismic shaking.

**Likelihood:** High and recurring. USGS mapped 231 landslides in Kitsap
County from lidar, covering 0.8 percent of the county's land area, and
found that 67 percent of the mapped landslide area lies within 500
metres of the present coastline. About 77 percent of mapped landslides
involve Vashon advance outwash deposits. Two mapped landslides sit
directly on the Dyes Inlet shoreline near Silverdale: landslides
104a/104b (27,200 m2 and 5,030 m2, high confidence, on the coastal
bluffs of the east shore and northern end of Dyes Inlet, with 'possible
evidence of recent activity'), and landslide 139 (8,990 m2, west of Dyes
Inlet, north of Chico). **Return period:** Not expressed as a return
period. Empirically: landslide-producing winters recur on a scale of
years, and Kitsap has about 338 km of saltwater coastline that is
'especially susceptible'. **Warning time:** Hours to days for the
rainfall that drives them (NWS forecasts the atmospheric-river storms),
but the failure itself is sudden and gives no useful warning.
Above-normal precipitation was the principal triggering mechanism in
1996-1997. The mechanism is a perched water table: permeable
Esperance-type advance outwash sand over the low-permeability Lawton
Clay, so pore pressure builds during sustained wet weather and the bluff
face fails.

### Winter windstorms

Peak values in that record reach 77 mph (14 Dec 2000) and 71 mph (30 Mar
1997); the 14 Dec 2006 storm produced a measured 64 mph gust.
Kitsap-specific documented effects: on 15 Nov 2006 about 8,000 people
lost power in Kitsap County, three roads were closed or partly blocked
by downed trees and power lines, and the Hood Canal Bridge was closed
for several hours. The largest killer is indirect: the 14 Dec 2006 storm
caused eight indirect fatalities from carbon monoxide poisoning from
improper indoor use of barbecues and generators during the outage. Trees
down across a heavily forested peninsula, multi-day power outages,
closure of the Hood Canal Bridge, and carbon-monoxide deaths from
generators and barbecues used indoors. In a peninsula community the
outage, not the wind, is the hazard.

**Likelihood:** Near-annual, and the most frequent damaging hazard here.
In NOAA's storm record for the NWS zones covering the Kitsap Peninsula,
1996-2025, 24 of 30 years had at least one High Wind or Strong Wind
event; 80 wind events total (45 High Wind, 35 Strong Wind). **Return
period:** Empirically about 0.8 High/Strong Wind events per year in
these zones over 1996-2025; a damaging regional windstorm roughly every
1-2 years, with the severe ones (65 mph+) clustering in 1997-2000 and
2006. **Warning time:** Hours to a few days. These are synoptic-scale
Pacific storms that are forecast well in advance, and NWS Seattle issues
High Wind Watches and Warnings for zone WAZ323. NWS does not publish a
fixed numerical lead time - each office sets local criteria - but this
is the one hazard on this list where preparation time is genuinely
available.

### Flooding (coastal/tidal and small-stream)

Localised rather than catastrophic - there is no large river here. NOAA
logs 18 Flood and 3 Coastal Flood events for these zones since 1996. The
coastal cases are tide-driven: on 17 Dec 2012 record high tides flooded
roads and properties around Puget Sound (the Seattle gauge recorded
14.48 ft) with flooding of a street and apartment parking lots in Port
Orchard; on 27 Dec 2022 flooding damaged business space in Port Orchard
and Hood Canal water rose above Seabeck Highway. Saltwater flooding of
the low Silverdale waterfront and Old Town at extreme high tides, and
floodway flooding along Clear, Barker and Chico Creeks. The great
diurnal range at the reference station, highest high to lowest low, is
about 11.7 ft (the mean range of tide, high to low, is 8.0 ft), so tide
stage governs.

**Likelihood:** Mapped and regulated. FEMA's National Flood Hazard Layer
shows a 1-percent-annual-chance (100-year) special flood hazard area
along the Dyes Inlet shoreline at Silverdale and up the creek corridors,
with 0.2-percent-annual-chance (500-year) Zone X beyond it. Point
checks: the Silverdale waterfront at the head of Dyes Inlet is Zone AE
with a base flood elevation of 14 ft; the Chico Creek mouth is Zone AE
with BFE 13 ft; the Barker Creek mouth is Zone AE FLOODWAY. The built-up
upland of Silverdale (CDP centroid, Clear Creek at Silverdale Way,
Bucklin Hill) is Zone X, area of minimal flood hazard. **Return
period:** 1 percent annual chance (100-year) for the mapped AE zones;
0.2 percent annual chance (500-year) for the shaded X zone. Kitsap
County FIRM panels covering Silverdale are effective 2010-11-04 and
2017-02-03, DFIRM 53035C, scale 1:6000. **Warning time:** Hours to days.
Coastal flooding here is the predictable combination of a high
astronomical tide, low barometric pressure and onshore wind, so NWS
coastal flood advisories are issued in advance; small-stream flooding
follows forecast heavy-rain events.

### Wildfire

The westside Douglas-fir fire regime is infrequent but stand-replacing:
the mapped historical events here are hundreds of thousands to over a
million acres. The modern risk is a late-summer/early-autumn ignition in
continuous second-growth timber during the annual summer drought, in a
wildland-urban interface with limited road egress off the peninsula.
Late-season timber fire in second-growth forest around Green and Gold
Mountains and the peninsula interior; the constraint is evacuation off a
peninsula, not fire behaviour alone.

**Likelihood:** Low by Washington standards, but not zero, and it is the
hazard most likely to be underestimated here. The interagency fire
perimeter history contains no modern (post-1900) mapped fire perimeter
within about 15 km of Silverdale; the only perimeters intersecting that
area are three very large pre-settlement fire events dated 1308, 1508
and 1701 CE. NOAA's storm record logs a single Wildfire event for these
zones in 30 years: 4-13 Oct 2012, 230 acres of timber north of Shelton,
after 'little or no precipitation had fallen since mid July'. **Return
period:** The mapped large-fire intervals near Silverdale are 1308,
1508, 1701 - roughly 200 and 193 years apart - with nothing mapped
since. Treat as a centuries-scale, high-severity regime rather than an
annual one. No published modern return period for this location was
found. **Warning time:** Days for the fire-weather setup (NWS issues Red
Flag Warnings for fire weather zone WAZ654), but minutes to hours for an
actual fire run.

### Heavy snow and ice (included because the record says it outranks wind by count)

Wet, heavy lowland snow near freezing, which loads trees and brings down
limbs and power lines; combined with the terrain it isolates hillside
neighbourhood. Power loss and road closures on the steep Bucklin Hill /
bluff-top roads; same generator carbon-monoxide risk as windstorms.

**Likelihood:** 48 Heavy Snow events, 19 Winter Weather, 4 Winter Storm
and 3 Ice Storm events in NOAA's record for these zones since 1996 -
more logged events than High Wind. Heavy-snow years occurred in 18 of
the 30 years. **Return period:** Roughly 3 of every 5 winters produce at
least one heavy-snow, winter-storm or ice-storm event in these zones.
**Warning time:** Days. Forecast well in advance by NWS Seattle.

## Who to ask

Some questions have no general answer, only a local one: what may be
taken, what may be planted, whether the shellfish are safe today, what
water you may legally draw. These are the bodies that answer them here.

- **WSU Extension, Kitsap County** (https://extension.wsu.edu/kitsap/)  
- **Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife** (https://wdfw.wa.gov/)  
- **Washington State Department of Health, Marine Biotoxins** (https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/shellfish/recreational-shellfish/illnesses/biotoxins). Check before every shellfish harvest, not once. Cooking and freezing do not destroy paralytic shellfish poison. Hotline 1-800-562-5632.  
- **Washington State Noxious Weed Control Board** (https://www.nwcb.wa.gov/). Lists the county board contacts, including Kitsap.  
- **Washington State Department of Ecology, Water Rights** (https://ecology.wa.gov/water-shorelines/water-supply/water-rights). WRIA 15 overrides the statewide groundwater permit exemption, so what you may legally draw here is not the statewide answer.  
- **WSU Puyallup, Soils** (https://puyallup.wsu.edu/soils/)
