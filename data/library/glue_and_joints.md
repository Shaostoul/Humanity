# Glue and Joints

When a chair wobbles, a gate sags or a drawer falls apart, the wood is
rarely what broke. It is the joint. The Army's carpentry manual says it
directly: "Weak points in a structure usually occur at the connections
(joints and splices) between pieces of lumber." It adds that those
connections can be sound if they are done correctly, and that weak ones
are usually a sign of poor workmanship.

There are three ways to make two pieces into one: shape them so they
lock together (the joint), hold them with metal (nails, screws and
bolts), or bond them (glue). Most good work uses two of the three. This
guide is about the first and the last: which joints are strong and
which only look it, how glue actually holds, how to choose a glue and
use it, and when glue needs fasteners beside it. Fasteners themselves
are covered in [Nails, Screws and Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts).

Its facts come from the Wood Handbook, the USDA Forest Service's
reference on wood as an engineering material, whose chapter on
adhesives is the main source here; from the Army's carpentry manual (FM
5-426) and the armed services' hand tool manual (TM 9-243); from the
Consumer Product Safety Commission's rule on flammable contact
adhesives; from the National Institute for Occupational Safety and
Health (NIOSH); from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA); and from
the federal Poison Help line. All are United States government
publications in the public domain. Where something is general practice
rather than a published rule, the text says so.

Other guides sit beside this one. [Working with
Wood](/library#working-with-wood) covers species, grain and
moisture. [Hand Tools](/library#hand-tools) covers the saw, plane and
chisel that cut a joint. [Square, Level and
Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb) covers getting pieces at the right
angle before you join them. [Rust, Rot and Decay](/library#rust-rot-and-decay)
explains why old paint must not be sanded.

## First: the ways glue work hurts people

The Wood Handbook's warning is general and worth taking literally: many
adhesives cure by chemical reaction and are hazardous before they cure,
while cured adhesives are usually safe to touch. The usual ways in are
through the skin and by breathing the vapour. It tells the user to read
the safety data sheet and follow the maker's instructions, and to assume
an uncured adhesive could be harmful in quantity or with repeated
exposure unless you know otherwise. The label on the tube is the short
version of that sheet; read it before you open the tube (general
practice).

### Solvent contact cement: no flame anywhere in the house

Contact cement is the glue spread on both surfaces, left to dry until
it is barely tacky, and then pressed together for an instant bond. The
solvent kinds give off a lot of flammable vapour while they dry. In a
rule published in December 1977 and in force from 1978, the Consumer
Product Safety Commission banned, for sale to consumers, the most
flammable of them, among other tests those with a flash point at or
below 20 F sold in containers of more than half a pint (16 CFR 1302),
because of burns from explosive vapour and flashback fires. Its findings
are the reason to be careful with every solvent cement:

- The vapour was ignited by pilot lights in stoves, ovens, water heaters
  and furnaces, by electric space heaters with no visible flame, by
  sparks from a refrigerator motor and a wall outlet, and by friction.
- Those sources were often in another part of the house from the room
  where the cement was being used.
- As little as a pint of the most flammable kind could make a
  substantial explosion hazard.
- Warning labels alone did not stop the injuries, partly because a
  visitor or someone in another room could set off the vapour without
  ever seeing the label.

The ban left the less flammable solvent cements on sale; the rule names
them, with non-flammable and water-based cements, as the alternatives.
The non-flammable ones are not harmless. They are made with chlorinated
solvents, and the Wood Handbook says: "Solvents in these adhesives are
generally toxic, but harmful effects can be avoided by providing
adequate ventilation and following the manufacturer's safety
instructions." The handbook also notes that construction and contact
adhesives contain solvents with low flash points whose vapour can build
up and explode in a small space without ventilation.

So, as general practice drawn from the CPSC's findings:

- Follow the label's directions on flames and ventilation.
- Before you open the can, put out every flame, turn off heaters, and
  unplug anything with a motor, the refrigerator included, because its
  motor starts by itself.
- Once vapour is in the air, do not work any switch and do not plug in or
  unplug anything: the CPSC counted sparks from a wall outlet among the
  things that lit it.
- Keep the work ventilated to the outdoors, and keep everyone else away.
- If your home has gas pilot lights, use a water-based contact cement
  instead; the CPSC rule notes that these dry more slowly in humid
  weather. If a pilot light has gone out or been put out, air the house
  out fully before it is relit, and relight it only as the appliance's
  own instructions say.

### Fumes

The EPA lists glues and adhesives among the household products that give
off volatile organic compounds, and says the levels can be very high
while a product is in use and can stay high after the work is done. Its
advice, which suits every glue with a solvent smell: "if a label says to
use the product in a well-ventilated area, go outdoors or in areas
equipped with an exhaust fan to use it. Otherwise, open up windows to
provide the maximum amount of outdoor air possible." It also says to keep
these products out of reach of children and pets, to buy only what you
will use soon, and to take old, unwanted containers to a household
hazardous waste collection rather than the bin. Adhesive removers can
contain methylene chloride, which the EPA says the body converts to
carbon monoxide: use them outdoors when you can.

### Skin and lungs: epoxy and polyurethane

The Wood Handbook warns that the amine hardeners in some epoxies are
strong skin sensitizers, and that chemical sensitivity can come from
repeated exposure to uncured adhesives. It calls the isocyanates in
polyurethane and
similar glues sensitizers capable of causing occupational asthma, and
says chronic contact with the uncured glue should be avoided. It adds
that the formaldehyde hardener used with resorcinol glues "is a severe
irritant." Wear
disposable gloves to mix and spread these glues, keep them off your
skin, wash any that gets on it, and work with air moving. If a rash or
any trouble breathing appears after using them, stop and see a doctor
(general practice).

### Super glue

Super glue is a cyanoacrylate. NIOSH's entry for one cyanoacrylate,
methyl-2-cyanoacrylate, notes that contact with moisture makes it set
rapidly (which is why super glue grabs damp skin so fast, our
explanation), and that it irritates the eyes, skin and nose, with
blurred vision and watering eyes. Its first aid for the eyes is to
irrigate them immediately. So: keep it below eye level and away from
your face; if it gets in an eye, flush the eye with water at once and
get medical care that day; if eyelids are stuck together, do not force
them open, get medical care; and do not pull glued skin apart (general
practice). For a swallowed glue or any
poisoning question, the Poison Help line, 1-800-222-1222, connects you
to your local poison centre from anywhere in the United States, at any
time.

### Hot glue, dust and old paint

- **A hot glue gun** melts its glue, and the nozzle and the glue are hot
  enough to burn. Rest it on its stand, keep fingers off the bead, and
  watch children using one (general practice).
- **Sanding dust.** The Wood Handbook says the lungs cannot handle fine
  dust, even wood dust, and to use a dust mask and ventilation.
- **Old paint.** Repairing an old painted chair or door can mean sanding
  paint that contains lead. [Rust, Rot and Decay](/library#rust-rot-and-decay)
  explains how to tell and what to do instead.

## How glue holds

The Wood Handbook describes a glued joint as a chain of links: the wood,
the zone where the glue has soaked into the wood, the boundary between
glue and wood, and the glue itself. The joint is only as strong as the
weakest link. For the glue to grip, it has to wet the wood, meaning
cover it completely at the molecular scale, and soak into sound wood,
typically two to six cells deep, so that it locks around the fibres.
The handbook's standard for an excellent bond is that the wood breaks
well away from the glue line, and that the bond is as strong as solid
wood or stronger.

Glue turns from liquid to solid in one of three ways, the handbook
explains: water or solvent leaves it, by evaporating or soaking into
the wood; a melted glue cools; or a chemical reaction sets it. It sets
in minutes or hours, but full strength can take hours to days, and some
glues weeks. That is why the clamps come off long before the joint can
take a load.

Everything that stops the glue reaching sound wood weakens the joint.
The handbook names oil, dirt, debris, a crushed or burnished surface,
and the natural oils of some woods such as teak.

## The grain decides

Wood is a bundle of long fibres, and glue holds to their sides far
better than to their ends. The Wood Handbook puts numbers on it:

- **Long grain to long grain**, as when two boards are glued edge to
  edge, can be as strong as the wood itself.
- **End grain to anything** is the weak case. The handbook says it is
  practically impossible to glue an end-grain butt joint strong enough
  for ordinary service, and that even with special techniques such a
  joint reaches only about 25 percent of the wood's strength along the
  grain. End grain soaks glue away from the joint, leaving it starved.
- **A scarf or finger joint** turns an end-to-end joint into a long-grain
  one by cutting both ends at a long slope. To approach the strength of
  clear wood, the glued area has to be at least 10 times the
  cross-section of the piece, and a slope of 1 in 12 or flatter gives
  the highest strength. A well-made scarf, finger or lap joint can reach
  up to 90 percent of the strength of clear wood.
- **End to edge**, the T and the corner where most furniture is joined,
  cannot carry much load glued plain. The handbook says these joints
  need interlocking shapes so that long grain meets long grain, and
  gives dowels, mortise and tenons, and rabbets as examples of strong
  ones.

The carpentry manual agrees from the building side, though it is rating
framing joints held with nails or screws, not glued ones. A butt joint,
the end of one board against another, is the weakest type of joint
because end grain is the weakest part of the wood. A miter joint is very
weak and should not be used where strength matters. The lap joint is the
strongest, and the half-lap, where half the thickness is cut from each
piece so they overlap flush, is relatively strong and easy to make.

A worked example: to join two 3/4 inch thick boards end to end with a 1
in 12 scarf, each end is cut at a slope 12 times as long as the board is
thick, 12 x 3/4 = 9 inches (our arithmetic).

The handbook adds two more points that save wasted effort. A tongue and
groove joint is usually no stronger than a plain, flat edge joint,
because the shaped faces never fit perfectly; its real use is lining
the pieces up while you clamp. And because wood swells and shrinks far
more across the grain than along it, joints that meet end grain to
edge grain suffer large stresses when the moisture changes, and should
be protected from changes in moisture once in use.

| Joint | Strength | What to know |
|---|---|---|
| Edge to edge (long grain) | Can equal the wood, glued | The best joint for glue alone (Wood Handbook) |
| Butt (end to face) | The weakest | Needs fasteners, blocks or a better joint (FM 5-426, rated as nailed or screwed framing) |
| Miter | Very weak | Not where strength matters (FM 5-426, rated as nailed or screwed framing) |
| Half-lap | Relatively strong | Easy to cut (FM 5-426, rated as nailed or screwed framing) |
| Dowel, mortise and tenon, rabbet | Strong, glued | Interlocking shapes give long-grain glue area (Wood Handbook) |
| Scarf or finger, 1 in 12 | Up to 90 percent of clear wood, glued | End to end (Wood Handbook) |

## Choosing a glue

The Wood Handbook's table of adhesives describes the families you will
find in a hardware shop. In short:

| Glue | Good at | Weak at |
|---|---|---|
| PVA, white or yellow "wood glue" | High dry strength; one part, sets at room temperature, cleans up with water; furniture and general home use | Low resistance to moisture and heat; a joint under steady load creeps ("joints yield under continued stress") |
| Cross-linkable PVA (some water-resistant wood glues are this type: general practice) | Like PVA, with better resistance to moisture and heat | Still not for constant wetting (general practice) |
| Polyurethane | Strong dry and wet; fills gaps; needs moisture to cure, so it is spread on one surface, best lightly misted with water | Repeated wetting and drying; isocyanate hazard (above) |
| Epoxy | Two parts mixed; fills gaps; needs only low clamping pressure; bonds wood, metal, glass and plastic | Wood formulations resist water but can come apart under repeated wetting and drying; skin sensitizer |
| Resorcinol | Very resistant to moisture and heat; for laminated timbers and joints in severe service; dark red glue line | Two parts; made mostly for industry; its formaldehyde hardener is a severe irritant (above) |
| Contact cement | Instant bond when the two dry faces meet; plastic laminate on counters | Low strength, creeps under steady load, weak against water; solvent hazard |
| Construction adhesive (caulking-gun tubes) | Fills gaps up to 1/4 inch; tolerates wet or frozen lumber; floors and walls | Needs nails or screws to hold the parts while it sets; strength builds over weeks |
| Hot melt | Gains strength quickly as it cools; fills gaps | Lower strength than wood glues |
| Animal (hide) glue | Applied warm; strong when dry; traditional for furniture | Not water resistant |
| Cyanoacrylate (super glue) | Sets in seconds on contact with moisture (NIOSH); small, close-fitting repairs (general practice) | Fills gaps poorly (general practice) |

The handbook also sorts glues by the job they can do. Structural
adhesives hold up a building for its life and are generally stronger
and stiffer than the wood. Semi-structural ones are strong but may creep
under long loads. Non-structural ones, including the furniture glues,
hold the parts together but often lose strength when wet. Exterior glues
keep their strength through repeated soaking and drying; interior ones
do not. For a job outdoors or in a damp place, check that the label says
the glue is made for it (general practice).

## Gluing up, step by step

1. **Make it fit first.** The Wood Handbook says most wood adhesives do
   not fill gaps well, so the pieces have to meet closely without glue.
   Assemble the joint dry, with the clamps, to check the fit and to
   rehearse the order of work (general practice).
2. **Use fresh, clean surfaces.** The handbook says surfacing within 24
   hours before gluing gives a clean, easily wetted surface, and that
   some woods glue better within minutes. A surface cut cleanly by a
   plane or jointer is best. Sanding is not as good, and feeding wood
   through a jointer too fast leaves a shiny, crushed surface (glazing)
   that glues badly. Its quick test: put a drop of water on the surface. Absorbed
   within 20 seconds is good; still standing as a bead after 40 seconds
   means trouble, and sanding through the surface can renew it.
3. **Check the moisture.** The handbook says most glues work best on wood
   at 6 to 14 percent moisture content, and that a piece should ideally
   be glued at the moisture it will live at. Indoor wood usually sits
   between 5 and 12 percent. Let wood bought from an outdoor rack settle
   in the room where it will be used before you glue it (general
   practice).
4. **Know the glue's times.** The handbook defines them: pot life, how
   long a mixed batch stays usable; open time, between spreading the
   glue and putting the pieces together; closed time, between putting
   them together and clamping; and clamp time. The label gives each.
   Warm temperatures shorten them all.
5. **Spread enough.** The aim, in the handbook's words, is a layer that
   under pressure flows into "a uniformly thin layer" and soaks in. On
   end grain the glue soaks away, which is one more reason end grain
   joints are weak.
6. **Clamp with care.** Pressure forces the pieces together, pushes out
   trapped air and drives the glue into the wood, the handbook explains,
   but too much squeezes so much glue out, or into the wood, that the
   joint is starved and weak. The strongest joints come from moderately
   high pressure. For the stiff structural glues it gives the strongest
   glue lines as 0.08 to 0.15 mm (0.003 to 0.006 inch) thick; thicker
   lines are weaker. TM 9-243's rules for clamps: choose one that opens
   about 1 1/2 to 2 inches wider than the work, protect glass and highly
   polished surfaces with wooden blocks, check the alignment and that the
   pressure is even across the whole surface, and never tighten a clamp
   with a wrench or a bar. Wooden hand screw clamps are made for holding
   wood while gluing, and their jaws must stay parallel.
7. **Look at the squeeze-out.** A thin, even bead along the whole joint
   shows there was glue everywhere; none at all suggests a starved joint
   (general practice). Clean it off as the label says.
8. **Wait.** The handbook says clamped lumber can need anything from a few
   minutes to 24 hours under pressure, and full strength takes hours,
   days or weeks after that. Do not load the joint until the label's
   cure time has passed (general practice).
9. **Wait again before planing flush.** Water from the glue swells the
   wood along the joint. The handbook warns that if you plane or sand it
   flush too soon, the joint later shrinks below the surface and shows
   as a sunken line under a glossy finish; at room temperature it
   suggests letting the work stand about 7 days first.

## Glue, fasteners, or both

- **Glue alone** suits long-grain joints that never need to come apart:
  edge-glued boards for a table top, the parts of a chair or a drawer.
- **Fasteners** suit joints that must come apart, joints into end grain,
  joints you cannot clamp, and anything people stand on or sit under.
  [Nails, Screws and Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts) covers choosing
  them.
- **Both together.** The Wood Handbook describes construction adhesive
  under the floor and wall sheathing of a house: the nails or screws hold
  the parts in place while it sets, the floor becomes stiffer, with
  fewer squeaks and popped nails, and if the glue ever fails, the
  fasteners still carry the load. It also says the strength the adhesive
  adds is not counted in the structural design.

## Gluing other materials

The Wood Handbook's advice for metal and plastic is that they are harder
than wood. Metal grows an oxide skin and picks up dirt from the air, so
it needs cleaning and roughening before it will bond well. Plastics have
low-energy, water-repelling surfaces that ordinary water-based wood
glues do not bond to well; epoxies, polyurethanes and isocyanate glues
can bond many of them to wood, and roughening the surface helps. Some plastics, among them the soft, waxy-feeling ones
used for many tubs and bottles, resist almost every glue sold to
households; check that the glue's label names the material you are
joining (general practice).

## Worked example: a loose chair rung

A kitchen chair has a rung that turns in its socket.

1. Take the joint apart rather than squirting glue into the gap. Glue on
   top of old glue, dirt or finish holds poorly (general practice; the
   handbook's chain of links explains why).
2. Scrape or pare the old glue off both the end of the rung and the
   inside of the socket, down to clean wood (general practice). If the
   chair is painted and might be old, read [Rust, Rot and
   Decay](/library#rust-rot-and-decay) before sanding anything.
3. Dry-fit. If the rung slides in loosely, a wood glue alone will not
   fill the gap, the handbook says; use a gap-filling glue such as epoxy,
   or build up the rung so it fits snugly (general practice).
4. Spread the glue in the socket and on the rung, push the joint home,
   and clamp it, with blocks under the clamp's jaws and the pressure
   even. A band clamp or a strong strap round the legs works for a chair
   (general practice).
5. Leave it clamped for the label's time, and do not sit on the chair
   until the full cure time has passed.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Anything that holds people up or hangs over them**: stairs, deck
  framing, ladders, swings, lofts, beams. The Wood Handbook calls
  structural bonds critical, because their failure could seriously
  damage the structure or hurt the people in it, and says construction
  adhesive is not counted in structural design. Join these with the
  fasteners and connectors a code or an engineer specifies, never with
  glue alone (general practice; see [Nails, Screws and
  Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts)).
- **Repairs to laminated beams, boats, aircraft and vehicles**: specialist
  work with specialist adhesives and inspection (general practice).
- **A rash or breathing trouble after using a glue**: a doctor (above).

## Traps

- **Gluing end grain and expecting it to hold.** It will not.
- **Filling a bad fit with wood glue.** It does not fill gaps.
- **Clamping as hard as you can.** A starved joint is a weak one.
- **Gluing over dust, oil, finish or old glue.**
- **Loading the joint the day it was glued.**
- **Ordinary PVA outdoors, or under a steady load.** It softens with
  moisture and creeps.
- **Solvent cement in a house with a pilot light burning.**
- **Bare hands in epoxy or polyurethane glue.**

## How the game models it

The game has one glue, and uses it as an ingredient.

- **Making glue.** At a stove, Make Glue boils 3 bones with 1 purified
  water into 2 tubes of glue, taking 10 seconds of game time
  (`make_glue` in `data/recipes.csv`). The recipe is the old way of
  making animal glue in outline; the item's own data lists its material
  as epoxy, and the game does not tell the two apart (`glue_0` in
  `data/items.csv`).
- **What uses it.** A tube each goes into duct tape, sandpaper, a blank
  book, an acoustic guitar and a violin. The duct tape, the book and the
  two instruments are Woodworking crafts. Furniture, doors and crates
  are held together with boxes of nails, not glue, as [Nails, Screws and
  Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts) describes.
- **Clamps and a vise** exist as tools you can make, but no craft in the
  game asks for them.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, materials
  are free while the Creative mode switch on the Inventory page is on,
  which it is unless you turn it off, so crafting uses up no glue. To see
  glue used up, turn that switch off, or switch Play mode to Normal.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches, so
  glue you made or used is back as the default home had it at the next
  launch.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there are
no joints to cut, no grain, no fit, no clamping, no moisture, no open or
cure time, and no glue that fails; glue is simply used up when a craft
needs it. Real gluing is mostly preparation and waiting, and the joint's
shape matters more than the glue.

## You own this when

- You read a glue's label before opening it, and work with solvent glues
  only with the air moving to the outdoors and no flame, pilot light or
  sparking motor in the house, and you touch no switch or plug once the
  vapour is about.
- You wear gloves for epoxy and polyurethane glue, keep super glue away
  from your face, and know to flush an eye at once and get it seen.
- You know the Poison Help number, 1-800-222-1222.
- You can say why an edge joint holds and an end-grain butt joint does
  not, and you choose a joint that puts long grain against long grain.
- You can work out the length of a 1 in 12 scarf.
- You choose the glue for the job: PVA indoors, a water-resistant or
  exterior glue for damp places, epoxy for gaps and mixed materials,
  construction adhesive with nails or screws for sheathing.
- You make the joint fit dry, glue fresh, clean surfaces at the right
  moisture, spread enough glue, clamp evenly but not crushingly, and wait
  for the full cure.
- You use fasteners, not glue alone, for anything people stand on, sit
  under or hang from.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- Reading the label before opening the tube.
- Which flames, heaters and motors to turn off for solvent cement before
  the can is opened, working no switch or plug once vapour is about,
  keeping others away, choosing a water-based cement in a home with gas
  pilot lights, and airing the house before a pilot is relit by the
  appliance's instructions, drawn from the CPSC's findings and the label.
- Gloves, washing skin and moving air for epoxy and polyurethane, and
  seeing a doctor for a rash or breathing trouble.
- Keeping super glue away from the face, getting an eye seen the same
  day, not forcing stuck eyelids open, and not pulling glued skin apart;
  the use and gap-filling of
  cyanoacrylate; and the explanation that its quick set on moisture is
  why it grabs damp skin.
- Hot glue gun habits.
- That cross-linkable PVA is still not for constant wetting, and checking
  the label for outdoor or damp use.
- Dry-fitting, letting wood settle in its room, reading squeeze-out, and
  not loading a joint before the label's cure time.
- The scarf length for a 3/4 inch board (arithmetic).
- Plastics that resist household glues, and checking the label for the
  material.
- The chair rung repair steps beyond what the handbook says about gaps
  and clean surfaces.
- Fasteners, not glue alone, for anything that holds people up, and
  specialist work on laminated beams, boats, aircraft and vehicles.
