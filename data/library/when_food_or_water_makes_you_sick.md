# When Food or Water Makes You Sick

Nearly everyone has had it: a sudden night of vomiting, a day of
diarrhoea, and a guess about the potato salad. The federal government
estimates about 48 million cases of foodborne illness in the United
States every year, about 1 in 6 Americans, with 128,000 people
hospitalised and 3,000 deaths (FDA). Most of it passes on its own; the
FDA's handbook of foodborne germs says "Most foodborne illnesses, while
unpleasant, go away by themselves and don't have lasting effects." This
guide is about the rest: telling an illness that will pass from one that
will not, and stopping it before it starts.

It covers the common illnesses that come from food and water, how long
after eating each one starts, who is most at risk, how to keep someone
who is sick from drying out, the signs that mean a doctor, how to stop
it spreading through a house, and the steps that prevent it in the
kitchen and when the water supply fails. The worked example is a stomach
bug going through a household in Silverdale, Washington, where the
simulation is set.

It leans on other guides and does not repeat them. [Microbes, Good and
Bad](/library#microbes-good-and-bad) explains the germs themselves and why
some need only a few to make you ill. [Telling Spoiled From
Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) covers why food that looks and smells
fine can still be dangerous, the poisons that cooking does not undo, and
which mouldy foods to throw out. [Making Water Safe to
Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink), [Storing Water
Safely](/library#storing-water-safely) and [Testing Water](/library#testing-water)
cover water. [Handwashing and Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene) is the
habit that stops most of what follows. [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) covers botulism, and [Reading the
Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) the shellfish poisons that come from algae.

Its sources are the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the
Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the National Institutes of Health's
digestive diseases institute (NIDDK), and, on a few points, the
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the Health Resources and
Services Administration (HRSA), all US federal and in the public domain,
and, for where to report an illness, the county's public health
district; the World Health Organization's packet text appears only in
the section on how the game models it. Where something is general
practice or our own reading,
the text says so. None of it replaces a doctor, and the first section
says when you need one.

## First, the signs that mean a doctor

Most of these illnesses pass in a day or a few days with rest and
fluids. The signs below mean an illness is not passing, or that the
person who has it cannot safely wait.

**See a doctor.** The CDC's list for food poisoning:

- bloody diarrhoea;
- diarrhoea that lasts more than 3 days;
- a fever over 102 F;
- vomiting so often that you cannot keep liquids down;
- signs of dehydration, which the CDC lists as not urinating much, a dry
  mouth and throat, and feeling dizzy when standing up.

NIDDK, writing about diarrhoea of any cause, says to talk with a doctor
right away for a change in mental state such as irritability or lack of
energy, frequent vomiting, severe pain in the abdomen or rectum, stools
that are black and tarry or contain red blood or pus, and symptoms of
dehydration. For adults it adds diarrhoea lasting more than 2 days, a
high fever, and six or more loose stools a day (restated). The CDC's food
poisoning page says 3 days; NIDDK, and the CDC's own page on *E. coli*,
say 2, and the *E. coli* page counts vomiting that lasts more than 2 days
as well. For an adult, act on the 2 days.

NIDDK also says that anyone with diarrhoea who is pregnant, over 65,
taking antibiotics or living with a weakened immune system is more
likely to have health problems from it and should keep in touch with
their doctor (restated). And the CDC, on antibiotics and antifungals: "If
you have been taking these drugs, tell your doctor if you have three or
more diarrhea episodes in 24 hours."

**Babies and young children: sooner.** NIDDK says to talk with a doctor
right away if a baby or child has diarrhoea lasting more than a day, any
fever in a baby or a high fever in an older child, frequent loose
stools, or, in a baby, refuses to eat or drink anything for longer than a
few hours. And it says to get a doctor's help right away if a child with
diarrhoea is younger than 12 months, was born prematurely or has other
medical conditions, or cannot drink enough to keep from drying out. Its
reason: "Diarrhea is especially dangerous in newborns and infants and can
lead to severe dehydration in just a day or two. A child with symptoms
of dehydration can die within a day if left untreated."

**Pregnant: call.** The CDC: "See your doctor if you are pregnant and
have a fever and other flu-like symptoms." For *Listeria*, which can
harm a pregnancy, its advice is: "Call the doctor right away if you have
a fever and feel more tired and achy than usual."

**Emergencies: go now.**

- **With or after an *E. coli* illness, whose diarrhoea the CDC says is
  often bloody:** little or no urine, losing the pink colour in the
  cheeks and inside the lower eyelids, unexplained bruising or a rash of
  tiny red spots, blood in the urine, being very tired or irritable, or
  less alert. These are the CDC's signs of hemolytic uremic syndrome
  (HUS), a kidney illness that Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* can
  trigger. The CDC: "HUS is a medical emergency."
- **Double or blurred vision, drooping eyelids, slurred speech, trouble
  swallowing or breathing, muscle weakness or difficulty moving the
  eyes:** botulism. The CDC: "Botulism is a medical emergency. If you or
  your child has signs and symptoms of botulism, immediately see your
  doctor or go to the emergency room. Do not wait." **In a baby the
  CDC's signs are different:** constipation, poor feeding, drooping
  eyelids, pupils slow to react to light, a face with less expression
  than usual, and a weak cry that sounds different from usual. Honey can
  carry the bacteria, so the CDC says to give no honey, nor anything made
  with it such as a honey pacifier, to a child under 1; it also says most
  botulism in babies cannot be prevented, so know the signs. [Why Canning
  Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains the pattern.
- **After raw shellfish, or a wound in sea water:** fever and chills with
  dangerously low blood pressure or blistering skin, or a wound that is
  red, swollen, painful, warm or leaking. These are the CDC's signs of a
  *Vibrio* bloodstream or wound infection: "Immediately see your doctor
  or go to the emergency room if you have symptoms of a Vibrio
  bloodstream or wound infection. Do not wait."
- **A stiff neck, confusion, loss of balance or seizures** with fever:
  the CDC lists these among the signs of *Listeria* that has spread
  through the body. Get medical help at once (our reading).
- **Someone hard to wake, confused or fainting:** call 911 (general
  practice). [First Aid Until Help Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives)
  covers what to do while you wait.

## What these illnesses are

NIDDK says viruses, bacteria and parasites cause most food poisoning
(restated). The CDC's list of the commonest symptoms is short: diarrhoea,
stomach pain or cramps, nausea, vomiting and fever. Most people have a
mild illness, but the CDC warns that some infections spread by food lead
to meningitis, kidney damage, HUS, arthritis, or brain and nerve damage,
and that for some people these last weeks or months, or never go away.

**The commonest one is a virus.** The CDC: "Norovirus is the leading
cause of vomiting and diarrhea, and foodborne illness in the United
States." People call it the stomach flu, but the CDC says it has nothing
to do with influenza, and NIDDK adds that the flu vaccine does not
protect against it. It spreads from food, from water and, above all,
from person to person: [Microbes, Good and
Bad](/library#microbes-good-and-bad) explains why a few virus particles are
enough.

**Water carries the same kinds of germs as food.** They get in by the
same route, the mouth. The FDA's handbook links norovirus outbreaks to
municipal water, well water, stream water, lake water, ice, swimming and
floodwater. The CDC says more Americans get sick from *Giardia* than from
any other parasite that lives in the gut, and that drinking untreated
water from springs, lakes, rivers or shallow wells, or swallowing water
while swimming, raises the risk. *Cryptosporidium*, another gut parasite,
can live in water, food, soil or on surfaces contaminated with infected
faeces (CDC). Treating doubtful water is in [Making Water Safe to
Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink).

## How long after eating

The time between swallowing a germ and falling ill is different for
every germ, and it is the best clue to what happened. These are the
CDC's figures for when symptoms usually start, with its list of common
sources, restated; the last three rows come from the CDC's pages on each
illness.

| Germ | Usually starts | Common sources |
|---|---|---|
| *Staphylococcus aureus* | 30 minutes to 8 hours | Foods not cooked after handling: sliced meats, puddings, pastries, sandwiches |
| *Vibrio* | Within 24 hours | Raw or undercooked shellfish, particularly oysters |
| *Clostridium perfringens* | 6 to 24 hours | Meat, poultry and gravies cooked in large batches and held at an unsafe temperature |
| *Salmonella* | 6 hours to 6 days | Raw or undercooked poultry and meat; eggs; raw milk and juice; raw fruit and vegetables; many animals, backyard poultry and reptiles among them |
| Norovirus | 12 to 48 hours | Leafy greens, fresh fruit, shellfish, contaminated water, people who are infected, surfaces with the virus on them |
| Botulism | 18 to 36 hours | Improperly canned or fermented foods |
| *Campylobacter* | 2 to 5 days | Raw or undercooked poultry, raw milk, contaminated water, pets |
| *E. coli* | 3 to 4 days | Raw or undercooked ground beef, raw milk and juice, raw vegetables and sprouts, contaminated water |
| *Cyclospora* | 1 week | Raw fruit, vegetables and herbs |
| *Listeria* (spread through the body) | 2 weeks | Soft cheeses such as queso fresco, raw sprouts, melons, hot dogs, pates, deli meats, smoked fish, raw milk |
| *Cryptosporidium* | 2 to 10 days, 7 on average | Water, food, soil or surfaces contaminated with faeces |
| *Giardia* | 1 to 2 weeks | Untreated or contaminated water, food and surfaces; infected people and animals |
| Hepatitis A | 2 to 7 weeks | Close contact with someone infected; contaminated food or drink |

Two lessons follow.

**The last thing you ate is probably not the culprit.** The CDC: "The
food that made you sick is usually not the last food you ate. It often
takes 2 to 3 days to start feeling sick after eating a contaminated
food. Sometimes, it takes even longer."

**Very fast usually means a poison made in the food** (our reading of the
table). Illness within a few hours is the pattern of *Staphylococcus
aureus*, whose harm comes from a poison the bacteria made in the food
before it was eaten (FDA).
Cooking does not destroy that poison, which is why [Telling Spoiled From
Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) puts the time food spends out of the
refrigerator ahead of any reheating.

How long it lasts varies too. The CDC says most people with norovirus
get better within 1 to 3 days; *Giardia* symptoms usually last 2 to 6
weeks, and *Cryptosporidium* 1 to 2 weeks.

## Who is most at risk

The CDC names four groups more likely to get very sick, and gives
numbers that are worth knowing.

- **People 65 and older.** "Nearly half of people aged 65 and older who
  have a lab-confirmed foodborne illness from Salmonella, Campylobacter,
  Listeria or E. coli are hospitalized."
- **Children under 5.** "Children younger than 5 are three times more
  likely to be hospitalized if they get a Salmonella infection. Kidney
  failure strikes 1 out of 7 children under age 5 who are diagnosed with
  E. coli O157 infection."
- **People with weakened immune systems,** which the CDC says can come
  from diabetes, liver disease, kidney disease, alcoholism, HIV,
  autoimmune disorders such as lupus, or chemotherapy or radiation
  therapy. Its example: "people on dialysis are 50 times more likely to
  get a Listeria infection."
- **Pregnant women.** "For example, pregnant women are 10 times more
  likely to get a Listeria infection."

*Vibrio* adds its own list. The CDC says liver disease, cancer, diabetes,
HIV, thalassaemia, treatment that suppresses the immune system, medicine
that lowers stomach acid and recent stomach surgery all raise the risk of
infection and of severe illness, as do eating raw seafood, oysters above
all, and letting an open wound touch coastal water or raw seafood.

And dehydration hits the youngest and oldest hardest. The CDC says
norovirus can lead to dehydration "especially in young children, older
adults, and people with other illnesses," and the FDA calls dehydration
its most common complication.

For these groups, the same food is a different decision. The CDC's list
of riskier foods is raw or undercooked poultry, meat, seafood or eggs;
raw or undercooked sprouts; unwashed fruit and vegetables; cut melon;
unpasteurised milk, juice, cider or soft cheese; and raw dough or batter
made with uncooked flour. [Salting, Curing and
Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking) has the CDC's advice on smoked
fish and dry sausages for people at higher risk from *Listeria*.

## Keeping someone hydrated

Vomiting and diarrhoea take water and salts out of the body, and losing
too much is what makes these illnesses dangerous (our reading; the FDA
calls dehydration the commonest complication of norovirus). NIDDK calls
the salts electrolytes, and says both have to be replaced.

### The signs of drying out

From the CDC and NIDDK together:

- peeing less than usual, or very dark urine;
- a very dry mouth and throat, and great thirst;
- feeling dizzy or lightheaded when standing up, or very tired;
- sunken eyes or cheeks; skin that, pinched and let go, does not flatten
  back straight away;
- in children, crying with few or no tears, and being unusually sleepy or
  fussy;
- in babies and toddlers, no wet nappy (diaper) for 3 hours or more, a
  sunken soft spot on the head, and drowsiness.

Any of them is a reason to see a doctor (CDC). In a child, remember
NIDDK's warning in the first section and do not wait to see whether
they pass (our reading).

### What to give

1. **Oral rehydration solution first.** The CDC, on norovirus: "Oral
   rehydration fluids that you can get over the counter are most helpful
   for mild dehydration." The CDC describes the solution as a
   prepackaged powder of salts and minerals mixed with water that has
   been boiled or treated, and NIDDK says these drinks contain glucose
   and electrolytes. Mix and give it exactly as the packet says (general
   practice).
2. **Little and often if they are vomiting.** NIDDK: "If vomiting is a
   problem, try sipping small amounts of clear liquids."
3. **Children:** extra fluids straight away, such as an oral rehydration
   solution. The CDC says dehydration can happen quickly in young
   children, and its words on this are short: "Do not wait."
4. **Babies:** keep giving breast milk or formula as usual, and talk with
   a doctor before giving a baby an oral rehydration solution (NIDDK).
5. **Older adults, and people with a weak immune system, diabetes, kidney
   disease or another health condition:** NIDDK says to talk with a doctor
   before drinking an oral rehydration solution, because a particular
   solution or amount may suit them better.
6. **Other drinks.** For a mild illness, NIDDK lists water, broths and
   sports drinks for adults, and the CDC says sports drinks and other
   drinks without caffeine or alcohol can help with mild dehydration,
   though "these drinks may not replace important nutrients and
   minerals." For heavy, watery diarrhoea the CDC's cholera advice is
   stricter: if there is no oral rehydration solution, drink safe water or
   broth, and avoid juice, soft drinks and sports drinks, because
   "Sugary drinks can make diarrhea worse." Our reading of the two: the
   worse the diarrhoea, the more it should be the rehydration solution.
7. **Food when they want it.** NIDDK says a restricted diet does not help
   and that, once the appetite returns, most people can go back to their
   normal diet even while the diarrhoea lasts (restated).

Keep oral rehydration packets in the house and in an emergency kit. A
2003 CDC report on diarrhoea in children puts it this way (ORS is oral
rehydration solution): "All families should be encouraged to have a
supply of ORS in the home at all times and to start therapy with a
commercially available ORS product as soon as diarrhea begins."
This guide gives no home recipe. NIDDK notes that people do make the
solution at home, but the same CDC report explains why it recommends
the packet: "Although producing a homemade solution with appropriate
concentrations of glucose and sodium is possible, serious errors can
occur."

### Medicines: what not to reach for

- **Anti-diarrhoea medicines.** The CDC says to talk to a healthcare
  provider before taking one, and not to use them with a high fever or
  bloody diarrhoea, not with a Shiga toxin-producing *E. coli* infection,
  where they can raise the chance of HUS, and not to give a child under
  12 a medicine containing bismuth subsalicylate. NIDDK says doctors
  usually advise against them for babies and children.
- **Antibiotics.** They do nothing for norovirus and the other stomach
  viruses (CDC). For *E. coli* the CDC says most people recover without
  them, and that antibiotics should not be used for a Shiga
  toxin-producing *E. coli* infection, because they can raise the chance
  of HUS. Whether an infection needs them is a doctor's decision.
- **Probiotics.** NIDDK says to talk with your doctor first (restated).

**When fluids are not keeping up,** the person needs a doctor. The CDC
says severe dehydration may need fluids through a vein in hospital.

## Stopping it spreading at home

Norovirus is the one most likely to go through a whole house, and the
CDC's rules for it are the rules to use for any stomach bug (our
reading).

- **The sick person makes no food for others.** The CDC: do not prepare
  or handle food, or care for others, while sick, and wait at least 2
  days (48 hours) after symptoms stop. It says this matters most for
  people who work in a restaurant, a school, a daycare or a care home.
- **Wash hands with soap and water.** The CDC: "Hand sanitizer does not
  work well against norovirus." Sanitiser can be used as well as
  washing, never instead of it.
- **Keep washing after everyone feels better.** The CDC says people can
  still spread norovirus for 2 weeks or more after they recover.

### Cleaning up vomit or diarrhoea

The CDC's steps, in order:

1. Put on rubber or disposable gloves. Wipe up the whole area with paper
   towels and put them in a plastic rubbish bag.
2. Disinfect the area, following the product's label. The CDC's bleach
   solution is 5 to 25 tablespoons of household bleach (5 to 8 percent)
   per gallon of water, or use a product registered by the EPA as
   effective against norovirus.
3. Leave the bleach on for at least 5 minutes.
4. Clean the whole area again with soap and hot water.
5. Wash the laundry, take out the rubbish, and wash your hands.

**Bleach safely.** The CDC: "Never mix bleach with ammonia or other
cleaners. This will produce a poisonous gas." Open windows and doors for
fresh air while you use it (CDC), and keep children and pets out of the
room until it is done (general practice). Keep the bottle and the mixed
solution out of children's reach, as HRSA, which funds the Poison Help
line, says of every household cleaner. If it splashes in someone's eyes,
rinse them with running water for 15 to 20 minutes (HRSA). For a splash
in the eyes or a mouthful swallowed, call Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, or
911 if the person is unconscious, struggling to breathe or having a
seizure ([First Aid Until Help Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives)).

**Laundry.** The CDC: take off and wash soiled clothes and bedding at
once, wearing gloves; handle them without shaking them; wash with
detergent and hot water on the longest cycle available, then machine dry
on the highest heat; then wash your hands.

## Preventing it at home: clean, separate, cook, chill

The CDC's four steps cover most of what goes wrong in a kitchen. The
detail, and the reasons, are in [Telling Spoiled From
Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) and [Cold Storage Without a
Fridge](/library#cold-storage-without-a-fridge).

- **Clean.** Wash hands before, during and after preparing food and
  before eating, for at least 20 seconds with soap. Wash utensils,
  boards and counters with hot, soapy water after each food. Rinse fruit
  and vegetables under running water.
- **Separate.** Keep raw meat, poultry, seafood and eggs, and their
  juices, away from food that will not be cooked, with a separate board
  for them. Do not wash raw chicken: the CDC says it can spread germs to
  other foods, the sink and the counter.
- **Cook.** "The only way to tell if food is safely cooked is to use a
  food thermometer." The CDC's temperatures: 145 F for whole cuts of
  beef, veal, lamb and pork, then a 3-minute rest; 145 F for fish with
  fins, or until the flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork;
  160 F for ground meats; 165 F for all poultry, and for leftovers and
  casseroles. Wild game is not on that list: cook it to 165 F, as
  [Salting, Curing and Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking) explains
  from the CDC.
- **Chill.** Perishable food goes in the refrigerator within 2 hours, or
  within 1 hour above 90 F. Keep the refrigerator at 40 F or below and
  the freezer at 0 F or below, and thaw food in the refrigerator, in cold
  water or in the microwave, never on the counter.

Three more steps that belong in the same list:

- **Animals.** The CDC lists backyard poultry, reptiles, amphibians,
  small mammals and pets among the sources of *Salmonella* and
  *Campylobacter*. Wash your hands after the hens, the feed and the
  litter ([Chickens and Eggs](/library#chickens-and-eggs)).
- **Vaccines.** The CDC: "The best way to prevent hepatitis A is by
  getting vaccinated." And if you may have been exposed in the last 2
  weeks, for example because someone in the house has hepatitis A or you
  ate a food named in an outbreak, call a doctor straight away: the CDC
  says a single shot of the vaccine within 2 weeks of exposure can help
  prevent illness, and that depending on your age and health a doctor
  might also recommend a treatment called immune globulin. NIDDK says two rotavirus
  vaccines, given by mouth to babies, protect against another common
  stomach virus; ask your baby's doctor.
- **Recalls.** The CDC says to throw away recalled food and clean the
  refrigerator if it was stored there.

### Shellfish from the Sound

In Silverdale the shellfish on the beach carry two different hazards, and
it is dangerous to confuse them (our reading).

- **Poisons from algae.** Cooking and freezing do not touch them, and the
  only protection is checking the state's closures on the day you
  harvest. [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) has the hotline and
  the rule.
- **Germs: *Vibrio* and norovirus.** These the cooking does deal with.
  The CDC says *Vibrio* bacteria live naturally in coastal water and are
  found in higher numbers from May to October, when the water is warmer,
  and that most people with vibriosis got it from eating raw or
  undercooked shellfish, oysters above all. Its norovirus advice is to
  cook oysters and other shellfish thoroughly, to at least 145 F, and not
  to rely on a quick steam: "Quick steaming processes will not heat foods
  enough to kill noroviruses." The CDC's advice for everyone is not to
  eat raw or undercooked oysters or other seafood, and it says this
  matters most for people at higher risk. A clam or an oyster in its
  shell cannot take a thermometer, so use the CDC's times instead: throw
  away any whose shells are open before cooking; boil them until the
  shells open and then 3 to 5 minutes more, or put them in a steamer that
  is already steaming and cook them 4 to 9 minutes more; eat only those
  that opened, and throw out any that did not open fully; boil shucked
  oysters for at least 3 minutes. The CDC's norovirus page goes further
  and says to avoid steaming shellfish at all, so where the two pages
  differ, boil (our reading). Keep an open wound out of sea water, cover
  it with a waterproof bandage if it could touch sea water or raw seafood
  and its juices, and wash it at once with soap and clean running water
  if it does (CDC).

A closed beach is closed whether or not you plan to cook what you dig.

## When the water is off or unsafe

### A drinking water advisory

When a water main breaks or a supply is contaminated, the utility or the
health department issues an advisory. The CDC describes three kinds.

**Boil water advisory:** germs may be in the water. Use bottled water or
boiled tap water. The CDC: bring it to a full rolling boil for 1 minute,
or 3 minutes above 6,500 feet, and let it cool. The EPA draws that
altitude line lower, at 5,000 feet, and [Making Water Safe to
Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) uses the stricter one: above 5,000
feet, boil for 3 minutes. And from the CDC's page:

- "Boil your tap water even if you filter it." A pitcher or home filter
  does not make it safe.
- Do not use water or ice from a refrigerator's dispensers, which are
  connected to the water line.
- Use bottled or boiled water for drinking, cooking, preparing food and
  brushing teeth.
- Babies: breastfeeding is the best option, and ready-to-use formula if
  possible. If powdered formula is the only choice, the CDC's formula
  guidance says to mix it with bottled water, or with tap water made safe
  by boiling and cooled to room temperature first. **The CDC makes one
  exception, even in an emergency:** if the baby is younger than 2
  months, was born early (before 37 weeks) or has a weakened immune
  system, bring the water, bottled or tap, to a full rolling boil as
  above, wait about 5 minutes, and mix the powder in while the water is
  still very hot. Powdered formula is not sterile, and water around
  158 F kills a germ in it, *Cronobacter*, that is rare but serious in
  young babies. Then let the bottle cool before feeding, and test a few
  drops on your wrist: it should feel warm, not hot.
- Handwashing: in many cases tap water and soap are fine; follow the
  officials' advice.
- Bathing: do not swallow the water, and think about giving babies and
  young children a sponge bath instead.
- Dishes: use disposable ones if you can; a dishwasher is generally safe
  if it has a sanitising cycle or a final rinse of at least 150 F. By
  hand, wash and rinse as usual in hot water, then soak the dishes for at
  least a minute in 1 teaspoon of unscented household liquid bleach per
  gallon of warm water, and let them air dry completely. Sanitise all
  baby bottles.
- Pets get bottled or boiled water; plants and the garden can have tap
  water.

**Do not drink advisory:** chemicals or toxins may be in the water, and
the CDC says boiling will not make it safe. Use bottled water for
drinking, cooking, brushing teeth, washing produce, preparing food,
mixing formula, making ice and giving water to pets. The formula
exception above still holds for the babies it covers: boil the bottled
water and wait about 5 minutes before mixing (CDC).

**Do not use advisory:** do not use the tap water for anything. The CDC
says these are rare.

Keep following the advisory until the utility lifts it ([Making Water
Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink)).

### No running water at all

When the taps simply stop, the dangers shift. Drink stored water or water
you have treated ([Storing Water Safely](/library#storing-water-safely),
[Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink)). Keep
washing hands with soap, from a jug or a tippy tap ([Handwashing and
Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene) shows how to make one and why
washing water need not be drinking water). And if the power is out too,
the refrigerator
keeps food cold for about 4 hours unopened and a full freezer about 48
hours, and perishable food that has been above 40 F for 4 hours or more
is thrown out (FDA; [Cold Storage Without a
Fridge](/library#cold-storage-without-a-fridge) has the rest).

## Telling someone about it

If you think a meal made you ill, the CDC asks you to do three things:
talk to your healthcare provider, who may test a sample; write down
everything you ate, the restaurants and events, and any contact with
animals in the week before you fell ill; and report it to your local
health department, which looks for groups of people with the same
illness who ate the same food. The CDC also suggests keeping food
receipts for at least a week, and the original labels, because they help
investigators trace a food.

## Worked example: a stomach bug in a Silverdale house

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on. It is February. The household is two parents, a
three-year-old and a grandfather of 72 who has diabetes. Norovirus is
going round the preschool.

1. **Saturday, 7 pm.** The three-year-old vomits without warning, then
   has diarrhoea in the night. From the table, norovirus starts 12 to 48
   hours after exposure, so he probably caught it some time between
   Thursday evening and Saturday morning, not from tonight's dinner.
2. **That night.** One parent cleans up with gloves, paper towels and the
   CDC's bleach solution, windows open, other cleaners put away, and
   washes the bedding hot without shaking it. The other parent does the
   cooking for the week (general practice). Everyone washes with soap
   and water; the sanitiser stays on the shelf for later.
3. **Fluids.** They give the child oral rehydration solution from the
   cupboard, a few sips every few minutes, because he keeps bringing up
   larger amounts. They watch for the signs: is he weeing, are there
   tears when he cries, is he unusually sleepy or fussy? Because he keeps
   bringing fluids up, they ring the doctor's after-hours line that
   night, as NIDDK says not to hesitate to do for a child with
   diarrhoea, and follow the advice they are given. Frequent vomiting,
   and irritability or a lack of energy, are on NIDDK's list of reasons
   to talk with a doctor right away, and the CDC counts unusual
   sleepiness or fussiness as signs of dehydration, so a grumpy,
   irritable child is a reason to call, not a reassuring sign. Had he
   stopped weeing, they would have taken him to the emergency room that
   night (general practice), and had he become hard to wake, they would
   have called 911. By Sunday morning he is drinking, weeing and playing
   again.
4. **Monday.** The grandfather starts vomiting. He is over 65 and has
   diabetes, so before he drinks oral rehydration solution they ring his
   doctor's line, as NIDDK advises, and they follow what the doctor says.
5. **Tuesday.** He feels dizzy when he stands and has hardly urinated
   since the morning: signs of dehydration, which the CDC says need a
   doctor. They take him that afternoon.
6. **The next two weeks.** The family keeps washing hands carefully,
   because the CDC says norovirus can spread for 2 weeks or more after
   recovery, and nobody who was ill cooks for the others until 2 days
   after their symptoms stopped.

Had several people fallen ill after the same restaurant meal, they would
have reported it to the health district. Had a boil water advisory been
in force, every drink and every rehydration packet would have been mixed
with bottled or boiled water.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Any sign in the first section** goes to a doctor, or to 911.
- **A baby under 12 months with diarrhoea** goes to a doctor right away
  (NIDDK).
- **Medicines**, antibiotics and anti-diarrhoea drugs included, are a
  healthcare provider's call for anyone with fever, bloody diarrhoea, or
  a child (CDC, NIDDK).
- **Whether a well or spring is safe to drink from** is a laboratory's
  question ([Testing Water](/library#testing-water)).
- **A suspected outbreak** belongs to the health department.

Yours to do: clean, separate, cook and chill; wash with soap; keep
rehydration packets in; know the danger signs; clean up safely; and keep
the sick away from the cooking.

## Traps

- **"It must have been lunch."** Most germs take a day or more; the CDC
  says it is usually not the last thing you ate.
- **"It's only the stomach flu."** Usually it passes, but a baby, a
  grandparent or someone with another illness can dry out dangerously.
- **"Water will do."** For heavy diarrhoea, plain water does not put back
  the salts; oral rehydration solution does.
- **"A big glass will help."** Not while someone is vomiting. Sips,
  little and often.
- **"Something to stop the diarrhoea."** Not with fever, blood in it, or
  in a child without a doctor's advice.
- **"Antibiotics will sort it out."** Not for a virus, and with some
  *E. coli* they make things worse.
- **"Sanitiser is enough."** Not for norovirus. Soap and water.
- **"Bleach and a bit of the bathroom cleaner."** Never mix bleach with
  other cleaners.
- **"I'm better, so I can cook again."** Wait 2 days after symptoms stop.
- **"The filter jug makes it fine."** Not under a boil water advisory.
- **"I'll cook the clams, so the closure doesn't matter."** Cooking deals
  with germs, not with the algae poisons a closure is for.

## How the game models it

The game has one illness that comes from food, and none from water.

- **Food Poisoning.** Eating a spoiled food always gives it, and some
  foods eaten as they are carry a chance of it: raw meat 40 percent, raw
  fish 25, eggs 15, fresh milk 5, raw vegetables 2 (`src/systems/food.rs`,
  `data/food_system.ron`). [Microbes, Good and
  Bad](/library#microbes-good-and-bad) explains the spoilage clock behind it.
  It starts the moment you eat, not hours later. The game tells you then
  what is happening, how long it will last and what helps, and tells you
  again when it has passed.
- **What it does: it dries you out.** While it lasts, Food Poisoning
  takes water from your Hydration bar, 1.5 litres a day on top of the
  2.5 litres a resting body loses anyway, and slows you a little; then it
  passes by itself. Settings > Gameplay > Illness decides how hard it is:
  Realistic runs it for two days of game time, and Forgiving, the
  default, for one day, taking half as much water. It does no harm of its
  own. Harm comes only if your Hydration runs out, when your health
  drains as it does for anyone who goes without water, a full bar over
  about a day. Drinking nothing at all, a Realistic bout empties a full
  Hydration bar in a little over a day, while a bottle of water every six
  hours keeps a healthy adult safe through all of it
  (`data/medical/illnesses.ron`, `data/status_effects.csv`,
  `src/systems/illness.rs`, `src/systems/food.rs`). If it does kill you,
  the death screen says "dehydration from Food Poisoning".
- **What to drink.** While you are ill, a drink of oral rehydration
  solution puts back all of its water, plain water and most other drinks
  three quarters of theirs, and sugary or caffeinated drinks (juice, the
  energy drink, coffee and tea) half. That is the guidance in this guide
  put into numbers, and the numbers are the game's own choice
  (`data/medical/illnesses.ron`). You start with four sachets of Oral
  Rehydration Salts, and the trading post sells more. Mix one into a
  litre of drinking water, from your pack or the home's tap, with the
  Crafting page's Mix Oral Rehydration Solution (Chemistry, by hand, no
  skill needed). The litre keeps 24 hours, as the WHO's packet says, and
  after that it counts as spoiled like any food (`data/items.csv`,
  `data/recipes.csv`, `data/world/player.ron`, `data/trade_goods.ron`,
  `data/food_system.ron`). There is no recipe for the salts themselves,
  for the reason this guide gives no home recipe.
- **Medicine does not cure it.** No medical item ends Food Poisoning.
  Antibiotics end only the effects the game marks as caused by bacteria,
  and if you press their Use button while you have Food Poisoning, they
  stay in your pack and the game tells you why, and what helps instead
  (`data/medical/treatments.ron`, `src/systems/treatment.rs`).
- **Nobody is more at risk.** Every character is a healthy adult. The
  game has no babies, older people or anyone already ill, so the faster,
  more dangerous course this guide warns about for them is not in it, and
  neither is vomiting that keeps you from drinking.
- **No illness from water.** Drinking water carries no chance of Food
  Poisoning in the game's food data, and it keeps ten years before its
  clock calls it spoiled. The home's tanks are filled by its water pump,
  by rain caught on the cistern and by the water its air handlers
  condense out of the air, a bottle or jerrycan filled at a tank holds
  drinking water, and nothing makes it otherwise: there is no *Giardia*,
  no boil water advisory and no unsafe water. The home's Water purifier
  draws power but moves no water, and its Purify Water recipe turns
  bottles that were already drinking water into Purified Water, which the
  game treats the same (`data/containers/fluids.ron`,
  `data/food_system.ron`, `data/food/item_profiles.ron`,
  `data/machines/home.ron`, `src/machines.rs`,
  `src/engine/home_spawn.rs`, `src/systems/plumbing.rs`,
  `data/recipes.csv`). Other drinks are another matter: fresh milk
  carries the 5 percent chance above, and juice 1 percent.
- **The water supply and the power.** Under the default ship life
  support, Station-supplied (Settings > Gameplay > Ship life support),
  the ship's reactor makes up any power your home does not make, so the
  home's water keeps coming. In Realistic, a water pump that loses its
  power stops filling the tanks, and they run down whenever the home uses
  more water than still comes in (`src/config.rs`,
  `src/systems/plumbing.rs`, `src/systems/ship_power.rs`).
- **Nobody catches it from anyone.** Illness does not pass between
  people, and the game's catalogue of illnesses, from a cold to
  parasites, is not given to anyone (`data/status_effects.csv`).
- **No doctor and no danger signs.** Your Hydration bar is the only
  sign you get. There is no doctor to call, no hospital and no fluids
  through a vein: drinking is the whole treatment.
- **Under the default settings.** The game starts in the Normal play
  mode, and the raw-food chances and Food Poisoning are the same in every
  play mode; the Illness setting starts at Forgiving. The illness runs on
  the game clock, so at the default Realistic time speed its days are
  real days, and a night asleep in a bed moves it along with the rest of
  the night. Your health and the conditions on you are saved with your
  game, and with Settings > Gameplay > "Start every session from the
  default home" off, as it is by default, a launch brings them back as
  you left them (`src/save_load.rs`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: how long
after a meal an illness starts, who is most at risk, the danger signs,
the germs that spread from person to person and in water, cleaning up
safely, and the habits in the kitchen that prevent all of it.

## You own this when

- You can list the signs that mean a doctor, and the ones that mean the
  emergency room now, from memory.
- You know that a baby under 12 months with diarrhoea goes to a doctor
  right away, and that a child with signs of dehydration cannot wait.
- You can read the timing of an illness: within a few hours (the CDC
  gives 30 minutes to 8 hours for *Staphylococcus aureus*) points to a
  poison made in the food; half a day to several days, to an infection
  such as norovirus or *Salmonella*; a week or more, to germs such as
  *Giardia* or hepatitis A.
- You can name the four groups most at risk and change what you serve
  them.
- You keep oral rehydration packets in the house, and know to give them
  in sips, to keep babies on milk or formula, and to call the doctor
  first for an older adult with diabetes or kidney disease.
- You would not give an anti-diarrhoea medicine to someone with a fever,
  bloody diarrhoea, or to a child without a doctor's advice.
- You can clean up after a stomach bug with gloves and bleach, without
  mixing cleaners, and you keep the sick away from the cooking for 2 days
  after they recover.
- You clean, separate, cook with a thermometer and chill within 2 hours.
- You know what a boil water, a do not drink and a do not use advisory
  each ask of you.
- You know who to tell when a meal makes several people ill.
