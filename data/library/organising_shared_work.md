# Organising Shared Work

Some jobs cannot be done alone. Raising the frame of a shed, getting a
field of hay in before the rain, moving a woodstove into a house,
clearing a fence line, a day of canning the harvest, rebuilding after
a storm. A group can do in a day what one person could not do in a
month, and the old name for it, a barn raising or a work bee, is a
reminder that people have been doing this for a long time.

A group is not just more hands. It is more people near the same tools,
under the same load, in the same heat, each with a slightly different
idea of the plan. Ten people with no plan get in each other's way, and
a single person who does not know the others are there can hurt
somebody. Organising is the skill of turning a crowd into a crew: a
clear goal, a plan, someone in charge, the hazards dealt with before
the first swing, and a way to learn from the day afterwards.

The structure in this guide is borrowed from the National Incident
Management System (NIMS), the way US emergency services organise people
who may never have worked together, published by the Federal Emergency
Management Agency (FEMA). The safety rules come from the Occupational
Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) and the USDA Forest Service's
guide for trail crews. The way to learn from the day comes from the US
Army, with practical formats from the National Wildfire Coordinating
Group (NWCG), and the advice on working with people from the Training
Within Industry programme the US government ran during the Second
World War. All except the NWCG's are United States government
publications, in the public domain; the NWCG material is restated in
our own words. Where something is general practice or our own
reasoning, the text says so.

[Estimating](/library#estimating) covers how long a job will take and what
it needs, [Making an Agreement That
Holds](/library#making-an-agreement-that-holds) covers promises of help
between neighbours, and [Teaching What You Know](/library#teaching-what-you-know)
covers bringing a beginner up to speed on the day.

## First, the hazards a group adds

A group work day has every hazard of the job itself, plus some of its
own. Plan for them before anyone arrives.

### Someone in charge of safety, who can stop the work

In NIMS, the ultimate responsibility for safe conduct rests with the
person in charge of an incident and with supervisors at all levels, and
a Safety Officer monitors the work and advises them on safety. NIMS
says: "The Safety Officer stops and/or prevents unsafe acts during the
incident." On a work day, that
means naming one person, not the busiest one, whose job is to watch
rather than to work, and who can stop everything (our adaptation).

Agree a stop word before you start, and tell everyone that **anyone
can call stop**, including the youngest and the newest; when someone
calls it, every tool goes down and every load is set down safely
(general practice).

### Walk the job and list what can go wrong

OSHA's guide to job hazard analysis is written for employers, but its
method fits any job. Break the job into its steps, then for each step
ask OSHA's questions:

- What can go wrong?
- What are the consequences?
- How could it arise?
- What are other contributing factors?
- How likely is it that the hazard will occur?

OSHA says to look first at jobs with the potential to cause severe or
disabling injuries, even with no history of accidents, at "Jobs in
which one simple human error could lead to a severe accident or
injury", and at jobs that are new. Much of what happens at a work day
fits those descriptions (our view).

Then deal with each hazard, in OSHA's order of preference. The most
effective controls change the thing itself (OSHA's term is engineering
controls): a ramp instead of a lift, a guard on the machine, the load
broken into smaller pieces. Next come administrative controls, changes
to how people work: written procedures, limits on time exposed, signs,
a buddy system, training. Protective gear comes last: OSHA treats it
as acceptable when the job cannot be changed enough, or while a better
fix is being put in place. (Why it comes last, that gear protects only
the person wearing it and only while they wear it, is general
practice.) OSHA adds that a hazard posing an immediate danger to life
or health should be dealt with at once, without waiting for the
analysis to be finished.

### Tools, and the space between people

The Forest Service's guide for trail crews puts the rule for swinging
tools in one sentence: "Chop only when you are clear of other workers."
Elsewhere it says "Always maintain safe distances between workers" and
"Be aware of nearby workers." Its other crew rules translate directly
to a yard full of volunteers:

- Carry tools at your side, with blades forward and the handles
  behind, and if you slip, drop the tool to the side so you do not
  fall on the blade.
- Designate a central drop point for tools near the work, so tools are
  not left lying where people walk.
- Inspect each tool before use: handles sound, heads tight, edges
  sharp.
- Wear protective gear suited to the job: the guide names hard hats,
  gloves and safety glasses.
- Some tools need training. The guide says you need training to use a
  crosscut saw and to "Be sure you are properly trained and certified
  before cutting either standing or fallen trees."

It sums up the spirit of a good crew: "Productive, safe crews depend
on each member being alert, informed, and caring."

On a mixed work day, keep each machine to one trained operator, and
keep everyone else out of its reach; if a machine must be serviced,
the isolation rules in [Keeping Things
Working](/library#keeping-things-working) apply (general practice for the
first part).

### Heavy loads

OSHA's guidance on materials handling says that lifting loads heavier
than about 50 pounds increases the risk of injury, and: "When lifting
loads heavier than 50 pounds, use two or more people to lift the
load." Better still, it says, use mechanical means: hand trucks,
pallet jacks, and ramps or lift gates rather than lifting into a
truck. OSHA's booklet on moving materials adds when to get help: when
a load is so bulky that you cannot properly grasp or lift it, when you
cannot see around or over it, or when you cannot safely handle it.

For a team lift, agree before lifting who calls it, where the load is
going, the route there (cleared of anything to trip on), and where it
will be set down. The caller counts, everyone lifts together, and
anyone can call stop (general practice). OSHA's own lifting advice
holds for every lifter: keep the load close to your body and your
spine straight, which usually means bending at the knees rather than
the waist (for a large, bulky load, OSHA says bending at the waist may
be better if it keeps the load closer), and turn by moving your feet
rather than twisting. [Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](/library#force-levers-and-mechanical-advantage) has more on
lifting and on letting a machine do the work.

OSHA also says to "Rotate tasks so employees are not exposed to the
same activity for too long" and to take regular breaks, warning that
working through breaks increases the risk of injuries and accidents
and lowers the quality of the work, because people are overtired.

### Heat, and the newcomer who wants to keep up

OSHA's heat guidance says: "Almost half of heat-related deaths occur
on a worker's first day on the job or a worker's first day back after
an extended absence." It notes that a new person may skip breaks or
ignore symptoms so as not to look lazy, and that even young,
seemingly fit people can be affected. A volunteer who turns up for one
hot day of hay is exactly that person. OSHA's protections for new
workers translate directly:

- Give newcomers shorter stretches of work in the heat, separated by
  breaks, and more frequent breaks than everyone else.
- "Use a buddy system and don't allow new workers to work alone."
- Let anyone with symptoms stop working, start first aid, and never
  leave someone with symptoms alone.

OSHA's rule for building heat tolerance is the "Rule of 20 percent": a
new worker works only 20 percent of the normal duration on the first
day in the heat and adds 20 percent each day after. A one-day volunteer
never gets past the first day, which is why they need the shorter
stints and the extra breaks (our application).

For everyone: provide cool water near the work and encourage people to
drink often, since OSHA says workers should drink "not just if they are
thirsty"; for work lasting more than two hours, provide drinks with
electrolytes as well; and give breaks in shade or a cool place. OSHA
is blunt that in hot conditions "skipping breaks is not safe".

If someone shows signs of heat illness, OSHA's first aid is to move
them somewhere cooler and cool them immediately, with cold water or
ice if you have it, and: "When in doubt, cool the worker and call
911." "Confusion, slurred speech, or unconsciousness are signs of heat
stroke": call 911 at once and keep cooling them until help arrives.
"Never leave a worker with heat-related illness alone."

### Emergencies

Before you start, know where the first aid kit is, who has first aid
training, where the nearest phone signal is, and the address and
directions you would give an ambulance (general practice). [Bleeding
and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) covers what to do with the kit.

### Children, visitors and liability

Keep children and visitors who are not part of the work out of the
work area, or give them a job well away from tools and loads (general
practice). The federal list of farm jobs too dangerous for hired
children under 16 is a sensible guide to what not to hand a child at
a work day; it is in [Teaching What You Know](/library#teaching-what-you-know).

Ask your insurer whether a friend injured while helping on your land
is covered (general practice). It is a question better asked before
the work day than after it.

## Organise the people

### One goal, written as something you can check

NIMS calls this management by objectives, and its first element is
"Establishing specific, measurable objectives". "Work on the barn"
cannot be finished. "Frame and sheathe the north wall of the barn by
4 pm" can be, and everyone can see whether it was. The wording
techniques in [Making an Agreement That
Holds](/library#making-an-agreement-that-holds) apply here too.

### A plan, on paper if the job is big

NIMS says: "Every incident should have an action plan; however, not
all incidents need written plans." A written plan becomes more
important as the job runs longer, grows more complex or involves more
groups. For a work day, a one-page plan is usually enough (general
practice): the goal, the tasks in order, who leads each one, the tools
and materials each needs, the hazards and how they are handled, break
times, and the emergency plan. [Estimating](/library#estimating) helps with
how long each task takes.

Have everything ready before people arrive (general practice). The
wartime Training Within Industry programme taught supervisors to
"Pre-position materials, tools and equipment at the best places in
the proper work area." Ten people standing around while someone fetches
a saw is ten people's time wasted, and bored people start improvising
(our reasoning).

### Each person answers to one person

NIMS: "Unity of command means that each individual only reports to one
person." It clears up who to listen to when two people give
conflicting directions. On a work day, split the group into small
crews, each with a crew lead, and have the crew leads answer to the
person in charge (general practice for the work-day form).

### Small crews

NIMS: "The optimal span of control for incident management is one
supervisor to five subordinates"; it calls this a guideline, and
lists what changes it: the nature of the task, the hazards, the
experience of the people, and how easily they can talk to each other.
So: crews of three to six, smaller where the work is dangerous or the
people are new (our application). Eight volunteers become two crews of
four, plus the person in charge.

The person in charge should be organising, not working flat out on
one task. If they are on the end of a lifting bar, nobody is watching
the whole job (general practice).

### Plain words

NIMS says using plain language and clear text, not codes, "is a matter
of public safety", and asks people to avoid jargon unique to one group.
A work day often mixes trades and strangers: say "the long ladder" or
"the 2 by 6s", not a nickname only half the group knows.

### Check in, check out

NIMS's accountability rules start with check-in: report in to receive
an assignment. At the end, checkout means finishing or handing over
work in progress, returning equipment, and briefing whoever takes
over. On a work day, keep a list of who came, who is in which crew and
which tools were borrowed, and count everyone at the end (our
adaptation). If the person in charge has to leave, NIMS's rule for a
transfer of command applies: brief the replacement on what they need
to carry on safely, and tell everybody who is now in charge.

### The briefing

Before the first tool is picked up, gather everyone for a few minutes
(general practice for the format, drawn from the sources above):

1. **The goal** and how you will know it is done.
2. **The plan:** the crews, who leads each, and what each crew does
   first.
3. **The hazards** of today's job and what is being done about each.
4. **The rules:** spacing with tools, the tool drop point, team lift
   calls, the stop word.
5. **Water, breaks and shade,** and the buddy system for newcomers.
6. **The emergency plan:** first aid kit, who is trained, how to call
   for help.
7. **Questions.** Ask for them, and wait for them.

## Keep people working well together

Shared work is mostly unpaid, so people come back only if the day was
worth their time (our reasoning). The Training Within Industry
programme's advice for working with people, written for wartime
supervisors in 1944, holds up well:

- "Let each worker know how he is getting along": say what you expect,
  and point out ways to improve.
- "Give credit when due", and tell people promptly when they did
  something well.
- "Tell people in advance about changes that will affect them." "Tell
  them WHY if possible."
- "Make best use of each person's ability." "Look for ability not now
  being used."
- "People Must Be Treated As Individuals."

Its method for improving a job is worth borrowing too: list every
detail of the job as it is done now, question each one (why is it
needed, where and when should it be done, who is best placed to do
it), then eliminate, combine, rearrange and simplify, and "Work out
your idea with others." In our view, the people doing the work usually
know where the time goes.

A few habits are general practice: share the unpleasant jobs and
rotate them; feed people; finish on time, or say early that you will
run late; thank everyone by name; and keep your own promise of help
when it is your turn.

## Afterwards: the after-action review

The US Army's guide to after-action reviews describes them as a
professional discussion that lets people "discover for themselves what
happened" and work out how to improve. Every review covers four parts:

1. **What was supposed to happen.** The plan and the goal.
2. **What actually happened.** Gathering as many people's views as
   possible to build a common picture.
3. **What was right or wrong with what happened.** The strong and weak
   points.
4. **How the task should be done differently** next time.

Two of its rules matter more than the format. "The AAR is not a
critique": no one, it says, regardless of rank, position or strength of
personality, has all the information or answers. And concentrate "on
identifying what went wrong and not on the person responsible"; if
needed, it says, name the role rather than the person.

The wildland fire community's version, from NWCG, restated: spend
about a quarter of the time on what was planned and what happened,
another quarter on why it happened, and half on what you will do next
time, focusing on what you can fix yourselves and on what went well so
it is repeated. When people are tired, NWCG suggests a quick version:
stand in a circle and have each person say one thing, such as one
thing that went well, one that did not, one thing they would do
differently, or one thing they learned. Hold the review as soon as
possible after the work, with the people who did it, and make sure
everyone speaks.

Write down what you decide, and put it with the plan for next time
([Keeping Records](/library#keeping-records)). A crew that reviews its work
days gets better at them; a crew that does not repeats its mistakes
(our reasoning).

## Worked examples

The numbers of people, times and tasks in these examples are ours, for
illustration.

### Framing a shed wall with eight people

- **Goal:** the north wall framed, raised, plumb and braced by 3 pm.
- **Crews:** the person in charge (watching, not hammering); a cutting
  crew of two at the saw station, one of them the only person running
  the saw; a nailing crew of three on the wall laid flat; a crew of two
  fetching and stacking lumber.
- **Hazards and controls:** the saw station roped off to everyone but
  its crew; hammers only with safety glasses on; the wall raised by
  everybody together, on one caller's count, with braces ready and a
  person at each brace; nobody standing on the far side of a wall being
  raised (general practice for these specifics). The checks for plumb
  and square are in [Square, Level and
  Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb).
- **Breaks:** at 10 and 12:30 and 2, water at the shade tree.
- **Review:** ten minutes at 3:15, four questions, notes in the
  building log.

### Moving a woodstove into the house

A cast-iron stove weighs far more than 50 pounds; the manual gives the
weight (general practice). By OSHA's guidance, that means mechanical
help and two or more people. Plan the route from the truck to the
hearth and clear it; measure the doors; use a hand truck rated for the
weight, or a ramp, rather than carrying it (OSHA suggests both); take
off the doors and any removable parts to lighten it if
the manual allows; one person calls every move. If the route has
stairs and you have no equipment for them, this is a job for movers
(general practice).

### A canning day in one kitchen

Six people, boiling water, pressure canners, knives, and children
underfoot. One person runs the canner and nobody else touches it; the
cutting is done at one counter, the filling at another; children have
a job in another room. The canning rules are in [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules), and what to do if someone is burned
is in [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns). The plan is
the tested recipe, followed exactly, and the review at the end is
whether every jar sealed and what slowed the line down (general
practice).

## Where the group's work should stop

Some jobs are beyond a willing crew, however good its organisation
(general practice unless noted):

- **Felling trees,** especially near buildings or power lines. The
  Forest Service says to be properly trained and certified before
  cutting standing or fallen trees.
- **Trenches and excavations** deep enough to bury someone; the federal
  rules are in [Square, Level and Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb).
- **Roof work** at heights where a fall could kill.
- **Anything with a crane, a hoist or overhead rigging.**
- **Electrical, gas and connected plumbing work,** which may need a
  licence and a permit (see [Trading Fairly](/library#trading-fairly)).
- **Large public events,** which may need permits, traffic control and
  insurance.

## How HumanityOS and the game model it

The game does not model shared work: nothing in it lets you give
another player or a character a task, a crew or a share of a job, or
plan a job for a group. The Leadership skill, which this topic
belongs to, appears in the skill list, but nothing in the game levels
it yet.

Outside the game, HumanityOS's Tasks page lays a job out as a board in
three columns, Todo, In Progress and Done, with a priority, an
assignee and labels on each task: the who-does-what list this guide
recommends. On the desktop app, a task you add there is not yet saved
or shared: it lasts only while the app is open, and connecting to a
server replaces the board with that server's own task list. For a work
day, keep the plan on paper as well.

## You own this when

- One person is in charge, one person watches for safety, and anyone
  can call stop.
- You walk the job beforehand, list its steps and what can go wrong,
  and fix the worst hazards first, preferring a change to the job over
  protective gear.
- People with swinging tools work clear of each other, tools go to a
  drop point, and machines have one trained operator.
- Loads over about 50 pounds get two or more people or a machine, and
  every team lift has one caller.
- Newcomers in the heat get shorter stints, more breaks and a buddy;
  everyone drinks before they are thirsty; and you know the signs of
  heat stroke and call 911 when in doubt.
- The goal can be checked, the plan fits on a page, and the materials
  are ready before people arrive.
- Each person answers to one crew lead, and crews are small.
- Everyone checks in and is counted at the end.
- You brief before starting and review afterwards: what was planned,
  what happened, why, and what to do next time, about the work rather
  than the people.
- You know which jobs belong to professionals, and you hire them.
