# Your First Tomato

You can grow a real tomato plant with no yard, no experience, and very
little money. One plant, one pot, one sunny spot: a windowsill that gets
strong sun, a balcony, a doorstep. This guide walks you through the whole
season, from choosing a plant to eating something you grew yourself.

One honest thing first: your first plant may struggle or even die. That
is normal and not wasted; every gardener killed plants while learning.
The plant is the lesson, the tomatoes are the bonus.

## What you need

Cheapest options first. Much of this can be free, and none of it needs
to be fancy.

- A container. A 5-gallon food-grade bucket (restaurants and bakeries
  often give these away) with holes drilled in the bottom works as well
  as a bought pot. Otherwise, any pot at least 10 inches across for a
  cherry tomato; 12 to 14 inches (about 4 to 5 gallons) is easier to
  keep watered and suits almost any container variety.
- A bag of potting mix, enough to fill your container. Sold as "potting
  mix" or "container mix." Not "garden soil" and not "topsoil." More on
  why below.
- One young tomato plant (called a "transplant" or "start"), a few
  inches tall, from a garden center, hardware store, or a neighbor.
  Buying a plant instead of seeds skips the hardest weeks; seeds are
  cheaper but add weeks of fussy indoor care and an extra way to fail.
- A small stake or tomato cage if your variety grows taller than about
  2 feet. Many patio types stay short and need nothing.
- Vegetable fertilizer, any cheap one. The package shows three numbers
  (the nutrient mix). Avoid ones where the FIRST number is the biggest;
  5-10-10 or anything labeled "for tomatoes" is fine, and an all-equal
  mix like 10-10-10 also works.

## Choosing a variety: small plant, small pot, big success

Tomato varieties range from compact bushes to sprawling vines taller
than you are. For a container, you want the compact end:

- Cherry tomatoes: small fruit, lots of it, earliest to ripen, and the
  most forgiving. Container-friendly names include Tumbler, Tumbling
  Tom, Tiny Tim, and Terenzo.
- Patio types: bred specifically for pots. Names like Patio, Patio
  Choice, and Patio Princess. Some stay only 18 inches tall.
- Compact "bush" slicers, like Bush Early Girl, if you want bigger
  fruit.

Skip anything labeled "indeterminate" (a vine that keeps growing all
season); it needs a bigger pot and constant tying-up. Labels saying
"determinate," "bush," "patio," or "container" are good territory.

## The container: size and drainage

Two rules, both absolute:

1. Big enough. At minimum a 2-gallon, 10-inch pot for a cherry type;
   4 to 5 gallons is more forgiving for anything. A too-small pot
   dries out in hours on a hot day.
2. Drainage holes in the bottom. Non-negotiable. Water must be able to
   run out, or the roots sit in a puddle and rot. If your container has
   no holes, drill or punch several.

Put a saucer or tray under the pot if it lives on a windowsill or
balcony you care about.

## The soil: why dirt from outside fails in a pot

Use potting mix, not soil dug from the ground. In a pot, garden dirt
compacts into a dense brick that drains poorly yet also dries out
fast, and it carries weed seeds, insects, and plant diseases. Potting
mix is a fluffy blend (usually peat or coir plus perlite, the little
white specks) designed to hold moisture and air at the same time. It
is the cheapest insurance you can buy for this project.

## Light: the real requirement

Tomatoes need direct sun, at least 6 hours a day, and they prefer 8 to
10. This is the requirement people most want to bargain with, and the
plant will not bargain; a bright-looking room can offer far less real
sun than you think. Before planting, watch your intended spot for a day
and honestly count the hours direct sunlight actually lands there.
South-facing spots (in the northern hemisphere) are usually best. With
less than 6 true hours, a cherry type in your sunniest spot is still
worth trying; expect a smaller harvest.

## Planting: the steps

Wait until all danger of frost is past and nights are reliably mild.
Tomatoes cannot tolerate frost at all, and cold nights below 55 F stall
them even without frost.

1. Fill the container with potting mix to a few inches below the rim.
2. Water the mix until water runs out the bottom, then let it drain.
3. Remove the plant from its little pot, loosen the roots gently, and
   plant it deep, so only the top 2 or 3 sets of leaves stick out above
   the mix (a set = one leaf-bearing point on the stem; when in doubt,
   bury about two-thirds of the plant). This feels wrong and is right:
   tomatoes grow new roots all along a buried stem. Pinch off leaves
   that would end up underground.
4. Firm the mix around the stem and water again.
5. If using a cage or stake, add it now, not later, so you do not
   stab established roots.
6. Put the pot in your sunny spot. If your plant came from indoors or
   a greenhouse, give it a few days of partial sun first so the leaves
   can toughen up before full sun. Done.

## Watering: the finger test

This is the skill that decides how your season goes.

Push a finger into the mix down to the second knuckle, about 2 inches.
If it feels dry at your fingertip, water slowly until water runs from
the drainage holes, then stop. If it feels damp, do nothing and check
again tomorrow.

That is the whole method. Check daily; in hot summer weather a
container tomato can genuinely need water every day, sometimes twice.
In cool cloudy weather it may need none for days. The finger decides,
not the calendar.

The two failure modes are not equal: a wilted, underwatered plant
usually recovers within hours of a good drink, but a constantly soggy
plant is quietly drowning, because waterlogged roots cannot get air
and they rot. Overwatering is the easier mistake, since it comes from
caring too much. Consistency also matters as much as amount; wild
swings between bone dry and soaked cause the fruit problems below.

## Feeding

Potting mix contains little food, so a container tomato must be fed.
Two easy approaches, either is fine:

- Mix a slow-release fertilizer into the pot at planting, following the
  package rate, and top up on the package schedule.
- Or use a water-soluble fertilizer (dissolved in the watering can)
  every week or two at the package rate, starting a couple of weeks
  after planting.

More is not better. Too much fertilizer, especially nitrogen-heavy
mixes, gives you a huge leafy plant with no tomatoes. Follow the
package. When in doubt, feed less.

## Temperature: what the plant can take

- Frost kills tomatoes outright. Whenever frost is forecast, or the
  night will drop near 32 F, bring the pot indoors or cover the plant
  overnight. This is the container's superpower: unlike a garden, a pot
  can be picked up and moved.
- Fruit set (flowers turning into tomatoes) needs nights above 55 F.
  Pollination works best between about 70 and 82 F.
- In heat waves, fruit set drops off as days climb into the upper 80s
  and beyond, and ripe red color develops poorly above 90 F. The
  plant is not dying; it is waiting. In extreme heat you can pick fruit
  that has started to color and let it finish ripening indoors.

## Healthy or struggling? What to look for

A happy plant has deep green leaves, a thick stem, steady new growth
at the top, and flowers within a few weeks. Leaves curling a little in
midday heat and relaxing by evening is normal. Warning signs: pale or
yellowing leaves, a stretched skinny stem leaning toward the light, no
new growth for a week, wilting that does not recover overnight, or
flowers falling off without fruit. Each appears in the list below.

## What can go wrong (and what it means)

- Leggy seedlings (tall, pale, thin, flopping): not enough light. Move
  the plant to more direct sun and rotate the pot so it stops leaning.
  When planting a leggy transplant, bury the extra stem deep, as in
  step 3 above; the flaw becomes roots.
- Yellowing lower leaves: a few old bottom leaves yellowing and
  dropping is normal aging. Many yellow leaves at once usually mean
  hunger (you skipped feeding) or drowning (soil constantly wet; check
  your drainage and ease off the watering).
- Flowers fall off without fruit (blossom drop): almost always
  temperature, nights below 55 F or days in the upper 80s and above.
  You cannot fix the weather; keep the plant watered and fed and it
  will set fruit when conditions return to normal.
- Black leathery sunken patch on the bottom of the fruit (blossom end
  rot): not a disease, and nothing is eating your plant. The fruit ran
  short of calcium while forming, which in practice is almost always
  caused by uneven watering, not bad soil. Remove the affected fruit
  and water consistently; later fruit will very likely be fine. Sprays
  are not the fix; steady water is.
- Wilting that does not bounce back overnight: check the extremes. If
  the pot is sitting in a full saucer or the mix is soggy, the roots
  are drowning; empty the saucer and let the mix dry down. If the mix
  is bone dry an inch deep, water thoroughly until it drains. Either
  way, evening recovery within a day or two is the sign you fixed it.
- No new growth for a week or more: usually cold nights (growth stalls
  below the 55 F range) or hunger. If nights are warm and you have not
  fed the plant in a few weeks, feed it; container mix holds little
  nutrition on its own.

## Harvest: the payoff

A tomato is ready when it reaches full color for its variety (check
the label) and feels slightly soft when gently squeezed; ripe fruit
twists off the stem easily. Pick cherry types the day they color fully,
since they can split if left long, especially after rain. Do not
refrigerate a tomato you are about to eat; cold dulls the flavor.

## The honest timeline

From planting a purchased transplant to your first ripe fruit is
roughly 65 to 90 days for most varieties, with cherry and patio types
at the early end. So: plant in late spring, expect your first tomato in
mid to late summer. Flowers come first, then green marble-sized fruit,
then a long, slightly agonizing wait while they size up and finally
color. Nothing is wrong during that wait. The plant then keeps
producing until cold weather ends the season.

## What this one plant teaches

By the end of one season you will have learned, in your hands and not
from a page: how to judge soil moisture, how much sun "full sun" really
is, how to read a plant's leaves, and how weather decides what plants
do. Those skills transfer to every crop there is. The natural next step
is one more container: a second tomato variety to compare, or an easy
herb like basil, which loves the same pot, mix, sun, and watering you
have already mastered. You are no longer a person who has never grown
food.
