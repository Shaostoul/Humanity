# Why Seasons Happen

Almost everything a grower, a builder or a traveller plans around follows
the seasons: when the soil is warm enough to sow, how long a day's light
lasts, how much a solar panel will make in January, when the first frost is
likely. Most people carry a wrong picture of why the seasons happen, and the
wrong picture leads to wrong plans. This guide gives the right one, well
enough to plan by:

- why the seasons come from the tilt of the Earth's axis and not from its
  distance from the Sun;
- what the tilt does: the angle of the sun and the length of the day, by
  latitude and by season, with real numbers;
- what solstices and equinoxes are, and why day and night are not quite equal
  at an equinox;
- why the hottest weeks come a month or more after the longest day;
- why coasts swing less than inland places;
- why the two hemispheres have opposite seasons;
- and what all of it means for growing food.

Units: temperatures are in degrees Fahrenheit with Celsius in brackets,
because the US climate records quoted here are kept in Fahrenheit. Latitude
is in degrees north or south of the equator. Dates of astronomical events are
in Universal Time (UT), the time at the Greenwich meridian, which is why an
event can fall on a different calendar date where you live.

## The tilt, not the distance

NASA's short answer is that Earth's tilted axis causes the seasons. The axis
is the line the Earth spins around once a day, and it does not stand upright
to the Earth's path around the Sun: NASA's Earth fact sheet puts the tilt, the
obliquity, at 23.44 degrees. The axis also keeps pointing the same way in
space all year, as the National Weather Service describes it, so for half of
the orbit the Northern Hemisphere leans toward the Sun and for the other half
it leans away.

**Distance is not the reason.** The Earth's orbit is slightly lopsided, and
many people assume summer is when we are closest. NASA's point is that the
opposite is true for the north. NASA's fact sheet gives the closest distance
(perihelion) as 147.1 million km and the farthest (aphelion) as 152.1 million
km, and the Naval Observatory puts them in 2026 on 3 January and 6 July. So
the Northern Hemisphere has its winter when the Earth is nearest the Sun.

The distance does make a small difference (worked here): sunlight falls off
with the square of the distance, and (152.1 / 147.1) squared is 1.069, so the
Earth receives about 7 percent more sunlight in early January than in early
July. Seven percent is real, but it is far smaller than what the tilt does,
which is the next section.

## What the tilt does: sun angle and day length

The tilt changes two things at once, and both push the same way.

### The angle of the sun

When your hemisphere leans toward the Sun, the Sun climbs higher at noon.
On any day there is one latitude where the Sun is straight overhead at noon.
It swings between 23.44 degrees north in June and 23.44 degrees south in
December, crossing the equator at the equinoxes, and the height of your noon
sun above a level horizon is 90 degrees minus the distance, in degrees of
latitude, between you and it. Worked from NASA's tilt:

| Latitude | Noon sun, June solstice | Noon sun, equinoxes | Noon sun, December solstice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equator | 66.6 degrees (to the north) | 90 (overhead) | 66.6 (to the south) |
| 23.44 N (Tropic of Cancer) | 90 (overhead) | 66.6 | 43.1 |
| 40 N | 73.4 | 50.0 | 26.6 |
| 50 N | 63.4 | 40.0 | 16.6 |
| 60 N | 53.4 | 30.0 | 6.6 |
| 66.56 N (Arctic Circle) | 46.9 | 23.4 | 0 |

A low sun spreads the same beam of light over more ground, the way a torch
held at a slant lights a long oval instead of a small circle. The share of the
full, overhead strength that reaches each square metre of level ground is the
sine of the sun's height (worked here): at 40 degrees north that is 0.96 at
noon in June and 0.45 at noon in December. The December noon sun at 40 degrees
north delivers less than half as much light to each square metre as the June
one.

### The length of the day

The same tilt makes the Sun's path across the sky longer in summer and
shorter in winter. These are the Naval Observatory's sunrise and sunset times
for 2026, asked for each latitude on the Greenwich meridian and turned into
day lengths here:

| Latitude | 20 March | 21 June | 23 September | 21 December |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Equator | 12 h 07 m | 12 h 07 m | 12 h 07 m | 12 h 08 m |
| 10 N | 12 h 07 m | 12 h 42 m | 12 h 06 m | 11 h 32 m |
| 20 N | 12 h 07 m | 13 h 20 m | 12 h 06 m | 10 h 56 m |
| 30 N | 12 h 07 m | 14 h 05 m | 12 h 06 m | 10 h 13 m |
| 40 N | 12 h 08 m | 15 h 01 m | 12 h 08 m | 9 h 20 m |
| 50 N | 12 h 10 m | 16 h 22 m | 12 h 08 m | 8 h 04 m |
| 60 N | 12 h 12 m | 18 h 52 m | 12 h 11 m | 5 h 52 m |
| 66.6 N | 12 h 16 m | Sun never sets | 12 h 13 m | 2 h 08 m |
| 70 N | 12 h 19 m | Sun never sets | 12 h 15 m | Sun never rises |
| 40 S | 12 h 09 m | 9 h 20 m | 12 h 10 m | 15 h 02 m |

Three things to read from it:

- **At the equator the day hardly changes.** Twelve hours and a few minutes,
  all year. The farther from the equator, the bigger the swing: at 40 degrees
  it is nearly six hours, at 60 degrees thirteen.
- **Beyond the polar circles the day can last all day, or not come at all.**
  At 70 degrees north on 21 December the Naval Observatory has the Sun
  continuously below the horizon, with only civil twilight, from 09:54 to 14:02
  UT on the Greenwich meridian, for light. At 66.6 degrees it still rises for
  2 hours 8 minutes, because sunrise is counted from the Sun's top edge and
  the air bends its light over the horizon, the subject of the equinox section
  below.
- **The south mirrors the north.** At 40 degrees south the June day is as short
  as the December day at 40 degrees north.

### The two together

Put the angle and the day length together and the difference between summer
and winter is larger than either alone. Worked here, from NASA's figure for
the sunlight arriving at the Earth's distance (1,361 watts on each square
metre facing the Sun) and the standard geometry of the Sun's daily path,
allowing for the Earth's distance from the Sun in each season, this is the
sunlight reaching level ground at the top of the atmosphere, averaged over the
whole 24 hours, in watts per square metre:

| Latitude | June solstice | Equinoxes | December solstice |
|---|---|---|---|
| Equator | 385 | 433 | 411 |
| 40 N | 483 | 332 | 156 |
| 60 N | 476 | 217 | 24 |
| North Pole | 524 | 0 | 0 |
| 40 S | 146 | 332 | 516 |

At 40 degrees north a June day brings about three times the sunlight of a
December day, and at 60 degrees about twenty times. And on the June solstice
the North Pole, with the Sun circling above it all day, receives more
sunlight than the equator does on any day of the year. The air takes a share
of all of these before the light reaches the ground, and the National Weather
Service adds that it scatters more of it when the Sun is low, because the
light has further to travel through the atmosphere; at the ground, winter
loses by even more than the table shows.

## Solstices and equinoxes

The **solstices** are the two moments when the tilt leans most toward the Sun
and most away from it. On the June solstice the Sun is overhead at the Tropic
of Cancer, 23.44 degrees north; on the December solstice at the Tropic of
Capricorn, 23.44 degrees south. The **equinoxes** are the two moments when
the Sun is overhead at the equator. The Naval Observatory's times for 2026
and 2027:

| Event | 2026 (UT) | 2027 (UT) |
|---|---|---|
| Perihelion (nearest the Sun) | 3 January, 17:15 | 3 January, 02:33 |
| March equinox | 20 March, 14:46 | 20 March, 20:25 |
| June solstice | 21 June, 08:24 | 21 June, 14:11 |
| Aphelion (farthest from the Sun) | 6 July, 17:30 | 5 July, 05:06 |
| September equinox | 23 September, 00:05 | 23 September, 06:02 |
| December solstice | 21 December, 20:50 | 22 December, 02:42 |

The September 2026 equinox at 00:05 UT on the 23rd is still the 22nd in the
Americas: the National Weather Service in the Quad Cities lists it as 7:05 pm
Central Daylight Time on 22 September.

**Day and night are not exactly equal at an equinox.** The name means "equal
night", but the Naval Observatory explains why it is not quite true. Sunrise
is counted from the moment the Sun's top edge appears and sunset until its top
edge disappears, and the air bends sunlight so that the Sun looks higher than
it is. Its tables are worked out for a Sun whose centre is 50 minutes of arc
(a little under one degree) below the horizon at sunrise and sunset. So on an
equinox the day is about 7 minutes longer than the night up to about 25
degrees of latitude, and 10 minutes or more at 50 degrees, which is what the
table above shows. The dates of truly equal day and night fall a few days
away from the equinoxes: about 17 March and 26 September at 40 degrees north.

**The longest day is not the only record.** The Naval Observatory tabulates
the longest and shortest days by latitude: at 45 degrees north, 15 hours 38
minutes and 8 hours 46 minutes. The National Weather Service adds that the
earliest sunset and the latest sunrise do not fall on the shortest day: in the
Quad Cities of Iowa and Illinois, the earliest sunset comes around 8 December
and the latest sunrise near 5 January.

**The seasons are not the same length.** NOAA's National Centers for
Environmental Information (NCEI) say the astronomical seasons vary between 89
and 93 days because the orbit is not a circle. From the Naval Observatory's
times (worked here), the northern spring of 2026 lasts 92.7 days, the summer
93.7, the autumn 89.9 and the winter 89.0; the northern summer half of the
year, from the March equinox to the September one, is 186.4 days, and the
winter half 178.9. The longest season is the one that contains aphelion, in
July.

**Astronomical and meteorological seasons.** NCEI explains that
astronomers count the seasons from the solstices and equinoxes, while weather
services count them in whole months so that each season's statistics can be
compared from year to year: in the Northern Hemisphere, spring is March, April
and May; summer June, July and August; autumn September, October and November;
winter December, January and February. Climate statistics are usually kept by
these three-month seasons, so a climate record's "summer" is June to August.

## Why the hottest weeks come after the longest day

If sunlight were all that set the temperature, the hottest day would be the
June solstice. It is not. NCEI's explanation, from its map of the warmest day
of the year:

> The temperature increase after the solstice occurs because the rate of heat input from the sun during the day continues to be greater than the cooling at night for several weeks, until temperatures start to descend in July and early August.

It is like a pot on a stove after the flame is turned down a little: the
water keeps warming until the heat it loses catches up with the heat it gets.
The delay is often called the seasonal lag. The 1991 to 2020 US Climate
Normals, NCEI's 30-year averages, show it in the dates when the normal daily
high is highest and the normal daily low is lowest:

| Place | Warmest normal high | When | Coldest normal low | When |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sacramento, California | 93.0 F (33.9 C) | 18 to 23 July | 38.1 F (3.4 C) | 20 to 24 December |
| Spokane, Washington | 87.3 F (30.7 C) | 27 to 31 July | 23.2 F (minus 4.9 C) | 23 to 29 December |
| Seattle, Washington | 79.0 F (26.1 C) | 28 July to 1 August | 36.7 F (2.6 C) | 18 to 22 December |
| San Francisco, California | 70.6 F (21.4 C) | 25 September to 8 October | 46.2 F (7.9 C) | 29 December to 6 January |

(The normals are smoothed, so the peak is a plateau of several days; the
dates are the whole plateau.)

Across the country the lag varies a great deal. NCEI's maps put the warmest
day anywhere from early June to October: in the desert Southwest it comes in
June, because the summer monsoon brings clouds and rain from July (11 June at
Terlingua, Texas), and on the Pacific coast as late as October, because of the
persistent marine layer (2 October in San Francisco). For the coldest day,
NCEI's companion maps show most of the West reaching it in December and most
of the East in January, with Hawaii and Puerto Rico as late as February and
early March.

**The lag makes spring cold and autumn warm.** On the two equinoxes the day is
about 12 hours long everywhere, and the same length at any one place, yet the
normal daily average temperature on 20 March and 22 September is very
different:

| Place | 20 March | 22 September | Autumn warmer by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sacramento | 55.8 F (13.2 C) | 71.5 F (21.9 C) | 15.7 F (8.7 C) |
| Spokane | 41.3 F (5.2 C) | 58.9 F (14.9 C) | 17.6 F (9.8 C) |
| Seattle | 47.6 F (8.7 C) | 61.1 F (16.2 C) | 13.5 F (7.5 C) |
| San Francisco | 55.6 F (13.1 C) | 63.0 F (17.2 C) | 7.4 F (4.1 C) |

The same light arrives, but in March the ground and the water around it are
still cold from the winter, and in September they are still warm from the
summer.

## Why coasts swing less than inland

Look at San Francisco in both tables above. Its warmest weeks come two
months after Sacramento's, and its seasons are much smaller. The reason is
the ocean.

NOAA explains that the ocean stores enormous amounts of the Sun's energy: the
water is clear enough for sunlight to reach 150 to 200 metres (500 to 650
feet) in clear tropical seas, and wind and weather keep stirring it, spreading
the warmth through a great depth of water. On land, the sunlight is absorbed in
the top few inches of soil. The US Geological Survey adds the other half:
water takes a great deal of heat to warm (4,184 joules to warm one kilogram of
water by 1 C, against 385 for copper), and it says this is why the change
between seasons is gradual rather than sudden, especially near the oceans, and
why cities near the ocean have less extreme temperatures than inland ones. Its
example: Nebraska has colder winters and hotter summers than Oregon, even
though Oregon is farther north.

The Climate Normals show it plainly. San Francisco and Sacramento are about
116 km apart and both close to sea level (worked from the stations'
coordinates):

| Monthly normal average | San Francisco (downtown) | Sacramento (Executive Airport) |
|---|---|---|
| Coldest month | January, 52.2 F (11.2 C) | December, 47.3 F (8.5 C) |
| Warmest month | September, 62.9 F (17.2 C) | July, 75.9 F (24.4 C) |
| Swing between them | 10.7 F (5.9 C) | 28.6 F (15.9 C) |

The same thing happens farther north. Seattle, near the salt water of Puget
Sound, swings 25.4 F (14.1 C) between its coldest month, December at 42.0 F,
and its warmest, August at 67.4 F. Spokane, at nearly the same latitude but
about 360 km inland, swings 41.9 F (23.3 C), from 29.1 F in December to 71.0
F in July. (Spokane is also 717 metres up, against Seattle's 113, which makes
it colder all year; it is the size of the swing that the inland position
explains.) The delay is small in this pair: Seattle's warmest normal highs
come from 28 July to 1 August, a day after Spokane's. San Francisco, open to
the ocean and its marine layer, is the strong case of a coast that both
shrinks and delays its seasons.

So a coastal garden has milder seasons than an inland one at the same
latitude, and an inland one has hotter summers and harder winters.
[The Climate Where You Live](/library#the-climate-where-you-live) shows how to read
your own station's normals.

## Opposite seasons in the two hemispheres

Because the two poles are at opposite ends of the tilted axis, NASA explains,
they have opposite timing for the seasons: when the North Pole leans toward
the Sun it is summer in the north and winter in the south. NCEI says the
southern seasons are reversed but begin on the same dates. The day length
table shows it: 40 degrees south has a 9 hour 20 minute day in June and a 15
hour 2 minute day in December, the northern figures swapped.

What follows for anyone using advice written for the other hemisphere
(worked here, not from a source): calendar-month advice must be moved by six
months. A seed packet from the north that says "sow in April" means sow in
October in the south. Advice written in terms of frost dates or soil
temperature needs no change, which is one more reason to plan by those.

The extra 7 percent of sunlight at perihelion falls in the southern summer,
yet it does not make the south's seasons the fiercer ones. In the NOAA
reanalysis data the game's climate is fitted to (How the game models it,
below), the southern seasons come out smaller than the northern ones at the
same latitude, over land and over sea: how much land and ocean there is
matters more than the distance, as the coastal comparison above shows.

## What it means for growing

**Day length is a signal, not just an energy supply.** Some plants time their
growth by the length of the day, or more exactly by the night.
[How Plants Work](/library#how-plants-work) explains the short-day and long-day
classes; the onion is the example every grower meets. University of Maryland
Extension explains it through the tilt: in summer the days are longer the
farther north you go, and onion types differ in the day length that starts
them forming bulbs. Short-day onions are for the South, generally below 36
degrees north, and start to form bulbs at 10 to 12 hours of daylight;
intermediate-day onions respond to 12 to 15 hours; long-day onions need 14 to
16 hours and do best north of 36 degrees.

Check a variety against the day length table (worked here): at 30 degrees north
the longest day of the year is 14 hours 5 minutes, barely enough for a
long-day onion's signal, while at 50 degrees the June day reaches 16 hours 22
minutes. A short-day onion in the north gets its 10 to 12 hour signal in late
winter and early spring: the Maryland writer works out that in Rockville,
Maryland, the day passes 10 hours around 25 January and 12 hours around 17
March, and that short-day onions there would try to form bulbs in January to
March, which is too cold.

**Light is the plant's energy budget.** The winter sun is lower and the day is
shorter, so a winter crop grows slowly however mild the afternoon.
[The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar) works through what that means
month by month at one place, and [Extending the Season](/library#extending-the-season)
explains why no cover can add the light a winter day lacks.

**Plant by the soil, not the daylight.** The seasonal lag means a March day
and a September day of the same length arrive with very different soil and air
temperatures: at Sacramento the normal daily average is 15.7 F warmer on the
September equinox than on the March one, with the same daylight. Seeds and
roots answer to temperature. [The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar)
covers soil temperature and frost dates, the two numbers that actually
govern sowing.

**Solar power has seasons too.** For this project's home site at Silverdale,
Washington (about 47.6 degrees north), NLR's PVWatts calculator gives a 400 W
panel tilted 40 degrees to the south 52.5 kWh in August and 17.6 kWh in
January, a third as much (the same query as in
[Batteries and Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage)). A home that lives on its
panels plans its batteries and its backup for January, not for the year's
average.

## How the game models it

The game's world has seasons, and since 27 September 2026 the air in it
follows them by latitude, land and sea. Some of what this guide describes is
modelled closely; some of it is not modelled at all, and the list below says
which.

- **The game year.** A game year is 120 game days, four seasons of 30 days
  each, starting with Spring (`src/systems/time.rs`). A game day lasts 20
  real minutes at normal speed, so a game year passes in 40 real hours. The
  seasons are read as the astronomical ones: Spring begins at the March
  equinox and Winter at the northern winter solstice, which sits three quarters
  of the way through the game year, on game day 90
  (`data/environment/climate.ron`). [Decision Briefs](https://github.com/Shaostoul/Humanity/blob/main/docs/design/decision-briefs.md)
  (Brief 6) lays out the open question of whether a full-realism mode should
  run on real 24 hour days; until it is decided, the day is 20 minutes.
- **The air by latitude and season.** Environment Layer 1
  (`src/systems/env_layer1.rs`, with each world's numbers in
  `data/environment/climate.ron` and the design in
  [Environment Fields](https://github.com/Shaostoul/Humanity/blob/main/docs/design/environment-fields.md), "Layer 1 as
  built") gives the air temperature anywhere on Earth for any fraction of the
  game year. Its seasonal term is proportional to the sine of the latitude, so
  it is zero at the equator and has opposite signs in the two hemispheres: the
  seasons are opposite, and the equator has none. Its seasonal sizes and
  timings were fitted separately for land and for sea, and for each
  hemisphere, to NOAA's NCEP/NCAR Reanalysis 1 monthly averages for 1991 to
  2020 (`scripts/climate-fit.js` reproduces them). A place's own land share,
  sampled at the place and on a ring about 111 km around it, blends the land
  and sea seasons.
- **What those fitted numbers give** (worked here from the model's formula):
  - At 45 degrees north, deep inland, the air swings from minus 5.2 C at the
    coldest to 23.2 C at the warmest, a 28.4 C swing, and peaks 8.5 game days
    after the summer solstice.
  - At 45 degrees north on the open sea it swings from 2.4 C to 15.6 C, a 13.3
    C swing, and peaks 15.5 game days after the solstice: less than half the
    swing, and later. Those are the coastal and seasonal-lag effects this guide
    describes.
  - At 45 degrees south the inland swing is 20.4 C and the open sea's only
    7.2 C: the fitted southern seasons are smaller than the northern ones.
  - Inland at 45 degrees north, the first day of Spring is 2.9 C and the first
    day of Autumn 15.1 C, at the two equinoxes, with the same sunlight: the
    equinox table above, in the game.
  - The lag scales with the game year: 8.5 of 120 game days is the same share
    of a year as about 26 real days, which puts the inland peak in mid July,
    where Sacramento's is.
- **What you feel.** The temperature at the player is Layer 1 at the player's
  place, plus the weather's own deviation, plus a day and night swing, and it
  feeds the body's heat balance.
- **What crops feel is simpler.** Fields and water bodies read one reference
  temperature for the whole planet, not Layer 1 at their own position: Earth's
  four-step seasonal table, 15 C in Spring, 30 C in Summer, 12 C in Autumn and
  minus 2 C in Winter, the values the game's farming balance was tuned against
  (`src/systems/body_environment.rs`, with the weather's deviation on top). A
  field in the tropics and a field in Norway get the same seasons, and the
  change comes in steps at each season's start, with no lag.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game:

- **The day never changes length.** The gameplay sun rises at 6:00 and sets at
  18:00 on every day of the year and at every latitude (`src/systems/time.rs`
  and `src/systems/solar.rs`), and the garden counts every day as half
  daylight (`DAYLIGHT_FRACTION` in `src/systems/farming/mod.rs`, held to the
  sun's curve by a test). There is no midnight sun and no polar night.
- **The sun's height never changes with the season.** The home's solar panels
  make the same power at noon in Winter as in Summer. The Construction page's
  power meter credits each panel with the site's real yearly average from
  PVWatts; the live simulation does not use it.
- **Plants do not read day length.** No crop bulbs, flowers or bolts on a day
  length signal; grow lights run on a fixed 18 hour timer
  (`data/garden/lighting.ron`).
- **Crops do not feel the seasonal lag or the coast**, as the list above says.
- **The Sun drawn in the sky follows the real date and time**, not the game's
  20 minute day, so the sky and the gameplay sun can disagree; Brief 6 records
  this.
- **Other worlds.** Mars is tilted 25.19 degrees, according to NASA's Mars fact
  sheet, so the real Mars has seasons. The game's Mars row carries no seasonal
  or latitude term yet, because no Mars climatology was in hand, and the file
  says so rather than inventing one. The Moon has no climate row at all.
