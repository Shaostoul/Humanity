# Knowing Which Way Is North

Most of finding your way comes down to one question asked again and
again: which way am I facing? Once you know where north is, a map turns
the right way round, a compass bearing means something, and "the road is
a kilometre east" becomes a direction you can walk. The same question
matters at home: which way a garden bed runs, which wall a window or a
solar panel should face, and how to tell somebody where something is.

There are four ways to answer it, and they are not equally good. A
compass you know how to use is the best of them in most weather. The sun
works when you can see shadows. The stars work on clear nights. The
land gives hints, some of them useful and some of them folklore. This
guide teaches all four, in that order of trust, and is plain about how
wrong each one can be.

Most of it comes from two Army manuals, *Map Reading and Land
Navigation* (FM 3-25.26, 2001) and *Survival* (FM 21-76, 1992), from the
US Geological Survey's fact sheet on map and compass, from NOAA's
National Centers for Environmental Information on the magnetic field,
and from NASA, the US Forest Service and the National Weather Service.
All are United States government publications. Where something is
general practice or our own reasoning rather than a published rule, the
text says so.

Three other guides sit beside this one. [The Sky as an
Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument) goes deep on Polaris, the noon
shadow and the size of the compass error where the simulation is set.
[Reading a Map](/library#reading-a-map) covers turning a map to match the
ground. [Estimating](/library#estimating) covers counting paces, which is how
you know how far you have walked along a direction once you have one.

## First: the mistakes that hurt people

Finding north is usually a calm job done in good time. The mistakes
that turn it into an emergency are few, and worth knowing before any
technique.

- **Never look at the sun to find it.** NASA says that outside the brief
  total phase of a total eclipse, it is "not safe to look directly at
  the Sun without specialized eye protection for solar viewing", and
  that looking at it through a camera lens, binoculars or a telescope
  without a proper solar filter "will instantly cause severe eye
  injury." Every sun method in this guide works from a shadow. You
  never need to look at the sun itself.
- **The sky gives a general direction, not a precise one.** The Army's
  survival manual says of its sun and star methods: "These methods,
  however, will give you only a general direction." It adds that you
  get a truer direction if you also know the land. Check one method
  against another whenever you can.
- **The watch method can walk you in circles.** The map reading manual
  says it "can be in error, especially in the lower latitudes, and may
  cause circling." Its fix is in the sun section below.
- **Carry a compass you know how to use, and do not count on a phone.**
  The Forest Service lists "A compass that you know how to use" among
  the essentials, and warns that a GPS device sometimes does not get a
  signal or its battery fails, and that "Cell phones also likely will
  not work because of a lack of signal."
- **Tell someone where you are going, and do not go alone.** The Forest
  Service asks you to give someone the exact details: where you are
  going, the trail, when you will be back, your vehicle and where you
  will park, and how many are going, and it ends that list with "do not
  go alone."
- **If you think you are lost, stop.** The Forest Service's advice is
  "stop, stay calm, stay put." Think back over how you got there, get
  out the compass and work out the directions where you stand, and "Do
  not walk aimlessly." Its next words are "If you are on a trail, stay
  on it." It says that if you are not very, very confident of the
  route, it is always better to stay put, and to stay in place if night
  is falling, you are hurt or you are near exhaustion. Following a
  stream downhill is its very last resort, and it warns that this
  "could be very dangerous."

## Two norths, and which one you are using

**True north** is the direction of the North Pole, the end of the
Earth's axis. Maps are drawn to it, and the sun and stars give it to
you directly.

**Magnetic north** is where a compass needle points. The USGS fact
sheet on map and compass is blunt: "a compass does not really point to
true north, except by coincidence in some areas." The needle follows
the Earth's magnetic field, and NOAA's National Centers for
Environmental Information (NCEI) say it points along the field where the
compass is, "and not to any single point".

The angle between the two is the **magnetic declination**. NCEI defines
it as "the angle between magnetic north and true north", counted as
positive when magnetic north lies east of true north and negative when
it lies west. It is different from place to place and it changes from
year to year, and NCEI says "There is no simple equation connecting
latitude and longitude to declination." So you look it up: NCEI runs an
online calculator where you enter your location (or, in the United
States, a zip code). [The Sky as an Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument)
gives the value for Silverdale, Washington: about 15 degrees east in
2026.

A map adds a third north, **grid north**, the direction of the grid
lines printed on it. [Reading a Map](/library#reading-a-map) explains it and
the declination diagram in the margin. The USGS fact sheet warns that
the declination printed on a map was worked out when the map was made
and "may not be current." Use today's value, not the map's.

### Converting a bearing

A bearing is a direction in degrees, measured clockwise from north:
east is 90, south 180, west 270. A bearing read off a compass is a
**magnetic** bearing. A bearing measured on a map is a **true** (or
grid) bearing. You have to convert between them, and getting the sign
backwards doubles the error instead of removing it.

NCEI's rule, from compass to true: **add the declination to the
magnetic bearing, counting east declination as positive and west as
negative.** The USGS fact sheet gives the other direction, from a bearing
measured on the map to the one you set on the compass: if the magnetic
north arrow on the map's diagram is to the right of true north (east
declination), subtract; if it is to the left (west), add. They are the
same rule run backwards.

**Worked examples** (arithmetic):

- Declination 15 degrees east. Your compass points at a hilltop and
  reads 300. True bearing: 300 + 15 = 315.
- Same place. The map says the road is due east, true bearing 90. The
  bearing to walk on the compass: 90 - 15 = 75.
- Declination 10 degrees west, counted as -10. The compass reads 30.
  True bearing: 30 + (-10) = 20.

How much does it matter? The Army manual's rule of thumb is that each
degree you are off moves you about 18 metres sideways for every 1,000
metres you walk. Ignore a 15 degree declination and you finish roughly
15 x 18 = 270 metres off line after one kilometre.

Some compasses have an adjustment that lets you set the declination
once, so the compass then reads true bearings. That removes the
arithmetic, and the chance of doing it backwards, every time you use it
(general practice; [The Sky as an Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument)
describes both ways of working).

## The compass

Two kinds of compass turn up most often. The **lensatic** compass the
Army manual describes has a hinged cover with a sighting wire, a lens
for reading the dial and a rotating bezel ring that clicks in 3 degree
steps. The **baseplate** compass sold for walking has a clear plastic
base with a direction-of-travel arrow, a rotating housing marked in
degrees, and an orienting arrow printed inside the housing. The parts
differ; the method is the same.

### Check it before you trust it

The Army manual asks for an inspection the first time you use a
compass: the dial or needle must swing freely and must not stick, the
glass must not be broken and the numbers must be readable. It also says
a compass has to be checked now and then against a known direction, and
that one more than 3 degrees out should not be used.

A known direction is easy to make at home: a true north line laid out
by the sun (below), plus your declination, tells you what the compass
should read along it. If it reads within a couple of degrees of that,
it is working (our method, using the manual's 3 degree limit).

Two less obvious checks:

- **A compass is balanced for one half of the world.** NCEI explains
  that the field dips into the ground at an angle, so a northern
  hemisphere compass carries a small weight on the south end of its
  needle to keep it level, and a southern one carries it on the north
  end. A compass taken to the other hemisphere without that change may
  drag and not swing freely.
- **Bubbles and a sticky needle** are reasons to replace a compass, not
  to trust it a little less (general practice).

### Keep it away from metal

A compass reads every magnet and every piece of iron or steel near it,
not just the Earth. The USGS fact sheet says to stay away from "pocket
knives, belt buckles, railroad tracks, trucks, electrical lines, and so
forth". The Army manual's separation distances are in [Reading a
Map](/library#reading-a-map): 55 metres from high-tension power lines, 18 from
a vehicle, 10 from barbed wire and telephone wires. Magnets in phone
cases, speakers, steel toecaps and a car door are worth adding at home
(general practice). NCEI notes that stray fields from power lines are
not in its models, so the declination it gives you assumes you are
standing clear of them.

A quick test: hold the compass still and bring the suspect object
slowly towards it. If the needle moves, keep them apart (general
practice).

### Taking a bearing on something you can see

With a baseplate compass (general practice; the principle is the Army
manual's):

1. Hold the compass level in front of you, at waist height, with the
   direction-of-travel arrow pointing at the landmark.
2. Without moving the base, turn the housing until the orienting arrow
   lines up under the north end of the needle (usually red).
3. Read the bearing where the housing meets the index line at the base
   of the direction-of-travel arrow. That is a magnetic bearing.

With a lensatic compass, the manual's preferred "centerhold technique"
is to open the compass flat, hold it steady with your elbows pulled into
your sides so it sits between your chin and your belt, "turn your
entire body toward the object", and read the bearing beneath the fixed
black index line. For the most careful reading it uses the
compass-to-cheek technique: look through the rear sight slot, line up
the sighting wire with the object, then glance down to read the dial.

Turn your whole body rather than twisting the compass, and hold it
level so the needle swings freely (general practice, and the reason the
manual has you pull your elbows in).

### Following a bearing

To walk a bearing you already have:

1. **Set it.** On a baseplate compass, turn the housing so the bearing
   sits at the index line. On a lensatic compass, the manual's method
   is to turn the compass until the bearing is under the index line,
   then turn the bezel until its luminous line lies along the
   north-seeking arrow.
2. **Turn yourself, not the compass,** until the needle sits in the
   orienting arrow (or along the luminous line). You are now facing the
   bearing.
3. **Pick a steering mark.** The manual's way is to sight along the
   bearing to a landmark on that line, put the compass away, and walk to
   the landmark; then do it again. It warns against walking with the
   compass held open in front of you, because it will not stay level or
   steady enough to read. Its advice on choosing the landmark: something
   you will still recognise as you get close (colour, shade, size or
   shape); the most distant of several, so you check the compass less
   often; and the highest, because a low mark blends into the background
   as you approach. Steering marks "should never be determined from a
   map study": you pick them as you go.
4. **Count paces** as you walk, so you know how far along the line you
   are ([Estimating](/library#estimating)).

At night, colours vanish and shapes turn to silhouettes, so the manual
says night steering marks need even more distinctive shapes.

**Going round an obstacle.** The manual's method is to leave the line at
right angles, walk a counted distance, walk parallel to your line past
the obstacle, then come back the same distance at right angles. Its
example, while walking on 90: turn to 180 for 100 metres, back to 90 for
150 metres, then 360 for 100 metres, and carry on at 90. You are back on
your original line, and you add the 150 metres to your count.

**The way back** is the back bearing: add 180 if the bearing is under
180, subtract 180 if it is over. [Reading a Map](/library#reading-a-map) has
the manual's warning to check that sum twice.

### A compass you can make

The survival manual describes magnetising a sewing needle or a thin
steel object by stroking one end repeatedly with a magnet: "Always rub
in one direction only." Hung from a nonmetallic thread, or floated on a
small piece of wood in water, it lines up north and south.

The same manual also says you can magnetise the needle by stroking it
on a piece of silk or through your hair. Treat that as the manual's
claim, and expect it to fail often: rubbing on silk or hair builds up a
static charge, which is not the same thing as magnetism, and a needle
treated that way may not be magnetised at all (our note, from basic
physics).

Whichever way you made it, test it before you trust it (our method):

1. Let it settle, then nudge it round with a fingertip or a twig, and
   let it settle again. Do this several times. A magnetised needle comes
   back to the same line every time; one that stops wherever it was
   pushed is not magnetised.
2. Compare that line with a shadow-tip line (below). The two should
   cross at close to a right angle, because the shadow-tip line runs
   east-west. Where your declination is large, expect them to be out by
   that much.

It tells you the north-south line, not which end is north. Check that
with the sun or a shadow before you rely on it (our note).

## The sun

### What the sun really does

The survival manual puts the basics carefully: "The sun always rises in
the east and sets in the west, but not exactly due east or due west."
In a northern summer it rises well north of east, and in a northern
winter well south of it. At Silverdale's latitude the June sun comes up
about 37 degrees north of due east, according to the Naval Observatory
figures in [The Sky as an Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument). A
sunrise is a poor compass except near the equinoxes in March and
September.

The one moment the sun is exact is when it is highest. The manual says
that in the northern hemisphere "the sun will be due south when at its
highest point in the sky", and that in the southern hemisphere the
noonday sun marks due north. So at that moment a shadow points due
north in the northern hemisphere. (Inside the tropics the noon sun can
stand north or south of overhead depending on the season, so this holds
north of the Tropic of Cancer, at about 23.4 degrees north, and south of
the Tropic of Capricorn in the southern hemisphere: our note, from the
geometry the sky guide works through.)

That moment is rarely twelve o'clock on the clock. Time zones and
daylight saving shift it, sometimes by more than an hour; the sky guide
explains why. Watch for the shortest shadow, not for noon on your
watch.

The survival manual adds that in the northern hemisphere shadows move
clockwise through the day, and in the southern hemisphere
counterclockwise. That holds outside the tropics. The map reading manual
is more careful: "Depending on your location and the season, the shadow
may move either clockwise or counterclockwise". Inside the tropics,
where the sun can pass north of overhead, do not rely on the direction a
shadow turns (our reading of the two).

### The shadow-tip method: quick

The survival manual's version, which it calls simple and accurate:

1. Push a straight stick about 1 metre long into level ground clear of
   brush, where it casts a clear shadow. Mark the tip of the shadow with
   a stone or a twig.
2. Wait 10 to 15 minutes, until the tip has moved a few centimetres.
   Mark the new tip the same way.
3. Draw a straight line through the two marks. That is roughly an
   east-west line.
4. Stand with the first mark on your left and the second on your
   right: you are facing north.

The rule to remember, in the manual's words: "This first shadow mark is
always west". The shadow moves away from the sun, and the sun moves
west, so the shadow tip moves east (our explanation).

It is approximate. The map reading manual calls the line "approximate",
and the sky guide explains why it bends furthest from true near the
solstices and at high latitudes. The same manual says the method is not
intended for polar regions, which it defines as above 60 degrees of
latitude.

### The equal-shadow method: slow and accurate

The survival manual gives a second method that it calls more accurate:

1. Set up the stick in the morning and mark the tip of its shadow.
2. Tie a string to the base of the stick and draw a clean arc around
   the stick, through that mark.
3. Through the middle of the day the shadow shortens, then lengthens
   again. In the afternoon, mark the point where the shadow tip touches
   the arc again.
4. A line through the two marks is an accurate east-west line.

A line from the stick at right angles to it is your north-south line,
and the shortest shadow of the day falls along it (geometry). For a
result you can trust, the stick must be truly vertical: check it with a
plumb line, as [Square, Level and Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb)
shows, because a leaning stick leans the answer (general practice).

### The watch method

Both Army manuals describe it, and the map reading manual says where it
applies: "In the north temperate zone only", meaning north of the
tropics. There, hold an analogue watch flat and point the hour hand at
the sun. Halfway between the hour hand and 12 o'clock is south (between
the hour hand and 1 o'clock if the watch is on daylight saving time).
In the south temperate zone, south of the tropics, the method is
different: point 12 o'clock at the sun, and halfway between 12 and the
hour hand is north. If you are unsure which end of the line is which,
the manuals remind you the sun is in the east before noon and in the
west after. Inside the tropics the manual gives no watch method, so do
not use one there.

To point the hour hand at the sun without looking at it, hold a small
stick or a pencil upright on the rim of the watch, at the tip of the
hour hand, and turn the watch until the stick's shadow runs back along
the hand towards the centre (general practice). Where the stick goes
matters: a stick at the centre, with its shadow along the hand, leaves
the hand pointing away from the sun, and the answer comes out exactly
backwards (our note, from the geometry).

Know its limits. The survival manual says it is accurate only if you
are using true local time, and better the farther you are from the
equator. The map reading manual's warning about circling comes with a
fix: make a shadow clock, set your watch to it, and after an hour of
travel take another reading and reset the watch if necessary. In
practice, treat the watch method as "roughly south", and check it with
a shadow when you can (our reading of both).

## The stars and the moon

### Polaris

In the northern hemisphere the most reliable night direction is
Polaris, the North Star. The map reading manual says it is less than 1
degree off true north, because the Earth's axis points almost straight
at it.

To find it, the survival manual uses two constellations. In the Big
Dipper, draw a line from the bottom outer star of the bowl through the
top outer star (the two "pointer stars") and carry it on about five
times the distance between them: Polaris lies along that line. On the
other side of Polaris is Cassiopeia, five stars in a W on its side, and
Polaris is "straight out from Cassiopeia's center star". Use both:
the manual notes that Polaris, which sits in the handle of the Little
Dipper, can be confused with the Big Dipper, and says using the two
constellations together prevents it. The manual says these two are
always visible on a clear night; how true that is depends on your
latitude, and [The Sky as an Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument)
covers what to do when the Dipper is low and how to measure the error.

Once you have Polaris, look straight down from it to the horizon and
pick a landmark under it. That landmark is north, and you can walk to it
or use it again in daylight (general practice, and the method the sky
guide uses).

Two limits from the map reading manual: Polaris cannot be seen south of
the equator, and above 70 degrees of latitude it is too high in the sky
to be useful.

### The Southern Cross

South of the equator there is no bright pole star. The survival manual
uses the Southern Cross, four bright stars in a tilted cross: extend the
long axis of the cross about five times its length, and that point is
in the general direction of south. Look straight down from it to the
horizon and pick a landmark to steer by. If you are staying put, it
suggests driving stakes into the ground at night to fix the direction,
so you can use it again in daylight.

### Steering by a star

A star near the horizon makes a good steering mark, but stars move. The
map reading manual says a star near the north horizon serves for about
half an hour, and that when travelling south you should check your
bearing every 15 minutes. When a star climbs too high or sinks behind
the western horizon, change to another.

### The moon

The survival manual offers a rough rule: if the moon rises before the
sun has set, its lit side is the west; if it rises after midnight, its
lit side is the east. It calls this only "a rough east-west reference",
and that is how to use it.

## The land

Some natural signs help. Some are folklore. The survival manual is
plain about the most famous one: "The old saying about using moss on a
tree to indicate north is not accurate because moss grows completely
around some trees."

What it does offer:

- **Slopes.** In the northern hemisphere, north-facing slopes get less
  sun than south-facing ones, so they are cooler and damper. In summer
  the north-facing slopes keep patches of snow longest, and in winter
  the south-facing slopes lose their snow first.
- **Wind,** but only where there is a prevailing direction and you
  already know what it is.
- **Land you know.** The manual's real advice is to learn the terrain:
  prominent features and landmarks. A ridge you know runs north-south
  beats any sign in the bark. [Reading a Map](/library#reading-a-map) shows
  how to match the land to a map.

One sign from the weather service is a useful rough guide: to see a
rainbow, the National Weather Service says, "You need to be standing
with the sun to your back and the rain in front of you." So a rainbow
always lies opposite the sun: roughly west of you in the morning and
roughly east in the evening (our reasoning from it). Only roughly,
because the sun does not rise due east. On a June morning at Silverdale
it rises about 37 degrees north of east, so just after sunrise a
rainbow lies about 37 degrees south of west (arithmetic).

Any single natural sign can mislead. Use several, and check them
against the sun or a compass (general practice).

## At home: a north line you can keep

A permanent true north line is one of the most useful things to put on
a property, and it costs an afternoon.

**Worked example.** On a clear day, set a straight stake upright in
level ground in the yard, checked with a plumb line. Use the
equal-shadow method: mark the morning shadow tip, draw the arc with a
string tied to the stake, and mark where the afternoon shadow tip
touches the arc. Drive a peg at each mark. The line between the pegs
runs east-west. A line from the stake at right angles to it runs
north-south, and two more pegs along it make your north line.

Then:

1. Stand on the line, face north, and read your compass. It should read
   your declination away from zero: about 345 where the declination is
   15 degrees east (360 - 15, arithmetic). If it does, both the line
   and the compass are good. If it is far out, look for metal nearby,
   then suspect the compass (our method).
2. Use the line to site things. Which way a bed of crops runs, which way
   a window or a solar panel faces, and where winter shade will fall all
   depend on true north. [Why Seasons Happen](/library#why-seasons-happen),
   [Choosing Where to Build](/library#choosing-where-to-build) and [Your First
   Solar Power](/library#first-solar-power) take it from there.
3. Note it in your records with the date, and the declination you looked
   up, so you can check the compass against it in years to come
   ([Keeping Records](/library#keeping-records)).

## Worked example: a planned walk to a road

An illustration with made-up numbers, to show the steps in order.

You are at a trailhead. The map shows a north-south road about 2
kilometres east, with a farm gate on it that you want to reach.
Declination is 15 degrees east.

1. **Tell someone** the route and when you will be back.
2. **True bearing** from the map: due east, 90.
3. **Aim off on purpose.** The map reading manual's "deliberate offset"
   is about 10 degrees to one side, so that when you reach the road you
   know which way to turn. Aim 10 degrees north of the gate: true bearing
   80. At about 18 metres per degree per 1,000 metres, that puts you
   about 10 x 18 x 2 = 360 metres north of the gate, so you will turn
   right (south) at the road.
4. **Compass bearing:** 80 - 15 = 65. Set it.
5. **Walk by steering marks,** checking the compass at each one, and
   count paces. Two kilometres is a long way to walk without checking
   the ground against the map; note each stream or ridge you cross.
6. **If the ground stops matching the map,** stop and work out where you
   are before going on.

## Know where your own direction-finding stops

- **Someone is missing or hurt.** Call the emergency services. Do not
  wait to work it out with a compass (general practice).
- **Avalanche slopes, glaciers, open water and the air.** Navigation
  there needs training, local knowledge and the right charts and
  instruments that this guide does not give (general practice).
- **Land boundaries.** A compass line is not a property line. That is a
  question for the deed and a licensed land surveyor (general practice).
- **Strange compass readings that will not go away.** NCEI says local
  magnetic anomalies of more than 10 degrees, though rare, do exist, and
  that one area in Minnesota has 16 degrees east declination a few miles
  from anomalies of 12 degrees west. Where the compass and the sun keep
  disagreeing, trust the sun and ask people who know the area (general
  practice).

## How the game models it

The game gives you a night sky and a compass item, but no working
compass.

- **The letters at the top of the screen are not a compass.** The N, E,
  S and W strip turns with your view. It is drawn from the camera's own
  turning angle, with N at zero. On a planet's surface that zero is
  built from the game world's own vertical axis, and this guide has not
  checked it against the planet's true north. Nothing in the game points
  a needle along a magnetic field: a body's field strength is stored in
  the planet data, but no compass reads it, so there is no declination
  to practise.
- **The Compass item shows no bearing yet.** There is a Compass in the
  item list, described as a magnetic direction indicator, and a recipe
  that makes one at a workbench from an iron ingot and a glass pane
  ("Magnetize a needle in a glass housing"). Crafting one is the first
  step of the "Initial Survey" quest, but carrying one shows no
  bearing. Under the default settings, two more things apply: the game
  starts in the Dev play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), where
  materials are free, so the recipe needs neither ingredient; and while
  "Start every session
  from the default home" is on, which it is by default, only your
  character carries between launches, so a compass you made is gone the
  next time you start the game.
- **The Navigation skill exists, but nothing levels it yet.**
- **The night sky is real.** The stars come from a real star catalogue,
  so you can practise finding the Big Dipper, Cassiopeia and Polaris in
  the game, as [The Sky as an Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument)
  describes.

What the game does not teach, so you do not learn it from the game:
declination, the effect of metal on a compass, the shadow methods and
the watch method. Those need a real stick, a real watch and a real
compass.

## You own this when

- You never look at the sun to find it; you use shadows.
- You carry a compass you know how to use, you tell someone your route,
  and you know the Forest Service's "stop, stay calm, stay put."
- You can say what declination is, look up today's value for where you
  are, and convert a bearing between compass and map without getting the
  sign backwards.
- You check a compass before you trust it, and keep it away from metal.
- You can take a bearing on a landmark, follow a bearing by steering
  marks, go round an obstacle and keep your line, and work out the way
  back.
- You can find north by the shadow-tip method in a quarter of an hour,
  and lay out an accurate north line with the equal-shadow method.
- You can use the watch method and say why you would not trust it alone.
- You can find Polaris from the Big Dipper and from Cassiopeia, and you
  know what replaces it south of the equator.
- You know moss is not a compass, and which natural signs do help.
- You have a true north line marked at home, and your compass agrees
  with it.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- The worked bearing conversions, the 270 metre error for 15 degrees
  and the planned walk are arithmetic on made-up numbers, using the
  sources' rules.
- Setting the declination once on an adjustable compass, as a way to
  avoid arithmetic mistakes.
- Checking a compass against a sun-made north line, and what it should
  read there.
- Replacing a compass with a bubble or a sticky needle; magnets in
  phone cases, speakers, steel toecaps and car doors as things to keep
  away; the slow-approach test for metal.
- The steps for a baseplate compass (the principle is the Army
  manual's).
- That rubbing a needle on silk or hair builds static charge rather than
  magnetism, so that method often fails; the nudge test and the check
  against a shadow-tip line for an improvised needle; that the needle
  shows the line but not which end is north.
- Why the first shadow mark is west; that the shortest shadow falls on
  the north-south line; checking the stick with a plumb line; that the
  noon-sun rule, and the clockwise turn of shadows, hold outside the
  tropics.
- Putting the stick on the rim at the tip of the hour hand to aim the
  watch, and the backwards answer from a stick at the centre; treating
  the watch method as "roughly south".
- Picking a landmark under Polaris.
- That a rainbow lies opposite the sun, so roughly west in the morning
  and roughly east in the evening, reasoned from the weather service's
  rule, and the June morning example worked from the sunrise figure.
- Using several natural signs at once and checking them.
- The limits: emergency services for a missing person, training for
  avalanche, glacier, water and air navigation, a surveyor for
  boundaries, and trusting the sun over a compass that keeps
  disagreeing.
