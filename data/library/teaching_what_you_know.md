# Teaching What You Know

Every skill on a homestead lives somewhere. Some of it is written down,
but most of it lives in somebody's hands: how hard to swing the maul,
what a healthy hen sounds like, when the jam has set, which noise means
the pump is about to fail. A skill that lives in only one person's
hands is one accident, one illness or one move away from being lost.
Teaching is how it survives you.

Teaching is also a skill of its own, and not the same one as doing.
Someone who is very good at a job can teach it badly, because they no
longer notice the small things they do without thinking (the 1940
bulletin quoted below says much the same). The good news is that the
United States worked out a plain method for teaching practical work
when it needed millions of beginners to learn new jobs fast.

In August 1940, before the United States was at war, the National
Defense Advisory Commission set up the Training Within Industry
programme to help defence production plants train their supervisors,
and through them their workers; by 1944, with the country at war, the
programme said there were "over twenty million workers on important
jobs which are relatively new to them". Its method for teaching a job
was called Job Instruction, and the line its trainers stressed
throughout is the best summary of this guide: "If the worker hasn't
learned, the instructor hasn't taught". The other
sources are the Occupational Safety and Health Administration
(OSHA), which has rules and guidance on how workers are trained, the
US Department of Education's Institute of Education Sciences, which
published what research shows about making learning last, the US
Department of Labor on what jobs are too dangerous for children, and
the USDA Forest Service. All are United States government publications,
in the public domain. One more, the Agency for Healthcare Research and
Quality (AHRQ), is a federal agency whose toolkit is restated here in
our own words. Where something is general practice or our own
reasoning, the text says so.

The Humanity Accord's view of how people learn is in [Curriculum: How
Humans Learn](/library#curriculum); this guide is the practice.
[Organising Shared Work](/library#organising-shared-work) covers bringing a
group together, and [Keeping Records](/library#keeping-records) covers
writing things down so they last.

## First, teaching something that can hurt

Most homestead skills involve something sharp, heavy, hot, fast or
alive. When you teach one, you are responsible for a beginner who does
not yet know what can go wrong. The published rules for teaching
dangerous work agree on a few things.

### Practise where a mistake is cheap, under your eye

OSHA's rule for training forklift drivers (29 CFR 1910.178(l)) is one
of the clearest statements anywhere of how to let a beginner practise
on a machine that can kill. Trainees may operate the machine only
"Under the direct supervision of persons who have the knowledge,
training, and experience to train operators and evaluate their
competence" and only "Where such operation does not endanger the
trainee or other employees." The training must combine formal
instruction, practical training ("demonstrations performed by the
trainer and practical exercises performed by the trainee") and an
evaluation of how the person actually performs.

The household version (our reading): the learner's first tries happen
with you close enough to stop them with a word, but outside the tool's
reach on every side, and never riding on, or walking beside, a moving
machine; and in a place where a mistake hurts nothing: the axe on a
clear patch of ground with nobody near, the knife on a carrot, not on
a chicken.

Keeping out of reach is the Forest Service's rule for anyone chopping:
"Chop only when you are clear of other workers." For a tractor, the
federal operating instructions for farm tractors (29 CFR 1928,
Subpart C, Appendix A) include: "Do not permit others to ride.";
"Where possible, avoid operating the tractor near ditches, embankments,
and holes."; "Stay off slopes too steep for safe operation."; and
"Securely fasten your seat belt if the tractor has a ROPS" (a rollover
protective structure, the frame that protects the driver if the
tractor turns over). So a first tractor lesson is in an empty, flat
field, away from ditches and banks, at walking pace, with the learner
alone on the tractor and you standing clear of its path (the field,
the pace and where you stand are general practice).

### Step in before harm, not after

The Training Within Industry programme's 1940 bulletin on breaking in
a new worker says, of watching a beginner try: "If a mistake is being
made which can result in injury to the worker or damage to equipment,
you must interfere before harm is done." Agree before the lesson how
you will stop them (a word such as "stop", loud enough to hear over
the work, so you never have to reach in towards a moving tool) and
that they will stop at once when you do (general practice).

The same bulletin is equally firm about the opposite mistake: "If you
take over the job too soon or too often, the new man is bound to
become discouraged, and the time it will take him to learn will be
increased." Minor mistakes, it says, can be allowed, provided you catch
them and explain afterwards how to avoid them. The skill is telling the
two kinds of mistake apart, and that comes from knowing the job well
yourself.

### Retrain when something changes

OSHA's forklift rule lists when a driver must be trained again: after
being seen operating unsafely, after an accident or a near-miss, after
an evaluation shows unsafe operation, when assigned a different type of
truck, and when a condition in the workplace changes in a way that
could affect safe operation. That list works for any dangerous skill
(our reading): if the learner has a near-miss, switches from your old
chainsaw to a new one, or goes from splitting on flat ground to a
slope, teach that part again.

### Gear first, and the teacher wears it too

If the job needs eye protection, gloves, boots or hearing protection,
the learner wears them from the first try, and so do you, because
beginners copy what they see more than what they hear (general
practice). The Forest Service's guide to hand tools names hard hats,
gloves and safety glasses among the protective devices for tool work.

### Children

Children learn real skills young on a homestead, and they should. Some
jobs, though, are dangerous enough that federal law bars employers from
hiring children under 16 to do them. The list (29 CFR 570.71), which
the Department of Labor declares "particularly hazardous for the
employment of children below the age of 16", includes:

- operating a tractor over 20 PTO horsepower, or hitching or unhitching
  implements to one, and riding on a tractor as a passenger or helper;
- operating or helping to operate a power-driven circular saw, band saw
  or chain saw, a power post-hole digger, a forklift, earthmoving
  equipment, and a long list of harvesting and feed machinery;
- working in a yard, pen or stall with a breeding bull, boar or stud
  horse, a sow with suckling pigs, or a cow with a newborn calf;
- felling, bucking, skidding, loading or unloading timber with a butt
  diameter of more than 6 inches;
- working from a ladder or scaffold more than 20 feet up;
- driving a bus, truck or car carrying passengers;
- working inside a manure pit, certain silos or an oxygen-deficient
  storage;
- handling the most toxic farm chemicals (those whose label carries the
  word "poison" with the skull and crossbones, or "warning"), explosives
  or anhydrous ammonia.

The rule does not apply to a child working for their parent on a farm
the parent owns or operates (29 CFR 570.70(b)), and the Department of
Labor's fact sheet notes exemptions for some 14 and 15 year olds in
vocational agriculture programmes or with 4-H or vocational
agriculture training certificates. It also says that where state law is stricter, the
stricter standard must be observed. The law is about employment, but
the list is a careful federal judgement of which farm jobs are too
dangerous for children, and it is a sensible guide for what not to
hand a child on your own land either (our reading).

### Teach only what you can do safely yourself

You cannot teach a key point you do not know. If you learned a skill by
getting away with bad habits, get taught properly before you pass it
on (general practice). Some skills need a proper course: the Forest
Service says to "Be sure you are properly trained and certified before
cutting either standing or fallen trees", and first aid, driving and
hunting have their own courses and, in many places, legal requirements
(general practice). Point the learner to those, and teach around them.

## Get ready before you teach

The Training Within Industry method starts before the learner arrives.
Its 1944 programme lists four things to get ready:

- **A timetable:** how much skill you expect the learner to have, and
  by what date.
- **A breakdown of the job:** list the important steps, and pick out
  the key points.
- **Everything ready:** the right equipment, materials and supplies.
- **The workplace arranged** just as the learner will be expected to
  keep it.

### Key points

The 1940 bulletin explains what a key point is: "there are one or two
key points in every operation which, if properly covered, automatically
care for the others." Key points, it says, are "knacks, special motions,
particular timing, or particular ideas which once mastered let the
worker do the job quickly, easily, and safely." And the warning that
makes them hard to teach: "You have these key points in your head, and
do your work without thinking about them." When you stress the key
points, it adds, include safe practices.

So before you teach, do the job once slowly, on purpose, and write down
each step and what you are actually doing at the moments that matter.
A breakdown sheet with two columns, the steps on the left and the key
points on the right, is the Training Within Industry form; a third
column for why each key point matters makes it a teaching document that
outlives you (our addition).

## The four steps

The 1944 programme taught the method as four steps. The wording below
is restated from its booklet, with two of its lines kept exactly.

1. **Prepare the learner.** Put them at ease. Say what the job is and
   find out what they already know about it. Get them interested in
   learning it. Put them in the correct position.
2. **Present the operation.** Tell, show and illustrate one important
   step at a time. Stress each key point. "Instruct clearly,
   completely, and patiently, but no more than he can master."
3. **Try out performance.** Have them do the job, and correct errors.
   Have them explain each key point to you as they do the job again.
   Make sure they understand. "Continue until YOU know HE knows."
4. **Follow up.** Put them on their own. Tell them who to go to for
   help. Check often. Encourage questions. Taper off the extra coaching
   and close follow-up.

The 1940 bulletin, written for an experienced worker breaking in a new
one, adds detail worth copying (restated):

- Let the learner first watch you do the whole job at normal speed, so
  they see what the job calls for; then slow down and explain the key
  points; then show the whole job again at normal speed.
- Let them start with the simple parts, the ones they are sure to get
  right, while you do the difficult ones.
- Then have them do the whole job slowly, telling you each next step
  and each key point as they come to it.
- Keep them aimed at a high standard; the bulletin says "Pretty good"
  is not going to be good enough.
- When they are on their own, keep an eye on the work for several
  days, enough to know they are not unlearning what you taught.

Notice what the method does not do. It does not hand the learner a
manual and walk away, and it does not let them watch you for a year.
The Forest Service's trail crew guide makes the same point about tools:
"Such knowledge cannot come from a manual." Writing it down matters,
and this guide comes back to it, but a practical skill passes from hands
to hands.

## Check understanding, not agreement

Asking "Do you understand?" tells you very little. Health care has the
same problem with patients' instructions, and AHRQ's toolkit for clear
health communication points out that people tend to answer yes whether
they understood or not. It recommends what it calls the teach-back
method, restated here: ask the person to explain, in their own words,
what they need to know or do. Questions that can be answered yes or no
do not count. Frame it as a check on how well you explained, not as a
test of them. If the explanation comes back wrong, explain it a
different way and ask again, until they can say it correctly in their
own words; a person who repeats your exact words may not have
understood. Do it in small pieces as you go, not all at the end. For a
hands-on task, the related show-me method has the person demonstrate
it.

The Training Within Industry method had already built this in: step 3
has the learner explain each key point while doing the job again. For a
practical skill, the best teach-back is the job itself, done while
talking (our reading of both).

## Make it last: practice spread over time

A lesson that went perfectly can still be forgotten by next season.
The Institute of Education Sciences' practice guide *Organizing
Instruction and Study to Improve Student Learning* (2007) reviewed the
research and graded each of its recommendations by the strength of the
evidence behind it:

| Recommendation | Evidence |
| --- | --- |
| Space learning over time: review key material after a delay of several weeks to several months | Moderate |
| Alternate worked examples with problems the learner solves | Moderate |
| Combine pictures with words | Moderate |
| Connect abstract ideas with concrete examples | Moderate |
| Use quizzes to re-expose learners to key content | Strong |
| Use questions before a new topic | Low |
| Teach learners to judge what they know, and use quizzes to find gaps | Low |
| Ask deep explanatory questions (why, how, what if) | Strong |

The guide was written for teachers of school students from about third
to twelfth grade, mostly for classroom subjects, so using it for adults
learning a practical skill is our extension. Translated to a homestead
(our application):

- **Come back to it.** Teach pruning in winter, then ask the learner to
  show you again a month later, and again next winter. Seasonal skills
  are the ones most easily lost, because a year passes between uses.
- **Show, then let them try, then show again.** A worked example (you
  doing it) followed by their attempt, alternating.
- **Draw it as you explain it.** A sketch of where the cut goes, beside
  the words.
- **Ask why.** "Why do we cut above the bud?" builds understanding that
  holds up when something unusual happens; "where do we cut?" only
  builds a habit.
- **Quiz, kindly.** "What would you check first if the pump stopped?"
  over dinner is a quiz, and the research says quizzes help people
  remember.

## Teaching adults

Adults are not large children, and OSHA's guidance on training workers
(*Resource for Development and Delivery of Training to Workers*, 2021)
summarises how they learn. Among its principles: adults learn best when
they have decided they need to, and need to see why the subject matters
to them; they bring life experience that should be acknowledged, and
they "resent an instructor who talks down to them or ignores their
ideas and concerns"; and "Adults learn best by doing". It recommends
that activity-based learning fill "at least two-thirds of training
hours (no more than one-third is lecture)", and that training be given
"in a language and vocabulary that the workers can understand". It also
says learners need clear objectives, with each new piece building on
the last, and need to hear things more than once.

In practice (our application): say why the skill matters before how to
do it; ask what they already know and use it; keep the talking short
and the doing long; and never make an adult feel stupid for not knowing
something you learned at twelve.

## Write it down so it outlives you

Hands-on teaching passes a skill to one person. Writing it down passes
it to people you will never meet. Do both (general practice).

- **Keep the breakdown sheets.** The steps, key points and reasons you
  wrote to teach a job are a procedure. File them with your records
  ([Keeping Records](/library#keeping-records)).
- **Take pictures or video.** OSHA's job hazard analysis guide suggests
  photographing or videotaping someone doing a job, as a reference for
  later.
- **Teach more than one person,** so no skill on the homestead lives in
  only one pair of hands (general practice).
- **Teach your learner to teach.** When a learner is ready to pass the
  skill on, teach them how to teach it first. The Training Within
  Industry programme's method for putting a training plan into action
  included "Train those who do the training." So before your learner
  teaches the next one, teach them the four steps, and watch their
  first lesson (our application). A skill that has been taught twice
  is much harder to lose (our reasoning).
- **Have someone check what you wrote.** A procedure written by the
  only person who knows the job will have gaps that person cannot see
  (our reasoning). The next section shows HumanityOS's own Library
  working this way.

## Worked examples

The details of these lessons are ours, for illustration, except where
a source is named.

### Teaching a teenager to split firewood

Check first: a maul or splitting axe is a hand tool, not on the federal
list above, but a power splitter is machinery you may want to keep for
later (our reading). Then get ready:

- **Timetable:** splitting rounds of easy, straight-grained wood on her
  own by the end of the month.
- **Breakdown sheet,** with key points drawn from the Forest Service's
  axe advice and from [Firewood](/library#firewood):
  1. Gear on: safety glasses, gloves, boots (the Forest Service names
     safety glasses and gloves for tool work).
  2. Check the area: the Forest Service says to check for adequate
     swing clearance, remove brush and overhanging branches, make sure
     your footing is stable, and chop only when you are clear of other
     workers. Key point: nobody within reach of the swing, on any side.
  3. Set the round on a chopping block, so a miss goes into the block or
     the ground, not into your shin ([Firewood](/library#firewood)).
  4. Stance and distance: the Forest Service's method for chopping is
     to stand with feet shoulder-width apart and measure the distance by
     holding the handle near its end and stretching your arms out to the
     wood; you should be able to touch the blade to it. Key point:
     "Stand so a glancing blow won't strike you."
  5. Read the round: wood splits along the grain and refuses at knots
     ([Firewood](/library#firewood)). Key point: aim along a crack already in
     the end of the round, away from knots (general practice).
  6. Keep the tool sharp: a dull axe is the dangerous axe
     ([Sharpening](/library#sharpening)).
- **The lesson:** show her the whole job at normal speed, twice. Then
  slowly, one step at a time, stressing each key point. Then she sets up
  the area and places each round, then steps back out of reach before
  you lift the maul, and nobody reaches toward the block while the maul
  is raised (general practice). Then she swings on easy rounds, telling
  you each key point as she goes, while you stand outside her reach; you
  stop her at once, with the word you agreed, if anyone comes within
  reach or her footing slips.
- **Follow-up:** she splits on her own with you nearby for a week, then
  on her own, with a check every few days. In a month, teach her the
  four steps, then have her teach the next learner, with you watching
  both.

### Teaching a neighbour to test a GFCI outlet

Low risk, quick, and a good first lesson in teach-back. Show the test
from [Working Out Why Something
Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke), then ask: "When you're
home tonight, what will you press, and what should happen?" If she
says it back correctly in her own words, and then does it on your
outlet, she has it.

### A seed saving afternoon for ten people

A group lesson is a work day with learning as the goal, so the
organisation in [Organising Shared Work](/library#organising-shared-work)
applies. Keep the talk to a third of the time or less and the doing to
two-thirds or more (OSHA's proportion). Pair beginners with people who
have done it before. Show a worked example at each table, have each
person do their own, and finish by asking everyone why seed should be
dried before it is stored (see [Saving Your Own
Seeds](/library#saving-your-own-seeds) for the reasons). Then put a date in
the calendar to check the stored seed together in a month.

## How HumanityOS and the game model it

**In the game, skills rise only by doing.** Crafting a recipe that
needs a skill gives experience in that skill, 10 points plus 5 for each
level of the recipe (15 for a level-1 recipe, 35 for a level-5 one);
recipes that need no skill train nothing. Finishing a structure from a
blueprint trains Shelter Building, harvesting trains Farming, mining
trains Mining, and quests can award experience too. Combat and
abilities train their skills as well: your killing blow trains Melee
Combat or Ranged Combat (10 points), and using an ability that needs a
skill gives that skill 5 points. Nothing in the game
lets one player teach another, or be taught by a character, and the
Leadership skill, which this topic belongs to, appears in the skill
list but nothing levels it yet.

**One setting matters here.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start every
session from the default home" is on, which is the default during
development, only your character's name, look and outfit carry between
launches. Your skill levels start over each time you launch. Turn the
setting off to keep them.

**HumanityOS's own Library is built the way this guide recommends.**
Its Curriculum view lists everything the Library intends to teach,
subject by subject, and marks each topic honestly: not yet written,
written, sourced, or checked by a second, independent pass. It says at
the top how many topics have been checked by a second pass, and it names
the topics with nothing written yet "so the gap is countable rather than
invisible". That is the advice of the last section applied to a whole
library: write it down, say where it came from, and have someone else
check it. This guide's own topic, Teaching what you know, is listed
there.

## You own this when

- Beginners practise dangerous work only under your eye, with you
  close enough to stop them with a word but outside the tool's reach,
  never riding on or walking beside a moving machine, in a place where
  a mistake hurts nothing, wearing the gear you wear too.
- You step in before a mistake that could injure someone, and let
  small mistakes happen and explain them afterwards.
- You teach again after a near-miss, a new tool or a new place.
- You know the federal list of farm jobs too dangerous for hired
  children, and use it as a guide for your own.
- You teach only what you can do safely yourself, and send learners to
  a proper course where one is needed.
- Before teaching, you set a timetable, write a breakdown of steps and
  key points, and get everything ready.
- You teach in four steps: prepare, present one step at a time, have
  them try it while explaining the key points, and follow up.
- You check understanding by having them explain or show it, not by
  asking whether they understand.
- You come back to a skill weeks and months later, ask why questions,
  and quiz kindly.
- You keep the talking short and the doing long, and never talk down to
  an adult learner.
- You write down what you teach, teach more than one person, and teach
  your learners how to teach before they teach the next one.
