# Where Water Comes From

You are thirsty and there is water nearby. A tap. A creek at the bottom
of the property. A spring somebody told you about. A barrel under the
downspout. The question you actually have is not how the water cycle
works. It is: can I drink this, and if not, what will it do to me?

This guide answers the first half of that. It walks through the sources
a person really has, says plainly what each one is good for and what is
genuinely wrong with it, and ranks them by risk so you can pick the best
water available before you spend any effort treating it. Choosing well is
the cheapest safety you will ever get, and it is the step most people
skip.

The other three water guides pick up where this one stops.
[Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) is what to do
about the water once you have chosen it, including which treatment fixes
which problem. [Collecting Rainwater](/library#collecting-rainwater) is the
build guide for catching your own. [Storing Water Safely](/library#storing-water-safely)
covers keeping a supply that is still good months from now. This guide
deliberately does not repeat any of them.

## How much you actually need

There are two numbers here, and confusing them is why people either panic
or badly under-prepare.

**To stay alive, it is small.** Ready.gov, the federal preparedness site,
says that "a normally active person needs about three quarters of a gallon
of fluid daily, from water and other beverages." That is roughly three
liters, and it includes what you get from coffee, soup and the rest.

**To live normally, it is enormous.** The USGS puts ordinary indoor
household use at "about 80-100 gallons of water per day" per person, with
toilet flushing the single largest share. That is more than a hundred
times your drinking need, and it is the number that explains why a broken
tap feels like a catastrophe when your actual thirst could be answered by
one large bottle.

The preparedness figure sits between the two. Both CDC and Ready.gov say
to store "at least 1 gallon of water per person, per day for 3 days," and
CDC adds "try to store a 2-week supply if possible." That gallon is not
just drinking. CDC describes it as covering "drinking, cooking, brushing
teeth, and other uses." Ready.gov calls it "drinking and sanitation."

Push the number up when the people or the conditions demand it. Ready.gov
names "children, nursing mothers and sick people" as needing more, notes
that "a medical emergency might require additional water," and warns that
"in very hot temperatures, water needs can double."

The practical consequence for source-hunting: your drinking water and your
washing water do not have to come from the same place. A poor source you
would never drink is perfectly good for flushing a toilet, washing a floor,
or watering a tree. Sorting your uses by how clean they need to be is a
real skill, and it takes pressure off the one source that has to be clean.

## The three things that can be wrong with water

The companion purification guide sorts problems by what fixes them. This
guide sorts them by where they came from, because that is what tells you
whether to trust a source in the first place.

**1. Things that are alive.** Bacteria, viruses and parasites. EPA lists
"Microorganisms" first among its categories of regulated drinking water
contaminants, and for private wells it is specific about the origin: these
are "found in human sewage and animal waste." They make people acutely
ill, usually within days, and they are the most common reason wild water
hurts somebody.

**2. Things that are dissolved in it.** EPA's remaining contaminant
categories are disinfection byproducts, disinfectants, inorganic chemicals,
organic chemicals and radionuclides. In plain terms: metals, nitrate,
solvents, fuel, pesticides. Some arrive from human activity; others were
always in the rock. EPA says flatly of wells that they "can be contaminated
by both naturally occurring sources and by human activities." Arsenic is
the classic example of the second kind, and EPA gives its origin as
"erosion of natural deposits" alongside orchard runoff.

**3. What the land above handed it.** Sediment, leaf litter, road grit,
manure, fertilizer, whatever the last storm rinsed off a parking lot. EPA
calls this nonpoint source pollution and describes the mechanism exactly:
"NPS pollution is caused by rainfall or snowmelt moving over and through
the ground. As the runoff moves, it picks up and carries away natural and
human-made pollutants, finally depositing them into lakes, rivers,
wetlands, coastal waters and ground waters." EPA also notes that "states
report that nonpoint source pollution is the leading remaining cause of
water quality problems."

That third category is not really a separate kind of poison. It is the
delivery system for the first two, and it is the one you can actually
investigate before you drink, by looking at a map instead of at the water.

## Clear water tells you almost nothing

This is the single most expensive wrong belief about water, and it is worth
putting on its own line.

The USGS defines turbidity as "the measure of relative clarity of a liquid,"
and then says the thing people need to hear: "although turbidity is not a
direct indicator of health risk, numerous studies show a strong relationship
between removal of turbidity and removal of protozoa." Clarity is a weak
correlate, not a test. And cloudiness is worse than it looks, because
"turbidity can provide food and shelter for pathogens."

The reverse error is just as common. USGS says of spring water, which is
usually gin-clear, that "microbes, viruses, and bacteria do not die just
from being underground, nor are any agricultural or industrial pollutants
removed." Clear, cold, sweet-tasting spring water can carry every problem
on the list.

Nothing you can see, smell or taste distinguishes water carrying nitrate
from water that is not. Nothing distinguishes arsenic. Nothing distinguishes
a virus. The only thing clarity reliably predicts is how well treatment
will work on it, which matters, but it is a different question from safety.

## The sources, one at a time

### Municipal supply

The tap in a town or city, and by a wide margin the best water most people
will ever have access to. CDC: "In the United States, 9 out of 10 people
get their tap water from a public water system." EPA sets the standards
those systems must meet, and the systems are monitored continuously against
them.

**What is good about it:** somebody whose job it is tests it constantly,
against a legal standard, and has to tell you when they fail.

**What is actually wrong with it:** three things. Treatment is only as good
as the day it was running, so main breaks, pressure loss and treatment
failures produce boil-water advisories. The standards cover regulated
contaminants and not every substance that exists. And the last stretch of
pipe is yours: lead reaches drinking water from household plumbing and
service lines, which is a problem your utility's test at the plant does not
see.

**The thing to actually do:** read your Consumer Confidence Report. EPA
requires community water systems to deliver one annually, and says "reports
must be sent by your water supplier each year by July 1." It has to tell you
"the lake, river, aquifer, or other source of the drinking water," which
contaminants were detected, and the health effects of anything found in
violation of a standard. Ten minutes with that document tells you more about
your water than any amount of looking at it.

### Private well

Water pumped from an aquifer on your own property. CDC's line is the whole
story: "if you get your water from a private well, you are responsible for
making sure it is safe to drink." Private wells are "not regulated or
monitored by officials."

**What is good about it:** the ground is a real filter for the things you
can see. USGS notes that "the ground is an excellent mechanism for filtering
out particulate matter, such as leaves, soil, and bugs." CDC agrees that
ground water "is naturally filtered, which might remove some germs and
chemicals," while adding the qualifier that matters: it "depends on how far
the water is below ground and the area's local geology."

**What is actually wrong with it:** depth and construction decide almost
everything. EPA distinguishes three kinds. Dug or bored wells are large
diameter and shallow, "approximately 10 to 30 feet deep," and lack
continuous casing. Driven wells are cased but also shallow, "approximately
30 to 50 feet deep," and EPA says "though driven wells are cased, they can
be contaminated easily because they draw water from aquifers near the
surface." Drilled wells "can be thousands of feet deep" and "have a lower
risk of contamination due to their depth and use of continuous casing."
USGS puts the same point in aquifer terms: "the risk of contamination is
greater for unconfined (water-table) aquifers than for confined aquifers
because they usually are nearer to land surface and lack an overlying
confining layer."

The other thing wrong with wells is what the rock donates. USGS: "as
groundwater flows through sediments, metals such as iron and manganese are
dissolved and may later be found in high concentrations in the water."
Arsenic, fluoride and radionuclides can be natural too. None of them smell
of anything.

**The thing to actually do:** test it. CDC and EPA give the same baseline:
test at least once a year for total coliform bacteria, nitrates, total
dissolved solids and pH. CDC also lists the events that should trigger an
extra test, and the list is worth memorizing because every item on it is a
day when people do not think about their water: problems reported with well
water in your area, "problems near your well, such as flooding, land
disturbances, or nearby waste disposal sites," replacing or repairing any
part of the well system, a change in the water's taste, colour or smell,
somebody in the household becoming pregnant, and a child moving in.

### Spring

Groundwater that has found its own way out. USGS: "a spring is a place where
water moving underground finds an opening to the land surface and emerges,
sometimes as just a trickle, maybe only after a rain, and sometimes in a
continuous flow."

**What is good about it:** it is groundwater, so it arrives pre-filtered of
particles, it flows without a pump, and it is usually cold and steady.

**What is actually wrong with it:** the water has already left the ground,
so it is surface water from the instant it emerges, and it is unprotected
in a way a cased well is not. Livestock drink at springs. Animals die near
them. USGS is blunt that being underground does nothing to the biology or
the chemistry: "microbes, viruses, and bacteria do not die just from being
underground, nor are any agricultural or industrial pollutants removed."
How mineral-heavy a spring is depends, as USGS explains, on "the rate of
flow and the length of the flowpath through the aquifer," which sets "the
amount of time the water is in contact with the rock."

A spring that runs hard and cold all summer is drawing from deep and slow.
A spring that only runs after rain is barely underground at all, and should
be treated as a stream.

### Stream and river

Moving surface water. The most available source in a wet region and the one
with the widest spread between best case and worst case.

**What is good about it:** volume, and movement. Flow means it is being
continually replaced rather than sitting and growing things, and between
storms much of that flow is groundwater. USGS: "base flow is the sustained
flow of a stream in the absence of direct runoff," and "natural base flow is
sustained largely by groundwater discharges." A clear stream in a dry spell
is largely spring water.

**What is actually wrong with it:** it has touched every acre upstream of
you. USGS notes that "surface runoff, especially when it runs across
surfaces like roads, can pick up and then deposit particulate matter and
sediment into the river." Everything in the nonpoint source list arrives
here. A stream is not one risk; it is the sum of its watershed, which is
why the upstream section below matters more than anything you can observe at
the bank.

### Lake and pond

Standing surface water.

**What is good about it:** it is easy to reach, it is there in quantity, and
deep cold lakes used as municipal reservoirs are genuinely decent raw water.

**What is actually wrong with it:** nothing leaves. A lake accumulates what
its whole watershed sends it, and in warm weather it grows its own hazard.
CDC describes harmful algal blooms as "the rapid growth of algae or
cyanobacteria in water that can harm people, animals, or the environment,"
favoured by water that is warm, slow-moving and "full of nutrients, such as
nitrogen or phosphorous" from fertilizer, sewage and runoff. Blooms "can
look like foam, scum, mats, or paint on the surface of the water" and can
"change the color of the water."

Here is the part that makes blooms different from everything else in this
guide, in CDC's own words: "boiling water containing toxins will not make it
safe to drink. Boiling does not remove toxins and can concentrate the
toxin." A bloom is not a treatment problem. It is a find-another-source
problem, exactly like a fuel spill. CDC's visible warning signs for a water
body are that it "smells bad," "looks discolored," "has foam, scum, mats of
algae, or paint-like streaks on the surface," or "has dead fish or other
animals washed up on its shore."

A small, warm, still farm pond with cattle access is close to the worst
drinking source in this guide that is not actually salty.

### Rainwater

Water that has not touched the ground yet.

**What is good about it:** at the moment it leaves the cloud it is about as
biologically clean as free water gets, and it is very low in dissolved
minerals, which is why it is prized for washing and for plants.

**What is actually wrong with it:** it stops being clean the instant it
lands on something. CDC lists rainwater systems among the private supplies
that are "not regulated or monitored by officials." Roof runoff picks up
bird droppings, dust and traces of the roof itself, which is why
[Collecting Rainwater](/library#collecting-rainwater) treats barrel water as
non-potable throughout and tells you to keep it off anything you will eat.
Read that guide before you build anything; it also covers the weight,
drowning and mosquito hazards, which are the ones that actually injure
people.

Rain caught directly in a clean, covered container, never touching a roof,
is a different and much better proposition than roof runoff. It is also
slow, which is the honest reason almost nobody does it for drinking water.

### Snow and ice

Precipitation you have to carry and melt.

**What is good about it:** chemically it is rain. Snow that fell on
undisturbed ground, taken from below the surface layer and away from animal
traffic and roads, is low-risk raw water by the same logic as rain.

**What is actually wrong with it:** three things. The surface of a snowpack
collects everything that has settled out of the air and everything that has
walked across it since it fell. Old snow near a road has road salt and grit
in it, which is one of the runoff pollutants EPA names. And it costs fuel:
snow must be melted before it is water, and it is mostly air.

Two practical points that are rules of thumb rather than measured values,
so treat them as orientation and not as numbers: a pot packed with loose
snow yields a surprisingly small amount of water, on the order of one part
water to ten parts snow by volume, so plan on melting far more than looks
necessary; and eating snow directly costs you body heat at exactly the time
you can least afford to lose it, so melt it first when cold is a factor.
Melted snow should be treated like any other surface water.

Snowpack is also a seasonal reservoir, not just a source in hand. USGS
describes "mountain snow fields, and glaciers, to a lesser extent" as acting
"as natural reservoirs for water in some areas, such as the western United
States and Canada," and says "as much as 75 percent of water supplies in
some western states are derived from snowmelt." If you live downstream of
snow, a thin snow year is a water-supply event several months before it is a
visible one.

### Groundwater seep

The place where a hillside is wet, a small flow emerges from a bank, or
water gathers in a low spot that never fully dries. A spring without the
spring.

**What is good about it:** very little, honestly, other than that it exists
where nothing else does. In arid country a seep can be the only water for
miles, and in that situation it is worth knowing how to find and treat one.

**What is actually wrong with it:** a seep is shallow groundwater that has
barely been underground, emerging slowly, usually pooling, usually within
reach of every animal in the area. It has the vulnerability of a
water-table aquifer that USGS describes, plus the standing-water problems of
a pond, plus a flow rate too low to flush itself. Septic effluent, which
USGS notes can "percolate (seep) down to the water table," moves through
exactly this kind of shallow ground.

Treat a seep as a last-resort source, and dig a small collection hole
uphill of any pooled water rather than scooping the pool.

### Salt water

The ocean, a tidal river, or any brackish water near a coast.

USGS gives the boundaries in parts per million of dissolved salt: fresh
water is "less than 1,000 ppm," slightly saline is "from 1,000 ppm to 3,000
ppm," moderately saline is "from 3,000 ppm to 10,000 ppm," highly saline is
"from 10,000 ppm to 35,000 ppm," and ocean water "contains about 35,000 ppm
of salt." Oceans make up about 97 percent of all the water on Earth, which
is the whole irony of being thirsty on a coast.

**What is wrong with it** is not a contaminant, it is chemistry, and it
cannot be filtered, boiled or disinfected away. NOAA explains the mechanism
precisely: "human kidneys can only make urine that is less salty than salt
water. Therefore, to get rid of all the excess salt taken in by drinking
seawater, you have to urinate more water than you drank." The conclusion is
equally plain: "eventually, you die of dehydration even as you become
thirstier." Drinking seawater is worse than drinking nothing.

Every treatment method in the companion guide moves contaminants out of
water. Salt is dissolved, so none of them touch it, and boiling is actively
counterproductive because the water leaves as steam and the salt stays
behind, concentrating what is left. The only route from salt water to
drinking water is to move the water instead of the salt: collect the steam
and leave the salt in the pot. That is distillation, and it is the reason
[Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) names it as the
one home method that separates water from dissolved chemicals and salts,
while declining to teach it in a paragraph. It is slow and it is very
fuel-hungry. Plan around it rather than on it.

Brackish water deserves its own warning. A tidal river tastes fresh well
upstream of where it is fresh, and the salt wedge moves with the tide and
the season. Coastal wells can also draw salt in when they are pumped hard or
when the water table drops. If the water near a coast tastes even faintly
salty, it is not a source.

## Ranking them by risk

The ordering below is a rule of thumb assembled from the sourced facts
above, not a published ranking from any agency. Use it to choose between
two options in front of you, not as a licence to skip treatment. Every
entry below the first two needs the treatment guide before anybody drinks
it.

| Source | Usual risk | The single fact that decides it |
|---|---|---|
| Municipal tap, in compliance | Lowest | Whether an advisory is in effect, and what your own plumbing is made of |
| Deep drilled well, cased, recently tested | Low | Depth and continuous casing, then the test result |
| Rain caught in a clean covered container | Low | That it never touched a roof or the ground |
| Snow from clean undisturbed pack | Low | Distance from roads, animals and the pack surface |
| Roof-collected rainwater | Moderate | The roof material, and whether the first flush was diverted |
| Spring at the point it emerges | Moderate | Whether it flows all year, and what grazes above it |
| Shallow dug or driven well | Moderate to high | It draws from near-surface water, so what is on the surface reaches it |
| Stream upstream of all people | Moderate | Everything in the watershed above you, including wildlife |
| Stream downstream of people | High | Human sewage means viruses, which most camping filters do not remove |
| Lake or pond | High | Nothing leaves a lake, and warm still water grows its own toxins |
| Groundwater seep | High | Shallow, slow, pooled and open to every animal nearby |
| Salt water | Unusable as-is | Salt is dissolved, so only distillation separates it |

Two sources are missing from this table on purpose. Floodwater is not
ranked because it is a mixture of sewage, fuel and whatever was in every
building it passed through, and it belongs in the same category as a
chemical spill. And CDC's standing rule covers the whole bottom of the
list: "you cannot make water containing fuel, toxic chemicals, or
radioactive materials safe by boiling or disinfecting it."

## The most useful thing to know about a surface source is upstream of it

If you learn one investigative habit from this guide, make it this one. For
any stream, river, lake or spring, the question that predicts risk better
than anything you can observe at the water's edge is: what is the land
above it doing?

EPA's definition of source water covers exactly this ground, calling it the
"rivers, streams, lakes, reservoirs, springs, and groundwater that provide
water to public drinking water supplies and private wells," and notes that
"protecting source water from contamination helps reduce treatment costs and
may avoid or defer the need for complex treatment." The same logic scales
down to one household. A better watershed is cheaper than better treatment.

What to look for, in rough order of how much it should worry you:

- **Human sewage.** Housing on septic, a campground, a sewage outfall, an
  RV dump. Human waste is what puts human viruses in water, and viruses are
  the class that ordinary camping microfilters do not remove. This single
  item is why the stream above a town and the stream below it are different
  sources.
- **Livestock.** Pasture, feedlots, animals with direct access to the
  water. EPA names "animal waste" alongside human sewage as the origin of
  waterborne microorganisms, and lists manure among the causes of nonpoint
  source pollution.
- **Row crops and fertilizer.** EPA gives nitrate's sources as "runoff from
  fertilizer use; leaking from septic tanks, sewage; erosion of natural
  deposits," with a drinking water limit of 10 mg/L. Nitrate is the quiet
  one: no taste, no smell, and EPA's stated health effect is that "infants
  below the age of six months who drink water containing nitrate in excess
  of the MCL could become seriously ill and, if untreated, may die."
  Fertilizer runoff is also what feeds the algal blooms above.
- **Roads and parking lots.** Surface runoff off pavement, in USGS's words,
  picks up and deposits "particulate matter and sediment into the river,"
  along with oil, grease and road salt.
- **Mines, mills, orchards and industry.** These put the dissolved
  contaminants in. EPA's list of heavy metal sources includes "mining
  operations, petroleum refineries, electronics manufacturers, municipal
  waste disposal, cement plants, and natural mineral deposits," and it
  attributes arsenic partly to "runoff from orchards."
- **Anything that was there before.** USGS raises the case of a well placed
  "in land that was once used for something like a garbage or chemical dump
  site." Old land use does not show on a current map, and neighbours who
  have been there thirty years often know what a county GIS layer will not
  tell you.

How to actually do this: find the water body on a map, trace uphill and
upstream, and name what is up there. Half an hour with a satellite view and
a county parcel map is genuinely more protective than any amount of staring
at the water.

The western Washington version of this is visible from the road. Rain is
abundant, surface water is everywhere, and that same abundance means every
creek has washed across a great deal of land before it gets to you. EPA's
list of nonpoint source causes names exactly what that land tends to be
made of here: bacteria and nutrients from livestock and from faulty septic
systems.

The public evidence of what the water carries sits at the shoreline.
Washington's health department runs a standing shellfish safety programme
because shellfish filter and concentrate whatever is in the water around
them. It monitors marine biotoxins, which are poisons made by certain
microscopic algae, and closes beaches when levels rise; it is explicit that
cooking and freezing do not destroy those toxins, since they are in the
meat itself. Anyone harvesting is expected to check the state's shellfish
safety map or its hotline, 1-800-562-5632, on the day.

Two things in that are worth carrying inland. The first is that the same
warm, nutrient-fed blooms that close a beach are the blooms that make a
summer lake unusable, and in both cases heat does not fix them. The second
is that a region with a live, funded, daily-updated system for telling
people what the water is doing has that system because the water sometimes
carries things. A closed beach is a public statement about the water that
came down the creeks, and it applies to the creek you were thinking of
drinking from, not only to the clams.

## The same stream is a different risk in August than in March

Water sources are not fixed things. The same creek can be two entirely
different propositions depending on the month, and for most of the year the
difference is invisible at the bank.

**Storms and snowmelt are the delivery events.** EPA's mechanism is
seasonal by definition: nonpoint source pollution is "caused by rainfall or
snowmelt moving over and through the ground." Contamination is not a steady
trickle; it arrives in pulses, during and immediately after rain, and after
a dry spell that pulse is worse because months of material has accumulated
with nothing to rinse it away. This is the same first-flush effect the
rainwater guide describes on a roof, running at watershed scale.

**Flow collapses in late summer, and concentration rises.** The USGS
snowmelt example is worth carrying: on the North Fork American River,
streamflow peaked around 1,200 cubic feet per second in March and fell to
roughly 55 to 75 by August. Whatever is entering that stream in August is
being diluted by a small fraction of the water. Low, warm, slow water is
also when blooms happen, when animals concentrate at the few remaining
water sources, and when standing pools form in what was a flowing channel.

**Spring is the flood and well season.** High water lifts contamination
into places it does not normally reach, which is why CDC lists flooding as a
reason to test a well. After a flood, CDC's advice is not to judge: "after a
disaster, it is safest to drink bottled water until you are certain that
your water is free of contaminants and safe to drink." Sample "for total
coliform and either E. coli or fecal coliform bacteria," and if the well has
been disinfected, "wait at least 7 to 10 days to test the water after
disinfection to ensure that the chlorine has been thoroughly flushed from
the system."

**Late summer is the bloom season.** Warm, slow, nutrient-rich water, in
CDC's list of favourable conditions, is a late-summer lake almost by
definition. A lake that was fine in May is not automatically fine in August,
and the one thing you must not do is boil your way through it.

In western Washington the pattern is sharp: a long wet winter with high,
turbid, fast water carrying everything off the land, and a genuinely dry
late summer when creeks drop to base flow and small ones stop entirely. The
turbid winter creek and the clear August creek are both worth suspicion, for
opposite reasons.

## What you can observe, and the much larger part you cannot

Some things a source will tell you, if you look.

**Signs worth acting on:**

- Foam, scum, mats, or paint-like streaks on the surface, or water gone
  green, blue, brown or red. CDC's bloom indicators, and a reason to leave.
- Dead fish or animals on the shore. CDC lists this among the warning
  signs of a bloom, and it is also a general signal that something in that
  water killed something.
- A fuel or solvent smell. This is the absolute stop. No home treatment
  applies.
- A rotten-egg or sewage smell. Hydrogen sulfide can be natural in
  groundwater, but at a surface source it points at septic or decay.
- Cloudiness that was not there yesterday, at a well or a spring. CDC
  names "a change in your water's taste, color, or smell" as a trigger to
  test a well, and the key word is change.
- The obvious physical ones: a dead animal upstream, a pipe discharging
  into the water, livestock standing in it, a culvert from a road, an
  oily sheen.
- Stagnation. No inflow, no outflow, and a surface film.

**Now the honest part, which is longer.** Not one of the following can be
seen, smelled or tasted at any concentration that matters: bacteria,
viruses, *Giardia*, *Cryptosporidium*, nitrate, arsenic, lead, most
pesticides, most solvents, radionuclides, or cyanotoxins. EPA's entire
regulated contaminant list exists because none of these announce themselves.
The USGS reminder that clarity "is not a direct indicator of health risk"
applies in both directions, and the USGS point about spring water is the
memorable version: being underground kills nothing and removes no chemical.

So the correct mental model is that your senses are a one-way alarm. If they
go off, believe them absolutely and walk away. If they stay quiet, you have
learned almost nothing. The only instrument that answers the second case is
a laboratory test, and for a well that is an annual habit, not a one-time
event. Your county or state health department will tell you which certified
lab to use and what your area's specific problems are, which is worth asking
about even if you never test, because the answer is usually a short and
surprising list.

## Whether you are allowed to

This surprises people, and it is a real constraint rather than a technicality.

As a rough orientation, and not as a legal statement about any particular
state: water law in the wetter eastern half of the country tends to follow
the land, so owning ground along a stream tends to come with some right to
use it, while the drier western states tend to run on a different doctrine
called prior appropriation, in which the right belongs to whoever put the
water to use first, regardless of who owns the bank. Every state writes its
own rules, so the only reliable version is your own state's. Washington is
used below as the worked example because it is this project's canonical
setting, not because it is representative of all fifty.

The Washington State Department of Ecology describes prior appropriation in
the standard phrase, first in time, first in right, meaning the earliest
established right is the senior one and a junior right may not impair it.
Rights are established and kept by putting the water to beneficial use,
which is why the doctrine is popularly summarized as use it or lose it.

The practical consequences in Washington, which are representative of the
western pattern, are worth stating concretely. Ecology's guidance is that
using any amount of surface water, from a river, stream, spring or lake, for
any purpose, requires a water right. Groundwater requires one too, above a
set of small exemptions in state law: domestic use up to 5,000 gallons per
day, industrial use up to the same, watering stock, and irrigating a lawn or
garden of half an acre or less. Those exemptions are generous enough that a
household well normally sits inside them, which is why most people never
encounter the rule. Note also that an exempt well is still a water right
established by use, and still sits in the seniority queue.

The equivalent constraint on rainwater already appears in the companion
guide: Colorado, for example, limits a single-family household to two
barrels totalling 110 gallons, with sealed lids, used outdoors on the same
property. Most states do not restrict rain collection at all. A few do.

None of this is likely to matter for filling a bottle from a creek while you
walk past it. All of it matters the moment you plan something permanent: a
new well, a pipe from a spring, a diversion for irrigation, a pond. The rule
to take away is not a set of numbers, because they differ in every state.
It is to check before you build. The answer lives with your state's water
resources or ecology department and your county health department, it is
usually free, and finding out afterwards is expensive.

## What can go wrong

- **You picked the cleanest-looking water.** The most common error in this
  guide. Clear tells you how well treatment will work, not whether the
  water is safe. Pick on what is upstream, not on appearance.
- **You boiled water from a lake with a bloom on it.** Boiling concentrates
  cyanotoxins rather than removing them, in CDC's explicit words. A bloom
  means find other water, the same as a fuel smell does.
- **You drank from a spring because springs are clean.** Spring water is
  groundwater that has just become surface water, and nothing about being
  underground removed the biology or the chemistry.
- **You used a hiking filter on a creek below a town.** Human sewage means
  viruses, and ordinary microfilters do not remove viruses. The
  treatment guide covers the pore sizes and the combination that does.
- **Your well has been fine for twenty years so you never test it.** Wells
  fail silently, usually with something that has no taste. Test annually,
  and test again after any flood, land disturbance, or repair.
- **You drank a little seawater to stretch your fresh water.** It costs you
  more water than it gives. It is worse than drinking nothing.
- **You collected from the same creek in August that was fine in March.**
  Low flow means less dilution, warmer water, and animals concentrated at
  what is left.
- **You treated the water perfectly and put it in a dirty jug.** See
  [Storing Water Safely](/library#storing-water-safely). Treatment does not
  survive a bad container.
- **You built a spring box or dug a well and then found out about water
  rights.** Check first. It takes one phone call.

## How you know it worked

- You can name every water source available to you, including the poor
  ones, because the poor ones are still good for washing.
- You know which is best and why, in terms of where it comes from rather
  than what it looks like.
- For any surface source you would use, you have looked at a map and can
  say what is upstream of it, specifically: houses on septic, livestock,
  crops, roads, old industry.
- You know what changes about your chosen source between winter and late
  summer.
- If you are on a well, you have a test from within the last year and you
  know what your area's local problems are.
- If you are on a municipal supply, you have read one Consumer Confidence
  Report and you know what your water's source actually is.
- You know which of your sources is off the table entirely: salt water
  without distillation, anything with a fuel smell, anything with a bloom
  on it, floodwater.

When those are true you have done the part of water safety that happens
before any equipment comes out. Take what you chose to
[Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) and treat it
properly, and put the result in a container prepared the way
[Storing Water Safely](/library#storing-water-safely) describes. Almost every
serious water mistake is made at the choosing step, not the treating step,
which is why this is the first guide in the sequence rather than the last.
