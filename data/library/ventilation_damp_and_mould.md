# Ventilation, Damp and Mould

Three things make people start reading about this.

The first is water running down the inside of a window on a cold
morning, pooling on the sill, and a sill that has started to go soft.

The second is a black or dark green patch in a corner, usually low
down, usually on an outside wall, very often behind furniture that
nobody has moved in two years.

The third is the feeling that a room is stuffy. Nothing is visibly
wrong. The air just feels used.

All three are the same fact in different clothing. Something is putting
water into the air inside your building, and not enough air is leaving.
This guide works backwards from what you notice to what is actually
happening, and then tells you what to do about it.

## This guide and the insulation guide are two halves of one thing

Read [Insulation and Heat Loss](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss) first if
you have not. It teaches air sealing, R-value, thermal bridging, and
the dew point, and this guide builds on all four without repeating them.

Here is the tension between the two documents, stated plainly, because
it is the real subject of this one.

Sealing a building tight is the right thing to do for heat. It is the
single highest return work in the whole of home energy. The insulation
guide is emphatic about it and it is correct.

Sealing a building tight is the wrong thing to do for air, unless you
then put the air back deliberately. A leaky building ventilates itself
badly, wastefully, and by accident, but it does ventilate itself. Seal
the accidents shut and the water your household generates has nowhere
to go, so it condenses on the coldest surface it can find and grows
something.

Federal regulation states the tension outright. HUD's construction
standard at 24 CFR 3280.505 sets a goal for limiting air leakage and
then bounds it in the same sentence: the goal is to reduce heat loss
and heat gain from infiltration as much as possible **without impinging
on health and comfort**, and within the limits of reasonable economics.
That clause is this guide.

The Department of Energy's Building America programme puts the resolved
version of the same point as a consensus statement: a tightly sealed
dwelling with a properly designed and operated mechanical ventilation
system is always the best choice. Not a leaky building. Not a sealed
building. A sealed building plus a fan you chose.

So: neither document is complete without the other. The insulation
guide tells you to close the holes. This one tells you what to open
instead.

## Where the numbers come from

Every figure here traces to a US government publication that was opened
and read: the EPA's Mold Course and indoor air quality pages, the
Department of Energy's Building America Solution Center, HUD's
construction and installation standards in the Code of Federal
Regulations, the CDC and NIOSH, the CPSC, NOAA's National Weather
Service, and a US Forest Products Laboratory paper on water vapour in
houses. Where a claim is a rule of thumb rather than something an
agency measured, it says so in the text.

One important exception. The governing ventilation standard for homes
in the United States is ASHRAE Standard 62.2. It is the authority, and
most state codes adopt it, but it is a copyrighted document. This guide
cites it as the authority and states its requirements in its own words.
It never reproduces its tables. Where you want a hard number you can
check, this guide gives you the federal regulation instead, because
that is public and free to read.

## The water is coming from inside the house

This is the part that surprises people, so take it slowly.

A roof leak is obvious and everyone thinks of it. A burst pipe is
obvious. Rising damp in a wall is at least a familiar idea. But in a
building with no leaks at all, the occupants are a water source, and in
an ordinary occupied house they are usually the main one.

You breathe out water. You sweat. You boil pasta with the lid off, you
run a shower, you wash dishes, you wash clothes. If you dry those
clothes on a rack indoors rather than outside or in a vented dryer,
every gram of water they were holding ends up in the room's air, by
definition, because that is what drying means.

How much? The honest answer from the research literature is: a lot, and
with enormous spread. A 2022 Forest Products Laboratory paper by Glass,
Kalamees, Pallin and Vinha, comparing simulations against field
measurements in Sweden, the United States and Finland, says the rates
are poorly characterised and vary widely with occupant behaviour, and
gives the range that had been published for a family of four as **less
than 5 kilograms a day (11 pounds) to more than 20 kilograms a day (44
pounds)**. The typical sources it lists are humans, pets, plants,
showering, bathing, cooking and washing.

Sit with the upper end of that. Forty-four pounds of water is about
five US gallons. A household can put several gallons of water a day
into the air inside its own walls without ever spilling anything. In a
sealed building that does not ventilate, that water has exactly two
places to go: out through a fan, or onto a cold surface.

The paper's honesty matters as much as its numbers. Nobody can tell you
your household's rate. What you can do is notice which of the sources
are yours and deal with those, which is what the rest of this guide is
for.

### The one moisture source people never suspect

Burning fuel makes water. It is not a side effect of a badly tuned
appliance, it is what combustion of a hydrocarbon does. The EPA states
it flatly: combustion always produces water vapour, and while water
vapour is not usually thought of as a pollutant, it can act like one,
because it produces high humidity and wet surfaces.

If the appliance has a flue, that water leaves up the flue with
everything else. If it does not, it all stays in the room. Building
America puts a number on it: an unvented combustion appliance produces
**1 gallon of water vapour for every 100,000 Btu**, so a 30,000 Btu
heater releases nearly a gallon of water into your rooms every three
hours of running.

An unvented propane or kerosene heater run through a cold evening is
therefore a humidifier that also emits carbon monoxide. Building
America's recommendation is unambiguous: do not install unvented
combustion appliances within the conditioned space of the home, and it
notes that the National Fuel Gas Code prohibits ventless combustion
heaters in bathrooms and bedrooms outright. The carbon monoxide half of
that problem has its own section below, and it is the part of this
topic that kills people.

### A short list of things to check before anything else

These are the moisture sources that are your own fault and cost nothing
to find.

- **A clothes dryer that vents indoors, into a garage, or into the
  crawlspace.** HUD's standard at 24 CFR 3280.708 requires every gas
  and electric clothes dryer to be exhausted to the outside through a
  moisture and lint duct, forbids connecting that duct to any other
  duct, vent or chimney, and forbids terminating it beneath the home.
  The reason the rule names moisture before lint is that moisture is
  the bigger problem.
- **A bathroom or kitchen fan that discharges into the attic.** More on
  this below, because it is common and it is worse than having no fan
  at all.
- **An unvented heater.** See above.
- **Washing dried indoors in winter with the windows shut.**
- **A humidifier running in a house that already has condensation.**
  EPA's Mold Course names humidifiers and steam radiators as moisture
  sources in their own right.

## Relative humidity, dew point, and the surface that decides

The insulation guide covers the dew point. This section builds on it
rather than repeating it, because the piece that matters for mould is
not the air, it is the **surface**.

The National Weather Service gives the two definitions you need.
Relative humidity measures water vapour relative to the temperature of
the air: it is the actual amount of vapour present compared with the
most that could exist in air at that temperature. The dew point is the
temperature to which air must be cooled to become saturated, that is,
to reach 100 percent relative humidity.

And the rule that makes everything else follow, in the NWS's own words:
warm air can possess more water vapour than cold air, so with the same
absolute amount of moisture present, air has a **higher** relative
humidity when it is cooler and a **lower** relative humidity when it is
warmer. NOAA's satellite service states the same principle for the same
reason.

Now put a building around that.

Your room is at, say, 20 C, and its air is carrying whatever water your
household put into it. That air is in contact with every surface in the
room, and those surfaces are not all at 20 C. The window glass is much
colder. The wall behind the sofa is colder. The corner where two
outside walls meet is colder still, because it loses heat in two
directions at once and gains warm room air from almost none.

Wherever a surface is colder than the air's dew point, the air touching
it cannot hold its water any more and puts it down as liquid. EPA's
Mold Course gives the everyday version: when warm, humid air contacts a
cold surface, condensation may form.

So there are only two levers, and this is the whole of moisture control
in one sentence:

**Either put less water into the air, or make the cold surfaces warmer.**

Ventilation is the first lever. Insulation is the second. That is
exactly why these two guides are a pair, and it is why EPA's list of
ten things to know about mould contains, in the same list, both "reduce
indoor humidity" and "reduce the potential for condensation on cold
surfaces such as windows, piping, exterior walls, roof or floors by
adding insulation."

The window running with water is not a defect in the window. It is the
coldest surface in the room doing its job as a dew point indicator. It
is telling you a number you did not measure.

### Why the corner behind the wardrobe

This is the classic spot, and the mechanism is worth understanding
because it explains a dozen other cases.

Three things stack up in that corner.

1. **It is already the coldest part of the wall.** An external corner
   has more outside surface than inside surface, so it drains heat
   faster than the flat wall beside it. The insulation guide's section
   on thermal bridging is the general version of this: framing, corners
   and fasteners are paths that go around the insulation.
2. **The wardrobe blocks the room's warm air from reaching it.** The
   wall behind a large piece of furniture pushed flat against it is not
   being warmed by the room at all. It runs several degrees colder than
   the wall either side.
3. **Nobody looks there.** By the time you move the wardrobe, the
   growth has had a winter or two.

EPA names the case specifically, listing walls in cold corners behind
furniture, where condensation forms, among the places hidden mould
commonly develops.

The fix follows from the mechanism and is mostly free: pull furniture a
few inches off exterior walls so room air can circulate behind it, and
if you are going to insulate anything, insulate that wall.

The same three-step logic explains cold water pipes sweating in summer,
mould on the inside of a poorly insulated closet on an outside wall,
and the frost ENERGY STAR warns you will find in attic insulation in
cold weather, where warm moist air from the house condenses and then
freezes as it hits the cold attic air. Same physics, different surface.

## What the EPA actually says about mould

There is a large amount of noise on this subject, much of it from
people selling testing or remediation. The federal position is short
and it does not change much:

- **You cannot get rid of the spores, and you should stop trying.** EPA:
  mould spores are ubiquitous, they are found both indoors and
  outdoors, and they cannot be eliminated from indoor environments.
  There is no practical way to eliminate all mould and mould spores in
  the indoor environment.
- **The control is moisture.** EPA: moisture is the most important
  factor influencing mould growth indoors, and moisture control is the
  key to mould control. Mould can grow on virtually any substance
  provided moisture is present.
- **Speed is the whole game.** Clean and dry any damp or wet building
  materials and furnishings **within 24 to 48 hours** and in most cases
  mould will not grow. This is the number to remember from the entire
  guide. A wall cavity that condenses every cold night is never dry for
  24 hours, which is precisely why hidden condensation is so much worse
  than a visible spill.
- **Keep the air between 30 and 60 percent relative humidity**, ideally
  30 to 50. CDC states the same target more tightly: keep humidity as
  low as you can, no higher than 50 percent, all day long.
- **Fix the water first.** EPA: fix the source of the water problem or
  leak to prevent mould growth. Cleaning without fixing the cause is
  repainting a leak.
- **Do not put carpet where there is a perpetual moisture problem.**

### Testing is almost always a waste of your money

This one deserves its own heading because it is where people get sold
things.

EPA: in most cases, if visible mould growth is present, sampling is
unnecessary. And the reason is the part that ends the argument: since
no EPA or other federal limits have been set for mould or mould spores,
sampling cannot be used to check a building's compliance with federal
mould standards. There is no number for a lab to compare your result
against.

CDC goes further and says plainly that it does not recommend mould
testing, because the health effects of mould differ between people, so
you cannot rely on sampling and culturing to know whether someone might
get sick.

NIOSH reaches the same place from the workplace side: finding and
promptly correcting sources of dampness is more effective at preventing
health problems than air samples for indoor mould.

The logic is simple. Whatever the test says, the action is the same:
find the water, stop the water, dry the material, clean the growth. A
result cannot change what you do, so it is not worth paying for. Spend
the money on a fan or a dehumidifier.

### Why the health question is about dampness, not species

You will see a lot written about particular species and their toxins.
The federal agencies frame it differently, and more usefully.

EPA says moulds produce allergens, irritants, and potentially toxic
substances, and that inhalation exposure indoors can cause health
effects in some people. CDC lists what those look like: a stuffy nose,
sore throat, coughing or wheezing, burning eyes, or skin rash. People
with asthma or a mould allergy may react severely; people who are
immune compromised or have chronic lung disease can get lung
infections.

NIOSH states the association at the building level: research studies
have shown that exposures to building dampness and mould have been
associated with respiratory symptoms, asthma, hypersensitivity
pneumonitis, rhinosinusitis, bronchitis and respiratory infections.
EPA's Mold Course points to the 2004 Institute of Medicine report
*Damp Indoor Spaces and Health* for the underlying review, and notes
that a damp building contains more than mould: bacteria, dust mites and
chemicals released from wet building materials are all part of the
picture.

That is the practical takeaway. The thing to be worried about, and the
thing to fix, is the dampness. Not a species name.

## Cleaning mould, and the bleach question

Start with the size rule, because it decides whether this is yours to
do. EPA: if the mouldy area is **less than about 10 square feet**,
which is roughly a 3 foot by 3 foot patch, in most cases you can handle
the job yourself. Above that, get professional help.

The Mold Course sets out three tiers of protection by area, and they
are a good guide to how seriously to take the job even if you are doing
it yourself:

| Mouldy area | Containment | Protection |
|---|---|---|
| About 10 square feet or less | Minimal, protect the work area | Gloves, N-95 respirator, goggles or eye protection |
| 10 to 100 square feet | A single layer of 6-mil fire-retardant polyethylene sheeting | Gloves, N-95 or half-face respirator with HEPA filter, disposable overalls, goggles |
| More than 100 square feet | Double layers of polyethylene sheeting with a decontamination airlock | Gloves, disposable full body clothing, head gear, foot coverings, full-face respirator with HEPA filter |

EPA notes that professional judgement still governs: heavy growth over
a small area can warrant stronger measures than light growth over a
large one.

### The respiratory protection question

At the smallest scale, EPA's wording is that you **may want to** wear an
N-95 respirator, and it notes the cost as about $12 to $25. That is not
a mandate, and it is honest of them not to make it one.

The judgement to make is about disturbance, not about area. Wiping a
patch of window-frame mould with a damp cloth puts very little into the
air. Scrubbing a dried-out patch, sanding, or tearing out wet drywall
puts a great deal into the air. Wear the respirator for the second kind
of work. And note the tier table: as soon as the job is bigger than a
yard square, EPA stops saying "may want to."

Wear gloves and eye protection regardless. CDC specifies rubber boots,
rubber gloves and goggles for cleanup, and says to open windows and
doors to provide fresh air while you work.

### What to clean with

EPA's routine method is the boring one: scrub mould off hard surfaces
with detergent and water, and dry completely.

On bleach, EPA is more cautious than most people expect. Its brief
guide says that the use of a chemical or biocide that kills organisms
such as mould, chlorine bleach for example, is **not recommended as a
routine practice** during mould cleanup. Read that carefully: it is not
a ban, it is a statement that reaching for bleach first is the wrong
habit. Detergent and water plus thorough drying is the default.

CDC does give a bleach recipe for hard surfaces where you want one: no
more than **1 cup of household laundry bleach in 1 gallon of water**.
And it gives the warning that has to travel with every bleach
instruction ever written: never mix bleach with ammonia or any other
cleaner, because that produces a poisonous gas. Ventilate while you
work.

### Porous materials do not get cleaned, they get replaced

This is the point people resist and it is the one that matters most.

EPA: absorbent or porous materials such as ceiling tiles and carpet may
have to be thrown away if they become mouldy. The reason is structural.
Mould grows into a porous material, not just on it. You can wipe the
surface of a mouldy ceiling tile and make it look clean, and the growth
inside it is untouched.

Hard, non-porous surfaces (glass, tile, metal, sealed wood, plastic)
clean up properly. Carpet, ceiling tiles, insulation, upholstery, paper
faced drywall and particle board generally do not.

And do not hide it. EPA: do not paint or caulk mouldy surfaces, clean
up the mould and dry the surface first, because paint applied over a
mouldy surface is likely to peel. The paint will tell on you within a
year.

## How much air a building actually needs

Now to the other half: putting air back on purpose.

The EPA describes three ways outdoor air gets into a building.
**Infiltration** is air leaking in through joints and cracks in walls,
floors and ceilings and around windows and doors. **Natural
ventilation** is air through opened windows and doors. **Mechanical
ventilation** uses fans and ductwork to remove indoor air continuously
and bring in filtered, conditioned outdoor air. The rate at which
outdoor air replaces indoor air is the air exchange rate, and EPA's
warning is the one this guide keeps circling: when there is little
infiltration, little natural ventilation and no mechanical ventilation,
the air exchange rate is low and pollutant levels can increase, to the
point of causing health and comfort problems.

That is the stuffy room, explained. It is not only water. It is
everything else that a house emits, sharing the same stalled air.

### What ASHRAE 62.2 requires, in our own words

ASHRAE Standard 62.2, *Ventilation and Acceptable Indoor Air Quality in
Low-Rise Residential Buildings*, is the governing residential
ventilation standard in the United States. Building America notes that
most state codes use it as their residential ventilation standard, and
that its rates are often higher than the ones local codes require. It
is a copyrighted document, so what follows is a description, not a
reproduction.

In outline, the standard does three things:

1. It sets a **whole-dwelling ventilation rate** that scales with the
   floor area of the dwelling and with the number of bedrooms, the
   bedroom count standing in as a proxy for how many people are likely
   to live there. Bigger house, more air. More bedrooms, more air.
2. It allows a **credit for the leakage the building already has**, so
   that a draughty old house is not required to install as much fan
   capacity as a new airtight one. This is the formal version of the
   idea that tightening a building is what creates the need.
3. It separately requires **local exhaust** at the two places where
   moisture and pollutants are actually generated: the kitchen and each
   bathroom. Whole-house ventilation does not excuse you from these,
   and they do not excuse you from it.

If you want a number you can look up and check for free, US federal
regulation gives you one. HUD's Manufactured Home Construction and
Safety Standards at 24 CFR 3280.103 require:

- **Whole-house ventilation** with a minimum capacity of **0.035 cubic
  feet per minute per square foot of interior floor space**, or its
  hourly average equivalent, and **in no case less than 50 cfm**. For a
  1,400 square foot home that is 49 cfm by the formula, so the 50 cfm
  floor governs; for a 2,000 square foot home it is 70 cfm.
- That capacity must be **in addition to any openable window area**. An
  openable window is not the ventilation system.
- **Kitchen exhaust** capable of 100 cfm to the outside, with the fan
  no more than 10 feet horizontally from the range or cook top.
- **Bathroom exhaust** capable of 50 cfm to the outside, for each
  bathroom and each separate toilet compartment.

Note that HUD's standard at 3280.103(d) explicitly allows a system
complying with ASHRAE 62.2 as an alternative to its own numbers. The
two are recognised as the same job done two ways.

Three details in the same regulation are worth lifting out, because
they are the mistakes people make:

- The system must **exchange air directly with the exterior**, and
  specifically must not draw or expel air from the space underneath the
  home, nor into the floor, wall, or ceiling and roof cavities, **even
  if those cavities are vented**. Ventilating your house into its own
  attic is not ventilating your house.
- The system must be designed so that outside air **reaches the
  bedrooms and main living areas**, not just the room the fan is in.
  Undercut doors or transom grilles are accepted as a way to achieve
  this. A bedroom with a well sealed door is a sealed box.
- The control must be labelled **"WHOLE-HOUSE VENTILATION"**, and the
  homeowner's manual must **encourage occupants to run it whenever the
  home is occupied**. Timers and humidistats are permitted. The
  regulation anticipated the real failure mode, which is somebody
  switching it off because it is a fan and fans seem wasteful.

## The three ways to move that air, and what each costs you

Building America sets out three strategies. There is no free one. Each
buys something and gives something up.

### Exhaust only

Fans pull stale air out of the house, usually the bathroom and kitchen
fans doing double duty. Replacement air comes in wherever it can, which
means through the leaks in the envelope or through passive vents.

- **For it:** cheapest equipment, about $100 to $250. Easy to install.
  Very little electricity, typically 5 to 12 watts. Highly reliable,
  quiet, works with ductless heating. Building America's worked
  example: a 10 watt fan running all year uses 88 kWh, about $10 a
  year at 11 cents a kWh.
- **Against it:** it **may depressurise the home**. The incoming air is
  uncontrolled and unfiltered, and comes from wherever the holes are,
  which may be the crawlspace, the attic, the garage, or a neighbour's
  unit. It is point-source, so it does not draw air evenly from the
  whole house. No heat recovery. And a homeowner can simply switch it
  off.

That depressurisation point is not theoretical. It is the combustion
safety problem, and it has its own section below.

### Supply only

Usually done as central fan integrated supply: an outdoor air intake is
ducted into the return side of the existing furnace or air handler, so
incoming air is filtered, tempered and distributed through the ducts
you already have.

- **For it:** uses ducts and a blower you already own. Air is filtered
  and tempered before it reaches you. You know where the intake is, so
  you can put it somewhere sensible. Pressurises rather than
  depressurises, which keeps crawlspace and garage air out. Installed
  cost roughly $500 to $900 where the location is accessible.
- **Against it:** requires a central forced-air system, which many
  homes do not have. **Much higher electricity use**, because you are
  running a big blower to move a small amount of outdoor air: Building
  America's example is a 300 watt air handler run 8 hours a day for
  ventilation using 876 kWh a year, about $96. It is hard to commission
  correctly, the intake flow rate is often low, and leaky ducts make it
  worse.

### Balanced, with heat recovery

A heat recovery ventilator (HRV) or energy recovery ventilator (ERV)
runs two ducted air streams at once, one out and one in, past each
other through a heat exchanger. Equal flows, so no net pressure on the
house.

- **For it:** no large pressure difference, so no depressurisation and
  no combustion safety problem created by the ventilation itself. Air
  can be distributed to many rooms. And it recovers most of the heat
  you would otherwise throw away: Building America gives sensible
  effectiveness for these units as **55 to 95 percent**.
- **Against it:** the most expensive, $400 to $5,000 for equipment plus
  $1,000 to $1,500 or more to install. More maintenance, including
  regular filter changes. Harder to install and commission. Uses more
  power than an exhaust fan, 30 to 200 watts. Can be noisy.

### HRV or ERV, and why it is a climate question

The difference is one word. An HRV moves **heat** between the two air
streams. An ERV moves **heat and moisture**.

In winter an HRV takes heat out of the air leaving your house and puts
it into the cold air coming in, and lets the moisture go out with the
exhaust. An ERV does the same with the heat and also hands some of the
outgoing moisture back to the incoming air.

Building America describes the ERV's moisture transfer as an advantage
in two cases: in hot humid climates, where it reduces the moisture the
ventilation system itself drags indoors, and in cold dry climates,
where winter indoor air is often uncomfortably dry and an ERV helps
retain moisture in the home. Its own advice on choosing between them is
to consult the manufacturer's specifications for the climate.

Draw your own conclusion for a cold **wet** climate, where the problem
you are solving is too much indoor moisture, not too little. Moisture
retention is not a feature you want. That reasoning follows from the
sourced descriptions above rather than from a quoted recommendation, so
treat it as such, and ask a local installer who knows the climate
before spending four figures.

What is not in doubt is why heat recovery matters more in a long cold
wet winter than in a dry one. In a dry climate you can often solve
humidity by opening a window for ten minutes. In a marine winter you
need to run ventilation more or less continuously for six months, and
continuous ventilation means continuously throwing heated air outside.
Recovering 55 to 95 percent of that heat is the difference between
ventilation you can afford to leave on and ventilation you turn off in
December, which is exactly when you need it.

## Spot ventilation: the bathroom fan and the range hood

Whole-house ventilation dilutes. Spot ventilation removes moisture at
the point where it is made, before it gets into the rest of the house.
It is cheaper and more effective per dollar than anything else in this
guide, and it is usually the thing that is broken.

### The bathroom fan

Building America's requirements: at least **50 cfm for intermittent
operation**, or at least **20 cfm if run continuously**. Noise matters
because a noisy fan gets switched off: no more than 3 sones for an
intermittent fan, no more than 1 sone for a continuous one.

Installation details that decide whether it works:

- Duct it **outdoors**, not into an attic, crawlspace, or the space
  between floors.
- Use smooth-surfaced duct with as few bends as possible, and allow two
  to three feet of straight duct from the fan outlet before the first
  elbow. Flexible duct full of sags is why so many fans move almost
  nothing.
- **Insulate the duct** where it runs through cold space, to minimise
  condensation forming inside it. An uninsulated duct crossing a cold
  attic condenses the very moisture it is carrying, and then drips back
  through your ceiling.
- Control it with a **timer switch** rather than a plain wall switch,
  and leave it running for an hour after a shower. The fan is not
  finished when you leave the room; the walls and the towels are still
  wet.

One more from ENERGY STAR, which is easy to miss: recessed can lights
in a bathroom ceiling cause moisture damage in the attic, because warm
wet air leaks up through them. If your bathroom has can lights and your
attic has a damp patch above the bathroom, you have found it.

### The range hood, and why a recirculating one does nothing

Building America: kitchen exhaust of **100 cfm or more for intermittent
use, or 25 cfm or more continuous**. ENERGY STAR suggests buying a fan
rated at 150 to 200 cfm for intermittent use so that it still delivers
100 after real-world installation losses.

Then the sentence that matters:

> Recirculating fans that draw air through a filter and discharge it
> back into the room are not acceptable.

A recirculating range hood, sometimes sold as ductless, pulls kitchen
air through a grease filter and often a charcoal filter and blows it
straight back at you. It catches some grease. It catches some smell.
**It removes no water whatsoever.** Water vapour is a gas; it goes
through the filter and comes back into your kitchen. If your hood does
not have a duct going outside, it is a light with a noise.

Other ducting rules from the same guide: smooth metal duct, the most
direct route outside with as few bends as possible, seams sealed with
mastic or spray foam and never with cloth-backed duct tape, and the
outside outlet at least 10 feet from any air inlet so you do not suck
your own exhaust back in.

### The fan that vents into the attic causes the problem it was meant to solve

This is common enough that ENERGY STAR lists it on the checklist of
things to inspect before you insulate, alongside a leaking roof and
rotted rafters: kitchen, bathroom and clothes dryer vents that exhaust
moist air directly into the attic space instead of outdoors.

Think about what such a fan does. It is working perfectly. It collects
the wettest air in the house, several times a day, and delivers it
efficiently to the coldest part of the building, which is unheated, has
wooden structure, and which nobody inspects. In the ENERGY STAR guide's
own terms, you are pumping a household's worth of water vapour into the
attic and then wondering why the rafters are mouldy.

Building America forbids it for every fan type. HUD's regulation
forbids it in regulation, requiring the ventilation system not to expel
air into the floor, wall, or ceiling and roof cavities even where those
cavities are vented, and requiring dryer ducts not to terminate beneath
the home.

Checking this costs nothing. Go into the attic and follow each duct.
Every one should end at a hood or vent through the roof or the gable
wall. A duct that stops in mid-air, or that has come off its fitting,
or that terminates neatly inside a soffit, is the fault.

## Carbon monoxide: the part of this topic that kills

Everything above ruins buildings and makes people wheeze. This section
is about the thing that kills people in their sleep, and it belongs in
a ventilation guide because it is a ventilation failure.

Carbon monoxide is produced whenever carbon-based fuel burns. You
cannot see it, smell it or taste it. Its early symptoms are, per CDC,
headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and
confusion, which are also the symptoms of most winter illnesses, which
is exactly why it is so dangerous.

### The numbers

- CDC: more than **400 Americans die** every year from unintentional
  carbon monoxide poisoning not linked to fires, more than **100,000
  visit an emergency department**, and more than **14,000 are
  hospitalised**.
- CPSC, counting only consumer products: more than **200 people a year**
  die from accidental non-fire carbon monoxide poisoning associated
  with consumer products, and **more than 100 of those are linked to
  portable generators**.
- CPSC, on scale: **one portable generator can produce the same amount
  of carbon monoxide as hundreds of cars.**
- EPA, on ordinary background levels indoors: average levels in homes
  without gas stoves are **0.5 to 5 parts per million**; near a
  properly adjusted gas stove, often **5 to 15 ppm**; near a poorly
  adjusted one, **30 ppm or higher**.

That last line tells you the shape of the risk. The difference between
a normal house and a dangerous one is not exotic. It is an appliance
that has drifted out of adjustment, or a flue that has stopped working.

### The four ways it gets into a house

**Unvented heaters.** A kerosene or propane heater with no flue puts
every combustion product into the room, which is the same reason it
puts a gallon of water per 100,000 Btu into the room. EPA's advice when
replacing one is to consider buying a vented heater instead. And the
rule with no exceptions: **never use a gas stove or oven to heat your
home.**

**Generators.** The biggest single killer on the CPSC list, and it
spikes after every storm. Never use a portable generator inside a home
or garage, **even with the doors and windows open**. Outside only, at
least **20 feet** from the house with the exhaust pointed away, and CDC
adds: more than 20 feet from any window, door or vent, because an open
window is a duct.

**Blocked, leaking or damaged flues and chimneys.** EPA: these release
harmful combustion gases and particles and even fatal concentrations of
carbon monoxide. A bird's nest, a collapsed liner, or a flue that was
never swept is enough. Have the heating system, water heater and any
fuel-burning appliance inspected and serviced by a qualified
professional every year. This is the single maintenance task in this
guide that is worth paying for.

**Backdrafting, which is the one this guide is really about.** A
natural-draft appliance, meaning one that relies on hot gas rising to
carry exhaust up a flue, needs the house to supply it with air. If you
depressurise the house, the flue can reverse, and combustion products
come **down** the flue and into the room.

What depressurises a house? Exhaust fans. A big range hood. A clothes
dryer. Exhaust-only whole-house ventilation. Building America states
the risk directly: depressurising the home can interfere with any
natural-draft combustion appliance located in the home. EPA notes that
pollutants from fireplaces and woodstoves without a dedicated outdoor
air supply can be backdrafted from the chimney into the living space,
**particularly in weatherized homes**.

So the two halves of this guide meet here, and it is the dangerous
meeting. Seal the house, then add a powerful exhaust fan, and you can
create a pressure condition that a leaky house would have shrugged off.

The resolutions, in order of how well they work:

1. **Remove the risk at the source.** Building America's best practice
   is to replace natural-draft combustion appliances with direct-vent
   sealed-combustion appliances, which draw their own air from outside
   and exhaust through their own sealed pipe, or with non-combustion
   appliances. A sealed-combustion appliance cannot backdraft, because
   it is not connected to your room's air at all.
2. **Choose a balanced ventilation system** rather than exhaust-only,
   if you have natural-draft appliances you are keeping. Equal flows in
   and out means no net pressure.
3. **Have it commissioned and tested.** Building America: any existing
   ventilation system should be assessed for effectiveness, and any new
   system should be commissioned by a trained HVAC technician.
4. **After any air sealing work, get the appliances checked.** ENERGY
   STAR's caution, verbatim in substance: after making energy
   improvements that result in a tighter house, there can be an
   increased opportunity for carbon monoxide to build up if
   gas-burning appliances are not venting properly, so have a heating
   and cooling technician check the combustion appliances, meaning the
   gas or oil fired furnace, the water heater and the dryer, for proper
   venting. The insulation guide says the same thing. Neither guide is
   being polite about it.

### Alarms

Federal regulation treats these as mandatory equipment, not an
accessory. HUD's standard at 24 CFR 3280.211 requires a carbon monoxide
alarm in every home that contains a fuel-burning appliance or has an
attached garage. The alarms must be listed to ANSI/UL 2034, installed
**outside each separate sleeping area in the immediate vicinity of the
bedrooms**, and if a fuel-burning appliance is inside a bedroom or its
attached bathroom, there must be an alarm in that bedroom too. Where
more than one is required they must be **interconnected**, so that one
sounding sets them all off. Each must be powered from the home's
electrical system with a **battery as secondary power**.

For an existing house, CPSC and CDC give the practical version: install
battery-operated alarms or alarms with battery backup **on every level
of the home and outside every sleeping area**. Check the batteries
regularly, and CDC suggests using the seasonal clock changes as the
reminder. **Replace the alarm itself following the manufacturer's
instructions, or every 5 years.** A carbon monoxide alarm is not a
smoke alarm; its sensor wears out on a schedule whether it has ever
detected anything or not.

If the alarm sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air first, then
call emergency services from outside. Do not go back in to investigate.

## Crawlspaces and attics, where the damp usually actually lives

In a marine climate the visible mould in the bedroom corner is often
the smallest part of the problem. The larger part is in the two spaces
nobody enters.

### The crawlspace

The traditional approach is to ventilate it to outdoor air. HUD's model
installation standard at 24 CFR 3285.505 states the classic ratio: a
crawlspace enclosed with skirting must have ventilation openings with a
minimum net area of **1 square foot for every 150 square feet of the
home's floor area**, reduced to **1 square foot for every 1,500 square
feet** where a **6-mil polyethylene** ground cover or other acceptable
vapour retarder is laid over the entire ground surface beneath the
home. The openings must sit as high above the ground as practicable, on
at least two opposite sides for cross-ventilation, screened against
rodents, and in freezing areas they must be adjustable so they can be
closed.

Read the two numbers together and notice what they are telling you: a
sheet of plastic on the dirt is worth **ten times** as much as an open
vent. HUD's companion section 3285.204 sets out how to lay it. A
minimum of 6 mil polyethylene or equivalent, over the entire area under
the home, joints overlapped at least 12 inches, and any voids or tears
repaired.

The ground is the moisture source. The vents are only a way of dealing
with what the ground releases, and they are the weaker tool by an order
of magnitude.

Now the awkward part, and it is the crux of the crawlspace question in
a marine climate. Building America explains why vented crawlspaces fail
in humid conditions: the outside air can contain more water vapour than
the air within the crawlspace, so the vents **encourage** condensation
by letting warm humid air in to condense on cool floor joists. A vent
is not a one-way valve. It is a hole, and in the wrong season it lets
weather in.

The modern alternative is an unvented, insulated, conditioned
crawlspace. The vents are sealed, insulation moves from the floor above
to the crawlspace walls, and the space becomes part of the house.
Building America's requirements:

- **6-mil polyethylene over the floor and up the walls**, lapped to
  within 4 inches of the top of the foundation wall, overlapped 12
  inches at seams, sealed with adhesive and tape.
- **A drying mechanism**, either a continuously operating exhaust fan
  or a conditioned air supply, at a minimum of **1 cfm for each 50
  square feet of under-floor area, or 50 cfm total, whichever is
  greater**. Sealing the vents without providing that is how you build
  a sealed damp box.

Whichever you choose, one detail from HUD applies to both: dryer vents
and combustion air inlets must **pass through the skirting to the
outside**. A dryer discharging into the crawlspace is the single worst
thing you can do to it.

### The attic

The attic takes the opposite advice, and it catches people out.

ENERGY STAR addresses the confusion head on, and its wording is worth
reading twice:

> At first it may seem odd to add insulation for warmth and then
> purposely allow cold air to enter the attic through vents, but this
> combination is the key to a durable and energy-efficient home.

The reasoning: in winter, letting outdoor air flow through the attic
keeps it **cold**, which reduces ice damming, where snow melts off a
too-warm attic and refreezes at the gutters. Insulation and air sealing
help keep the attic cold in winter by blocking heat and moist air from
below. In summer, the same airflow carries superheated air out,
protecting the shingles and removing moisture.

The rule follows: a well insulated attic should also be well
ventilated. The insulation goes on the attic **floor**, the ventilation
goes **above** it, and the two do not fight.

HUD's standard at 24 CFR 3280.504(d) gives the numbers. Attic and roof
cavities must be vented with a minimum free ventilation area of not
less than **1/300 of the attic or roof cavity floor area**. At least 50
percent of that must come from ventilators in the upper part of the
space, and at least 40 percent from eave, soffit or low gable vents, so
that air actually crosses the whole attic instead of short-circuiting.
A **clear air passage at least 1 inch high** must be kept between the
top of the insulation and the roof sheathing, with baffles where
needed. A mechanical system is allowed instead, at a minimum of 0.02
cfm per square foot of attic floor area.

Which brings us to what ENERGY STAR calls the most common mistake
homeowners make when installing insulation: blocking the flow of air at
the eaves. Their instruction is in capital letters in the original, so
it will be here too: **NEVER COVER ATTIC SOFFIT VENTS WITH
INSULATION.** Use rafter vents (also called baffles or insulation
stops) at each rafter bay to hold the channel open, and soffit vents to
feed it.

A last note from the same guide, for anyone tempted by a powered attic
fan: if the soffit vents are blocked and the attic is not well sealed
from the house below, an attic fan will pull conditioned air up out of
your house rather than outdoor air in through the vents. It will cost
you money and, in this guide's terms, it will pull your indoor moisture
into the attic on its way. Fix the sealing and the passive vents first.

## The Silverdale case

Silverdale, Washington is the worked example throughout this
curriculum, and for this topic it is close to the hardest case in the
country. Every figure below is from
`data/locales/silverdale_wa/climate.json`, which holds NOAA 1991-2020
normals from the Bremerton station 8.5 km south, with full provenance.

Here is the shape of the problem.

**Annual precipitation: 1,446 mm.** That is 57 inches, and it is not
spread evenly. Adding the monthly normals in the file, October through
March carry **1,154 mm of that 1,446**, which is 80 percent of the
year's water falling in the six coldest, darkest months. (That
arithmetic is ours on the file's own monthly table.)

**December alone drops 252 mm.** The whole of April through September
together drops 294 mm. One December is very nearly an entire summer
half-year's worth of rain.

**And it is never very cold.** December runs a mean high of 7.6 C and a
mean low of 1.7 C. January runs 8.1 and 2.0. The Koppen classification
in the file is Csb, warm-summer Mediterranean, and the winter design
temperature is -6 C.

**And it is dark.** 8.4 hours of daylight at midwinter.

Now assemble a building out of those four facts.

A Silverdale winter is not a freeze. It is **five months of wet, mild,
dim weather in which nothing outdoors ever really dries**. Wall
surfaces, crawlspace joists and roof sheathing sit for months at
temperatures that are cold enough to condense indoor air but nowhere
near cold enough to stop anything growing. EPA's 24 to 48 hour drying
window is not a tight deadline here; it is a deadline the building
misses continuously from October to March.

Compare that to a genuinely cold climate, where the same building in
January is frozen solid and biologically inert, or to a hot dry
climate, where whatever gets wet is dry by the afternoon. Silverdale
gets the worst option: warm enough to grow, wet enough to grow, for
most of the year.

Two things follow.

### Why the answer is not to leave the building leaky

It is tempting to conclude that if sealing a building traps moisture, a
marine climate should keep its buildings loose. That is wrong for three
reasons.

First, the [insulation guide](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss) puts western
Washington in climate zone 4C, the marine zone, and the attic and floor
insulation targets for 4C are the same as for genuinely cold zones 5
and 6, because mild and long adds up to as much heating as cold and
short. The heating bill argument for sealing is fully in force here.

Second, a leaky building does not ventilate where you need it. Leaks
are wherever the holes happen to be, which is usually the attic and the
crawlspace, which is to say the two places you least want air moving
between. That is why the insulation guide's chimney-effect section
describes warm air leaving through the attic and cold air being drawn
in through the basement: the air really is moving, just along the worst
possible path, and carrying your moisture into your structure on the
way.

Third, an uncontrolled leak is uncontrolled in **both** directions. The
Building America point about vented crawlspaces is the general case: in
a marine climate, outdoor air is often wetter than the air inside the
cool cavity it is entering, so the hole that was supposed to dry the
building sometimes wets it instead. A fan with a duct only ever goes
one way.

So the answer for Silverdale is the same as the answer everywhere, only
more so: **seal the building properly, then ventilate it deliberately,
continuously, and through ducts you chose.**

### Why ventilation dries a house here even when it is raining

This is the part that feels wrong until you follow it, and it is the
single most useful idea in this guide for anyone in a marine climate.

Take the NWS rule from earlier: warm air can hold more water vapour
than cold air, so the same quantity of moisture gives a **higher**
relative humidity in cooler air and a **lower** relative humidity in
warmer air.

Now take Silverdale outdoor air in December. It is around 4 C and it
may well be raining, so call it close to saturated: 100 percent
relative humidity. That sounds like the last thing you want to bring
indoors.

But saturated air at 4 C contains very little water in absolute terms,
because cold air cannot hold much. Bring it inside and warm it to 20 C
and nothing has been added to it, while its capacity has gone up a
great deal. Its relative humidity falls sharply. It arrives in your
room **thirsty**, and it leaves carrying your shower, your cooking and
your breath out with it.

That is why running a bathroom fan in a Pacific Northwest January
works, and why leaving a trickle vent open in a downpour is not the
madness it looks like. The rain outside is liquid water going past your
wall; the air it fell through is, once heated, drier than your kitchen.

Two honest limits on that. It stops being true in summer, when outdoor
air can be warmer and wetter than the cavity it enters, which is
exactly the vented crawlspace failure above. And it is not free: every
cubic foot of cold air you warm up costs fuel, which is the whole
argument for heat recovery in this climate. Recovering 55 to 95 percent
of that heat is what makes six months of continuous ventilation
affordable.

### The Silverdale checklist

If you own or rent a building on the Kitsap Peninsula, this is the
order to work in.

1. **Find out where the bathroom, kitchen and dryer ducts actually go.**
   Free. This is the highest return hour in the entire guide.
2. **Get a working bathroom fan on a timer**, at least 50 cfm, ducted
   outside, insulated where it crosses cold space, and run it for an
   hour after every shower.
3. **Get a range hood that has a duct.** If yours recirculates, it is
   doing nothing for moisture.
4. **Put 6-mil polyethylene over the crawlspace floor**, lapped 12
   inches at the seams. Ten times the value of a vent, per the ratio in
   the regulation above, and a job you can do yourself in an afternoon
   if you can stand the crawl.
5. **Dry laundry outside in summer and in a vented dryer in winter.**
   Never on a rack in a closed room from October to March.
6. **Pull the furniture off the exterior walls.** Free.
7. **Check that the soffit vents are not buried** and that there are
   baffles holding the 1 inch channel open at the eaves.
8. **Get a carbon monoxide alarm outside each sleeping area**, on every
   level, and write the replacement date on it.
9. **Then, when you are ready to spend real money**, size a whole-house
   ventilation system, and in this climate look hard at balanced
   ventilation with heat recovery, because you will be running it all
   winter.

## If you have very little money

In rough order of value returned per dollar. This ordering is a rule of
thumb assembled from the priorities in the sources below, not a
measured ranking.

1. **Trace every exhaust duct to where it ends.** Costs nothing but a
   torch and an hour. Finding a dryer duct that fell off in the
   crawlspace is worth more than anything else on this list.
2. **Run the fans you already have, properly.** An existing bathroom
   fan left on for an hour after a shower removes more water than most
   equipment upgrades.
3. **Pull furniture off exterior walls, and open closet doors on
   outside walls.** Free.
4. **Stop drying laundry indoors.**
5. **Ground cover in the crawlspace.** A roll of 6-mil polyethylene is
   the cheapest large improvement in this entire subject.
6. **Weatherstrip and seal the attic hatch** (from the insulation
   guide), so you stop pushing wet house air into the attic.
7. **A timer switch for the bathroom fan.** Under $30, and it fixes the
   behaviour problem rather than relying on you.
8. **A carbon monoxide alarm.** Cheap, and it is the only item on this
   list that is about not dying.
9. **A dehumidifier**, if you have a specific damp room and no way to
   duct a fan out of it. It treats the symptom rather than the cause,
   but it treats it.
10. **Then the ducted fans, then whole-house ventilation, then heat
    recovery**, in that order, because each one only pays off once the
    cheaper ones are done.

## What can go wrong

- **The windows stream with water now that the house is sealed.** The
  moisture that used to leak away is staying in. Manage it with
  ventilation and humidity, not by reopening the leaks. Target 30 to 60
  percent relative humidity, ideally 30 to 50.
- **You cleaned the mould and it came back in the same spot.** You
  treated the growth and not the water. Something is putting water on
  that surface, or that surface is below the dew point. Ask which.
- **You cleaned the mould and it came back somewhere new.** Same
  answer, and it means the moisture is general rather than local, which
  points at ventilation rate rather than at one leak.
- **The fan runs but the mirror still fogs and stays fogged.** Check
  where the duct goes, check for sags and crushed flexible duct, and
  check that the bathroom door is undercut so replacement air can get
  in. A fan with no makeup air path moves very little.
- **You installed a beautiful range hood and the kitchen still steams.**
  Check whether it is ducted or recirculating. A recirculating hood
  removes no water at all.
- **The attic has frost on the insulation in winter.** ENERGY STAR
  names this: warm moist air from the house is condensing and freezing
  as it meets cold attic air. That is an air sealing failure in the
  ceiling, plus possibly a fan discharging into the attic.
- **Ice dams, or a wet patch on the ceiling under the bathroom.** Heat
  and moisture escaping into the attic. Check the can lights, the
  bathroom fan duct, and the attic hatch.
- **The crawlspace smells and the floor above feels cold and damp.**
  Ground cover first, then decide between properly vented and properly
  sealed. Do not half-seal it.
- **You sealed the crawlspace vents and it got worse.** Sealing without
  a drying mechanism makes a damp box. Building America's figure is 1
  cfm per 50 square feet or 50 cfm total, whichever is greater.
- **A new exhaust fan made the wood stove or water heater smell.** Stop.
  You may be backdrafting a flue. This is the failure mode in this
  guide that can kill you the same night. Turn the fan off, ventilate,
  and get the appliance and the ventilation system checked by someone
  qualified.
- **The carbon monoxide alarm sounds.** Everyone outside into fresh
  air, then call emergency services from outside.
- **Somebody offered to test your air for mould.** Reread the testing
  section. EPA says sampling is usually unnecessary when growth is
  visible, and there is no federal standard for a lab result to be
  compared against.

## How you know it worked

- You can name where the water in your indoor air is coming from, and
  at least two of those sources now leave the building through a duct.
- Every exhaust fan and dryer in the building discharges outdoors, and
  you have personally followed each duct to its end.
- The bathroom fan runs after the shower, not just during it.
- The range hood has a duct.
- You know which surfaces in your house are the cold ones, and there is
  nothing pushed flat against them.
- Your indoor relative humidity sits between 30 and 50 percent, and you
  know that because you own a $12 hygrometer, not because you assume it.
- Nothing that got wet has stayed wet for more than 48 hours.
- The crawlspace has plastic on the ground.
- The soffit vents are clear and there is a channel above the
  insulation.
- There is a carbon monoxide alarm outside each sleeping area, its
  batteries work, and you know the year it has to be replaced.
- Your combustion appliances have been checked since the house got
  tighter, and you can say whether any of them are natural draft.
- And the test that ties both guides together: **the house is warmer at
  the same thermostat setting, and the windows are dry.** Either one
  alone is half a job.

When all of those are true, you have understood the actual trade. A
building is not trying to be airtight and it is not trying to be
draughty. It is trying to hold heat while letting water and stale air
out on a path you chose, through a hole you put there, at a rate you
decided. Everything in these two guides is a version of that one
sentence.
