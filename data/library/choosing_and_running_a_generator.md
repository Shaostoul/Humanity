# Choosing and Running a Generator

When the power goes out for days, a generator can keep the refrigerator
cold, the lights on, the phones charged and, wired in properly, the
furnace fan and the well pump running. It can also kill everyone in the
house while they sleep. The same machine does both, and which one it
does depends almost entirely on where it stands and how it is connected.

This guide is about choosing a portable or standby generator, working
out how big it has to be from the things you actually need to run,
putting it where its exhaust cannot reach anyone, connecting it without
sending power back down the street, and running it through rain, night
and refuelling without hurting yourself.

It sits beside several other guides and does not repeat them.
[Where Your Own Electrical Work Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops)
has the Consumer Product Safety Commission's generator warnings,
including the danger label required on every portable generator made or
imported since May 2007, and the line between what a household may do to
its wiring and what belongs to an electrician.
[Electricity and How It Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows) explains
watts, volts and amps and why a cord can overheat. [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould) covers carbon monoxide alarms and
where they go. [Sound and Hearing](/library#sound-and-hearing) works out how far
away a generator has to be before its noise stops being a problem.
Storing the fuel is the subject of its own guide, [Fuels and Their
Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards); this one says only where fuel must
never be kept. Heating the house without power, and doing it safely, is
in [Heating a Home Safely](/library#heating-a-home-safely).

Its sources are the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the
Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the Federal Emergency Management
Agency (FEMA) and its Ready.gov site, and the National Oceanic and
Atmospheric Administration's storm records, all US federal and in the
public domain; and, restated in our own words, a table of appliance
loads from an Oregon public utility, a Washington public utility's
advice, and Washington State's electrical rules. OSHA's rules and fact
sheets are written for employers and their workers; they are used here
because they say clearly what keeps people alive around a generator, not
because they bind a household (our reading). Where something is general
practice or our own reading, the text says so.

## First: how generators hurt people

Carbon monoxide comes first, because it kills the most people (the CPSC's
figures, below); the others follow in no particular order.

**Carbon monoxide.** OSHA's fact sheet calls portable generators
internal combustion engines used to generate electricity, and like any
engine their exhaust carries carbon monoxide (CO), a poison you cannot
see or smell. The CPSC says that "An average of about 100 consumers die in the U.S.
each year from CO poisoning from portable generators". It also warns
how fast it happens: "CO poisoning from portable generators can happen
so quickly that exposed persons may become unconscious before
recognizing symptoms such as nausea, dizziness or weakness." The CDC
adds the people most at risk on a night without power: "People who are
sleeping, drunk, or under the influence of other substances can die from
CO poisoning before they have symptoms." Most of this guide is about
keeping the exhaust away from people.

**Electrocution.** A generator makes the same electricity as the mains,
with the same ability to kill, and two habits make it worse: running it
wet, and connecting it to the house wiring through an outlet. That
second one can also kill a line worker a long way off, as explained
below.

**Fire.** OSHA's fact sheet on portable generators: "Generators become
hot while running and remain hot for long periods after they are
stopped." Fuel spilled on a hot engine can catch fire, and an overloaded
generator can overheat. Candles and heaters used during the same outage
start fires of their own; see [Emergency Shelter](/library#emergency-shelter).

**Burns, noise and lifting.** The muffler stays hot long after the engine
stops (OSHA, above). OSHA lists noise and vibration among a generator's
hazards and says to keep portable generators "as far away as possible
from work areas and gathering spaces", or to wear hearing protection.
And a generator is heavy: [Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](/library#force-levers-and-mechanical-advantage) works through
getting one into a pickup without hurting your back.

## What kind of generator

**Portable generators** sit outside on the ground and supply power
through outlets on their own panel. Appliances plug into them with
extension cords. They are the kind the CPSC's death figures above are
about, and the kind people carry into garages and sheds (our reading).

**Inverter generators** are a quieter, usually smaller type of portable
generator whose electronics smooth the power and let the engine slow
down at light loads (general practice). Everything in this guide about
exhaust, cords, rain and refuelling applies to them exactly as it does
to any other portable generator.

**Standby generators** are installed permanently beside the house, run on
natural gas or propane, and start themselves through an automatic
transfer switch when the mains fail (general practice). They are not a
do-it-yourself purchase: the wiring is an electrician's job and the gas
line a licensed gas fitter's, both under permits (general practice; the
transfer switch is covered below). A standby generator is still an
engine, and the CDC lists small engines among the things whose fumes
carry CO. Placed and vented as its maker and the code require, it still
needs working CO alarms in the house (general practice).

**A CO shut-off feature.** The CPSC's advice for anyone buying a portable
generator: "Look for portable generators that have a CO shut-off safety
feature." It explains: "This safety feature automatically shuts off the
generator when high levels of CO are present around the generator.
Models that are certified to the latest PGMA G300-2018 and UL 2201
safety standards are estimated to reduce deaths from CO poisoning by 87%
and 100%, respectively." It adds that "UL 2201 certified models have
reduced CO emissions in addition to the CO shut-off feature." A shut-off
is a backstop, not a licence to run the generator anywhere near the
house: it acts on the air around the generator, not on the air in your
bedroom (our reading).

**Two ratings on the label.** A generator is sold with two figures: the
power it can supply continuously (its rated or running watts) and a
higher figure it can supply for a moment (its maximum, starting or surge
watts). You need both, for the reason in the next section (general
practice).

## How big: size it by what you actually run

FEMA's power outage sheet: "Plan to prioritize the things you will need
to plug in. You may not be able to power all appliances at one time."
That is the whole method. A generator is sized by the loads that will run
at the same moment, not by everything in the house.

### The loads, from the labels

Every appliance carries a label with its watts, or its volts and amps,
which multiply to watts. [Electricity and How It
Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows) works an example. Things with
motors are the exception: they draw extra for a moment as they start.
That guide gives NIOSH's figure for motors in general, up to twice the
nameplate current. For the motors that matter most in an outage,
refrigerators, freezers and well pumps, the utility table below allows
far more than that for the first moment (our comparison of the two), so
look in the appliance's manual for its starting figure, or ask the maker
(general practice).

The Eugene Water & Electric Board, a public utility in Oregon, publishes
a sizing sheet with typical figures and a method (restated here; it says
its figures are a general guide only and to check with a generator
seller, manufacturer, contractor or electrician). Some of its typical
running watts, and the extra watts some loads need for the moment they
start:

| Load | Running watts | Extra starting watts |
| --- | --- | --- |
| LED light bulb | 15 | none listed |
| Phone charger | 20 | none listed |
| Laptop | 60 | none listed |
| Refrigerator or freezer | 700 | 2,200 |
| Furnace fan blower, 1/3 HP | 700 | 1,400 |
| Well pump, 1/3 HP | 1,000 | 2,000 |
| Microwave oven, 1,000 W | 1,000 | none listed |
| Space heater | 1,500 | none listed |
| Electric water heater | 4,500 | none listed |

Its method, in three steps: add up the running watts of everything you
will use at the same time; find the single biggest extra starting
figure among them; add the two. The generator's running rating must cover
the first total, and its starting rating the second. The method assumes
only one motor starts at a time, so leave some margin, because a
refrigerator and a pump can kick in together (our reading).

### Worked example: what one household needs

The figures are the utility's typical ones; the arithmetic is ours.

**Through extension cords only** (a portable generator with nothing wired
to the house): a refrigerator (700), a chest freezer (700, using the same
row), four plug-in lamps with LED bulbs (60), two phone chargers (40) and
a laptop (60). Running total: 1,560 watts. Biggest extra starting
figure: 2,200, for the refrigerator or the freezer. Starting total: 3,760
watts. So this household needs a generator rated for at least about
1,600 watts running and 3,800 watts starting, plus a margin.

Add a 1,000 watt microwave and the running total becomes 2,560 watts,
and the starting total 4,760. Either buy a generator that big, or use the
microwave only while the refrigerator and the freezer are both switched
off: then the load is 1,160 watts, with no motor to start (our
arithmetic). Switching off the freezer alone is not enough, because the
refrigerator starting on top of the microwave still asks for 4,060 watts,
more than the 3,800 sized above (our arithmetic).

**Through a transfer switch** (wired in properly by an electrician,
below): add the gas furnace's fan blower (700 running, 1,400 extra to
start) and a 1/3 horsepower well pump (1,000 running, 2,000 extra).
Running total: 3,260 watts. Biggest extra starting figure: still 2,200.
Starting total: 5,460 watts.

Three lessons from that arithmetic:

- **Heat is the hardest load.** A space heater alone is 1,500 watts, and
  an electric water heater 4,500, more than a small portable generator
  can make at all (our comparison). A space heater should also be
  plugged straight into a
  wall outlet: the CPSC says, of electric portable heaters, "ALWAYS use a
  wall outlet; NEVER a power strip", and a generator on cords offers no
  wall outlet (our reading). Plan to stay warm with layers and one room,
  as in [Emergency Shelter](/library#emergency-shelter), not with an electric
  heater on a generator (our reading).
- **The furnace and the well pump usually cannot be plugged in.** They
  are normally wired into the house, so only a transfer switch can feed
  them (general practice; the furnace still needs its gas or oil).
- **The refrigerator does not need to run all day.** Ready.gov: "The
  refrigerator will keep food cold for about four hours. A full freezer
  will keep the temperature for about 48 hours." So a small generator
  can run the refrigerator and the freezer in turns, a few hours each,
  instead of both at once (our reading). FEMA's sheet says to "Keep a
  thermometer in the refrigerator and freezer so that you can make sure
  food stays at a safe temperature", and the thermometer tells you
  whether your schedule is working. Ready.gov's line is 40 F: "Throw away
  any food that has been exposed to temperatures 40 degrees or higher for
  two hours or more, or that has an unusual odor, color or texture."

**Do not overload it.** OSHA: "Do not overload a generator; this can
lead to overheating which can create a fire hazard." Start the biggest
motor first and add the small loads after it, so the surge does not
arrive on top of everything else (general practice).

## Where it goes

The CPSC says most of the generator incidents reported to it involve CO
from generators used indoors or in partly enclosed spaces: a generator
in the wrong place. The rules, from the CPSC, the CDC, OSHA and
Ready.gov:

- **Outside. Never inside anything.** The CPSC's safety alert: never use
  a generator inside homes, garages, crawlspaces, sheds or similar areas,
  "even when using fans or opening doors and windows for ventilation",
  because deadly CO "can quickly build up in these areas and can linger
  for hours, even after the generator has shut off." Basements too:
  OSHA's fact sheet names them, and adds that open windows and doors may
  not prevent CO from building up in an enclosed space.
- **Not against the house.** The CPSC, in its winter storm advice of
  December 2025: "Don't operate a generator on a porch or in a carport.
  It's too close to the home and puts your family at risk of CO
  poisoning."
- **At least 20 feet away.** Ready.gov: "Generators and fuel should
  always be used outdoors and at least 20 feet away from windows, doors
  and attached garages." The CDC's figure is the same distance from
  vents too: "Only use generators outside, more than 20 feet away from
  any windows, doors, and vents." Twenty feet is a minimum, not a target
  (our reading).
- **Exhaust pointed away, windows shut.** The CPSC: "Direct the
  generator's exhaust away from your home and other buildings where
  someone can enter. Close windows and seal off vent openings that are
  near the generator or in the path of its exhaust." "Other buildings"
  includes your neighbour's house and the shed someone works in (our
  reading).
- **Clear space around it.** OSHA: "Make sure a generator has 3 to 4
  feet of clear space on all sides and above it to ensure adequate
  ventilation."
- **On firm, level ground, where water cannot pool under it,** and away
  from where children play: the muffler stays hot (general practice;
  OSHA on heat, above).

**The cords.** The CPSC's rules: heavy-duty extension cords made for
outdoor use, "long enough to allow the generator to be placed outdoors
and far away from windows, doors and vents", each one checked for cuts
and tears along its whole length, with all three prongs on the plug, and
"Make sure the wattage rating for each cord exceeds the total wattage of
all appliances connected to it." It adds: "Protect the cord from getting
pinched or crushed if it passes through a window or doorway." A door or
window held open for a cord is an opening the exhaust can find, so bring
the cord in on a side of the house away from the exhaust, not through the
nearest door, and close the gap round it as far as it will go (our
reading of the CPSC's rule to close windows near the generator). With
the generator 20 feet out, a 25-foot cord barely reaches the nearest
wall; reaching round to the far side of the house usually takes 50 feet
or more (our estimate). A longer cord loses more voltage and makes more
heat for the same load, so it needs a heavier gauge: [Electricity and How
It Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows) works a long-cord example.

**Ground fault protection.** OSHA's fact sheet says to use GFCIs,
"especially where electrical equipment is used in or around wet or damp
locations", and notes that GFCIs and extension cords with GFCI protection
built in are sold at hardware stores. Many generators have them on their
outlets (general practice). The GFCI itself is explained in [Where Your
Own Electrical Work Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops). OSHA also
says to make sure a generator is properly grounded and to follow the
maker's instructions for how; a generator that supplies only cords
plugged into its own outlets is treated differently from one connected to
a building, as OSHA's grounding fact sheet explains (restated).

## Carbon monoxide alarms

If the generator is the most important thing you put outside, the CO
alarm is the most important thing you put inside. The CPSC's December
2025 advice: battery-operated alarms or alarms with battery backup on
each level of the home and outside separate sleeping areas, tested
monthly, and "Interconnected CO alarms are best; when one sounds, they
all sound." The CDC's generator rule includes one: "When using a
generator, use a battery-powered or battery backup CO detector in your
home." A plug-in alarm with no battery is dark when the power is out
(our reading). [Ventilation, Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould)
has the placement rules in full, and when to replace an alarm.

**When the alarm sounds, or anyone feels ill.** The CPSC: "Never ignore an
alarm when it sounds. Get outside immediately, then call 911." Its
generator alert, for anyone who starts to feel sick, dizzy or weak near a
running generator: get to fresh air "RIGHT AWAY. DO NOT DELAY." OSHA
adds that nobody goes back in until trained and properly equipped
responders say it is safe. Turn the generator off only if you can do it
without going back into the area it has filled (our reading).

The CDC lists the common symptoms of CO poisoning as headache,
dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion,
often described as flu-like. When several people in one house feel like
that at once during an outage, think of CO before you think of flu (our
reading).

## Connecting it to the house

### Never through an outlet

The tempting shortcut is a cord with a plug on each end, one end in the
generator and the other in a wall outlet, so that the whole house wiring
is fed backwards. The CPSC's warning is plain: "NEVER try to power the
house wiring by plugging the generator into a wall outlet, a practice
known as 'backfeeding.' This is extremely dangerous and presents an
electrocution risk to utility workers and neighbors served by the same
utility transformer." It adds: "It also bypasses some of the built-in
household circuit protection devices." OSHA says how far the danger
reaches: "Attaching a generator directly to a building electrical system
without a properly installed transfer switch can energize wiring systems
for great distances." The transformer on the pole works in both
directions, so the 120 or 240 volts you push out can come back up as
thousands of volts on lines a crew believes are dead (our reading of
both). The bare pins of a double-plug cord are also live in your hand as
soon as the generator end goes in (general practice). Ready.gov's
evacuation advice is simply never to connect a generator to your home's
electrical system; the exception OSHA allows is the next one.

### A transfer switch, installed by an electrician

OSHA's rule for a generator feeding a building: "Never attach a generator
directly to the electrical system of a structure (home, office, trailer,
etc.) unless a qualified electrician has properly installed the generator
with a transfer switch." Its fact sheet on grounding adds that the switch
must be approved for the purpose and installed by a qualified electrician
following the maker's instructions, and calls for an "open-transition"
switch.

What that means in practice (our explanation; Clark Public Utilities, a
Washington public utility, describes the same thing): the switch
disconnects the house from the utility before it connects the house to
the generator, so the two can never be joined, and the generator then
feeds only the circuits chosen when the switch was installed. A
generator plugs into it through an inlet box outside, using a cord made
for the job (general practice).

**Interlocks.** A cheaper device does the same job at the main panel: a
mechanical plate, made for that model of panel, that lets either the
main breaker or the generator's breaker be on, never both (general
practice). It is still new wiring, it still needs the inlet, and it is
still an electrician's job under a permit (general practice).

**Standby generators** come with an automatic transfer switch, which does
the same disconnecting by itself (general practice).

**In Washington,** connecting a generator to a house is electrical work
under the state's electrical law, with a permit and an inspection, and
[Where Your Own Electrical Work Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops)
explains who may do it and how the permit works. Washington's rules add
one detail for standby systems: the warning sign the national electrical
code requires for an optional standby system must also be placed at the
meter base, unless the meter base is within sight of the place the code's
sign goes and within five feet of it (WAC 296-46B-702). Its purpose, as
we read it, is that whoever works on the meter learns there is a second
source of power.

## Running it

### Before the outage

- **Read the manual and run it once, outdoors, in daylight,** with the
  cords you will use and a load you know (general practice). The CPSC:
  "Regularly check and maintain your portable generator to ensure it
  will work properly when needed. Read and follow all labels,
  instructions and warnings on the generator and in the owner's manual."
  The routine maintenance, and what to do before it sits for a season,
  is in [Keeping Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working).
- **Write down the load list** from the arithmetic above, and which
  things take turns (general practice).
- **Fit the CO alarms and test them** (above).
- **Talk to your doctor if anyone depends on a powered medical device or
  refrigerated medicine.** Ready.gov: "Talk to your medical provider about
  a power outage plan for medical devices powered by electricity and
  refrigerated medicines."
- **Decide where the generator will stand,** 20 feet or more from every
  window, door and vent, before the night you need it (our reading).

### Starting it

Put it in place outside, start it with nothing plugged in, let it settle,
then plug in the biggest motor load and add the rest one at a time
(general practice). Ready.gov: "Always connect the generator to
appliances with heavy-duty extension cords."

### In the rain

Water and a generator together can electrocute you. Ready.gov: "Keep the
generator dry and protected from rain or flooding. Touching a wet
generator or devices connected to one can cause electrical shock." The
sources say how, and they do not quite agree, so here are all three:

- OSHA, writing for workers: "Keep a generator dry; do not use it in the
  rain or wet conditions. If needed, protect a generator with a canopy."
- The CPSC, if you must run it when it is wet outside: "Operate the
  generator under an open, canopy-like structure on a dry surface where
  water cannot reach it or puddle or drain under it", and dry your hands
  before you touch it.
- The CPSC again, in December 2025: follow the maker's instructions about
  shock hazards in bad weather, "which may include use of an NFPA-rated
  non-combustible generator tent or may advise to wait until rain or
  snow passes."

What none of them allows is moving it into the garage, the porch or the
carport to keep it dry. An open canopy means open: walls and closed sides
trap the exhaust, which is the reason for being outside at all (our
reading). Use the cover the generator's manual allows, or wait for the
rain to pass. A home-made roof of plywood or plastic sits over a hot
engine and muffler, and the tent the CPSC names is a non-combustible one
made for the purpose, not whatever is to hand (our reading). Never handle
the generator's electrical parts while you are wet or standing in water
(OSHA). Electrical equipment that has been under water must be
"thoroughly dried out and properly evaluated" before it is used (OSHA);
what a flood means for the house's own wiring and gas controls is in the
section on where your own work stops, below.

### Refuelling

Shut it off and let it cool first. The CPSC: "Before refueling the
generator, turn it off and let it cool down. Gasoline spilled on hot
engine parts could ignite." Ready.gov says the same. Because the engine
stays hot for a long time after it stops (OSHA, above), cool means cool to
the touch, not stopped for a minute (our reading). Fill it outdoors,
with nothing burning nearby (general practice), and if you spill, wipe it
up before you restart (general practice).

Fuel for it is never stored in the home. The CPSC's safety alert says
gasoline, propane, kerosene and other flammable liquids "should be stored
outside of living areas in properly-labeled, non-glass safety
containers", and adds: "Do not store them near a fuel-burning appliance,
such as a natural gas water heater in a garage." How to store it is in
[Fuels and Their Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards).

### At night

Night is the most dangerous time, because a sleeping person cannot
notice the symptoms (our reading of the CDC's warning above). If it runs
overnight, the alarms must be working, the generator must be where you
put it in daylight and not moved closer for convenience, and the windows
on its side of the house shut (general practice, from the CPSC's rules
above). Switching it off at bedtime removes the generator's carbon
monoxide risk for the night (our reading), but it costs the refrigerator
its cold.

A night is about twice the four hours Ready.gov gives a closed
refrigerator, so by morning the refrigerator may be at 40 F or more. A
full freezer, with its 48 hours, has time to spare (our reading of
Ready.gov's figures above). So before bed, move the food that spoils
first, such as meat, milk and leftovers, into the freezer or into a
cooler with ice, which Ready.gov suggests ("Use coolers with ice if
necessary"), and keep every door shut (general practice). Medicine that
must stay cold goes in the cooler too; Ready.gov says to ask your medical
provider how long yours can be kept warmer. In the morning, read the
refrigerator thermometer before anything else and apply Ready.gov's rule
above: anything that has been at 40 F or higher for two hours or more is
thrown away, and when nobody can say how long it has been that warm,
Ready.gov's answer is "When in doubt, throw it out!"

### When the mains come back

Ready.gov: "Turn off or disconnect appliances, equipment, or
electronics. Power may return with momentary surges or spikes that can
cause damage." Move the loads back to the mains, or put the transfer
switch back to the utility, as its instructions say; switch the generator
off; let it cool; and do whatever its manual says before it is stored
(general practice).

## Worked example: a winter windstorm near Silverdale

The project's home place is Silverdale, on the Kitsap Peninsula (see
[Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa)), and its real
record shows what an outage there does. NOAA's storm record, its Storm
Events Database, says the windstorm of 14 and 15 December 2006 knocked
out power to close to 1.5 million customers in western Washington. In
the forecast zone that covers Seattle and Bremerton it records eight
deaths from carbon monoxide poisoning after the storm, from
barbecues or generators used improperly or indoors, and 275 people
treated for CO poisoning at hospitals. In the same storm a man in
Spanaway died in a house fire apparently started by a candle used for
light, and a man in Gig Harbor was electrocuted walking near a downed
power line. The wind did not do most of the killing; what people did
during the outage did (our reading, with [The Hazards Where You
Live](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live)).

Now an illustration, with made-up details. A household near Silverdale
has a portable generator rated 3,500 watts running and 4,000 watts
starting, two 50-foot outdoor cords, and battery CO alarms on both
floors. A December storm takes the power out in the evening.

1. **Where.** Not the carport, which is tempting because it is dry. The
   generator goes on the gravel at the back of the lot, 30 feet from the
   house and from the neighbour's bedroom window, exhaust pointed away,
   with OSHA's 3 to 4 feet of clear space around and above it. It is
   raining, and its manual allows a non-combustible generator tent made
   for that model, so it runs under one; had the manual said to wait for
   the rain to pass, the household would have waited (the CPSC's advice
   to follow the maker's instructions for bad weather; our example). The
   cords come in on the far side of the house, away from the exhaust.
2. **What.** The refrigerator and the chest freezer, and the lamps,
   phone chargers and laptop on the second cord: 1,560 watts running by
   the table above, and 3,760 watts at the moment a refrigerator starts,
   both inside the generator's two ratings. The margin is small: if both
   compressors started at the same moment, the generator would be asked
   for 5,960 watts and could trip, which is the reason for the margin
   above (our arithmetic). The furnace cannot be plugged in, so the house
   is kept warm by closing off rooms and dressing warmly (see [Emergency
   Shelter](/library#emergency-shelter)).
3. **When.** It runs the refrigerator and freezer through the evening.
   At bedtime the household moves the meat, the milk and the leftovers
   into the chest freezer and a cooler with ice, switches the generator
   off, and keeps every door shut. First thing in the morning, before the
   generator starts again, someone reads the refrigerator thermometer.
   After a night that long it may well read 40 F or more, and then
   whatever easily spoiled food was left in the refrigerator goes,
   because nobody can say how long it was that warm (Ready.gov's rule,
   above; our example).
4. **Refuelling.** Once a day, generator off, an hour to cool, filled in
   daylight.
5. **Afterwards.** The household asks a licensed electrical contractor
   about an inlet and transfer switch so the furnace fan and the well
   pump can run next time. With those two loads added, the arithmetic
   above gives 5,460 starting watts, more than this generator's 4,000, so
   they would have to take turns with the refrigerator and the freezer,
   or the household buys a bigger generator (our arithmetic). The
   contractor pulls the permit from the Department of Labor and
   Industries, which issues the permits and inspects electrical work
   around Silverdale: its list of the cities that do their own, read 4
   October 2026, names none in Kitsap County (see [Where Your Own
   Electrical Work Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops)).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Any connection to the house wiring:** a transfer switch, an inlet
  box, a panel interlock, a standby generator's wiring. A licensed
  electrician, under a permit (OSHA's qualified electrician; Washington's
  rules in the electrical guide).
- **A standby generator's gas supply:** a licensed gas fitter, under a
  permit (general practice).
- **Anything that has been flooded:** OSHA says equipment that has been
  under water must be thoroughly dried and properly evaluated before use.
  For a flooded house, the CPSC says not to touch wet appliances that are
  still plugged in, and, before any appliance is used, to have a
  professional or the gas or electric company evaluate the home and
  replace every gas control valve, all the electrical wiring, and every
  circuit breaker and fuse that has been under water. Replaced, not
  checked.
- **Repairs to the generator's electrical parts:** the maker's service
  agent (general practice).
- **A CO alarm sounding, or anyone ill:** outside, then 911.
- **A downed line, or a line touched by anything:** the utility and 911;
  see [Where Your Own Electrical Work
  Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops).

Yours to do: choosing the size from your own load list, placing it, the
cords, the alarms, refuelling cold, and deciding what runs when.

## Traps

- **"Just in the garage, with the door up."** The CPSC's label says never
  inside a home or garage, even with doors and windows open.
- **"The carport is outside."** The CPSC names porches and carports as too
  close.
- **"It has a CO shut-off, so it's fine anywhere."** It senses the air
  around the generator, not the air in your bedroom.
- **"A double-ended cord into the dryer outlet will run the whole
  house."** That is backfeeding, and it can kill a line worker a long way
  down the line.
- **"Topping it up while it runs saves time."** Fuel on a hot engine can
  ignite.
- **"I'll bring it in out of the rain."** Use a cover its manual allows,
  with no walls round it, or wait.
- **"I'll knock up a little plywood roof over it."** That is a wooden roof
  over a hot engine. Use the non-combustible tent the manual allows, or
  wait.
- **"The fridge was off all night, but it still feels cold."** Read the
  thermometer. At 40 F or more for two hours, the food goes.
- **"The microwave's only a minute."** The refrigerator can start during
  that minute. Switch both the refrigerator and the freezer off first, or
  buy bigger.
- **"Bigger is always better."** A generator far bigger than the loads
  burns more fuel and is harder to move; one too small trips or
  overheats. Size it by the list (general practice).
- **"Plug-in CO alarm, so it's covered."** Not with the power out, unless
  it has a battery backup.

## How the game models it

The homes in the game have a backup generator, and it is honest about
one thing: a generator is a backstop, not a source.

- **The machine.** Both of the game's homes have one "Generator
  (backup)" in the Plant room: `generator_portable` in
  `data/machines/home.ron` and `data/machines/home_solo.ron`, modelled on
  a small inverter generator that supplies 1,800 watts while it runs on
  about 1.1 litres of fuel an hour (the data file records the maker's
  manual it took the figures from). The Construction page lists it as a
  backstop generator, with the watts it supplies while it runs and the
  fuel it burns an hour.
- **It runs only when it has to.** Under Settings > Gameplay > Ship life
  support, the default, Station-supplied, ties the home to the ship's
  reactor, which covers whatever shortfall the home's own sources and
  batteries leave, so the generator never starts and never burns fuel.
  In the Realistic setting it starts only
  when the home's own sources fall short of the load, its batteries are
  below a quarter full, and its drum holds fuel, and it burns that fuel
  from its own 200-litre drum while it runs; an empty drum means no power
  (`src/systems/electrical.rs`). The play mode makes no difference to
  this.
- **Overload is handled for you.** When the power on a circuit is less
  than its load, the game switches loads off, the optional ones before the
  critical ones ([Batteries and Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage) has the
  detail). Real generators do not do that; you do it, by the load list.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the
game's generator stands indoors, in the Plant room, and nothing comes out
of it, no exhaust, no carbon monoxide and no noise that anything reacts
to. A real one there would poison the household. There are also no
starting surges, no extension cords, no transfer switch, no backfeeding,
no hot engine, and no fire risk in its fuel.

## You own this when

- You know that carbon monoxide is what kills most people around
  generators, and that sleeping people can die before they feel it.
- You would never run one inside a home, garage, basement, crawlspace or
  shed, or on a porch or in a carport, whatever is open.
- You place it at least 20 feet from every window, door, vent and
  attached garage, with the exhaust pointed away from every building,
  yours and your neighbour's.
- The home has battery or battery-backup CO alarms on every level and
  outside sleeping areas, tested monthly, and you know to get out and
  call 911 when one sounds.
- You can size a generator from a load list: running total, plus the
  biggest starting surge, plus a margin.
- You run the refrigerator and freezer in turns with a thermometer, and
  heat the house without an electric heater on the generator.
- If you switch it off for the night, you move the food that spoils first
  into the freezer or a cooler with ice, and read the thermometer before
  anything else in the morning.
- You use heavy-duty outdoor cords rated above their load, and never a
  cord into a wall outlet.
- You know a transfer switch or interlock is an electrician's job under a
  permit.
- You refuel only with the engine off and cool, and keep it dry under a
  cover its manual allows, or wait out the rain, never by moving it
  indoors.
- You would not touch a wet appliance that is still plugged in, and after
  a flood you would have a professional or the utility replace what was
  under water before anything is used.
