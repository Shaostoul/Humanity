# Framing a Building

Framing is the skeleton of a wood building: the sills, joists, studs,
plates, headers and rafters that the floors, walls and roof are hung on.
A wood-frame house, shed or garage is built this way. It is a skill a careful beginner can learn, with
cheap lumber, ordinary tools and a lot of nails, and it is also the
skill where a small mistake can hide behind the drywall for twenty years
and then show up as a crack, a sag or a door that will not close.

This guide is about how a frame goes together and why each piece is
where it is. It follows the US Army's carpentry manual (FM 5-426, 1995)
for the method, the Forest Products Laboratory's *Wood Handbook* for the
lumber, the Department of Energy's Building America Solution Center for
the connections that tie a frame together, the joint National Institute
for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) and Occupational Safety and
Health Administration (OSHA) guide to nail guns and OSHA's rules for its
safety, and a guide prepared for the Department of Housing and Urban
Development (HUD) for what goes wrong in old frames. All are United
States government publications. Where something is arithmetic, general
practice or our own reading, the text says so.

Read [How a Building Stands Up](/library#how-a-building-stands-up) first if
you have not: framing is the load path built in wood, and this guide
assumes you know what that means. [Foundations and
Ground](/library#foundations-and-ground) is what the frame stands on, and
[Square, Level and Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb) is how to get it
straight. [Working with Wood](/library#working-with-wood) explains
why boards shrink, cup and split, and [Nails, Screws and
Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts) covers the fasteners.

## First: where framing hurts people

### Nail guns

In house building, framing and sheathing are where most nail gun
injuries happen. The NIOSH and OSHA guide
*Nail Gun Safety: A Guide for Construction Contractors* (2011) says
nail guns cause an estimated 37,000 emergency room visits a year, and
that in residential construction "About two-thirds of these injuries
occur in framing and sheathing work." It concentrates on framing nailers
because "they fire the largest nails, are the most powerful, and are
considered to be the most dangerous to use."

Its first rule is about the trigger. "The full sequential trigger is
always the safest trigger mechanism for the job": it fires only when the
safety contact tip is pushed into the work first and the trigger is
squeezed second, and both must be released before the next nail.
The guide reports that studies of residential carpenters found the risk
of a nail gun injury "twice as high when using contact trigger nail guns
compared to using sequential trigger nail guns", and it names framing
jobs, "building walls and nailing blocking, fastening studs to plates
and blocks to studs, and installing trusses", as work that should at a
minimum have full sequential triggers. Its other rules include keeping
your hands at least 12 inches (30 cm) from the nailing point, or using
clamps instead; shooting away from your body and from other people; and
disconnecting the air whenever you leave the nailer, go up or down a
ladder or stairs, pass it to someone, clear a jam or do any maintenance. The
guide's full list, and the reasons for each rule, are in [Nails, Screws
and Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts); read it before you pick up a
nailer.

### Falls

Framing puts you on top of walls, on open floor decks, beside stair
openings and on roofs. OSHA's construction rule (29 CFR 1926.501(b)(13))
requires each worker in residential construction "6 feet (1.8 m) or more
above lower levels" to be protected by a guardrail, a safety net or a
personal fall arrest system. Treat that height as yours too: cover or
rail floor openings as soon as they are framed, and do not walk the tops
of walls (general practice). The ladder rules are in [Keeping Things
Working](/library#keeping-things-working).

### Raising walls

A framed wall is heavy and catches the wind like a sail. Raise it with
enough people, stand it on the deck with its bottom held so it cannot
slide off the edge, and brace it the moment it stands (general
practice). The Army manual's own order is to plumb the corner posts and
brace them temporarily before the walls between them are straightened
and held with temporary braces, and then the permanent bracing and the
rafters go on. Its caution for concrete forms applies to wall frames as
well: "Do not raise large form panels in heavy gusts of wind." (Applying
it to wall frames is our reading.)

### Saws and other tools

The circular saw and the other tools that cut framing lumber are covered
in [Working with Wood](/library#working-with-wood), and hand tools in
[Hand Tools](/library#hand-tools). Wear eye protection and hearing
protection while framing (general practice; the nail gun guide asks for
both).

### Cutting into a frame that is already there

Before you cut a notch, a hole or a piece out of an existing joist,
rafter, stud, beam or truss, read the first section of [How a Building
Stands Up](/library#how-a-building-stands-up). HUD's inspection guide lists
cut and drilled members, removed supporting walls and altered trusses
among the causes of sagging floors and spreading roofs (restated).

## Lumber: what you are buying

### Sizes

Framing lumber is sold by names that are not its size. The Army manual
puts it plainly: "the familiar quoted size 2 x 4 is the rough or nominal
dimension, but the actual dressed size is 1 1/2 x 3 1/2 inches." The
*Wood Handbook*'s table of standard sizes (FPL-GTR-282, 2021) gives the
dry, dressed sizes that the American Softwood Lumber Standard (PS 20)
sets for dimension lumber:

| Called | Actual (inches) | Actual (millimetres) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 x 4 | 1 1/2 x 3 1/2 | 38 x 89 |
| 2 x 6 | 1 1/2 x 5 1/2 | 38 x 140 |
| 2 x 8 | 1 1/2 x 7 1/4 | 38 x 184 |
| 2 x 10 | 1 1/2 x 9 1/4 | 38 x 235 |
| 2 x 12 | 1 1/2 x 11 1/4 | 38 x 286 |

The handbook adds that length, unlike width and thickness, is given as
the real length, and that most construction lumber comes in lengths
that are multiples of 2 feet (restated). Plan with the actual sizes: a
wall of 2 x 4 studs is 3 1/2 inches thick before anything is added to
it, not 4 (our arithmetic).

### Dryness

Wood shrinks as it dries, mostly across its width and thickness, so a
frame built from wet lumber can twist and open gaps as it dries in place
(general practice; [Working with Wood](/library#working-with-wood)
explains the shrinkage). The *Wood Handbook*
says dry boards and dimension lumber are lumber dried to a maximum
moisture content of 19 percent; its list of abbreviations reads
"S-DRY" as surfaced dry, lumber at 19 percent moisture content, and
"S-GRN" as surfaced green, unseasoned and over 19 percent, with "KD"
for kiln dried (restated). For framing, look for a stamp saying dry or
kiln dried (general practice). The Army manual says built-up and solid
girders should be of seasoned wood, and warns: "Precautions must be
taken to avoid or counteract any future settling or shrinking, which
would cause distortion of the building."

### The grade stamp

Structural lumber is graded, and the stamp on it tells you what you
have. The *Wood Handbook* (restated) says each piece of softwood lumber
made under the American Softwood Lumber Standard may be stamped with
its grade, its species, the mill that made it, how dry it was when it
was planed, and the mark of the agency that supervised the grading. It
adds two warnings worth keeping: the stamp shows the quality when the
piece was graded, and bad storage, poor drying or careless handling can
leave it below that grade later; and cutting or reworking a graded piece
can change its grade.

The Army manual's rule for framing is "No. 2 common lumber is used for
framing". Grading sorts wood by the defects that weaken it. The
handbook's chapter on stress grades (restated) lists knots, slope of
grain, checks and splits, shake, density, decay, and wane among the
features graders judge; it says knots weaken wood more in tension than
in compression, and that decay is prohibited or severely restricted in
structural grades because its effect on strength is often greater than
it looks. Choosing a straight board at the yard is in [Working with
Wood](/library#working-with-wood).

### Board feet

Lumber is often priced by the board foot, a piece 1 foot by 1 foot by 1
inch. The Army manual's formula: board feet = number of pieces x
thickness (inches) x width (inches) x length (feet), divided by 12. For a
2 x 4 that is 8 feet long, 1 x 2 x 4 x 8 / 12 = 5.33 board feet (our
arithmetic, on the nominal size, which is how it is sold).

## Platform framing, from the bottom up

The Army manual describes three kinds of light framing, and says the
platform (or western) frame "is preferred for one-story structures since
it permits both the bearing and nonbearing walls (which are supported by
the joist) to settle uniformly." In platform framing, each floor is
built as a deck, a platform, and the walls of the storey above are built
on it. It is the usual way to frame a house or a shed (general practice),
and it is what this section follows.

### The sill

The sill plate is the first wood on the foundation. The manual says it
"is anchored to the foundation wall for supporting and fastening
joists", that a thin bed of mortar or a sill sealer under it keeps air
out and gives it a solid base, and that insulation and a termite shield
can go under it. Wood on concrete needs a capillary break between them
to keep the concrete's moisture out of the wood ([Foundations and
Ground](/library#foundations-and-ground)), and anchor bolt sizes and spacing
come from the building code (general practice).

### Girders and posts

Where the floor is too wide for one joist to span, a girder (a beam)
runs down the middle on posts. The manual's rules:

- "The ends of wood girders should bear at least 4 inches on posts."
- A girder is better made deeper than wider; the reasons are in [How a
  Building Stands Up](/library#how-a-building-stands-up).
- A built-up girder, several pieces of framing lumber nailed together,
  must be nailed securely so the pieces cannot buckle on their own, with
  its joints staggered and no more than one joint in each span between
  supports.
- A post needs "a bearing plate at the top and bottom which distributes
  the load evenly across the column." Basement posts under girders sit on
  masonry footings, and posts are fastened at the top to what they carry
  and at the bottom to their footing.

### Floor joists

Joists are the parallel members, set on edge, that the floor is laid
on. From the manual:

- "Joists are spaced 16 or 24 inches apart, center to center" (406 or
  610 mm).
- "The joist is always placed crown up." The crown is the high side of
  the slight curve along a joist (general practice). The manual says
  placing it up "counteracts the weight on the joists", and that "In
  most cases there will be no sag below a straight line."
- "To support heavily concentrated loads or a partition wall, you may
  need to double the joist or place two joists together."
- Where a joist is notched to sit on a sill or girder, it "should not be
  notched to the sill and girder over one-third of its depth to prevent
  splitting".
- The manual says joists tend to twist from side to side, especially
  over a long span, so they are bridged with short pieces between them. Bridging stiffens the floor and "enables an
  overloaded joist to receive some help from the joists on either side
  of it." The manual's rule: "One line of bridging should be placed on
  joists more than 8 feet long. On joists more than 16 feet long, two
  lines should be used."
- A floor opening, for stairs or a chimney, is framed with doubled
  headers across the joists and doubled trimmers along them, so the
  joists that are cut short hand their load around the hole.
- "The subfloor is normally laid before the walls are framed so that it
  can be walked on while walls are being framed."

How long a joist of a given size may span is not in this guide, because
it depends on the species, grade, spacing and load. Your building
department will tell you which span table applies (general practice),
and the reasons the numbers fall so fast with length are in [How a
Building Stands Up](/library#how-a-building-stands-up). The manual's one rule
of thumb is a warning, not a table: 2 x 6 joists "are too small for
floors with spans over 10 feet".

## Walls

### The parts

The Army manual's wall is made of studs, plates, headers and braces:

- **Studs** are the upright pieces, "usually closely spaced, slender, 2
  x 4 vertical members", which "support the top plates and the upper
  part of the building". The manual says "The bearing strength of stud
  walls is determined by the strength of the studs."
- **Spacing.** "Studs are normally spaced 12, 16, or 24 inches on
  center, depending upon the outside and inside finish material." The
  spacing is set so that sheets of sheathing and wall board land on
  studs at their edges (general practice).
- **The bottom plate**, or sole plate, carries the bottom ends of the
  studs; the manual says it should be used in every case "as it helps
  distribute the load over a larger area."
- **The top plate** ties the tops of the studs together and carries the
  rafters or the floor above. "Double top plates are used to tie walls
  and partitions together", with their joints staggered and the upper
  plate lapped over the lower at the corners.
- **Corner posts** are "usually built up from three or more ordinary
  studs to provide greater strength", which also gives a nailing surface
  for the wall finish inside the corner. Where a partition meets an
  outside wall, a T-post does the same job.
- **Fire blocks** are among the parts the manual lists. Rules for where
  they go come from the building code (general practice).

### Bearing and nonbearing

The manual distinguishes two kinds of inside wall: "The bearing type
supports ceiling joists; the nonbearing type supports only itself, and
may be put in at any time after the other framework is installed." It
frames partitions the same way as outside walls. A bearing wall is part
of the load path; a nonbearing partition only divides space. How to tell them
apart in an existing house is in [How a Building Stands
Up](/library#how-a-building-stands-up).

### Openings: headers and the studs beside them

A door or window takes studs out of the wall, and the load they carried
has to go somewhere. The manual's method: "cut away the studs and for
equivalent strength, double the studs on each side of the opening to
form trimmers. Insert a header at the top. If the opening is wide, the
header should also be doubled and trussed." The short studs that hold up
the ends of the header, the trimmers or jack studs, stand on the bottom
plate beside the full-height studs. The Department of Energy's guide to
the load path puts the reason in one sentence: "Accumulated loads on
headers are transferred to the studs on the sides of the opening."

So a header is a small beam, and it is sized like one: the wider the
opening and the more it carries above it, the deeper it must be. Header
sizes for a bearing wall come from the building code's tables (general
practice).

### Bracing

A frame of studs and plates nailed at their ends is a row of rectangles,
and a rectangle can fold into a parallelogram under a sideways push. The
manual explains what bracing is for: "Bracing is used to stiffen framed
construction and make it rigid. Bracing may be used to resist winds,
storms, twists, or strains. Good bracing keeps corners square and plumb.
Bracing prevents warping, sagging, and shifting that could otherwise
distort the frame and cause badly fitting doors and windows and cracked
plaster." Its three kinds:

- **Let-in bracing**, a 1 x 4 or 1 x 6 set diagonally into notches cut
  in the edges of the studs, flush with the surface. "The studs are
  always cut to let in the braces; the braces are never cut."
- **Cut-in bracing**, 2 x 4 pieces cut at an angle and nailed between
  the studs in a diagonal line.
- **Diagonal sheathing.** "The strongest type of bracing is diagonal
  sheathing."

A triangle is the one shape that cannot fold without one of its sides
changing length, which is why every one of these methods adds a diagonal
(our explanation). Many walls today are braced by structural sheathing
panels (general practice), and the Department of Energy's guide notes that the residential
building code gives prescriptive details and requirements for braced
wall lines and braced wall panels for different wind speeds. How much of
a wall must be braced, and how, is a code question for your area
(general practice).

The manual also bridges walls, nailing short pieces between the studs
halfway up; it says this stiffens the wall and helps straighten the
studs, and that diagonal bridging is more effective than horizontal.

### Plumb and straight

The manual plumbs every corner post with a level or a plumb bob, braces
it, then stretches a line from corner to corner along the top plate and
braces the wall straight to it, using a block of the same thickness at
each end of the line to gauge the gap. The method is in [Square, Level
and Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb).

## Roofs

### Rafters and the walls they push on

The manual says "Rafters rest on the top wall plate" and "Rafters are
nailed to the plate, not framed into it." A pair of rafters leaning
against each other at the ridge pushes outward at the bottom, as a
stepladder's legs do when it is not opened right, and something has to
stop the walls being pushed apart (our explanation).

The manual's answer for small roofs is the collar tie, a board fastened
across a pair of rafters, of which it says: "This type of beam keeps the
building from spreading." It also says ceiling joists are nailed to both
the plates and the rafters where possible, which ties each pair of
rafters together at the bottom. HUD's inspection guide (restated)
describes what happens when that tying is missing: the roof spreads
down and outward, an uncommon but potentially serious problem, and it
lists missing collar beams, rafters and ceiling joists poorly tied
together at the eaves, and ceiling joists that act as tension members
from one side of the roof to the other but are not properly joined, as
things to look for. It adds that collar beams are sometimes removed when
an attic is turned into a room, which is how a roof that stood for
decades can start to spread (our reading).

The manual adds a warning for long rafters: "In long spans, the roof
would sag in the middle if it were not strengthened in some way."

### Trusses

A truss is a frame of short members joined so that each one is only
pulled or pushed along its length. The manual's definition: "A truss is
a framed or jointed structure composed of straight members connected
only at their intersections in such a way that if loads are applied at
these intersections, the stress in each member is in the direction of
its length." It adds: "The web members of a truss divide it into
triangles." That is what makes a truss light and strong, and also what
makes it unforgiving: every member and every joint is part of the
design, and HUD's guide lists cut, failed, removed or disconnected truss
members among the causes of a spreading roof (restated). Never cut,
notch, drill or remove any part of a truss, and do not hang new loads
from one, without the truss designer's or an engineer's say (general
practice).

### Holding the roof down

Wind lifts roofs, so the load path has to work upward as well as down.
The Department of Energy's guide lists the connections a continuous load
path needs: roof sheathing to framing, roof to wall, wall above to wall
below, and wall to foundation. A rafter that only rests on its plate,
however well it carries snow, is held down by little more than its
nails, and in high-wind areas metal connectors tie it to the wall
(general practice; which connectors your area requires is a code
question).

## Joints and fasteners in a frame

The Army manual's rules for joints, from [How a Building Stands
Up](/library#how-a-building-stands-up), apply to every piece of framing: weak
points "usually occur at the connections", the end grain "is the
weakest part of a piece of wood when used in joints", and "The lap joint
is the strongest joint." Framing is mostly butt joints held by nails, so
the nailing is what makes them strong, and the code gives a nailing
schedule, how many nails of what size for each connection (the
Department of Energy's guide refers to it; the schedule itself is a
code question for your area).

### Holes and notches for pipes and wires

Pipes, wires and ducts have to pass through a frame, and every hole and
notch takes wood out of a member. The *Wood Handbook* (restated) warns
that a notch, slit or hole with sharp inside corners concentrates stress
at the corners, so that even moderate loads can start a crack there that
runs along the grain, and it says it is generally more economical to
avoid sharp notches in wood beams altogether. It also explains that a
beam's bending stress is greatest at its top and bottom edges. HUD's
inspection guide (restated) tells inspectors to look especially for
holes cut at or near the top or bottom of joists, rafters and beams.

So, as our reading of both: keep holes in joists and rafters near the
middle of their depth, keep them small, keep them away from the ends
where the members bear, and cut notches in the edges as little as
possible. The building code sets exact limits on how large a hole or a
notch may be and where; ask for them before you cut (general practice).
A stud is weakened by holes too, and studs in bearing walls have their
own limits.

## Worked example: an 8 foot wall

You are framing a plain wall, 8 feet (96 inches) long, with 2 x 4 studs
at 16 inches on centre and a double top plate. This is arithmetic for
counting and ordering, not a design.

1. **Studs.** From one end, the studs fall at 0, 16, 32, 48, 64, 80 and
   96 inches: 96 / 16 = 6 spaces, so 7 studs. At 24 inch centres it
   would be 96 / 24 = 4 spaces, 5 studs (our arithmetic). Corners,
   T-posts and openings each add studs.
2. **Plates.** One bottom plate and two top plates, each 8 feet: three
   more 2 x 4 x 8s.
3. **Height.** Three plates take 3 x 1 1/2 = 4 1/2 inches. For the top
   of the wall to be 96 inches above the floor, the studs are 96 - 4 1/2
   = 91 1/2 inches long (our arithmetic).
4. **Order.** Ten 2 x 4 x 8s at 5.33 board feet each is about 53 board
   feet, before extras for corners, openings, waste and the boards you
   reject at the yard (our arithmetic; the extra is general practice).
5. **Add a door.** Take the door's rough opening width from the door
   you will hang. The studs that fall inside the opening come out; a
   full-height stud stays on each side, a trimmer (jack stud) is nailed
   inside each one to carry the header, a header goes across the top,
   and short cripple studs fill above the header to the plate at the
   usual spacing. The header size comes from the code's table for that
   width and that load (general practice).

### Joist spacing and size, as arithmetic

Each joist carries the strip of floor halfway to its neighbours on each
side, a strip as wide as the spacing. So joists at 24 inches carry 1.5
times the load of joists at 16 inches (our arithmetic). Going up one
size helps more than you might think: a 2 x 10 is (9 1/4 / 7 1/4)
squared, about 1.6 times as strong as a 2 x 8 of the same wood, and
cubed, about 2.1 times as stiff (our arithmetic from the formulas in How
a Building Stands Up). That is why span tables give each size and
spacing its own row.

## Know where your own work stops

Framing a shed, a playhouse or a partition wall is work you can learn.
These are past this guide:

- **Bearing walls, beams, headers in bearing walls, floor and roof
  framing for anything people live in.** Sizes come from the code's
  tables or an engineer; the building department will tell you which,
  and what needs a permit (general practice).
- **Changing any existing structural member**, and anything to do with
  trusses.
- **Anything in a high-wind or earthquake area beyond the code's
  prescriptive details.** The Department of Energy's guide says that in
  hurricane-prone regions a licensed structural engineer must determine
  the continuous load path connections.
- **Roof work**, if you are not sure of your footing, your ladder and
  your fall protection (general practice).

## Traps

- **Planning with nominal sizes.** A 2 x 4 is 3 1/2 inches wide.
- **Wet lumber.** It shrinks in place, and the frame moves with it.
- **Crown down.** A joist set crown down starts with its sag built in.
- **A cut brace.** Let-in bracing is the one piece you never cut.
- **Rafters with nothing holding the walls together.** Collar ties and
  ceiling joists tied to the rafters keep the walls from being pushed
  apart.
- **A hole at the edge of a joist.** The edges do the work.
- **The contact trigger.** Twice the injury risk of a full sequential
  trigger.

## How the game models it

The game's walls and roofs are not framed, and nothing in this guide is
practised in it.

- **Walls are single pieces.** A Wood Wall from the Crafting page is one
  piece 4 metres long, 3 metres high and 0.2 m thick, made from 6 Wood
  Planks and nothing else: no studs, plates, headers, braces or nails.
  A Wood Wall with Door takes 9 planks and has a 1 by 2.1 metre doorway,
  and a Wood Wall with Window takes 7 planks and a pane of glass for a 1
  by 1 metre window, without any header above either opening. The roof
  is a flat slab of 10 planks, with no rafters, trusses or ties
  (`data/blueprints/basic.ron`).
- **The Wood Plank is the only lumber the pieces use.** It is a generic
  sawn board
  (`data/items.csv`), cut from logs at a sawmill or by hand with the Saw
  Planks recipes (`data/recipes.csv`). There are no lumber sizes, grade
  stamps or moisture contents.
- **Nails are for furniture.** Nail Boxes are used in recipes for
  chairs, tables, beds and shelves, not in the building pieces.
- **Nothing is checked.** A wall stands with or without a foundation,
  and a roof stays up if its walls are taken down, because the game does
  not yet carry loads through what you build ([How a Building Stands
  Up](/library#how-a-building-stands-up) has the details). The squaring,
  plumbing and levelling the pieces do for you are described in [Square,
  Level and Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb).
- **Building trains you.** Finishing a piece gives experience in the
  Shelter Building skill (`src/systems/construction/mod.rs`).

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, crafting
  takes no materials, so sawing planks needs no logs, but building a
  wall from the Crafting page still takes its planks from your pack or
  the home's storage, and on a planet only from what you carry.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches, so
  what you built and the experience it earned start over at the next
  launch. Turn the setting off to keep them.

What to practise outside the game: all of it. Frame a small shed wall
on the ground with real 2 x 4s, check it square by its diagonals, raise
it, brace it and plumb it, and you will have learned more about framing
than any number of game walls can show.

## You own this when

- You use a full sequential trigger, keep your hands clear, and know the
  rest of the nail gun rules.
- You stay protected at heights of 6 feet and more, cover floor
  openings, and brace walls the moment they stand.
- You plan with actual sizes, buy dry lumber, and can read a grade
  stamp.
- You can name every piece of a platform frame and say what load it
  carries and where it hands it on.
- You set joists crown up, at the spacing the plan calls for, with
  bridging and doubled joists where the manual says.
- You can frame an opening with a header, trimmers and cripples, and
  you know the header is a beam.
- You can brace a wall, and you know why a diagonal is the brace.
- You know why rafters push walls apart and what stops them.
- You never cut a truss, and you keep holes in the middle of a joist's
  depth and within the code's limits.
- You get spans, header sizes, bracing and nailing from the code or an
  engineer, not from guesswork.
