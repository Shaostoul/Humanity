# Water and Wind Power

A stream that falls through your land, or a hilltop that is always
windy, looks like free electricity. Sometimes it is. Sometimes the
first honest measurement shows a trickle of power, or a good winter and
nothing at all in August. This guide is about finding out which, before
you spend anything: how to judge whether a site can make useful power
from moving water or moving air, what the measurements are, how the
arithmetic works, who has to say yes, and where these machines hurt
people.

It does not teach you to build a turbine or wire one into a house. A
water or wind system ends in electrical work, and often in work in a
stream or up a tower, and the guide says where your own part stops.

It leans on three other guides. [Batteries and
Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage) is where the power goes when you are
not using it. [Your First Solar Power](/library#first-solar-power) is a
small, plug-together first step, and explains watts and watt-hours.
[Moving Water Without Power](/library#moving-water-without-power) explains
head, pressure and pipes, which a water turbine depends on.

Its sources are the Department of Energy (DOE), the Bureau of
Reclamation, the US Geological Survey (USGS), the National Weather
Service, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the
Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) and the United States Code,
all public domain; a 1983 handbook written for the DOE by its contractor
EG&G Idaho, whose facts are restated here in our own words; a regional
climate centre's wind table; and Washington State law, also restated.
Where something is general practice rather than a published rule, the
text says so.

## First, where water and wind power hurt people

### Fast water, even shallow

Judging a stream means standing beside it, and often in it, to measure.
The National Weather Service's flood safety page is blunt about how
little moving water it takes: "A mere 6 inches of fast-moving flood
water can knock over an adult." Measure in the low-water season, never
in a flood; wade only where the water is shallow and slow and you can
see the bottom; have someone on the bank; and get out of cold water
before you are chilled (general practice; [Cold and
Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) explains the cold).

### Below a dam or a weir

Do not build a dam to make power without an engineer and the permits
below, and keep away from the downstream face of any small dam or weir.
The Bureau of Reclamation's report on public safety at such structures
opens with the reason: "Low-head hydraulic structures may create
dangerous currents, hydraulic forces, and other hazardous conditions to
someone trapped downstream of the structure". A low drop with smooth
water below it does not look dangerous, which is part of the danger
(general knowledge).

### Water under pressure in a pipe

A water turbine is fed by a pipe (the penstock) running down from where
the water is taken off, and every foot of drop adds pressure at the
bottom: the EPA's figure, used in [Moving Water Without
Power](/library#moving-water-without-power), is 0.433 pounds per square inch
for each foot. A drop of 100 feet gives about 43 psi at a closed valve
(arithmetic). That guide gives the NRCS rule for choosing pipe rated
well above the pressure it will carry.

Closing a valve on moving water is its own hazard. The 1983
microhydropower handbook written for the DOE warns that a valve shut
quickly sends a surge
of pressure up the pipe that can make it jump and, if high enough,
burst, causing damage or injury; that water pushing on a half-closed
butterfly valve can slam it the rest of the way and snatch the handle
out of the operator's hand; and that valves on a penstock are closed
slowly. Close every valve on a pipe like this slowly (general practice,
following the handbook).

### Things that turn

A water turbine, its belts and its shaft, and a wind turbine's rotor
are machines with no off switch you can see: they turn whenever the
water or the wind is moving. The 1983 handbook's safety list for a
small powerhouse includes guards on all belts and shafts, rails around
the water intake where a fall would be dangerous, locks on the valves
and the gate that lets water in, fences around the powerhouse and the
intake, a fire extinguisher rated for electrical fires, and a clearly
labelled emergency shutdown switch at the generator.

A water turbine can also run away. The handbook explains that when a
generator loses its load while the turbine still has its full flow and
head, the turbine and generator can speed up to two or three times
their normal speed, and that this is most serious at a high-head site.
That is why, when a turbine is started with its generator not yet
carrying a load, the handbook says never to open the inlet valve fully,
under any circumstances: the turbine would quickly reach runaway speed.
Open the inlet valve a little at a time, and keep everyone clear of the
machine while it comes up to speed (general practice, following the
handbook).

For wind, the DOE's Small Wind Guidebook notes: "Most turbines have
automatic overspeed-governing systems to keep the rotor from spinning
out of control in extremely high winds." That is a safety device, not
permission to go near a turning rotor. Stop and secure the machine the
way its manual says before working on it, and treat it as live until
it is (general practice; isolation is in [Working Out Why Something
Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke)).

### Towers and height

The DOE's guidebook says that, in general, the higher the tower, the
more power a wind system can produce. A tall tower means work at height
and a heavy object that can fall. OSHA's construction rule
requires protection from falling for each employee working on a surface
with an unprotected edge 6 feet or more above a lower level (29 CFR
1926.501(b)(1)). The rule binds employers; a fall from a tower is the
same for a homeowner (our reading). The DOE's guidebook lists the questions to ask before
installing a turbine yourself, among them: "Can I pour a proper cement
foundation?" and "Do I have access to a lift or a way to safely erect
the tower?" Its answer for anyone who says no to any of them: "you
should probably hire a system integrator or installer."

Three tower facts from the same guidebook are safety facts too.
Tilt-down towers let small turbines be lowered to the ground for
maintenance, and lowered ahead of hurricanes and other dangerous
weather. A guyed tower's wires reach out one-half to three-quarters of
the tower's height, which is the space it needs. And: "Aluminum towers
are prone to cracking and should be avoided."

While a tower is going up or coming down, keep everyone out of the
ground it could hit if it fell: a circle around its base whose radius
is at least the tower's full height plus the length of one blade, with
a margin beyond that. A falling tower reaches that far from its base,
so a circle only as wide as the tower is tall covers half the distance.
And raise it nowhere near overhead power lines (general practice;
[Keeping Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working) has the ladder
version of the power line rule).

### Electricity that does not switch off

A generator driven by a stream or the wind makes voltage whenever it
turns, so its wiring is never dead just because you have stopped using
the power (our reading).

Two more things to know (general practice). A battery bank is a
second source of power: stopping the turbine does not make the
batteries or their wiring safe, and [Batteries and
Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage) describes their own hazards. And a
small battery-charging turbine is generally meant to stay connected to
its batteries or controller whenever it turns, because the load is
part of what holds its speed and voltage down; disconnected while it
spins, it can overspeed and its voltage can rise. Do not disconnect its
batteries or controller while it turns unless its manual says you may:
stop it first the way the manual describes, and treat the maker's
manual for your turbine as the authority.

The 1983 handbook says the National Electrical
Code should govern everything from the generator's terminals to where
the power is used, that you should always get an electrical permit and
have the system inspected, that any electrician you hire should be
qualified to work with generators and protection equipment, and that
you should never connect anything to the utility's lines yourself: the
utility makes that connection.

The reason is the people working on those lines. The DOE's guidebook
says a utility's main concern is that a turbine automatically stops
sending power into its lines during an outage, because otherwise line
workers and the public, believing a line is dead, "might be hurt or
even killed by the power from your turbine." The CPSC makes the same
point about plugging a generator into a house: backfeeding presents
"an electrocution risk to utility workers and neighbors served by the
same utility transformer."

## Power is something you can measure

### Water: height times flow

The power in falling water depends on two things: how far it falls (the
head) and how much of it falls (the flow). The Bureau of Reclamation's
booklet on hydroelectric power: "More head or faster flowing water means
more power." Its formula, with flow in cubic feet per second (cfs) and
head in feet, gives the theoretical horsepower:

    theoretical horsepower = (flow in cfs x head in feet) / 8.8

and one horsepower is 746 watts. In metric units the same physics reads:

    theoretical watts = 9.81 x flow in litres per second x head in metres

(The two agree to within rounding; the metric line is the standard
physics of falling water, weight times height per second.)

No machine gets all of that. The 1983 handbook puts the efficiency of a
microhydropower system at 40 to 75 percent, depending on the site, the
equipment and the installation, and suggests 60 percent for a first
estimate, to be refined as you learn more about the site. Apply that 60
percent to the full drop, from the water surface where the water is
taken off to the water surface below the turbine (the handbook calls
this the pool-to-pool head): the handbook says the figure already
allows for the head a typical site loses at its intake and in its pipe,
so do not take the pipe loss off a second time. Friction in the penstock is part
of that loss; when you come to design the pipe itself, the handbook
suggests planning for 5 to 10 percent of the head to be lost in it as a
starting point.

Two consequences are worth seeing in numbers (arithmetic from the
formula):

- **Head and flow count equally.** Twice the drop with the same water,
  or twice the water with the same drop, doubles the power.
- **A little water falling a long way beats a lot of water falling a
  little.** A spring of 30 gallons a minute falling 100 feet has
  theoretically about the same power as 300 gallons a minute falling 10
  feet, and the high-head site needs a much smaller pipe and machine.

### Wind: the cube

The DOE's guidebook gives the power in the wind as

    power = Cp x 1/2 x air density x rotor swept area x wind speed^3

where Cp, the share a turbine actually captures, ranges from 0.25 to
0.45 for real machines against a theoretical maximum of 0.59. Its own
comment on the formula: "Notice that the wind speed (V) has an exponent
of 3 applied to it. This means that even a small increase in wind speed
results in a large increase in power."

In numbers (arithmetic): wind twice as fast carries eight times the
power; wind 20 percent faster carries about 73 percent more. That is
why the height of the tower and the exact site matter so much, and why
an average wind speed of 5 mph and one of 10 mph are not "half as good"
but an eighth.

For a first estimate of a year's output, the guidebook gives

    annual energy (kWh) = 0.01328 x (rotor diameter in feet)^2 x (average wind speed in mph)^3

and says the better figure comes from the maker's power curve applied to
your site's measured wind, adjusted for height and elevation.

## Judging a water site

### Measure the head

The simplest way, from the 1983 handbook, needs a garden hose and a
pressure gauge. Lay the hose along the stream from where the turbine
would sit, anchor its upper end under water facing across the current
(not into it, which reads high), let water run through until there is no
air in the hose, then fit the gauge to the lower end and read it with
the water still. Move the hose up the stream a length at a time,
measuring from the last mark, until you reach where the water would be
taken off. Add the readings and divide by 0.433 to get the head in feet.
The handbook suggests a gauge reading 0 to 10 psi, accurate to a
quarter psi or better, which means keeping each length to a drop of no
more than about 20 feet.

A level and a measuring staff, worked up the slope in steps, does the
same job; [Square, Level and Plumb](/library#square-level-and-plumb) covers
levelling.

### Measure the flow

- **A bucket and a watch.** Where all the water can be led into a
  container, at a small spring, a pipe or a culvert, time how long it
  takes to fill a container of known size. This is the 1983 handbook's
  container method. A 5-gallon bucket filled in 10 seconds is 30 gallons
  a minute (arithmetic).
- **A float.** On a bigger stream, the handbook's float method: pick a
  straight stretch at least 30 feet long with a smooth bed, measure the
  depth every foot or two across it at both ends to get the average
  cross-section, and time something floating down it, on a calm day.
  Water near the bed moves slower than at the surface, so the handbook
  multiplies the surface speed by 0.8 for a smooth bed or 0.6 for a rocky
  one. Flow is the cross-section times that corrected speed.
- **Several days, not one.** The handbook asks for measurements on at
  least four different days at different flows, several times a day, and
  a comparison with the nearest stream gauge so the short record can be
  stretched to a year.

### Design for the worst month

The 1983 handbook divides people building small hydro into two kinds:
those who want the most energy from the stream for the money, and those
who want to supply their own needs, with power for as much of the year
as possible. For the second kind, it says, the system is designed for
the minimum stream flow of the year, the low flow of late summer, so
that it keeps working when the stream is at its smallest. Where a flow
duration curve has been worked out for the stream (a graph of how often
each flow is equalled or exceeded), the handbook suggests taking the
flow that is equalled or exceeded 95 percent of the time as that
minimum, measured at your site rather than at the gauge. For a
household, that is the number that matters. A stream's average flow
tells you very little about August.

### Worked example: a spring on a hillside

The numbers here are invented, for practice. You lead a spring-fed brook
through a pipe into a bucket in late August, its lowest month, and it
fills a 5-gallon bucket in 10 seconds: 30 gallons a minute, about 1.9
litres a second. Your hose readings up the slope add up to 40 psi: 40 /
0.433 is about 92 feet of head, about 28 metres.

- Theoretical power: 9.81 x 1.9 x 28 is about 520 watts (or, in the
  Reclamation formula, 30 gallons a minute is 0.067 cfs, and 0.067 x 92 /
  8.8 x 746 is about 520 watts).
- At the handbook's first-guess 60 percent, applied to the full 92 feet:
  about 310 watts, steadily, day and night.
- Over a day: about 7.5 kWh. The DOE's guidebook puts a typical home's
  use at about 10,649 kWh a year, about 29 kWh a day, so this spring
  would cover about a quarter of an average home, every day of the year,
  if August is truly its lowest (all arithmetic from the figures above).
- The pipe: 92 feet of head is about 40 psi at a closed valve, before
  any surge from closing it.

That is a good small site. The same arithmetic shows how fast a site
loses its value without height: the same water falling 10 feet makes
about 34 watts.

### Worked example: a Kitsap creek through the year

The streams around Silverdale, Washington, the project's canonical
place (see [Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa)), show
why the worst month decides everything. The USGS gauged Chico Creek,
which drains 15.3 square miles near Bremerton, daily from July 1947 to
September 1950 and again from April 1961 to October 1974, 6,119 days in
all. Averaged over that record (our calculation from the USGS daily
values), its flow was about 92 cfs in January and about 2.0
cfs in August, roughly a forty-sixth as much, and its lowest daily
reading on record was zero. A turbine sized for the winter creek would
sit idle all summer; one sized for August would use a sliver of the
winter water.

### Who has to say yes

Taking water from a stream to make power needs several permissions.
Washington's version, restated from the statutes (read
2026-10-04), is the example; other states have their own.

- **A water right.** Anyone wanting to use or divert surface water must
  apply for and receive a permit before using or diverting it (RCW
  90.03.250). Leading water out of a stream to a turbine is diverting
  it, even if every drop goes back (our reading); [Where Water Comes
  From](/library#where-water-comes-from) explains how Washington's water
  rights work.
- **A permit to work in the stream.** Any construction or work that will
  use, divert, obstruct or change the natural flow or bed of the state's
  waters is a hydraulic project, and needs a permit from the Department
  of Fish and Wildlife before work starts, so that fish life is protected
  (RCW 77.55.011 and 77.55.021).
- **A fish screen.** Water may not be taken from a lake, river or stream
  with game fish unless the intake has a screen to keep fish out, and a
  way to return them if needed, with plans approved before it is built
  (RCW 77.57.070).
- **The federal government.** The Federal Power Act makes it unlawful to
  build or run a dam, water conduit, powerhouse or similar works for
  generating power on navigable waters, on federal public lands, or using
  surplus water from a government dam, without a federal licence. Anyone
  intending to build such works on other streams over which Congress has
  power to regulate commerce "shall before such construction file
  declaration of such intention with the Commission" (16 U.S.C. 817,
  section 23(b) of the Act). The Commission is the Federal Energy
  Regulatory Commission (general knowledge), and if it finds interstate
  commerce would be affected, a licence is needed before building.
- **Dams.** The 1983 handbook adds that a dam above a size each state
  sets needs a state dam safety permit. Its list of agencies is out of
  date; ask your state's water resources agency.

None of these is a reason not to look into a site. All of them are a
reason to ask before you build anything, because putting something in
a stream without them can mean taking it out again.

## Judging a wind site

### How windy is windy enough

The DOE's guidebook gives two rough thresholds. A turbine connected to
the grid can be practical where the average annual wind speed is at
least 10 mph (4.5 metres a second); an off-grid hybrid system, where it
is at least 9 mph (4 metres a second). It also describes how to find
out:

- **Airport and weather station data** are a rough screen only. They are
  usually measured at about 30 feet (10 metres), often far from your
  site and over different ground. Average wind at a typical hub height of
  80 feet may be 15 to 25 percent higher.
- **Wind maps** show general patterns, not your hilltop, and should match
  your tower height.
- **Trees** that have grown permanently bent by the wind ("flagging")
  are a clue to a windy site.
- **Measuring on the site** for at least a year, as near hub height as
  possible, is the reliable way, and several years are better.

### Height and obstacles

The guidebook's rule of thumb in its section on towers: "install a wind
turbine on a tower with the bottom of the rotor blades at least 30 feet
(9 meters) above any obstacle that is within 300 feet (90 meters) of the
tower." Its section on choosing a site asks for more: 30 feet above
anything within a 500-foot radius, upwind of buildings and trees, and
allowing for trees that have not reached their full height. Turbulence
near buildings, trees and ridges can cut a year's output by 15 to 25
percent.

It is cool about turbines on roofs: "all wind turbines vibrate and
transmit the vibration to the structure on which they are mounted",
which can mean noise inside the building, and the air over a roof is
turbulent, shortening the turbine's life and cutting its output.

And towers meet zoning: "Most zoning ordinances have a height limit of
35 feet." The local building or planning office is where to ask before
buying anything.

### Worked example: a small turbine at Silverdale

The nearest measured wind to Silverdale is at Bremerton's airport, about
11 miles away (project locale data). The Western Regional Climate Center's table of average
wind speeds, from hourly airport data for 1996 to 2006, gives it an
annual average of 5.3 mph, ranging from 4.6 mph in August to 6.3 mph in
March. That is about half of the DOE's 10 mph line for a grid-connected
turbine.

Using the guidebook's formula (all arithmetic from the figures above):

| Rotor diameter | Average wind 5.3 mph | Average wind 10 mph |
|---|---|---|
| 3.84 feet (1.17 m, a small battery-charging turbine) | about 29 kWh a year | about 196 kWh a year |
| 10 feet | about 200 kWh a year | about 1,330 kWh a year |

Against the guidebook's typical home of about 10,649 kWh a year, the
small turbine at Silverdale's airport wind would supply about a quarter
of one percent. Putting it higher, where the wind might be a fifth
faster, would raise that by about 70 percent, which is still very
little. A ridge or an open shoreline nearby could be much windier than
the airport; only measuring there would tell.

## Connecting it to anything

The guidebook describes two ways a home uses wind power, and a water
turbine fits the same two (our reading):

- **Off the grid,** the turbine charges batteries through a charge
  controller, and the house runs from the batteries; [Batteries and
  Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage) is about that half. The guidebook
  recommends hybrids for most off-grid homes: in much of the country the
  wind is weak in summer, when the sun is strongest, and strong in
  winter, so wind and solar panels together, with batteries and often a
  fuel generator as backstop, make power more of the time.
- **On the grid,** an inverter makes the turbine's power match the
  utility's, and the utility credits what you send it. The guidebook
  says this needs the utility's permission, usually an interconnection
  agreement, that utilities typically require equipment meeting the IEEE
  1547 standard, and that federal law (the Public Utility Regulatory
  Policies Act of 1978) requires utilities to connect with and buy power
  from small wind systems.

Either way, the guidebook expects the installation to follow the
electrical code your local government enforces, usually based on the
National Electrical Code, which has sections for small wind systems.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Measuring head, flow and wind** is yours, carefully, in low water.
- **Anything in a stream** (an intake, a diversion, a screen) waits for
  the permits above, and a dam needs an engineer as well.
- **A penstock with a big drop** is a pressure system; size it with the
  maker of the turbine or an engineer (general practice).
- **Raising a tower and working on it** belongs to someone trained and
  equipped for work at height, unless you can honestly answer yes to the
  DOE's questions.
- **The wiring,** from the generator to the batteries, the house and
  above all the utility's lines, is for a licensed electrician who knows
  generators, with a permit and an inspection. The utility connects to
  its own lines.

## Traps

- **The nameplate.** A turbine's rated watts are what it makes at a wind
  speed or flow you may never have. What matters is a year of energy at
  your site.
- **The average month.** A stream sized for its average flow fails in
  August. Design for the lowest flow you are prepared to live with.
- **The airport number.** It is a rough screen, measured low and
  somewhere else.
- **The roof.** Vibration, noise and turbulence, by the DOE's account.
- **The permit you did not ask about.** Water rights, stream permits,
  fish screens, federal licensing and zoning can each stop a project
  after the money is spent.
- **A pipe with no surge plan.** Closing a valve fast on a long drop can
  burst the pipe.
- **A dead line that is not dead.** A turning generator is live, and so
  is anything it feeds, and so is the battery bank after the turbine
  stops.
- **A turbine with no load.** A water turbine with its valve wide open
  and nothing to drive runs away; a small wind turbine cut off from its
  batteries can overspeed.

## How the game models it

The game has wind power, simplified, and no water power.

- **One small wind turbine per home.** Both shipped homes carry a Wind
  turbine (`wind_turbine_small` in `data/machines/home.ron` and
  `home_solo.ron`), modelled on a real small battery-charging turbine: its
  data quotes the maker's figures of 160 watts at 28 mph (12.5 metres a
  second), a start-up wind of 7 mph and a rotor 1.17 metres across.
- **It makes what that turbine would make at Silverdale.** The game takes
  the Bremerton airport's 2.37 metres a second (5.3 mph) average, assumes
  the spread of wind speeds that small turbines are rated by, and gets
  about 4.4 watts on average, 0.11 kWh a day. Its stat line in the game
  says so: "+0.11 kWh/d here: the wind averages 2.4 m/s, under the 3.1 it
  starts at". The calculation is in `src/machines.rs`, and a test there
  holds the data to it. That is about 40 kWh a year, the same size as the
  DOE formula's 29 kWh a year above.
- **It feeds its own circuit.** Like every generator in the game, it
  powers only the loads wired to the same circuit
  (`src/systems/electrical.rs`); [Batteries and
  Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage) describes the rest.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no wind in the game's power, so the turbine makes the same 4.4 watts
every hour, calm or gale, and its tower height, the obstacles around it
and the turbulence they make change nothing. There is no water turbine
at all: no stream can be used for power, so head, flow, the dry season
and the permits do not come up. And nothing turns, falls or shocks: no
tower to climb, no rotor to stop, no surge in a pipe, no utility line to
backfeed.

**Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
every session from the default home" is on, which is the default during
development, only your character carries between launches, so the
home's machines, the turbine and the batteries it charges start each
session as they are in the default home.

## You own this when

- You measure streams only in low water, never wade fast water, and keep
  away from the downstream face of dams and weirs.
- You close valves on a long pipe slowly, and you know why.
- You treat a turning generator, everything it feeds and its battery
  bank as live, you would not disconnect a turning turbine from its
  batteries or controller, and you would never connect anything to the
  utility's lines.
- You open a water turbine's inlet valve a little at a time and never
  fully with no load on the generator.
- You would not climb a tower without fall protection, or raise one near
  power lines or with anyone closer than its height plus a blade.
- You can measure head with a hose and a gauge and flow with a bucket or
  a float, and turn them into watts.
- You can explain why wind power goes with the cube of the speed, and
  estimate a year's output from a rotor size and an average wind.
- You size for the worst month, not the average.
- You can name the permits a stream project needs where you live, and
  you ask before you build.
- You know what your own site's wind and water could give, in kWh a
  year, before anyone sells you a machine.
