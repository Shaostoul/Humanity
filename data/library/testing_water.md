# Testing Water

The rest of the water run teaches you where water comes from, how to catch
it, how to keep it, and how to treat it. Every one of those guides makes a
decision for you, and every one of those decisions rests on an assumption
about what is in the water.

This guide is about replacing the assumption with a number.

It matters more here than in most subjects, because the things that make
water dangerous almost never announce themselves. A spring that runs clear,
cold and sweet can carry nitrate from a field two miles uphill, arsenic out
of the rock it flowed through, and the bacteria from a dead animal nobody
found. Not one of those changes how it looks, smells or tastes.
[Where Water Comes From](/library#where-water-comes-from) puts it as bluntly as it
can be put: your senses are a one-way alarm. If they go off, believe them.
If they stay quiet, you have learned almost nothing.

A test is the instrument that answers the quiet case. This guide is about
what that instrument actually measures, which is narrower than most people
think, and how to use it so the answer means something.

## What a test is, and what it is not

A drinking water test is a snapshot of one sample, drawn from one tap, on
one day, analysed for the specific things you paid somebody to look for.

Read that sentence again with each limit in mind, because all three are
real and all three are routinely forgotten.

**One sample, one day.** The result describes the water that came out of
that tap at that moment. It does not describe last week's water and it does
not promise anything about next month's. Contamination is not usually a
steady trickle. EPA's account of nonpoint source pollution is explicitly an
event mechanism: "NPS pollution is caused by rainfall or snowmelt moving
over and through the ground. As the runoff moves, it picks up and carries
away natural and human-made pollutants, finally depositing them into lakes,
rivers, wetlands, coastal waters and ground waters." Water that is clean in
August can be contaminated in November, by the same well, with nothing
about the well having changed.

**One tap.** Some of what a test finds was in the ground before you owned
the place. Some of it was added by the pipe between the well and the glass.
Those are different problems with different fixes, and which one you
measured depends entirely on how you drew the sample. This is the single
most misunderstood part of water testing and it gets its own section below.

**One list.** A laboratory reports what it was asked to analyse. Nothing
else. A sheet that says everything passed means everything on that sheet
passed. If arsenic was not on the list, the sheet is silent about arsenic,
and silence reads exactly like a pass to someone skimming it.

The consequence worth carrying out of this section is that **when you
sample is as much a decision as what you test for**, and most people never
make it consciously. They test when it is convenient. Convenient is usually
a dry week, because that is when you get around to errands, and a dry week
is when a well is at its cleanest.

## When to sample

Three kinds of timing, in order of how much they change the answer.

**The routine one.** Once a year, as a habit, ideally at the same time each
year so the results are comparable. Pick the part of the year when your
water is most likely to be bad rather than least, which in a wet-winter
climate means during or just after the wet season rather than in a dry
August. That last point is a rule of thumb rather than an agency
recommendation, but it follows directly from EPA's rainfall and snowmelt
mechanism above.

**The trigger ones.** CDC and EPA give nearly the same list, and every item
on it is a day when nobody is thinking about their water. Test again,
outside the annual cycle, when:

- you are notified that there are problems with well water in your area;
- there are problems near your well, such as flooding, land disturbances,
  or nearby waste disposal sites, or, in EPA's wording, new construction or
  industrial activity;
- you replace or repair any part of your well system;
- you notice a change in your water's taste, colour or smell;
- you or someone you live with becomes pregnant;
- a child starts living with you.

Note what the last two have in common with the first four. Nothing about
the water changed. What changed is who is drinking it, and for nitrate and
lead that is the whole risk calculation. EPA adds the same thought for
frequency: you may want to test more often if small children or elderly
adults live in the house, or if someone is pregnant or nursing.

**The after-something-happened one.** If the well has been flooded or
disinfected, the timing is specific. CDC's guidance after a disaster is not
to judge by eye: "After a disaster, it is safest to drink bottled water
until you are certain that your water is free of contaminants and safe to
drink." Sample "for total coliform and either *E. coli* or fecal coliform
bacteria to confirm that the water is safe to drink." And if you have shock
chlorinated the well, wait: "Wait at least 7 to 10 days to test the water
after disinfection to ensure that the chlorine has been thoroughly flushed
from the system." Testing a chlorinated well tells you the chlorine worked
that afternoon. It tells you nothing about whether the contamination comes
back.

## The annual test for a private well

If you are on a public supply, most of this is being done for you and the
results arrive in your Consumer Confidence Report once a year. If you are
on a well, CDC's position is the whole story: "if you get your water from a
private well, you are responsible for making sure it is safe to drink."
Private systems, CDC says, are "not regulated or monitored by officials,"
and that covers rainwater collection systems too. USGS says the same thing
about domestic wells: "The quality and safety of water from domestic wells
are not regulated by the Federal Safe Drinking Water Act or, in most cases,
by state laws. Instead, individual homeowners are responsible for
maintaining their domestic well systems and for monitoring water" quality.

CDC and EPA give an identical baseline, which is unusual and makes it easy
to remember. CDC: "Test your well water at least once every year for total
coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH level." EPA:
"Test your private well annually for total coliform bacteria, nitrates,
total dissolved solids, and pH levels."

Four things. Here is what each is actually for.

| Test | What it answers | Why it is on the annual list |
|---|---|---|
| **Total coliform bacteria** | Is there a path from the surface into my water? | The cheapest early warning that the well's seal, casing or setting has been breached |
| **Nitrate** | Is fertilizer, sewage or animal waste reaching the aquifer? | The one contaminant on this list that kills people, specifically infants, and it has no taste |
| **Total dissolved solids** | How much dissolved mineral is in this water, in total? | A single number that moves when almost anything changes, including salt |
| **pH** | Is this water acidic or alkaline? | Acidic water corrodes plumbing, which is how lead and copper get into it |

Two of those are direct health questions and two are sentinels. TDS and pH
rarely hurt anybody at the levels a household well produces; they are on
the list because they change when something else changes, and they are
cheap.

That baseline is the floor, not the ceiling. CDC's own qualifier is the
important half: "Contact your health department to find out what other
germs or chemicals to test for based on where you live." The extras that
come up most often are arsenic, lead, radon, volatile organic compounds,
pesticides and herbicides, and which of them apply to you is a local
geology and local land use question, not a general one. Your county or
state health department can usually answer it in one phone call, and the
answer is normally a short and surprising list.

## Coliform and E. coli: the indicator is not the pathogen

This is the most misread result on a water report, in both directions.

Total coliforms are a family of bacteria that are mostly harmless. EPA is
explicit: "Total coliforms are a group of related bacteria that are (with
few exceptions) not harmful to humans." They are measured because of what
their presence implies. "Total coliforms are used to determine the adequacy
of water treatment and the integrity of the distribution system," and "EPA
considers total coliforms a useful indicator of other pathogens for
drinking water."

So a coliform positive is not a diagnosis. **It says a path exists between
the outside world and your water. It does not say what came down that
path.** Something got in; the test does not know whether that something was
soil bacteria from a cracked well cap or sewage from a failed drainfield up
the hill.

*E. coli* narrows it considerably. EPA describes *E. coli* and fecal
coliforms as "types of coliform bacteria that are directly associated with
fresh feces." In a public system a positive *E. coli* result "can signify
an acute MCL violation, which necessitates rapid state and public
notification because it represents a direct health risk." Faecal matter has
reached the water, recently. That is the result that means stop drinking it
today, not at the weekend.

EPA's health goal for both is zero. The MCLG, the level "below which there
is no known or expected risk to health," is zero for total coliforms and
zero for *E. coli*. The compliance number you may see quoted for public
systems, an MCL of 5.0 percent of monthly samples positive, is a
system-level standard for a utility taking dozens of samples a month. It is
not a household allowance. For a single well with a single sample, read the
result as present or absent.

Two errors to avoid at this point. The first is treating a coliform
positive as proof of illness, which it is not; the second, far more common
and far worse, is treating a coliform negative as proof that the water is
safe. A clean coliform result says nothing at all about nitrate, arsenic,
lead, solvents or pesticides, because those are not organisms and the test
was not looking for them.

## Nitrate: the one with a body count

Nitrate deserves its own section because it is invisible, it is common
wherever people farm or use septic systems, no home treatment in the
companion guide touches it, and the people it kills cannot tell you they
feel unwell.

EPA's federal limit, the MCL, is **10 mg/L, measured as nitrogen**. The
MCLG, the non-enforceable health goal, is also 10 mg/L, and the two matching
is worth noticing. EPA sets an MCL "as close to MCLGs as feasible using the
best available treatment technology and taking cost into consideration," so
for many contaminants the enforceable limit lands above the health goal.
Arsenic, in the next section, is the clear case: its goal is zero and its
limit is not. For nitrate the two coincide, which means the legal line and
the health line are the same line.

EPA's stated health effect, in full: "Infants below the age of six months
who drink water containing nitrate in excess of the MCL could become
seriously ill and, if untreated, may die. Symptoms include shortness of
breath and blue-baby syndrome." EPA's private well material gives the
mechanism: nitrate is converted to nitrites in the body, producing
methemoglobinemia, with "shortness of breath and blueness of the skin" in
infants under six months.

EPA gives nitrate's sources as "Runoff from fertilizer use; leaking from
septic tanks, sewage; erosion of natural deposits." Read that list against
your own upstream. Row crops, pasture, a neighbour's drainfield and your
own drainfield are all on it.

Nitrite, the related compound, has an MCL of 1 mg/L, also measured as
nitrogen.

**The units trap.** EPA's contaminant is named "Nitrate (measured as
Nitrogen)," and that phrase is load-bearing. A laboratory may report nitrate
on either of two bases: as nitrogen, or as the whole nitrate ion. The two
numbers describe the same water and are not the same number. The federal
limit of 10 is stated on the nitrogen basis. If your sheet does not say "as
N" or "as nitrogen" next to the nitrate row, do not compare it to 10 until
you have asked the laboratory which basis they used. This is the single
most likely way to misread a water report in a direction that matters.

**And the thing nitrate shares with arsenic and lead.** No taste, no smell,
no colour. It is not removed by boiling, filtering through a camping
filter, or chlorinating, and boiling actively concentrates it, because the
water leaves as steam and the nitrate stays in the pot.
[Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) has the full
account of what home treatment does and does not reach; nitrate sits
squarely in the category it calls chemicals, the one nothing in the
household kit fixes.

## Arsenic, lead and the metals, and the question you are actually asking

Metals split cleanly into two groups, and the split is not chemical. It is
about where they joined the water.

**What the ground handed you.** Arsenic is the archetype. USGS: "Arsenic
occurs naturally as a trace component in many rocks and sediments," and it
"can be released into groundwater as a result of human activities, such as
mining, and from its various uses in industry, in animal feed, as a wood
preservative, and as a pesticide." EPA's version of the same point lists
arsenic's sources as "Erosion of natural deposits; runoff from orchards,
runoff from glass and electronics production wastes."

Arsenic's MCL is **0.010 mg/L**, in force since 2006. Its MCLG is **0**,
meaning EPA names no level at which it expects no risk. The enforceable
limit sits above that goal for the reason EPA gives in its own definition:
an MCL is set "as close to MCLGs as feasible using the best available
treatment technology and taking cost into consideration." So a result of
0.008 mg/L is legal and is not the same as safe, and the sheet will not say
so. EPA's health effect is "Skin damage or problems with circulatory
systems, and may have increased risk of getting cancer."

How common is it? USGS found arsenic above the MCL in about **7 percent**
of sampled drinking water wells nationwide, and about **16 percent** in the
Southwest, with arsenic "detected more frequently and at higher
concentrations in wells in the West than in the East." USGS estimates about
**2.1 million people** drink from private wells with arsenic above the
standard. Nobody is going to tell those households. Arsenic is not on the
annual four, so it gets found only when somebody asks for it.

**What your plumbing added.** Lead is the archetype on this side, and the
distinction is stated by EPA directly: "Lead can enter drinking water when
plumbing materials that contain lead corrode, especially where the water has
high acidity or low mineral content that corrodes pipes and fixtures." And:
"In homes with lead pipes that connect the home to the water main, also
known as lead services lines, these pipes are typically the most significant
source of lead in the water."

That is why pH is on the annual list. Corrosive water is not itself a health
problem; it is the mechanism that turns your own pipes into the
contaminant.

Lead's MCLG is **zero**. There is no MCL in the ordinary sense: lead is
regulated by treatment technique, with an action level of **0.015 mg/L**,
revised to **0.010 mg/L** effective 1 November 2027. An action level is a
trigger for a water system to act, not a safety line for a household, and
with an MCLG of zero there is no amount of lead that EPA describes as
harmless.

Three facts from EPA that people get wrong:

- "Since you cannot see, taste, or smell lead dissolved in water, testing is
  the only sure way of telling whether there are harmful quantities of lead
  in your drinking water."
- "Use only cold water for drinking, cooking and making baby formula.
  Remember, boiling water does not remove lead from water."
- Testing "costs between $20 and $100," and "a list of certified
  laboratories are available from your state or local drinking water
  authority."

EPA's other metals of concern in well water come with the same message that
they arrive from both directions: household plumbing and service lines on
one hand, and "mining, petroleum refineries, electronics manufacturers,
municipal waste disposal, cement plants, natural mineral deposits" on the
other, with health effects EPA summarizes as "acute and chronic toxicity,
liver, kidney, and intestinal damage, anemia, and cancer."

### First draw versus flushed, which is the whole thing

Here is the part that most people get wrong, and getting it wrong does not
produce an error message. It produces a clean-looking result.

**To find out what is in your source**, you flush the line first. EPA's
general sampling procedure is to "Select a cold water faucet for sampling
which is free of contaminating devices such as screens, aeration devices,
hoses, purification devices or swiveled faucets," then "Open the faucet and
thoroughly flush. Generally 2 to 3 minutes will suffice, however longer
times may be needed, especially in the case of lead distribution lines.
Typically the water temperature will stabilize which indicates flushing is
completed." You are deliberately throwing away the water that sat in the
pipes, because you want the aquifer's water, not the plumbing's.

**To find out what your plumbing adds**, you do the exact opposite. EPA's
instruction for lead and copper compliance samples: "If you are collecting a
first-flush sample for lead/copper, allow the water to sit undisturbed for
at least six hours. DO NOT intentionally flush the water line before the
start of the 6 hour period. Place a wide-mouth 1 L bottle under the faucet.
Open the faucet and collect the first water out of the tap." And, contrary
to every other sample you will ever take: "DO NOT remove any screens or
aeration devices."

Same tap. Opposite procedures. Opposite questions.

| | Source sample | First-draw lead sample |
|---|---|---|
| Question | What is in the aquifer or the main? | What is my plumbing putting in? |
| Standing time | Irrelevant | At least 6 hours undisturbed |
| Flush first | Yes, 2 to 3 minutes, until temperature stabilizes | No, and do not flush before the 6 hours either |
| Aerator or screen | Remove it | Leave it on |
| Bottle | As the laboratory supplies | Wide-mouth 1 litre |
| What you collect | Water that has been moving | The very first water out |

The failure mode is obvious once you see it and invisible otherwise.
Somebody worried about lead runs the tap for two minutes because that is
what you do before drawing water, fills the bottle, and gets a low number
back. They have measured the water in the main, which was never the
question. The lead is in the last few metres of pipe and the fixture, and
they flushed it down the drain before sampling.

Two further details from the same EPA guidance. Choose a tap "free from
devices that are designed to change the water composition, such as water
softeners or point of use filters," and "Use the bathroom tap if the kitchen
tap has a water softener or point of use filter on it." And if you want to
locate lead in a service line rather than just detect it, "8 to 10
sequential sample bottles (the number depends on how far the lead service
line is from the tap) would be needed to see the profile of lead
distribution." Each bottle is a slice of pipe; the one that spikes tells you
which slice.

## Hardness, iron, manganese and the rest of the aesthetic list

Everything above this point has a health number attached. This section does
not, and being honest about that is the point of it.

EPA publishes a second set of values called secondary maximum contaminant
levels. They exist so that water is usable, not so that it is safe, and EPA
says so twice over: "EPA does not enforce these 'secondary maximum
contaminant levels' (SMCLs)," and "These contaminants are not considered to
present a risk to human health at the SMCL."

| Contaminant | SMCL | What you notice |
|---|---|---|
| Aluminum | 0.05 to 0.2 mg/L | |
| Chloride | 250 mg/L | salty taste |
| Color | 15 color units | |
| Copper | 1.0 mg/L | |
| Corrosivity | Non-corrosive | |
| Fluoride | 2.0 mg/L | |
| Foaming agents | 0.5 mg/L | |
| Iron | 0.3 mg/L | rusty colour, sediment, metallic taste, reddish or orange staining |
| Manganese | 0.05 mg/L | black to brown colour, black staining, bitter metallic taste |
| Odor | 3 threshold odor number | |
| pH | 6.5 to 8.5 | |
| Silver | 0.1 mg/L | |
| Sulfate | 250 mg/L | |
| Total dissolved solids | 500 mg/L | hardness, deposits, coloured water, staining, salty taste |
| Zinc | 5 mg/L | |

Hardness is not on that list because it is not a contaminant at all. USGS:
"The simple definition of water hardness is the amount of dissolved calcium
and magnesium in the water," and "Hardness is a property of water that is
not a health concern, but it can be a nuisance." The usual bands, in
milligrams per litre as calcium carbonate:

| Classification | Range |
|---|---|
| Soft | 0 to 60 mg/L |
| Moderately hard | 61 to 120 mg/L |
| Hard | 121 to 180 mg/L |
| Very hard | above 180 mg/L |

**Now the honest part, which cuts both ways.** A secondary exceedance is not
a health finding and should not be treated as one. Orange staining in the
bath is iron, and iron at 0.4 mg/L will not hurt anybody. Plenty of money
gets spent on treatment systems because a number on a sheet was above a
line, without anybody noticing that the line was an aesthetic one.

But the aesthetic parameters are also the cheapest clues you will ever buy,
because they move when the water changes. Three in particular:

- **Corrosivity and pH** point at lead and copper, for the reason in the
  previous section. Water that eats plumbing is a metals risk even when the
  metals test is clean today.
- **Chloride and TDS** point at salt, which on a coast means something
  specific. Next section.
- **A change** in any of them, against last year's sheet, is a signal that
  something in the system or the aquifer is different, and that is exactly
  the situation CDC names as a reason to test outside the annual cycle.

One more piece of vocabulary, since it appears on some reports. EPA also
issues health advisories, which "identify the concentration of a contaminant
in drinking water at which adverse health effects and/or aesthetic effects
are not anticipated to occur over specific exposure durations." These are
not standards: "HAs are not to be construed as legally enforceable federal
standards and are subject to change as new information becomes available."
So a report can carry three different kinds of line, and only one of them is
an enforceable health limit.

## Chloride and conductivity: the coastal early warning

If your well is anywhere near salt water, this is the section that matters
most, and it is the cheapest testing in the whole guide.

The mechanism is a pressure balance rather than a barrier. USGS: "Groundwater
pumping can reduce freshwater flow toward coastal areas and cause saltwater
to be drawn toward the freshwater zones of the aquifer." The boundary is not
a line but "a diffuse zone where freshwater and saltwater mix," called the
zone of dispersion or zone of transition.

The reason to watch it with instruments rather than with your tongue is that
chloride's secondary standard, 250 mg/L, is a **taste** threshold. By the
time water tastes salty it has been changing for a long time, and
[Where Water Goes](/library#where-water-goes) explains why that is a problem you
only get to have once: salt is dissolved through a body of rock, not sitting
in a spot, and turning the pump down stops it getting worse without making
it better on any timescale a household plans around. USGS lists the outcome
plainly: "Saltwater intrusion decreases freshwater storage in the aquifers,
and, in extreme cases, can result in the abandonment of wells."

So watch the trend, not the threshold. Washington's Department of Ecology
specifies exactly what to watch for wells under its coastal monitoring
conditions, and the specification is a good template anywhere on a coast:
measure **chloride**, **conductivity** and **static water level**, twice a
year, in April and September, with the chemical analysis done by a
state-accredited laboratory. Ecology reports that some coastal wells in
Washington are already unusable because of intrusion.

Three parameters, and each does a different job. Chloride is the direct
evidence of sea salt. Conductivity is the cheap proxy, because dissolved ions
are what make water conduct at all, and USGS notes that "even a small amount
of ions in a water solution makes it able to conduct electricity" and that
"the most common dissolved substances are salts, common table salt (sodium
chloride (NaCl)) is the one we know best." A conductivity meter therefore
responds to salt without being able to name it, which is exactly what a
screening instrument is for. Static water level is the cause
rather than the symptom: it tells you whether you are drawing the aquifer
down, which is what lets the salt move in the first place. April and
September bracket the wet and dry ends of the year, so the pair of readings
shows you the seasonal swing rather than a single ambiguous point.

**Here, specifically.** The Kitsap Peninsula has no upstream river. USGS
describes the hydrologic setting as similar to that of an island, and
groundwater is the normal domestic source and the only one: every litre of
fresh water on this peninsula fell on this peninsula. The most detailed
federal study found no widespread seawater contamination of peninsula wells,
and named the local areas where chloride in well water exceeds 25 mg/L: the
southern Longbranch peninsula, Horsehead Bay, Point Evans, Sinclair Inlet,
Eagle Harbor, Fletcher Bay, the north end of Bainbridge Island and the north
tip of the Kitsap peninsula. Silverdale and Dyes Inlet are not on that list.
Sinclair Inlet, about 11 km south, is.

Two things about that reassurance. The first is its date: it rests on 1980
field data, which makes it 45 years old, so a shoreline well should be
tested for chloride rather than assumed clean. The second is the number
itself. Twenty-five milligrams per litre is one tenth of the SMCL, far below
anything you could taste, and it was chosen as an indicator of intrusion
precisely because it is low. Waiting for 250 is waiting for the answer to
stop being useful.

Local well depths give a sense of what you would be sampling: near
Silverdale the median recorded depth is 98 feet, about 30 metres, with half
of local wells between 41 and 255 feet. The shallow end sits in the
unconfined surficial unit, which is the most exposed both to surface
contamination and to seasonal decline.

## Home test kits, honestly

The question everybody asks, so here is the answer without the hedging.

**What a strip or a home kit actually resolves.** Colour-change chemistry is
good at a small number of things that are present in large amounts and have
a simple, distinctive reaction: pH, total hardness, free chlorine,
occasionally iron, occasionally nitrate at coarse resolution. For those, a
strip gives you a band rather than a number, and the band is usually enough,
because the decisions those parameters drive are coarse decisions. Knowing
your water is very hard rather than soft is useful. Knowing it is 190 rather
than 185 mg/L is not.

**What it cannot do**, and this is the part the packaging is quiet about.
Every analytical method has a floor below which it cannot distinguish a real
result from noise. The federal definition of that floor, the method
detection limit, is "the minimum measured concentration of a substance that
can be reported with 99% confidence that the measured concentration is
distinguishable from method blank results." The health limits that matter
most are very low numbers: arsenic at 0.010 mg/L, lead's action level at
0.015 mg/L falling to 0.010. A colour comparison against a printed chart is
not a technique that resolves ten parts per billion, and no amount of
careful squinting makes it one. The same applies to a coliform result, which
is a culture with an incubation time and a controlled medium, not a dye
reaction.

**What the federal system requires instead.** EPA "protects drinking water
by requiring that laboratories become certified to analyze drinking water
samples and that they use analytical methods approved by EPA." Both halves
matter: a certified laboratory and an approved method. CDC's instruction is
one line, "Use a state-certified laboratory to test your water," and EPA's
is "Only use laboratories that are certified to do drinking water testing."

So the honest split is this, and the ranking within it is a rule of thumb
rather than an agency recommendation:

- **Use a home kit** for pH, hardness and chlorine, for checking a treatment
  system is still doing something, and for watching a trend between real
  tests. A conductivity meter belongs in this category too, and on a coastal
  well it is the most useful cheap instrument you can own, because it costs
  nothing to read weekly and it moves before anything tastes wrong.
- **Use a certified laboratory** for every number you would act on: coliform
  and *E. coli*, nitrate, arsenic, lead and the other metals, volatile
  organic compounds, pesticides, radionuclides. Anything with a health limit
  attached, anything you would spend money to treat, and anything you would
  reassure a pregnant household member with.

**How to find one.** Your state or local health department maintains the
list. CDC: health and environmental departments "should have a list of the
state-certified (licensed) laboratories in your area that test for different
germs and chemicals." EPA points to your state's laboratory certification
programme, and notes that your local health department "may provide private
well testing for free." EPA itself will not do it: "Unfortunately, EPA is
unable to test residential or commercial drinking water upon request." The
federal Safe Drinking Water Hotline, 800-426-4791, can point you at sampling
information and at your state's programme.

## How to take a sample

A badly taken sample is worse than no sample, because it returns a number
and the number is wrong in an unknown direction. Most of the ways to ruin
one are invisible.

**The rule that comes before all the others:** ask the laboratory first and
use their bottles. EPA's own guidance opens with it. "Check with the
laboratory before collecting samples to ensure that sampling equipment,
preservatives, and procedures for sample collection are acceptable. It is
best to obtain sampling supplies directly from the laboratory performing the
analyses." A bacteriology bottle is sterile and may contain sodium
thiosulfate; a metals bottle may already contain nitric acid. Neither can be
improvised, and a rinsed-out jam jar is not a substitute for either.

**The general procedure**, which applies to most chemistry:

1. Collect "in an area free of excessive dust, rain, snow or other sources
   of contamination."
2. "Select a cold water faucet for sampling which is free of contaminating
   devices such as screens, aeration devices, hoses, purification devices or
   swiveled faucets. Check the faucet to be sure it is clean. If the faucet
   is in a state of disrepair, select another sampling location."
3. Use a tap "high enough to put a bottle underneath, generally the bath tub
   or kitchen sink, without contacting the mouth of the container with the
   faucet."
4. "Open the faucet and thoroughly flush. Generally 2 to 3 minutes will
   suffice... Typically the water temperature will stabilize which indicates
   flushing is completed. Once the lines are flushed, adjust the flow so it
   does not splash."
5. Record the site, the sampler's name, the date and time of collection, the
   method, the analysis requested and any preservative used.
6. Deliver or ship so that holding times are met. "Holding time starts at
   sample collection and ends at preparation and/or analysis," so leave the
   laboratory time to process it.

**For bacteria**, the rules tighten. Sterile 125 or 150 mL plastic bottles
must be used. Then, in EPA's words: "Do not rinse the bottles. The bottles
are sterile so care must be taken not to contaminate the bottle or cap. Once
the distribution line is flushed and the flow reduced, quickly open the
bottle (but do not set the cap down), hold the cap by its outside edges
only, and fill the sample bottle to just above the 100 mL line leaving a one
inch headspace. Cap the bottle immediately and place it into a cooler with
ice for delivery or overnight shipment to the laboratory."

EPA's short list of tips for coliform sampling is worth pinning above the
sink:

- remove any attachments on the faucet;
- allow water to flow for 5 or 6 minutes before sampling;
- do not rinse or overfill the container;
- always collect cold water, never sample hot water;
- do not touch the inside of the sample bottle or its cap.

And the sampling sites EPA says to avoid for total coliform if possible:
outdoor faucets; faucets connected to cisterns, softeners, pumps, pressure
tanks or hot water heaters; new plumbing and fixtures or those repaired
recently; faucets that hot and cold water come through; threaded taps; swing
spouts; faucets positioned close to the sink or ground; and leaky faucets.
Every item on that list is a place where something other than your water
supply can contribute bacteria, and a positive from one of them tells you
about the tap rather than about the well.

**For metals**, the bottle is preserved with nitric acid to pH below 2, and
if the preservative is already in the bottle, do not rinse it. Fill to
within one to two inches of the top.

**Holding times**, which are the constraint people plan around worst:

| Analysis | Time from collection |
|---|---|
| Coliform and *E. coli*, drinking water | 30 hours |
| Coliform, source water compliance samples | 8 hours |
| Coliphage | 48 hours |
| Metals, mercury | 28 days |
| Metals, other | 6 months (acid preserved); received within 14 days if not preserved |
| Nitrate, nitrite, chloride, conductivity, TDS and other unpreserved chemistry | short; deliver the same day or ship overnight, and check with the laboratory |

The 30 hours is not a guideline, it is federal regulation: 40 CFR 141.21
states that "The time from sample collection to initiation of analysis may
not exceed 30 hours." The practical consequence is that a bacteriology
sample is a logistics problem before it is a chemistry problem. Do not draw
one on a Friday afternoon. Do not draw one before a long drive. Get the ice
ready before you get the bottle out, and know when the laboratory's courier
runs.

Keep the sample cold the whole way. EPA's preservative for biological
samples is to cool below 10 C, and to cool unpreserved chemistry to about
4 C, in both cases without letting the sample freeze.

## Reading the result sheet

A laboratory report is a list of numbers next to a list of limits, and the
limits are not all the same kind of thing. Four kinds appear, and confusing
them is the usual source of both false alarm and false comfort.

| Term | What it means | Is it a health limit? |
|---|---|---|
| **MCLG** | "The level of a contaminant in drinking water below which there is no known or expected risk to health." They "allow for a margin of safety and are non-enforceable public health goals" | Yes, and it is the purely health-based one. Not enforceable |
| **MCL** | "The highest level of a contaminant that is allowed in drinking water," set "as close to MCLGs as feasible using the best available treatment technology and taking cost into consideration" | Yes, enforceable, but it is a feasibility line as well as a health line |
| **Action level / treatment technique** | An action level triggers a required response; a treatment technique is "a required process intended to reduce the level of a contaminant in drinking water" | Indirectly. Used where measuring at the tap is not a clean compliance test, as with lead |
| **SMCL** | A secondary standard, for taste, odour, colour and staining | **No.** EPA does not enforce them and says the contaminants are "not considered to present a risk to human health at the SMCL" |

Add health advisory values as a fifth category if your report carries them:
informative, not enforceable.

**Now the number that is most often misread: "not detected."**

It does not mean zero. It means the laboratory's method, at its detection
limit, could not distinguish this sample from a blank. The detection limit
is defined federally as "the minimum measured concentration of a substance
that can be reported with 99% confidence that the measured concentration is
distinguishable from method blank results." Everything below that floor
reports the same way, whether it is genuinely absent or sitting just beneath
the floor.

Which makes the detection limit itself a number you should read. A report
that says arsenic was not detected, at a detection limit of 0.001 mg/L, has
told you something useful: arsenic is at most a tenth of the MCL. A report
that says arsenic was not detected, at a detection limit of 0.02 mg/L, has
told you almost nothing, because the floor of the method is above the legal
limit. Both sheets read "ND" in the same column and in the same typeface.

The questions to ask of any sheet, in order:

1. **What was on the list?** Everything not analysed is unanswered, not
   passed.
2. **What kind of limit is each comparison against?** Health, aesthetic, or
   advisory.
3. **What are the units, and on what basis?** Milligrams per litre against
   micrograms per litre is a factor of a thousand. Nitrate as nitrogen
   against nitrate as the ion is a different scale again.
4. **What is the detection limit for each non-detect?** And is it well below
   the limit that matters?
5. **How was the sample drawn?** For any metal, first draw or flushed, and
   therefore which question did this answer.
6. **When was it drawn?** Wet season or dry, before or after an event.

## When a result comes back bad

There is a correct order here, and the mistake is nearly always to start at
step four.

**1. Stop drinking it.** Immediately, before anything is diagnosed, and with
particular urgency if the household includes an infant, a pregnancy, or
anybody immunocompromised. CDC's post-disaster instruction generalizes well:
drink bottled water "until you are certain that your water is free of
contaminants and safe to drink." Water for washing floors, flushing and
watering is a separate question with a much lower bar, and separating your
uses by how clean they need to be takes the pressure off, as
[Where Water Comes From](/library#where-water-comes-from) describes.

**2. Confirm it.** One sample is one sample, and sampling error is real,
particularly for bacteria where a contaminated tap or a warm bottle can
manufacture a positive. Resample, properly, ideally from a different tap as
well, and tell the laboratory what you found the first time.

**3. Find the cause.** This is the step people skip and it is the one that
decides everything after it. A coliform positive is a path, so go looking
for the path: the well cap and its seal, the casing, the grouting, the
surface grading around the wellhead, a nearby drainfield, a flooded
depression, recent digging. A nitrate result points uphill at fertilizer,
sewage or animal waste. A lead result points at your own pipes, and the
first-draw versus flushed pair tells you whether it is the service line or
the fixture. An arsenic result points at the rock, which means it is not
going away and not caused by anything you did.

**4. Fix the cause.** A cracked cap, a failed seal, a drainfield, an
abandoned well nearby used as a shortcut for surface water. Where the cause
can be fixed, fixing it is cheaper and permanent, and it fixes the
contaminants you did not test for at the same time.

**5. Only then, treat.** And treat for the specific thing you found, with
the specific method that reaches it, which is what
[Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) is for. Its
central table exists because no single method covers everything: boiling
handles every germ and no chemical; camping microfilters miss viruses;
chlorine misses *Cryptosporidium*; nothing in the household kit removes
nitrate or arsenic. If you disinfect a well, remember the timing above:
wait 7 to 10 days after disinfection before retesting, so you are measuring
the well rather than the chlorine.

**Treating without finding the cause is the mistake.** It is popular because
it is purchasable, and it fails in two specific ways. A treatment system
sized for the contamination you measured does not know about the
contamination you did not, and the path that let one thing in is still open.
And every treatment system is a maintenance obligation: a filter past its
service life is not neutral, it is a reservoir. Fix the hole, then filter
what still gets through.

## Surface water and rainwater: what a test tells you there, and why it is less

The sibling guides cover both sources in full.
[Collecting Rainwater](/library#collecting-rainwater) is the build guide and treats
barrel water as non-potable throughout;
[Where Water Comes From](/library#where-water-comes-from) ranks streams, lakes and
springs by risk. This section is only about what testing adds, which is less
than it adds for a well, and for a reason worth understanding.

A well draws from a body of water with a long residence time. It changes
slowly, which is exactly what makes an annual snapshot meaningful: this
year's result is a reasonable guide to next month's, absent an event.

Surface water and roof runoff have no such stability. EPA's nonpoint source
mechanism is a description of pulses: rainfall and snowmelt move over the
ground, pick up pollutants, and deposit them. A creek sampled on a dry
Tuesday and the same creek sampled two hours into the first storm after a
dry spell are, in any practical sense, two different waters. A clean result
from the dry Tuesday does not transfer.

The sampling logistics say the same thing. EPA's national beach guidance for
recreational water holds bacteriological samples at 1 to 4 C and says "Do not
hold samples longer than 6 hours between collection and initiation of
analysis," against 30 hours for drinking water. Short holding times exist
because the population in the bottle is changing.

So what is testing surface water or rainwater good for?

- **Characterising a source you will use repeatedly**, by sampling several
  times across the year rather than once, specifically including during and
  just after rain. A single result is close to worthless; a set of six that
  brackets the seasons is a real description.
- **Answering a chemical question**, which is the half that does not change
  hour to hour. Metals, nitrate and dissolved solids in a stream reflect the
  watershed and the rock, and those move slowly. This is where a test earns
  its money on surface water.
- **Checking a roof system for what the roof itself contributes**, which is
  a fixed property of the materials rather than a weather event.
- **It is not good for** certifying a batch as safe to drink. For that, the
  answer is treatment every time, matched to the pathogen classes, because
  the biological result you would need has already expired by the time it
  comes back.

Two structural facts underneath all of that. CDC's line about private
supplies, which explicitly covers rainwater collection systems as well as
wells: "These private water systems are not regulated or monitored by
officials." Nobody else is checking. And the natural filtration that makes
groundwater the better bet, in CDC's careful phrasing, is conditional:
ground water "is naturally filtered, which might remove some germs and
chemicals. How much is filtered out depends on how far the water is below
ground and the area's local geology."

One footnote on bottled water, since it is what people fall back on while a
well is under investigation. FDA regulates it rather than EPA, and requires
producers to obtain water from an approved source and to "Sample and test
both source water and the final product for contaminants." It is a tested
product, which is exactly the property you want while your own supply is an
open question.

## What can go wrong

- **You tested once, five years ago, and it was fine.** A test is a
  snapshot. Wells fail silently, usually with something that has no taste,
  and the annual habit exists because the failure gives no other warning.
- **You tested in August, when the well is at its cleanest.** Sample when
  the water is most likely to be bad, and at the same point in the year each
  time so the numbers compare.
- **Your coliform test was clean, so you assumed the water was safe.** A
  coliform test looks for one thing: a path from the surface. It is silent
  on nitrate, arsenic, lead, solvents and pesticides.
- **You got a coliform positive and concluded there was sewage in your
  water.** Maybe. The indicator says a path exists, not what used it. *E.
  coli* is the result that means faeces, and it is the one that means stop
  today.
- **You ran the tap for two minutes before taking a lead sample.** You
  measured the main. The lead was in the water you flushed away. First-draw
  means six hours undisturbed, no flushing, aerator left on.
- **You compared a nitrate number to 10 without checking the basis.** The
  federal limit is 10 mg/L measured as nitrogen. If the sheet reports the
  nitrate ion instead, the comparison is wrong.
- **You saw "not detected" and read it as zero.** It means below the
  method's floor. Read the detection limit next to it, and check it sits
  well below the limit that matters.
- **You spent money treating an aesthetic exceedance.** Secondary standards
  are for taste, colour and staining, and EPA neither enforces them nor
  treats them as health limits.
- **You drew a bacteriology sample on a Friday afternoon.** Thirty hours is
  a federal limit, not a suggestion, and the clock starts when you fill the
  bottle.
- **You rinsed the sterile bottle out first, because that is what you do
  with bottles.** You have just contaminated it. Do not rinse, do not
  overfill, do not touch the inside of the bottle or the cap.
- **You bought a treatment system before finding the cause.** The path that
  let one contaminant in is still open, and the system only removes what it
  was sized for.
- **You have a shoreline well and have never tested chloride.** Waiting for
  a salty taste means waiting for 250 mg/L, and intrusion is the one
  contamination in this guide that does not undo.

## How you know it worked

- You have a laboratory result from within the last year, from a
  state-certified laboratory, covering at minimum total coliform bacteria,
  nitrate, total dissolved solids and pH.
- You have asked your county or state health department what else your area
  specifically needs, and you have tested for whatever they named.
- You can say, for each metal result you hold, whether it was a first-draw
  or a flushed sample, and therefore which question it answered.
- You know which numbers on your sheet are health limits and which are
  aesthetic, and you have not confused a stained bath for a hazard.
- For every non-detect you are relying on, you know the detection limit, and
  it is well below the limit that matters.
- You know the date of every sample and what the weather had been doing.
- If you are on a coast, you have a chloride and conductivity baseline, not
  an assumption, and you intend to repeat it at the wet and dry ends of the
  year.
- If anything came back bad, you looked for the cause before you shopped for
  a filter.

When those are true, the other four water guides stop being guesswork. You
can choose a source knowing what is in it, treat for the thing that is
actually there, store it without wondering what you sealed in, and say to
somebody else, with a piece of paper behind it, that the water in this house
is fit to drink. That last one is the part that is worth the fifty dollars.
