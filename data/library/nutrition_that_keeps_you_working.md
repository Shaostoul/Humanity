# Nutrition That Keeps You Working

Take a potato out of the sack and put it on the kitchen scale. A middling
one runs about 150 grams. Now weigh a head of lettuce. That runs about
300 grams, twice as much.

The potato holds about 115 calories. The lettuce holds about 45.

Twice the weight, under two fifths of the energy. Hold that comparison in
your head, because it is the whole of this guide in one line, and because
almost everything that goes wrong in a self-provisioned diet goes wrong
at exactly that point: somebody grows a great deal of food by volume and
not very much food by energy, and does not find out until the winter.

## What this guide is, and what it refuses to do

This is not a healthy-eating article. There are millions of those and
they are mostly about a problem you do not have, which is choosing among
an abundance you did not grow.

This guide is about a different question. **Will what you are growing and
eating actually keep a person doing physical work?** By the end you
should be able to look at a garden plan or a full pantry shelf and answer
three things for yourself: is there enough energy here, is there enough
protein here, and what is missing that will not show up for months.

Three refusals, stated up front so you can hold me to them.

**This guide will not tell you how many calories you need.** Not a
number, not a range narrowed to your case, not a formula with your weight
in it. Published figures are population estimates and every federal
source that publishes one says so in its own words. The Dietary
Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030 puts it plainly: "The calories you
need depend on your age, sex, height, weight, and level of physical
activity." I will give you the published ranges, tell you exactly what
they are estimates of, and then show you the arithmetic so you can work
on your own numbers instead of mine.

**This guide will not prescribe a diet or a macro split.** It will not
tell you what percentage of your energy should come from fat. There is a
specific reason for that refusal and it is not modesty: in 2024 a
National Academies committee reviewed the Acceptable Macronutrient
Distribution Ranges, the 20-to-35-percent-this and 10-to-35-percent-that
figures that fill diet writing, and recommended that the federal working
group "remove the Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Range from the
panel of nutrient intake values," concluding that "the approach used to
derive the AMDRs is not consistent with current evidence-based
standards." Percentages of energy are the most-quoted numbers in
nutrition and they are, right now, the ones under the most serious
official challenge. So this guide works in grams and calories, which are
measurements, rather than in ratios, which are judgments.

**This guide does not diagnose anything.** There is a section near the
end on recognising trouble, and every line in it points at a clinician,
not at a remedy.

What it will do is arithmetic, out loud, from numbers you can check.

## Part one: energy, because nothing else matters without it

### What a calorie actually is

A calorie in food is a kilocalorie, and it has a physical definition. The
US Army's nutrition regulation states it about as cleanly as anyone: one
kcal is "the amount of heat necessary to raise 1 kg (liter) of water from
15 C to 16 C."

That is worth sitting with for a second. A calorie is not a nutrition
concept. It is a heat measurement, borrowed. When you read that a potato
holds 115 calories, the literal claim is that burning that potato
completely would heat 115 litres of water by one degree Celsius. Your
body is not a furnace and does not extract all of it, which is why the
figures on a food table are already adjusted, but the underlying quantity
is heat, and that is why energy is the one thing in nutrition you can
genuinely add up.

**A warning about the word itself, because it is a trap.** The "calorie"
on a food label is a thousand of the calories a physicist means. If you
look up the conversion in a units table you will find something like
"calorie (thermochemical) to joule, multiply by 4.184," and that is the
small calorie: a food calorie is 4,184 joules, not 4.184.
[Units and Converting Them](/library#units-and-converting-them) makes the
general version of this point, which is that several different
definitions of the calorie are in circulation and the qualifier is part
of the unit. Food tables almost never print the qualifier. When a
nutrition source says "calorie" it means kilocalorie, every time, and
that is the convention this guide uses throughout.

The three things in food that carry energy carry different amounts of it.
The US Food and Drug Administration's label guidance gives the
conversions:

- **Protein: 4 calories per gram.**
- **Carbohydrate: 4 calories per gram.**
- **Fat: 9 calories per gram.** FDA's own comment on that: "Dietary fat
  has more than twice the calories per gram as either carbohydrate or
  protein, so calories from fat can add up quickly."

Everything else in food, including water, fibre you do not digest, and
every vitamin and mineral in this guide, carries no usable energy at all.
Water is most of the mass of most vegetables. That is the entire reason
lettuce loses to potato.

### What published ranges actually say

The current Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030, released in
January 2026, is a nine-page document, and it does not publish a table of
calorie needs. It gives the principle ("Eat the Right Amount for You")
and sends you to your own circumstances.

The most recent federal edition that did publish such a table is the
2015-2020 Dietary Guidelines, Appendix 2. Its headline figures:

> "Estimates range from 1,600 to 2,400 calories per day for adult women
> and 2,000 to 3,000 calories per day for adult men."

Now the part that matters more than the numbers, which is what they are
estimates *of*. That appendix defines three activity levels, and the
definitions are narrow:

- **Sedentary:** "a lifestyle that includes only the physical activity of
  independent living."
- **Moderately active:** "physical activity equivalent to walking about
  1.5 to 3 miles per day at 3 to 4 miles per hour, in addition to the
  activities of independent living."
- **Active:** "physical activity equivalent to walking more than 3 miles
  per day at 3 to 4 miles per hour, in addition to the activities of
  independent living."

Read the third one again. **"Active," in the table everybody quotes,
means walking more than three miles a day.** It does not mean digging a
bed, splitting a cord of wood, carrying water, or roofing a shed. The
published table simply does not extend to sustained manual labour, and if
you have been using its top figure as your working number, you have been
using a walking figure for a working day.

The table's own arithmetic is built on reference bodies: a reference man
of 5 feet 10 inches and 154 pounds, a reference woman of 5 feet 4 inches
and 126 pounds, run through the Estimated Energy Requirement equations
from a 2002 Institute of Medicine report. The appendix says of its own
output: "These are only estimates."

### What heavy work adds

For an actual published figure on physical work, the cleanest federal
source is military, because armies have to feed people doing hard labour
and have to write the number down. Army Regulation 40-25 sets its
allowances for "moderately active" personnel, and defines a moderately
active day as "8 hours of sleeping, 12 hours of light activity, and 4
hours of moderate to heavy activity." Then:

> "For military personnel doing heavy work or involved in prolonged,
> vigorous physical training, the recommended caloric allowance should be
> increased by at least 25 percent (approximately 500 to 900
> kilocalories)."

For field rations supporting extended operations, the same regulation
sets the standard at 3,600 kilocalories a day. For troops moving with
heavy gear over snow for hours, it goes to 4,500 for men and 3,500 for
women. And in the cold generally, below a mean temperature of about 14 C,
it puts the energy cost of work roughly 5 percent higher, plus 2 to 5
percent more for the weight and awkwardness of cold-weather clothing,
which it calls the "hobbling" effect: an extra 150 to 350 kilocalories a
day.

The same regulation says, of all of it: "The allowances are not to be
interpreted as individual requirements."

Two cautions on those figures, and they cut in opposite directions. The
regulation dates from 1985 and its allowances derive from a 1980 revision
of the Recommended Dietary Allowances, so its micronutrient figures have
been superseded several times over and should not be quoted as current.
But an estimate of what four hours of heavy work costs is a
physiological measurement, not a policy position, and it has not moved
much. Use it as a magnitude, not as a prescription: hard days cost
several hundred calories more than easy ones, cold days cost more than
warm ones, and if you are planning food for people doing real work you
should plan above the walking figure rather than at it.

**Under-eating on a working homestead is not a slow, gentle problem.**
The same regulation notes that its restricted ration, meant to hold
performance together when resupply is impossible, delivers 1,100 to 1,500
kilocalories and is rated for *up to 10 days*. A shortfall you would
barely notice in a sedentary week shows up in a working week as clumsiness,
poor judgment, and the accidents that follow from both.

## Part two: the arithmetic

This is the heart of the guide. Everything here comes from
`data/food/crop_nutrition.ron`, which carries per-100-gram energy,
protein, fat and carbohydrate for every food crop in the simulation,
along with a grams-per-yield-unit figure that converts an abstract
harvest count into real mass. The numbers are USDA FoodData Central
magnitude. I checked several of them against FoodData Central directly
while writing this, and potato, green-leaf lettuce and sunflower-seed
kernels match the USDA rows exactly.

Do the multiplication yourself as you read. That is the skill.

### The density ladder

Per 100 grams of raw edible food:

| Crop | Calories | Protein (g) | Fat (g) | Carbohydrate (g) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lettuce | 15 | 1.4 | 0.2 | 2.9 |
| Cabbage | 25 | 1.3 | 0.1 | 5.8 |
| Kale | 35 | 2.9 | 1.5 | 4.4 |
| Carrot | 41 | 0.9 | 0.2 | 9.6 |
| Potato | 77 | 2.0 | 0.1 | 17.5 |
| Dry bean | 333 | 23.6 | 0.8 | 60.0 |
| Wheat | 340 | 13.2 | 2.5 | 72.0 |
| Oat | 389 | 16.9 | 6.9 | 66.3 |
| Sunflower seed | 584 | 20.8 | 51.5 | 20.0 |

Top to bottom, that is a factor of thirty-nine. Lettuce and sunflower
seed are both plants you can grow in the same bed in the same season, and
one of them carries thirty-nine times the energy of the other per unit of
weight.

**One note on the bean row before you compare guides.** "Dry bean" is not
one food. USDA's own figures for specific beans, checked while writing
this, run from 333 calories per 100 g for kidney beans to 341 for black
beans, with protein from 21.6 to 23.6 grams depending on which bean.
The table above uses the simulation's generic bean row at 333, while
[Growing Food You Can Live On](/library#growing-food-you-can-live-on) uses
USDA's black bean row at 341. Both are right about their own food. The
difference is under 3 percent and changes nothing you would decide, but
it is a good illustration of why you should always know which row a
number came from before you compare two of them.

The reason is not mysterious. The leafy vegetables at the top are
overwhelmingly water. The seeds and grains at the bottom are a plant's
own stored energy reserve, deliberately dehydrated by the plant so it
keeps. **The crops that store well and the crops that carry energy are
mostly the same crops, for the same reason.**

### How much of each is a day

Pick a working figure. I will use 2,600 calories throughout, not because
it is right for you, but because it sits inside the published adult ranges
and makes the arithmetic legible. **Substitute your own figure and redo
every sum below; that is the point of showing the working.**

At 2,600 calories, from one crop alone:

| Crop | Mass needed | In pounds | As harvest units |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lettuce | 17.33 kg | 38.2 lb | about 58 heads |
| Potato | 3.38 kg | 7.4 lb | about 23 tubers |
| Dry bean | 0.78 kg | 1.7 lb | about 16 handfuls |
| Wheat | 0.76 kg | 1.7 lb | about 15 handfuls |
| Sunflower seed | 0.45 kg | 1.0 lb | about 9 handfuls |

Check one so you trust the rest. Lettuce is 15 calories per 100 grams,
which is 0.15 calories per gram. 2,600 divided by 0.15 is 17,333 grams,
which is 17.33 kilograms, which at 453.6 grams to the pound is 38.2
pounds. Fifty-eight heads of lettuce at 300 grams each.

**Nobody eats seventeen kilograms of lettuce.** That is the finding. It
is not that lettuce is bad; it is that lettuce is not food in the energy
sense, and a bed of it is a garnish crop wearing the costume of a staple.
The same day's energy is three and a third kilograms of potatoes, or
three quarters of a kilogram of wheat, or under half a kilogram of
sunflower seed.

### How many plants that is

Now push it back to the ground, which is where garden plans go wrong.

The simulation's potato rows yield between 4 and 10 harvest units per
plant, and a unit is one tuber of about 150 grams. So one potato plant
produces 600 to 1,500 grams of potato, which is 462 to 1,155 calories.

For a 2,600 calorie day from potatoes alone, that is **2.25 plants on a
good yield and 5.6 plants on a poor one.**

Over a year: **roughly 820 potato plants at the good yield, and about
2,050 at the poor one, to feed one adult.**

Sit with that. Eight hundred to two thousand potato plants, for one
person, for one year, from the single most energy-productive crop a
beginner can grow. The same sum on lettuce, whose plants the simulation
yields at 2 to 4 heads, gives 14 to 29 plants *a day*, which is 5,000 to
10,500 plants a year, and that is before anybody has eaten a single gram
of protein or fat.

**Two honest limits on that last calculation.** First, the per-plant
yields in the simulation are a game model calibrated against one anchor
crop, not agronomic measurements, and they behave sensibly for things
harvested as discrete objects (a tuber, a head) and much less sensibly
for grains, where a "plant" in the data plainly represents a sown patch
rather than a single stalk. Do not carry the grain-per-plant figures into
the real world. Second, for real planning yields you want real extension
figures, which is a different question and one that
[Growing Food You Can Live On](/library#growing-food-you-can-live-on) already
answers with published rows-per-hundred-feet numbers from two state
extension services. Read that for the planting side. Use this guide for
the eating side.

The reason both guides exist is that they disagree about nothing and
cover opposite halves of the same problem. That guide tells you what a
plot produces. This one tells you what a person consumes. You need the
two numbers to meet.

### A real day, built from four crops

Single-crop arithmetic proves the point but nobody lives that way. Here
is a day assembled from four things a temperate garden actually produces,
with every column multiplied out:

| Food | Mass | Calories | Protein (g) | Fat (g) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Potato | 1,500 g | 1,155 | 30.0 | 1.5 |
| Dry bean | 200 g | 666 | 43.2 | 2.4 |
| Wheat | 150 g | 510 | 20.6 | 3.8 |
| Sunflower seed | 50 g | 292 | 10.4 | 25.8 |
| **Total** | **1,900 g** | **2,623** | **104.2** | **33.4** |

That is a plausible working day: 2,623 calories, 104 grams of protein, 33
grams of fat, from under two kilograms of food.

Three things to notice, because each is a lesson.

**One: the fifty grams of sunflower seed supply 77 percent of the day's
fat.** Remove that one handful and the whole day drops to 7.6 grams of
fat. Fat is concentrated in very few crops and it disappears the moment
you stop growing them deliberately.

**Two: the protein takes care of itself once the energy does.** At 104
grams this day comfortably clears the Dietary Guidelines' stated protein
goal of 1.2 to 1.6 grams per kilogram of body weight per day, which for
the 154-pound reference man works out to about 84 to 112 grams. It does
so mostly on the strength of 200 grams of beans. Protein shortage in a
garden diet is usually a symptom of energy shortage, not a separate
problem.

**Three: 1,900 grams of food is a lot of chewing and it is the realistic
end of the range.** Push the same energy through vegetables instead and
you are eating volumes people physically cannot manage day after day,
which is the mechanism by which a vegetable-heavy diet quietly
under-feeds a working adult. It does not feel like going hungry. It feels
like being full and getting tired.

## Part three: protein, and why quality is a separate question from quantity

Protein is not a fuel you burn, it is a material you rebuild yourself
out of, and the body cannot stockpile it the way it stockpiles fat. The
useful distinction is not how much protein a food has but which amino
acids it has.

FDA's label guidance sets out the structure. There are 20 amino acids; 9
are essential, meaning "they cannot be made by the body and must be
obtained from food," and 11 are nonessential, meaning the body can build
them. Then:

- **Complete proteins** "contain all of the essential amino acids in
  adequate amounts. Animal foods (such as dairy products, eggs, meats,
  poultry, and seafood) and soy are complete protein sources."
- **Incomplete proteins** are "missing, or do not have enough of, one or
  more of the essential amino acids." And: "Most plant foods (such as
  beans, grains, nuts, peas, seeds, and vegetables) are incomplete
  protein sources."

Which is a problem with a known, old and very cheap solution. FDA again:

> "Complementary proteins are two or more incomplete protein sources
> that, when eaten in combination (at the same meal or during the same
> day), compensate for each other's lack of amino acids. For example,
> grains are low in the amino acid lysine, while beans and nuts (legumes)
> are low in the amino acid methionine. When grains and legumes are eaten
> together (such as rice and beans or peanut butter on whole wheat
> bread), they form a complete protein."

Grains are short on lysine, legumes are short on methionine, and each
covers the other's gap. Every traditional peasant cuisine on earth
arrived at this independently: rice and beans, tortillas and frijoles,
dal and rice, hummus and pita, beans on toast. Nobody worked out the
amino acid chemistry first. They worked out that the combination kept
people upright.

### The same-meal question, stated honestly

Older advice was emphatic that complementary proteins had to be eaten in
the *same meal*. You will still hear this. Here is the current federal
position, which is the sentence quoted above, and note the parenthesis:
"at the same meal or during the same day." FDA's own guidance treats the
day as the relevant window, not the meal.

**Label this one as not fully settled and move on.** The relaxation from
meal to day is the mainstream position and it is what the federal
guidance says, but the underlying question of whether 24-hour
complementation is fully equivalent to per-meal complementation is still
being actively studied, including in trials specifically designed to test
whether meals that are individually incomplete but complementary across a
day support muscle protein synthesis as well as complete meals do. I have
not read a result that closes the question, and I am not going to invent
one.

What this means in practice is completely undramatic. If you eat beans
and grains in the same week, in roughly the proportions any real cuisine
uses, you are fine. Do not build a scheduling ritual around it, and do
not lose sleep if the beans were at lunch and the bread at supper.

### How much

FDA's Daily Value for protein is 50 grams per day on a 2,000 calorie
diet. The Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030 states a higher
goal, "1.2-1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day,
adjusting as needed based on your individual caloric requirements," which
for a 70-kilogram person is roughly 84 to 112 grams. Those are different
kinds of number: the Daily Value is a labelling reference, the Guidelines
figure is a dietary goal, and it is not surprising that they differ.

FDA's other observation is worth keeping in view: "Most Americans get the
recommended amounts of protein to meet their needs." Protein is the
nutrient people worry about most and run short of least. In a
self-provisioned diet with beans in it, energy runs out first.

## Part four: fat, the hardest thing to grow

Fat does three separate jobs and only one of them is energy.

It is the densest energy there is, at 9 calories per gram. It carries the
fat-soluble vitamins: as Army Regulation 40-25 puts it, fats "transport
fat soluble vitamins and aid in their absorption," which for this guide
means vitamins A and D specifically. And it supplies two fatty acids the
body genuinely cannot build.

On that last point, the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements is precise
about the chemistry: "The human body can only form carbon-carbon double
bonds after the ninth carbon from the methyl end of a fatty acid.
Therefore, ALA and linoleic acid are considered essential fatty acids,
meaning that they must be obtained from the diet." The Adequate Intake
for alpha-linolenic acid is 1.6 grams a day for adult men and 1.1 grams
for adult women.

### What its absence actually does, honestly

Here is where a lot of homestead writing overreaches, so let me give you
the sourced version.

Classical essential fatty acid deficiency is **rare**, and the reason is
that you carry a reserve. ODS: "During periods of dietary-fat restriction
or malabsorption accompanied by an energy deficit, the body releases
essential fatty acids from adipose-tissue reserves. For this reason,
clinical signs of essential fatty-acid deficiency are usually only found
in patients receiving parenteral nutrition that lacks PUFAs." When it
does occur, the signs are "rough, scaly skin and dermatitis." ODS states
flatly that "classical essential fatty acid deficiency in healthy
individuals in the United States is virtually nonexistent."

So the honest framing of the fat problem on a homestead is not "you will
develop a deficiency syndrome." It is this:

**A low-fat diet is a low-energy-density diet, and low energy density is
how a working adult ends up underfed while eating constantly.** Go back
to the arithmetic. A day of 2,600 calories entirely from potatoes
contains 3.4 grams of fat, which is about 4 percent of FDA's 78-gram
Daily Value, and it requires you to eat three and a third kilograms of
potato. The four-crop day above, with one handful of sunflower seed in
it, holds 33 grams of fat and needs less than two kilograms of food. The
fat did not just add fat. It made the day physically possible to eat.

### Where fat comes from in a garden

Look back at the density table. Sunflower seed is 51.5 grams of fat per
100 grams; every leafy and root crop on that table is under 2. The
concentrated plant sources are seeds, nuts and a handful of oil-bearing
fruits: in the simulation's crop data, sunflower (51.5), walnut (65.2),
hazelnut (60.8), sesame (49.7), peanut (49.2), rapeseed (44.0), coconut
(33.5), olive (10.7). Everything else is essentially fat-free.

That list is short, and most of it is trees and oilseeds rather than
annual vegetables. **Fat is the thing a temperate garden is worst at, and
therefore the thing to plan for first**, whether that means planting nut
trees a decade ahead, growing an oilseed and finding a way to press it,
or keeping animals. It is not an afterthought you can add later in the
season.

One thing the current Dietary Guidelines does say about fat quality: "In
general, saturated fat consumption should not exceed 10% of total daily
calories." It also says, in the same paragraph, "More high-quality
research is needed to determine which types of dietary fats best support
long-term health," which is an unusually candid admission and worth
repeating whenever somebody tells you the fat question is settled.

## Part five: the micronutrients that actually fail

Everything so far can be added up. Nothing in this section can, and that
is exactly why it is dangerous: you can do the energy and protein
arithmetic perfectly and still walk into a deficiency, because the
deficiency does not announce itself for weeks or years.

For each one below: what it does, what carries it, and roughly when
running out starts to show. Every figure is from the NIH Office of
Dietary Supplements unless stated otherwise.

### Vitamin C: the fast one

**What it does.** Builds collagen, which is the structural protein of
connective tissue and the reason wounds close. Also substantially
improves the absorption of iron from plants.

**Recommended intakes:** 90 mg a day for adult men, 75 mg for adult
women. People who smoke need 35 mg a day more.

**How long the reserve lasts.** This is the shortest fuse of any nutrient
in this guide. Total body content runs from about 300 mg at near-scurvy up
to about 2 grams. ODS: "The signs and symptoms of scurvy can appear within
1 month of consuming less than about 10 mg/day of vitamin C."

**One month.** That is why scurvy was a sailing problem and not a farming
problem: it needs a sustained absence of fresh food, which is exactly what
a long sea voyage was and exactly what a badly planned winter store can be.

**What it looks like.** The first symptoms are fatigue, malaise and
inflamed gums. Then, as collagen synthesis fails, ODS lists "petechiae,
ecchymoses, purpura, joint pain, poor wound healing, hyperkeratosis, and
corkscrew hairs," along with swollen bleeding gums and loosening teeth.
**Left untreated, scurvy is fatal.** It is also, once recognised, one of
the cheapest things in medicine to fix.

**What carries it.** Per serving, from ODS: raw sweet red pepper half a
cup, 95 mg. One medium orange, 70 mg. Cooked broccoli half a cup, 51 mg.
Sliced strawberries half a cup, 49 mg. Cooked Brussels sprouts half a cup,
48 mg. Cooked cabbage half a cup, 28 mg. **One medium baked potato, 17
mg.** One medium raw tomato, 17 mg. ODS notes that "consuming five varied
servings of fruits and vegetables a day can provide more than 200 mg."

That potato figure deserves a moment. A potato is a mediocre source of
vitamin C by the spoonful, but if you are eating three kilograms of them
a day it is not a mediocre source at all. Historically, potato-based
diets did not produce scurvy for precisely this reason. Quantity
compensates for concentration, which is a general principle worth
carrying: **the nutrient content of a staple matters more than the
nutrient content of a garnish, because you eat vastly more of it.**

**The trap.** Vitamin C is water-soluble and destroyed by heat. ODS: "The
vitamin C content of food may be reduced by prolonged storage and by
cooking because ascorbic acid is water soluble and is destroyed by heat.
Steaming or microwaving may lessen cooking losses." A cellar full of
vegetables stored since September is not a vitamin C supply in February.
See the preservation section below.

### Vitamin A: the slow one with two forms

**What it does.** Vision, immune function, cell growth.

**Recommended intakes:** 900 mcg RAE a day for adult men, 700 for adult
women.

**The two forms, and why the units are strange.** Preformed vitamin A
(retinol) comes from animal foods. Provitamin A carotenoids, mostly
beta-carotene, come from plants and have to be converted. They are not
equivalent, which is why the unit is retinol activity equivalents. ODS:
"One mcg RAE is equivalent to 1 mcg retinol, 2 mcg supplemental
beta-carotene, 12 mcg dietary beta-carotene, or 24 mcg dietary
alpha-carotene or beta-cryptoxanthin."

Twelve to one, for beta-carotene from food. When somebody tells you a
carrot has as much vitamin A as liver, that ratio is what they have
skipped.

**How long the reserve lasts.** ODS says most of the body's vitamin A is
stored in the liver as retinyl esters, and gives a striking detail about
how deep that store is: blood retinol levels "do not decline until vitamin
A levels in the liver and other storage sites are almost depleted." In
other words the reserve is large enough that the ordinary blood test only
turns abnormal near the end of it. **ODS does not publish a figure for
how many months the liver store lasts, and I will not invent one.** What
it does publish is the sequence: plasma retinol falls, then the eye's
reserves deplete, then xerophthalmia develops, and "the first sign is
night blindness."

**Night blindness is the symptom to know.** Difficulty seeing in low
light, appearing before anything else. Untreated, xerophthalmia damages
the cornea and can end in permanent blindness; ODS notes vitamin A
deficiency is one of the top causes of preventable blindness in children
worldwide.

**What carries it.** One whole baked sweet potato in its skin: 1,403 mcg
RAE, 156 percent of the Daily Value in a single root. Half a cup of boiled
frozen spinach: 573. Half a cup of raw carrots: 459. Three ounces of
pan-fried beef liver: 6,582.

**One real hazard, at the point where you would meet it.** Preformed
vitamin A is toxic in excess in a way beta-carotene is not. Acute
hypervitaminosis A "occurs within days to weeks after someone ingests one
or a few very high doses (typically more than 100 times the RDA)," causing
severe headache, blurred vision, nausea, dizziness, aching muscles and
coordination problems, and in severe cases raised cerebrospinal fluid
pressure, coma and death. **Liver is a concentrated source and it is
possible to overdo it.** ODS advises women who are or might be pregnant
and those lactating not to take more than 3,000 mcg RAE (10,000 IU) daily
from supplements, because excess preformed vitamin A causes birth
defects. Beta-carotene from vegetables does not carry this risk; its worst
common effect is carotenodermia, harmless orange-tinged skin that reverses
when you stop.

### Iron: the one plants carry badly

**What it does.** Carries oxygen in the blood. Running short of it is
directly a reduction in how much work you can do.

**Recommended intakes:** adult men 8 mg a day. Women 19 to 50: **18 mg**,
more than twice as much, because of menstrual losses. Women 51 and over:
8 mg. Pregnancy: 27 mg.

**And then the multiplier.** ODS: "The requirement for iron is 1.8 times
higher for people who follow vegetarian diets than those for people who
include animal products in their diet." Which puts a vegetarian woman of
reproductive age at roughly 32 mg a day. This is, by a wide margin, the
hardest single number in this guide to hit from a garden.

**Why plant iron is worse.** Two forms. Heme iron, in meat and seafood,
absorbs well. Nonheme iron, which is all the iron in plants, does not. AR
40-25 puts the absorption at "approximately 23 percent" for heme against
"approximately 3 to 8 percent" for nonheme. ODS gives whole-diet figures:
"approximately 14% to 18% from mixed diets that include substantial
amounts of meat, seafood, and vitamin C ... and 5% to 12% from vegetarian
diets."

**What you can actually do about it.** This is the most actionable
paragraph in the guide, because nonheme absorption is manipulable.

- **Vitamin C, eaten with the iron.** AR 40-25: "The nonheme iron
  absorption rate can be more than doubled when nonheme iron is consumed
  with a modest serving of meat, fish, poultry, or a source of ascorbic
  acid (vitamin C) at the same meal." Note that this one genuinely is a
  same-meal effect. Beans with tomatoes, lentils with peppers, greens with
  a squeeze of citrus.
- **Meat, poultry or seafood**, even in small amounts, enhances the
  absorption of the nonheme iron eaten alongside it.
- **Working against you:** phytate, which ODS notes is "present in grains
  and beans," and polyphenols in cereals and legumes. Calcium may reduce
  absorption of both forms.

**The spinach correction.** Spinach is the folk answer to iron and it is a
poor one. ODS: "Some plant-based foods that are good sources of iron, such
as spinach, have low iron bioavailability because they contain
iron-absorption inhibitors, such as polyphenols." It contains iron. You do
not get much of it.

**What carries it.** Canned white beans, 1 cup: 8 mg. Boiled lentils, half
a cup: 3 mg. Firm tofu, half a cup: 3 mg. Canned kidney beans, half a cup:
2 mg. Chickpeas, half a cup: 2 mg. One medium baked potato with skin: 2
mg. Cooked oysters, 3 ounces: 8 mg. Beef liver, 3 ounces: 5 mg.

**How it fails, in order.** ODS describes three stages. First, storage
depletion: ferritin falls, nothing is wrong yet. Second,
iron-deficient erythropoiesis: stores are gone, transferrin saturation
falls, **but haemoglobin is usually still in the normal range.** Third,
iron deficiency anaemia. The functional costs ODS lists include "weakness;
fatigue; difficulty concentrating; and impaired cognitive function, immune
function, exercise or work performance, and body temperature regulation."

**That middle stage is the one that matters here.** You can be
functionally iron-depleted, working badly, sleeping badly and feeling cold,
with a blood count that reads normal. This is a reason to take persistent
unexplained fatigue to a clinician rather than to the internet, and it is
not something you can resolve by reasoning about your diet.

### Vitamin D: the one you cannot grow

**What it does.** Enables calcium absorption and bone mineralisation.
Without it, calcium intake barely matters, because the gut cannot take
calcium up properly without it.

**Recommended intakes:** 15 mcg (600 IU) a day for adults 19 to 70, 20 mcg
(800 IU) over 70. One mcg equals 40 IU.

**The crucial detail about those numbers:** ODS states that "the FNB based
the vitamin D RDAs on the assumption that people receive minimal sun
exposure." The recommendation already assumes you are not getting any from
the sky.

**What carries it, and this is the whole problem.** From the ODS food
table: cod liver oil, 1 tablespoon, 34 mcg. Cooked trout, 3 ounces, 16.2.
Cooked sockeye salmon, 3 ounces, 14.2. **White mushrooms exposed to UV
light, half a cup, 9.2.** Fortified milk, 1 cup, 2.9. One large egg, 1.1.

And then, from the same table, the figure for each of these: broccoli,
carrots, almonds, apple, banana, brown rice, whole wheat bread, boiled
lentils, sunflower seeds, cooked edamame. **Zero. Every one of them.**

There is essentially no vitamin D in a vegetable garden. The plant
kingdom's one meaningful contribution is mushrooms that have been exposed
to ultraviolet light, and that is an unusual enough route that most people
have never heard of it. Otherwise it is oily fish, liver, eggs, fortified
food, sunlight, or a supplement.

**Sunlight, and what the sources will and will not say.** ODS lists the
factors that affect how much you make: "Season, time of day, length of
day, cloud cover, smog, skin melanin content, and sunscreen." Older people
and people with darker skin synthesise less. UVB does not pass through
glass, so a sunny window produces none at all. ODS then says something
important: these factors "make it difficult to provide guidelines on how
much sun exposure is required for sufficient vitamin D synthesis." Some
researchers suggest 5 to 30 minutes of exposure to face, arms, hands and
legs, particularly between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., daily or at least twice a
week, but ODS presents that as a suggestion from expert bodies rather than
as its own recommendation, and pairs it with the reminder that UV radiation
is a carcinogen and the most preventable cause of skin cancer.

**You will often read that above roughly 37 degrees north the sun produces
no vitamin D at all in winter. That specific claim is not on the NIH page,
and I am not going to repeat a latitude threshold I could not source.**
What the page does say is that season and day length are among the
governing factors, and that is enough to reason with.

Here is the local reasoning, for Silverdale, Kitsap County, Washington, at
about 47.6 degrees north. From
[The Sky as an Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument), which carries US
Naval Observatory figures for this exact spot: on the June solstice the
sun reaches 65.8 degrees above the horizon and the day is 15 hours 59
minutes long. **On the December solstice the sun peaks at 18.9 degrees and
the day is 8 hours 26 minutes.** That is a noon sun under two fists above
the horizon, for a third of a day, in the season when you are wearing
everything you own.

Whatever the exact threshold is, a December noon sun at 18.9 degrees
through Puget Sound cloud, striking a face and two hands, is not doing the
work that a June noon sun at 65.8 degrees does on bare arms. That is also
the season of least fresh food, least outdoor time and most indoor work.
[The Climate Where You Live](/library#the-climate-where-you-live) has the local
cloud and precipitation record if you want to see how much sky there
actually is. **If you live at this latitude, vitamin D is a question to
put to a clinician rather than to a garden plan**, and it is the single
nutrient in this guide where "grow more food" is not even the beginning of
an answer.

**How it fails.** Rickets in children, in which growth cartilage fails to
mineralise and the skeleton deforms permanently. Osteomalacia in adults:
softened, painful, poorly mineralised bone. ODS reports the Food and
Nutrition Board's thresholds as a risk of deficiency below 30 nmol/L (12
ng/mL) of serum 25-hydroxyvitamin D, possible inadequacy from 30 to 50,
and sufficiency for most people at 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) or above, while
noting that levels above 125 nmol/L (50 ng/mL) can be associated with
adverse effects. Those are laboratory numbers, not something you can
assess by how you feel.

### Iodine: the one that depends on your dirt

**What it does.** The thyroid uses it to make the hormones that set your
metabolic rate. There is no substitute and no synthesis; the element comes
in or it does not.

**Recommended intake:** 150 mcg a day for adults. The whole body holds
only 15 to 20 mg of it, 70 to 80 percent of that in the thyroid.

**Why growing your own food does not solve this.** ODS: "The earth's soils
contain varying amounts of iodine, which in turn affects the iodine
content of crops. In some regions of the world, iodine-deficient soils are
common, increasing the risk of iodine deficiency among people who consume
foods primarily from those areas." And: "Most fruits and vegetables are
poor sources of iodine, and the amounts they contain are affected by the
iodine content of the soil, fertilizer use, and irrigation practices."

**This is the one nutrient in the guide that gets worse the more
self-sufficient you become**, because the whole modern defence against
iodine deficiency is a distribution system: iodised salt, and food
manufactured with it. Eat entirely from one patch of ground and you
inherit that ground's iodine content, whatever it is.

**What carries it.** Seaweed is the best food source by a distance,
although ODS warns the content varies enormously, from 16 mcg per gram to
2,984 mcg per gram across commercially available seaweeds, which is a
spread of nearly two hundred to one and a reason not to treat any seaweed
figure as reliable. Fish, other seafood and eggs are good sources. And iodised salt:
ODS reports that US iodised salt contains 45 mcg of iodine per gram of
salt according to its label.

**The specific trap worth knowing.** ODS: "Specialty salts, such as sea
salt, kosher salt, Himalayan salt, and fleur de sel, are not usually
iodized." A household that switched to sea salt because it seemed more
natural, and that also grows most of its own food, has quietly removed
both of its iodine sources. **Check the box.** It says whether the salt is
iodised.

**How it fails.** Persistently low iodine leaves thyroid-stimulating
hormone elevated, and the thyroid enlarges trying to trap more, which is
goitre. ODS describes iodine deficiency as "the most common cause of
preventable intellectual disability in the world," and states that "during
pregnancy and early infancy, iodine deficiency can cause irreversible
effects." That last sentence is the reason this unglamorous mineral is in
this guide at all.

### Vitamin B12: the one with a years-long fuse

**What it does.** Blood cell formation and nerve function.

**Recommended intake:** 2.4 mcg a day for adults. A tiny quantity, and it
is still hard to get from plants, because there is none there.

ODS, without qualification: **"Plant foods do not naturally contain
vitamin B12."** The exceptions are foods that have been fortified with it,
principally breakfast cereals and nutritional yeasts, which ODS notes are
"readily available sources of vitamin B12 that have high bioavailability."

**How long the reserve lasts, and why that is the danger.** ODS: "Because
the body stores about 1 to 5 milligrams (mg) vitamin B12 (or about 1,000 to
2,000 times as much as the amount typically consumed in a day), the
symptoms of vitamin B12 deficiency can take several years to appear."

Several years. Somebody can move to a diet with no animal food in it, feel
excellent, conclude they were right, and be running down a reserve the
whole time. This is the clearest case in nutrition of an absence of
symptoms meaning nothing at all.

**How it fails, and the specific reason to act early.** The classic sign
is megaloblastic anaemia, with large abnormal red cells; ODS also lists
low blood cell counts, a sore tongue, fatigue, palpitations, pale skin,
dementia, weight loss and infertility. And the neurological changes,
"numbness and tingling in the hands and feet."

**Here is the sentence to remember: ODS states that these neurological
symptoms "can occur without anemia, so early diagnosis and intervention is
important to avoid irreversible damage."** Irreversible. The blood test
everyone thinks of can be normal while nerve damage is accumulating, and
the damage does not necessarily undo when the vitamin comes back. If you
eat little or no animal food, this is a thing to raise with a clinician
deliberately and in advance, not something to watch for.

The current Dietary Guidelines is direct about this population: it advises
that people eating vegetarian or vegan diets "monitor nutrient status
periodically, especially for iron, vitamin B12, vitamin D, calcium, and
iodine." Note that four of those five are in this section.

### Calcium: the one your bones lend you

**What it does.** Almost all of it, 98 percent, is structural: it is
bone. The small circulating fraction runs muscle contraction, nerve
transmission and blood clotting, and the body will strip bone to keep that
fraction correct. Which is why a calcium shortage produces no symptoms for
years and then produces a fracture.

**Recommended intakes:** 1,000 mg a day for adults 19 to 50. Women 51 to
70: 1,200. Everyone over 70: 1,200. And **1,300 mg for ages 9 to 18**,
which is the highest figure on the table, because that is when the
skeleton is being built.

**Absorption is where the plant story gets complicated.** About 30 percent
from dairy and fortified foods. But oxalic and phytic acid bind calcium
into salts you cannot digest, and ODS gives the worked comparison:
"absorption of calcium is only 5% for spinach, whereas it is much higher,
at 27%, for milk." It lists spinach, collard greens, sweet potatoes,
rhubarb and beans as high in oxalic acid.

**And the good news, which is specific.** ODS: "The bioavailability of
calcium from other plants that do not contain these compounds including
broccoli, kale, and cabbage is similar to that of milk, although the
amount of calcium per serving is much lower." **Brassicas are the garden's
real calcium crops.** Kale, broccoli, cabbage and bok choy give up their
calcium as readily as milk does; they just carry less of it, so you need
more of them. Spinach and chard, which look like the same category of
food, do not.

Canned sardines and canned salmon with the bones in are the other strong
non-dairy source, and the bones are the point.

**How it fails.** Osteoporosis: fragile bone, and the falls and fractures
that follow. Also osteomalacia, and rickets in children, though ODS notes
those two are more commonly caused by vitamin D deficiency than calcium
deficiency. And remember the coupling: without adequate vitamin D you
cannot absorb calcium properly however much of it you eat. At this
latitude those two questions are the same question.

## Part six: what storing and preserving cost

You now know the nutrient content of fresh food. Almost nothing you store
stays fresh, so the next question is what survives the winter.

The blunt version: **energy survives, minerals mostly survive, and the
fragile vitamins do not.** Starch and fat are chemically stable; a stored
potato has the same calories in March as in September. Iron and calcium
are elements and cannot be destroyed, only leached away into water. It is
the vitamins, and above all vitamin C, that get destroyed by heat, oxygen,
light and time.

Army Regulation 40-25 names the vulnerable set in a single line, when
instructing ration planners to "compensate for losses of nutrients, such
as ascorbic acid, thiamin, riboflavin, niacin, and pyridoxine (vitamin
B6), which may occur during storage." Ascorbic acid is at the head of
that list for a reason.

### The USDA retention figures, and the one lesson in them

USDA's Agricultural Research Service publishes a Table of Nutrient
Retention Factors giving, for about 290 foods and 26 nutrients, the
proportion of each nutrient that survives each cooking method. It defines
"true retention" as "the proportion of the nutrient remaining in the
cooked food in relation to the nutrient originally present in the raw
food."

Its own worked example, for vitamin C in greens: raw spinach holds 28 mg
per 100 g; the retention factor for boiled greens is 85 percent; so boiled
spinach holds 28 times 0.85, which is 23.8 mg per 100 g.

But the most useful thing in that table is not any single number. It is
the comparison between two adjacent rows.

- **Greens boiled with the cooking water used:** every nutrient in the
  table retained at 95 to 100 percent.
- **Greens boiled and drained:** the same nutrients fall away, and the
  worst-hit drop to **30 percent.**

And for potatoes:

- **Boiled in the skin:** 95 to 100 percent retained across the board.
- **Boiled pared and drained:** down to 55 percent for the worst-hit
  nutrients.
- **Baked in the skin:** down to 70 percent.

**The single highest-value habit in this entire guide is therefore: keep
the cooking water and cook things in their skins.** Not because draining
is wrong, but because the water-soluble vitamins and a good share of the
minerals are, quite literally, in the water you are pouring down the sink.
Soup, stew and pan gravy are not peasant thrift for its own sake. They are
the difference between 95 percent retention and 30.

### Which method costs what

[Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) is the guide for how to
preserve things; read it for the procedures. What it does not cover, and
what belongs here, is the nutritional side of choosing between them:

- **Root cellaring** costs no heat at all, which protects the heat-labile
  vitamins better than any processing method. What it costs instead is
  time: vitamin C declines with prolonged storage even unheated.
- **Freezing** is the best of the four for nutrient retention overall,
  because it is a brief scald followed by cold. The blanching step that
  Keeping What You Grew insists on is itself a heat step and does cost
  some vitamin C, and it is still worth doing, because the enzymes it
  stops would otherwise destroy more.
- **Drying** removes water and concentrates everything left per gram. It
  is a sustained heat exposure, so the fragile vitamins take a real hit,
  but minerals, protein, fat and energy come through concentrated.
- **Canning** is the hottest process of the four and costs the most
  vitamin C, and in the case of pressure canning, by a good margin. It
  buys the longest and most reliable shelf life of any method. That is the
  trade, and it is usually worth making.
  [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) covers why none of
  those temperatures are negotiable.

**Do not let nutrient retention talk you out of a safe process.** A jar
of green beans that lost half its vitamin C is food. A jar of green beans
processed at a lower temperature to protect the vitamin C is a botulism
risk, and botulism is a lethal hazard while a lower vitamin C figure is
an arithmetic one. Follow the tested process, then make up the vitamin C
somewhere else.

And the somewhere else is worth naming: the fresh things that grow in the
cold. Kale, cabbage, Brussels sprouts and the other overwintering
brassicas carry real vitamin C, keep growing at this latitude through the
winter, and do not have to survive any process at all.
[The Growing Calendar Where You Live](/library#the-growing-calendar) covers when
they go in.

## Part seven: recognising trouble

This is what the hazard rating on this guide is about, so I will be
direct about the limits of it. **Nothing in this section is a diagnosis
and nothing in it is a substitute for a clinician.** What follows is a
list of things that should make you stop reasoning about food and start
talking to somebody qualified.

### Unintended weight loss

This is the first and most important sign, and it has a published
threshold.

MedlinePlus, the National Library of Medicine's consumer health service,
defines unintentional weight loss as losing about 10 pounds, or 5 percent
of your usual body weight, over six to twelve months or less, without
knowing why. Its advice is to contact a health care provider if you have
lost that much without a reason, or if there are other symptoms alongside
it. A 2011 clinical review in CMAJ uses a comparable threshold for older
adults: a loss of 5 percent of body weight in one month, or 10 percent
over six months or longer.

**If a person is losing weight without trying, the answer is a clinician,
not a bigger garden.** Unexplained weight loss is a general medical
warning sign with a long list of possible causes, most of which have
nothing to do with what is on the plate. The CMAJ review's approach is
"a comprehensive history together with a physical examination should be
the first step," with testing directed by what that turns up. Treating it
as a food problem and quietly eating more is how a treatable illness gets
six months of head start.

Weigh yourself, or the person you are worried about, on the same scale at
the same time of day, and write it down. A trend over months is real
information. A single reading is not.

### Fatigue out of proportion to the work

Everybody is tired after a hard day. The signal is tiredness that does not
match the day, that does not resolve with rest, or that is new.

The reason to take this seriously rather than push through it is the
middle stage of iron deficiency described above: measurably impaired work
performance and cold intolerance can arrive while the ordinary blood count
is still normal. Fatigue is also an early symptom of vitamin C deficiency,
of B12 deficiency, and of a great many things that are not nutritional at
all. It is a symptom that means "get looked at," not a symptom you can
decode at home.

### Poor healing

Wounds that are slow to close, or that reopen, are worth noticing
specifically because of the collagen connection: poor wound healing is on
the ODS list of scurvy signs, alongside bleeding gums, bruising and
petechiae. **The gums are the giveaway.** Bleeding, swollen or inflamed
gums in someone who has been eating out of a winter store, with no fresh
food for weeks, is a specific combination worth raising quickly.
[Bleeding and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) covers the wound care side;
this is about the healing side.

### The other things worth naming

- **Night vision that has got worse**, particularly in someone eating
  little animal food and few orange or dark green vegetables. The first
  sign of vitamin A deficiency.
- **Numbness or tingling in hands or feet** in someone eating little or no
  animal food. This is the B12 sign that can appear without anaemia and
  that can become permanent. Do not wait on this one.
- **A visible swelling at the front of the neck.** Goitre.
- **Feeling cold when others are not**, which is on the ODS list of iron
  deficiency effects.
- **Bone pain, or a fracture from a small fall.**

### The honest boundary

Every one of those has a dozen explanations, most of them not
nutritional. This guide can tell you which nutrient *could* produce which
sign, and that is genuinely useful for knowing what to mention. It cannot
tell you which one is happening, and neither can you, and neither can
anyone without tests.

**Do not treat a suspected deficiency by taking large doses of the
nutrient you suspect.** Vitamin A is the clearest case of why: preformed
vitamin A at high doses is genuinely toxic and causes birth defects, so
self-treating a suspected deficiency with high-dose supplements can do
more harm than the deficiency. A suspected deficiency is a reason to be
tested, not a reason to dose.

## Part eight: who is not an average adult

Everything above is the arithmetic for one healthy adult. A household plan
built on one adult's numbers will underfeed several kinds of person, and
here is who, from the current Dietary Guidelines:

**Children.** Not small adults. Per kilogram of body weight their needs
are higher, and specific nutrients matter for development in ways they do
not for a grown body. The Guidelines note for middle childhood that
"full-fat dairy products are important for children to help meet energy
needs and support brain development," which is to say that the low-fat
habits an adult might adopt are actively wrong for a child. The calcium
recommendation peaks at 1,300 mg a day from ages 9 to 18, higher than at
any point in adult life, because that is when the skeleton is built.

**Adolescents.** The Guidelines: "Adolescence is a rapid growth period
with increased needs for energy, protein, calcium, and iron, especially
for girls due to menstruation. Adequate calcium and vitamin D are vital
for peak bone mass." The iron table bears this out: girls aged 14 to 18
are at 15 mg a day, then 18 mg from 19.

**Pregnancy.** The Guidelines name "iron, folate, and iodine as top
priorities," and advise talking to a health care professional about a
daily prenatal vitamin. Two hazards from this guide land specifically
here: iodine deficiency during pregnancy and early infancy can cause
irreversible effects, and excess preformed vitamin A causes birth
defects. **Pregnancy is not a situation for working it out from a
guide.**

**Lactation.** Increases both energy and nutrient needs. The vitamin C
recommendation goes from 75 mg to 120 mg, the highest figure on that
table. Note also that the Dietary Guidelines recommends a daily 400 IU
vitamin D supplement for breastfed infants, starting shortly after birth,
because breast milk does not supply enough.

**Older adults.** The trap is the opposite shape. The Guidelines: "Some
older adults need fewer calories but still require equal or greater
amounts of key nutrients such as protein, vitamin B12, vitamin D, and
calcium." Less food, same or more nutrients, which means every calorie has
to carry more. The calcium recommendation rises to 1,200 mg, and the
vitamin D recommendation to 800 IU over 70.

**Anyone ill or recovering.** Illness, infection, injury and healing all
change requirements, usually upward and sometimes in specific directions.
Army Regulation 40-25 excludes exactly this group from its own allowances,
noting they "may not apply to personnel requiring special dietary
treatment for conditions such as infection, chronic disease, trauma,
unusual stress, pregnancy, lactation, or weight reduction."

**Anyone eating little or no animal food.** The Guidelines is specific:
vegetarian diets "often fall short in vitamins D and E, choline, and
iron," while vegan diets "show broader shortfalls in vitamins A, D, E, B6,
and B12; riboflavin; niacin; choline; calcium; iron; magnesium;
phosphorus; potassium; zinc; and protein," with the advice to "monitor
nutrient status periodically, especially for iron, vitamin B12, vitamin D,
calcium, and iodine." A plant-based diet is entirely workable and
billions of people eat one. It is workable *deliberately*, with attention
to a known list, and not by accident.

**The practical rule: size a household's food to the largest requirement
in it, not to the average.** An average is not a person. The teenager and
the pregnant woman and the recovering patient are not going to be fed by a
plan built on the household mean.

## What the simulation models

The medical system in HumanityOS carries one record for this whole
subject, and its limits are worth knowing so you do not mistake the game
for the world.

The condition is `malnutrition` in `data/medical.ron`. It is rated
severity "moderate", affects the whole body, and presents as three status
effects: `hungry`, `weakness` and `slow`. Its description reads "Chronic
dietary deficiency. Muscles waste and immune system weakens." Recovery
time is 604,800 seconds, which is seven days. It has no untreated
progression, which is a simplification: real progressive undernutrition
does not plateau.

The treatment is the `nutrition_therapy` procedure in the same file. It
requires the `medicine` skill at level 2, needs no tools, consumes two
units of `bread_0` and one of `dried_meat_0`, takes 7,200 seconds (two
hours) and succeeds 90 percent of the time. Its description is a
"Structured caloric intake plan to reverse malnutrition."

Notice that the consumables are bread and dried meat: a grain and an
animal protein, which is the complementary pairing this guide has been
describing, plus the one class of food that carries B12. That is a better
piece of design than it looks.

Notice also what the model cannot represent. It has one malnutrition
state, so it cannot distinguish running out of energy from running out of
iodine, and those are completely different problems on completely
different timescales. The seven-day recovery is right for an energy
deficit and wrong by years for a B12 reserve. And the per-crop nutrition
data in `data/food/crop_nutrition.ron` carries energy, protein, fat and
carbohydrate only, and **not one micronutrient**, so the entire second
half of this guide is outside what the simulation can currently compute. The
game can tell you whether you grew enough calories. It cannot yet tell you
whether you grew enough vitamin C, and in real life that is the question
that kills people.

## What can go wrong

- **The garden produced beautifully and the winter was hungry anyway.**
  Almost always a density problem rather than a yield problem. Weigh what
  you actually grew and run it through the per-100-gram table. A summer of
  salad crops and a summer of staples can look identical in a photograph
  and differ by a factor of ten in calories.
- **You are eating constantly and still losing weight.** First, see the
  section on unintended weight loss, because that is a medical question
  before it is a dietary one. If the cause is dietary, it is energy
  density: too much water and fibre, not enough starch, fat or oil. That
  is fixed with grains, tubers, seeds and fat, not with more vegetables.
- **The protein arithmetic works but the meals do not satisfy.** Usually
  fat. A day can clear every protein target and still feel thin because
  there is almost no fat in it. See the four-crop day above and note what
  one handful of seeds did.
- **Nothing green from November to March.** This is the vitamin C setup.
  Plant overwintering brassicas, and keep the stored crops that carry some
  vitamin C, and if there is genuinely a gap, that is a conversation with
  a clinician about whether a supplement is warranted, not a thing to
  ride out.
- **You switched to a fancy salt.** Check whether it is iodised. Most
  specialty salts are not.
- **Somebody in the household stopped eating animal food and nobody
  planned for it.** B12 is the urgent one, because its fuse is years long
  and some of its damage is permanent. This is a conversation to have
  early.
- **You boiled everything and threw the water away.** You poured a
  measurable fraction of the year's vitamins down the drain. Keep the
  liquid, or steam, or cook things in their skins.
- **The published figures did not match your experience.** Correct. They
  are population estimates built on reference bodies, and every source
  that publishes one says so. Your own weight trend over months, measured
  on the same scale, is better evidence about you than any table is.

## How you know it worked

- You can state, in grams, roughly what you eat in a day, and roughly what
  its energy content is.
- Your stores contain a staple that carries energy, a legume that carries
  protein, and something that carries fat, rather than only vegetables.
- You know which of the seven nutrients in Part five your own diet is
  weakest on, and you can say why.
- You know whether your salt is iodised.
- Your weight is stable across a season of hard work, measured rather than
  guessed.
- When something is off, your first move is to a clinician and your second
  is to the garden, rather than the other way round.

The purpose of all this arithmetic is not to make eating complicated. It
is to let you stop worrying about it on good evidence. A household that
has done these sums once, and grows staples, legumes and a fat crop
deliberately, can eat by habit for years and be right. A household that
has never done them can garden enthusiastically for a decade and be one
bad winter from a real problem, without ever having had a reason to
suspect it.

NIH, FDA, the Department of Defense) are in the public domain under 17 USC
105 and are quoted directly. MedlinePlus and the journal article are
restated in our own words.

the current edition):

- https://realfood.gov/ (landing page listing the current documents)

### What could not be sourced

- **A latitude threshold for winter vitamin D synthesis.** The widely
  repeated claim that no vitamin D is synthesised above roughly 37 degrees
  north between November and February does not appear on the NIH Office of
  Dietary Supplements vitamin D fact sheet, which lists season, day length
  and time of day as factors without giving a cutoff. The guide therefore
  reasons from the local solar figures instead and states no threshold.
- **How many months the liver's vitamin A reserve lasts.** ODS describes
  the store and notes that blood retinol does not fall until it is nearly
  depleted, but publishes no duration. None is stated here.
- **Whether complementary proteins must share a meal.** FDA's guidance
  says "at the same meal or during the same day." Whether those are fully
  equivalent is still an open research question and the guide says so
  rather than picking a side.
- **The numeric Acceptable Macronutrient Distribution Ranges.** The
  National Academies chapters that were opened describe the AMDR and
  recommend its removal but do not restate the percentage ranges in the
  text available, so no percentages are quoted here.
- **dietaryguidelines.gov itself would not load** during this session,
  timing out repeatedly. The current guidelines were obtained from
  realfood.gov, which the USDA announcement names as the distribution
  site, and the 2015-2020 edition from health.gov. The NIOSH criteria
  document for occupational heat exposure, which would have given a
  metabolic-rate-by-workload table, returned 403 and is not cited.
