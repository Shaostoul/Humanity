# Reading the Weather

The National Weather Service's forecasts draw on satellites, radar and
computer models, and for anything more than a few hours ahead they will
beat anything you can see from your doorstep (general knowledge). But
the sky over your head is the newest information there is. It tells
you whether the clouds building this afternoon are turning into a
storm, whether the barometer that has been falling since breakfast means
rain by evening, and whether a clear, still evening will end in fog. A
person who can read those signs makes better choices about the next few
hours: when to bring in the washing, when to get off the hill, when to
cover the seedlings, when to go indoors.

This guide teaches that reading, for the next several hours where you
stand. It takes its facts from the National Weather Service's online
weather school, JetStream (now hosted on noaa.gov), and other National
Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) pages, from the National
Weather Service glossary, and from the Federal Aviation Administration's
*Aviation Weather Handbook*, written to teach pilots the weather they fly
through. All are United States government publications. Two parts are
restated rather than quoted: JetStream's cloud definitions, which it
takes from the World Meteorological Organization's International Cloud
Atlas, and the FAA handbook, which was prepared under contract. Where
something is general practice or our own reading, the text says so.

Several other guides sit beside this one. [The Climate Where You
Live](/library#the-climate-where-you-live) is the long run, the averages and
the frost dates; this guide is the next afternoon. [The Hazards Where
You Live](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) covers the warnings and the
warning time for your place. [Knowing Which Way Is
North](/library#knowing-which-way-is-north) lets you name a wind's direction,
and [The Sky as an Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument) uses the sun
and stars. [Sound and Hearing](/library#sound-and-hearing) explains why thunder
is heard after the flash, and [Cold and
Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) what wind and rain do to a body.

## First: the warnings that beat your own eyes

Reading the sky adds to the official forecast and warnings. It never
replaces them, and a warning always wins over your own reading (our
rule, and the point of this section).

**Know the two words.** NOAA puts it simply: "In general, a Watch means
impacts are possible; a Warning means impacts are expected or
happening." A watch means get ready and know what you will do; a warning
means do it now.

**Tornadoes.** The same NOAA page spells out both words for tornadoes: "A
Tornado Watch means a tornado is possible: know your safe place and be
ready to act quickly if a Warning is issued. A Tornado Warning means a
tornado is happening or about to happen - immediately seek shelter in
your safe place!" The National Weather Service says where that safe
place is:

- At home: your basement, a safe room, or an interior room away from
  windows.
- Outside: get inside a sturdy building at once. Sheds and storage
  buildings are not safe, and neither is a mobile home or a tent.
- In a vehicle: it is not safe. Drive to the closest shelter; if you
  cannot reach one, either get down in the car and cover your head, or
  leave the car for a low spot such as a ditch or ravine.

Choose your safe place, and show everyone in the house, before the
season's storms begin (general practice).

**Have a way to hear warnings.** NOAA advises turning on Wireless
Emergency Alerts on your phone to receive warnings. NOAA Weather Radio
broadcasts the warnings, watches and forecasts of your nearest National
Weather Service office around the clock; JetStream notes that it needs a
special receiver, not an ordinary AM/FM radio, and that most receivers
sound an alert tone when a watch or warning goes out. JetStream also
reminds us that detailed forecasts are accurate only out to seven days
at best, so check again before you set out.

**Lightning.** JetStream's lightning safety page is blunt: "If you hear
thunder, then lightning is near." It adds that most people struck are
struck not at the height of a storm but before or after its peak, and
that lightning reaches far from the storm itself: "Lightning can strike
more than 10 miles away from the location of rainfall." So do not wait
for the rain to start before you go in, and do not come out just because
it has stopped. And:

- At the first thunder, go to safe shelter: a substantial building with
  electricity or plumbing, or an enclosed, metal-topped vehicle with the
  windows up. Sheds, picnic shelters, carports, porches, convertibles
  and open vehicles such as tractors and golf carts are not safe. Rubber
  soles and rubber tyres give no protection.
- Stay inside until 30 minutes after the last thunder.
- Indoors, stay off corded phones and anything plugged in, keep away
  from plumbing (no bath, shower or washing up), and keep away from
  windows and doors. A cell phone is safe to use.
- If you are caught outside, there is no safe place, only less bad ones.
  Never lie flat on the ground, never shelter under a lone tree or a
  rock overhang, get out of and away from water, and keep away from wire
  fences, power lines and windmills.

**Flash floods.** JetStream says more people die in floods than in any
other weather hazard except heat, and gives the numbers: "Just six
inches of fast-moving flood water can knock you off your feet, 12 inches
can carry away a car, and 24 inches can pick up an SUV or truck." Nearly
half of flash flood deaths involve vehicles. Never drive or walk into
flooded road: you cannot see how deep it is or whether the road beneath
is still there. Never go around a barrier. If your vehicle stalls in
rising water, leave it and get to higher ground. Do not camp or park
beside streams and dry washes when storms threaten, take special care
at night, and remember that a flash flood can develop within minutes.
JetStream's slogan is Turn Around Don't Drown.

**Cold and heat.** [Cold and Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia)
explains why wind and wet are what turn a cold day dangerous, and what to
do about it. [Heat and How It Moves](/library#heat-and-how-it-moves) gives
the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke and what to do for each. On
heat, the National Weather Service also says it is never safe to leave a
child, a disabled person or a pet locked in a car, even in winter.

**Reading is not chasing.** Watching a storm build from a safe place is
the skill. Driving towards one is a different activity with different
training. The National Weather Service trains volunteer storm spotters
through its SKYWARN programme; the training is free and usually takes
about two hours.

## What you need

Your eyes, a watch, a notebook, and a way to tell north from south. A
thermometer helps, and a barometer helps most of all: an ordinary dial
(aneroid) barometer from a hardware store is enough. The National
Weather Service's forecast and its current observations for the nearest
station complete the kit. (General practice.)

## Pressure: the barometer's slow story

Air has weight, and a barometer measures it. JetStream gives the units
you will meet: weather broadcasts in the United States use inches of
mercury, and standard pressure at sea level is 29.92 inches; weather
maps use millibars, and standard sea-level pressure is 1013.2 millibars.
A hectopascal is the same as a millibar.

Pressure falls as you climb, so, as JetStream explains, every weather
station converts its reading to what it would be at sea level, so that
stations at different heights can be compared. A home barometer reads
the pressure where it hangs. Set it once to the sea-level figure from a
nearby official station, then watch how it changes (general practice).
The change is what matters, far more than the number.

**Pressure moves every day on its own.** JetStream describes a
twice-daily rise and fall caused by the sun's heat, a kind of daily
tide in the air: pressure is lowest around 4 in the morning and 4 in the
afternoon and highest around 10 in the morning and 10 at night, and the
swing is largest near the equator. So a reading at 4 p.m. lower than
one at 10 a.m. may be nothing more than that daily tide. Compare
readings taken at the same time of
day, and treat as a signal only a change bigger than the usual daily
swing where you live; your log (below) will show you how big that is
(our reading of JetStream's description).

**Falling and rising.** JetStream reprints the barometer rules a writer
named Brewer set down in England in 1848, and they still read well. The
summary line is: "Its motion upwards, indicates the approach of fine
weather; its motion downwards, indicates the approach of foul weather."
Among the rest: in fair weather, a barometer that falls a lot and stays
low means much rain in a few days, and probably wind; a sudden fall
means high wind; and when it is unsteady, expect unsteady weather.

Why it works: near low pressure, air flows inward and has nowhere to go
but up, and rising air cools and forms cloud and rain; around high
pressure, air sinks and warms, and clouds dry up. That is JetStream's
explanation, and NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory gives the same one.

**How fast is fast.** Official weather observations add a remark when
pressure is falling rapidly: the FAA's handbook defines it as a fall of
at least 0.06 inch of mercury an hour, with a change of 0.02 inch or more
at the time of the observation, and the same for a rapid rise. That is
about 2 millibars an hour (our arithmetic). A fall that fast is a reason
to look at the latest forecast and warnings straight away (our reading).

**Where the rules do not hold.** JetStream warns that the rules work in
many places but not everywhere. In the United States the largest
pressure changes with storms happen in Alaska and the northern half of
the country, and "In the tropics, except for tropical cyclones, there is
very little day-to-day pressure change, and none of the rules apply."

## Wind

**A wind is named for where it comes from.** JetStream's example: a west
wind of 15 mph is blowing from the west. A flag streams away from the
wind, leaves blow away from it, and aircraft take off and land into it. To name a wind, face into it and find which way you are facing
([Knowing Which Way Is North](/library#knowing-which-way-is-north)).

**Wind circles highs and lows.** JetStream explains that the Earth's
rotation turns moving air to the right in the Northern Hemisphere and to
the left in the Southern. The result, north of the equator, is wind
blowing clockwise around high pressure and counter-clockwise around low
pressure, crossing the lines of equal pressure slightly, outward from a
high and inward towards a low. The closer together those lines, the
stronger the wind. It follows that in the Northern Hemisphere, if you
stand with your back to the wind, the low pressure lies to your left and
a little ahead of you (our derivation from JetStream's description; the
rule is often called Buys Ballot's law). In the Southern Hemisphere it
is on your right.

**Turning winds.** The National Weather Service glossary calls a
clockwise shift of the wind veering (south to west, say) and a
counter-clockwise shift backing (south to east).

**Fronts turn the wind.** A front is the boundary between two masses of
air, and the FAA's handbook describes what happens at the surface as one
passes: temperature changes, winds converge, and pressure usually falls
as the front approaches and rises after it passes. For a typical warm
front (the handbook is written for pilots in the United States), it
describes the wind from the south-southeast ahead of the front and from
the south-southwest after it; for a typical cold front, gusty, shifting
winds as it passes and then wind from the west-northwest behind it. So a wind that veers from
south round to west-northwest, with the temperature dropping and the
barometer starting to rise, is a cold front going by (our summary of the
handbook).

**Local winds.** On calm, sunny summer days near the coast the FAA
describes a sea breeze, blowing from the cooler sea towards the warmer
land, and at night and in the early morning a weaker land breeze blowing
back out. In hilly country, the handbook says, the cooler, denser air
sinks into the valleys overnight and collects there. Local winds like
these are easiest to pick out when the large-scale winds are weak, and
on such days they can matter more to you than the weather map (the
handbook says the first; the second is our reading).

## Clouds

JetStream sorts clouds into the ten basic types of the World
Meteorological Organization's cloud atlas, by height. In our own words:

| Height | Type | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| High (ice) | Cirrus | White wisps and filaments, often silky |
| High | Cirrocumulus | Small ripples or grains, each narrower than your little finger held at arm's length |
| High | Cirrostratus | A thin whitish veil, often across the whole sky; usually makes a halo around the sun or moon |
| Middle | Altocumulus | White or grey patches or rolls, each one to five degrees wide (wider than the little finger, narrower than three fingers) |
| Middle | Altostratus | A grey sheet; the sun shows as if through frosted glass, with no halo and no shadows on the ground |
| Middle | Nimbostratus | A dark grey layer with steady rain or snow, thick enough to hide the sun |
| Low | Cumulus | Heaped, cauliflower clouds with flat dark bases |
| Low | Stratocumulus | Grey patches or rolls with darker shading, each more than five degrees wide |
| Low | Stratus | A flat grey layer that may drizzle |
| Low to high | Cumulonimbus | The thunderstorm cloud: a tower, often with a flattened anvil top |

The finger widths come from the same page, and the way to measure an
angle with your hand is in [The Sky as an
Instrument](/library#the-sky-as-an-instrument).

What the clouds say about the next few hours:

- **Small cumulus that stay small.** JetStream describes fair-weather
  cumulus over land on clear days: they appear in the morning, grow, and
  more or less dissolve towards evening.
- **Cumulus that keep climbing.** A cumulus that grows taller and taller
  through the day can be a thunderstorm starting. The FAA handbook calls
  the first stage of a thunderstorm cell the towering cumulus stage, and
  the whole life of a single cell is typically about 30 minutes. The
  anvil top forms as the storm matures, and is often what is left when
  it dies. Watch the tallest clouds after midday on warm, humid days
  (general practice), and take thunder as your signal to go in.
- **A sky that thickens and lowers for hours.** The FAA handbook
  describes cirriform and stratiform clouds ahead of a warm front, with
  light to moderate rain, snow or drizzle and a barometer that keeps
  falling until the front passes; JetStream describes nimbostratus as
  thickened altostratus. Put together: wisps of cirrus, then a veil of
  cirrostratus with a halo, then a grey altostratus sheet that dims the
  sun, then a dark, low nimbostratus and steady rain. That sequence over
  many hours, with a falling barometer, means rain is on its way (our
  summary of the two sources). Warm fronts move slowly, the handbook
  says, typically 10 to 25 mph.
- **A line of towering clouds.** The handbook describes a
  cold front as often bringing a narrow band of showers and
  thunderstorms, sometimes a squall line, followed by clearing skies,
  cooler air and gusty winds. Cold fronts move faster, typically 25 to
  30 mph and occasionally up to 60.
- **Rings around the sun or moon.** A halo, a large ring, is made by
  cirrostratus. A corona, a small coloured ring close to the sun or moon,
  red on the outside, comes from thin altocumulus passing in front of it.
  Both are from JetStream.

## Dew point, fog and frost

The National Weather Service glossary defines the dew point as the
temperature the air must be cooled to before it is saturated: the higher
the dew point, the more moisture in the air. The gap between the air
temperature and the dew point is called the spread, or the dew point
depression. As the FAA handbook explains, the smaller the spread, the
higher the relative humidity; at zero the air is saturated.

The handbook adds a rule worth remembering: fog seldom forms when the
spread is more than 2 C (4 F). So, on an evening when the forecast or a
nearby station shows the temperature only a few degrees above the dew
point, and the night will be clear and calm, expect fog by morning (our
reading of the handbook).

The handbook's fogs:

- **Radiation fog** forms over land on clear nights with light wind and
  moist air near the ground, as the ground cools the air above it to its
  dew point. It is common the morning after rain followed by clearing
  skies. Shallow ground fog usually burns off soon after sunrise, and
  other radiation fog generally clears before noon unless cloud moves in
  over it.
- **Valley fog** forms when cold air drains into valleys overnight; it is
  densest around sunrise, most often in spring and autumn.
- **Advection fog** forms when moist air moves over a colder surface, as
  on many coasts, the West Coast of the United States among them. It is
  more extensive and lasts longer than radiation fog, and can move in at
  any hour.

JetStream separates mist from haze the same way: mist when the spread is
3 F (1.7 C) or less, haze when it is greater.

The same clear, calm nights that make fog make frost when it is cold
enough, and cold air pools in the low spots first. [The Climate Where
You Live](/library#the-climate-where-you-live) covers frost nights and cold air
drainage in detail.

## Thunderstorms

The FAA handbook says a thunderstorm needs three things: enough water
vapour, unstable air, and something to lift it, such as a front, air
forced up a slope, a sea breeze or a converging wind. Its life has three
stages:

1. **Towering cumulus**: a strong updraft builds the cloud upward.
2. **Mature**: rain reaches the ground and drags down a strong downdraft.
   The downdraft spreads along the ground ahead of the storm as a mass of
   cool, gusty air, the gust front. This is when the dangers peak.
3. **Dissipating**: the downdraft takes over, the rain eases, and an
   anvil may be left behind.

So a sudden cool, gusty wind on a warm, humid afternoon, with dark cloud
nearby, means the storm is close; by then you should already be
indoors (our reading of the handbook). The handbook's chapter on
turbulence adds that a gust front can run as far as 15 miles ahead of
the storm's rain, often marked by a low shelf or roll cloud along the
storm's leading edge, or by a line of blowing dust. Storms often come in
clusters or lines that last for hours, with new cells forming as old
ones die, the handbook adds, so one storm passing is no promise that the
next will not follow.

What makes a storm severe is set by the National Weather Service: a
thunderstorm counts as severe when it brings at least one of "Hail that
is one (1) inch in diameter or larger" or "Winds of 58 miles per hour
(mph) or greater". JetStream gives the same hail figure. A tornado has
warnings of its own (above). JetStream also defines a squall, a word you
will hear with storms, as a sudden rise in wind of at least 18 mph that
holds at 25 mph or more for at least a minute, and notes that the word
refers only to that rise in wind, not to any other weather with it.

## The sayings, tested

**"Red sky at night, sailor's delight; red sky in the morning, sailors
take warning."** NOAA's Global Monitoring Laboratory explains the
saying. High pressure tends to trap dust and haze near the ground, and
sunlight passing through that hazy air at sunrise or sunset looks red.
In the middle latitudes, weather systems move from west to east. A red
sky in the morning, looking east, means the high has already passed and
a low, probably with a storm, may be following it in from the west. A
red sky in the evening, looking west, means a high is coming. Then the
condition: "Note that this only works in the belt of westerlies, from
about 30 degrees to 60 degrees latitude in both the northern and
southern hemispheres." In the trade wind belt nearer the equator, it
says, the saying works the other way round.

**"A ring around the moon means rain."** A halo is cirrostratus, and the
FAA handbook puts cirriform clouds ahead of warm fronts. A halo can be
the first sign of a front a day away, or just a patch of high cloud
that comes to nothing; read it together with the barometer (our
reading).

**The barometer rules** above are the sayings that have held up best.

## A worked day

A spring day, somewhere in the Northern Hemisphere's middle latitudes,
with a barometer on the kitchen wall and a notebook (an illustration,
not a record):

| Time | Barometer | Wind | Sky | Temperature / dew point |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7 a.m. | 30.12 in | Light, north-west | Clear | 45 F / 38 F |
| 10 a.m. | 30.14 in | Light, west | Wisps of cirrus from the west | 55 F / 39 F |
| 1 p.m. | 30.06 in | South, freshening | Cirrostratus, halo round the sun | 60 F / 44 F |
| 4 p.m. | 29.96 in | South-south-east, 10 to 15 mph | Grey altostratus, sun dim | 58 F / 48 F |

Reading it. Between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m. the daily tide alone lowers the
barometer somewhat, so check your log: if your usual fall over those
hours is a few hundredths of an inch, then this fall of 0.18 inch over
six hours is a real change, and it is speeding up (0.10 inch in the last
three hours, though still slower than the 0.06 inch an hour that counts
as falling rapidly). The wind has backed from west through south to
south-south-east. The
sky has gone from cirrus to cirrostratus to altostratus, and the dew
point is climbing, so moisture is arriving. Every sign matches the
approach of a warm front. Expect rain this evening or tonight, steady
rather than stormy, and look at the forecast to see what it says about
timing (our reading of the signs in this guide; the figures are made
up for the example).

A summer afternoon looks different. The morning is hot and humid, the
spread small. By 1 p.m. cumulus are rising in towers to the west. At
3 p.m. you hear a distant rumble. That rumble is the warning: go in now,
before the rain, and stay in until 30 minutes after the last thunder.

## Keep a weather log

The fastest way to learn your own sky is a log. Twice a day at the same
times, write down the pressure and how it has changed, the temperature
and dew point (or humidity), the wind's direction and strength, the
clouds and how much of the sky they cover, and anything falling. Then
write what happened next, and what the forecast had said. After a season
you will know how big your daily pressure swing is, which wind brings
your rain, and how much warning your storms give (general practice).
[Keeping Records](/library#keeping-records) covers the habit of a good log.

## Know where your own reading stops

- **Warnings and evacuation orders** come from the National Weather
  Service and local officials, and they win over anything you see (our
  rule).
- **Your reading covers a few hours, at your spot.** A storm on radar
  fifty miles away is invisible to you until it arrives (general
  practice).
- **Severe weather**: go in early. If you want to report it, the
  National Weather Service's SKYWARN training (above) is the way in.
- **At sea, in the mountains and in the air**, use the specialist
  forecasts made for them, the marine, mountain and aviation forecasts
  (general practice).

## How the game models it

The game has weather on worlds with air, and you feel it when you are
outside.

- **The line under the clock** shows the air where you stand: the
  condition (clear, cloudy, rain, storm, snow, fog or sandstorm), the
  temperature, and the wind's speed in metres per second with the point
  of the compass it blows from. Inside your home it reads "Indoors" with
  the home's own temperature and "still air". When the sun or a clear
  night sky makes the air feel warmer or colder than it is, the line adds
  what it feels like.
- **What you feel.** The game's body heat model takes heat away faster
  in wind, in rain and in wet clothes. A roof keeps the rain off, and a
  wall on the side the wind comes from keeps the wind off. Settings >
  Gameplay > Body heat chooses Forgiving, the default, or Realistic;
  Forgiving uses the same weather and shelter but lets your temperature
  swing only half as far, so harm starts much later (only where the real
  balance would have you below about 27 C or above about 43 C) and then
  comes at half the rate for each degree you read past the harm lines.
- **The wind** where you stand is the region's prevailing wind, such as
  the trade winds or the westerlies, plus the weather's own wind.
- **How the weather changes.** Every 6 to 18 hours of game time the game
  picks the next condition at random, with odds set by the season (in
  spring, for example, 35 percent clear, 30 cloudy, 25 rain, 5 fog and 5
  storm), and the change takes half an hour of game time. The weather's
  own wind takes a new, random direction at each change. At the default
  Realistic time speed, a game hour is a real hour. Whether what falls is
  rain or snow is worked out from the air where you stand.
- **Rare events.** About every 18 game hours there is a 35 percent chance
  of an extreme event fitting the season, temperature and wind: a
  thunderstorm, a blizzard, a tornado or a meteor shower. Its name
  replaces the condition on the weather line, and a tornado adds a
  warning to the line when you are near its core. No weather event does
  any harm yet.
- **F11** opens a weather panel for setting the condition and wind by
  hand. It is a development tool for looking at the sky and the sea, not
  a forecast.

The Play mode does not change the weather. "Start every session from the
default home" (Settings > Gameplay) is off by default, so each launch
resumes the game clock where you left it, and the season, and with it
the weather's odds, goes on from there. With that setting on, the clock
restarts at each launch, so the season starts at spring every time (the
first quarter of the game year is spring). The weather itself is not
saved: each launch starts clear and rolls its first change about a
minute of game time in.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: its
weather gives no warning. The air pressure where you stand depends on
the place and your height, not on the weather, so it does not fall
before a storm; clouds do not build or
thicken ahead of a change; there are no fronts, so the wind does not
back or veer before rain; there is no lightning and no thunder, no gust
front, no flash flood, and no fog that forms because the evening was
clear and the dew point close. The next weather is a roll of the dice,
not something on its way that you could have seen coming. Use the game
to practise getting under a roof and out of the wind; learn the reading
from the real sky.

## You own this when

- You know the difference between a watch and a warning, have Wireless
  Emergency Alerts or a NOAA Weather Radio, and check the forecast before
  you go out.
- You know your tornado safe place, and go to it at once when a Tornado
  Warning is issued.
- At the first thunder you go to a building or a hard-topped vehicle, and
  you stay there 30 minutes after the last.
- You never drive or walk into flood water.
- You can set a barometer, read its trend at the same times each day,
  and tell the daily tide from a real fall.
- You can name a wind by where it comes from, say whether it is veering
  or backing, and, in the Northern Hemisphere, point to the low with the
  wind at your back.
- You can name the ten cloud types and say which of them warn of rain,
  of a front, or of a thunderstorm.
- You can read the temperature-dew point spread and predict a foggy
  morning.
- You know the red-sky rule and where it works.
- You keep a weather log, and you have learned what your own place's
  weather does before it changes.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- Choosing a tornado safe place, and showing it to everyone in the house,
  before the storm season.
- That a warning wins over your own reading of the sky, and that official
  forecasts draw on satellites, radar and computer models and beat
  doorstep reading beyond a few hours (general knowledge).
- The kit list; setting a home barometer to a nearby station's
  sea-level figure; keeping a weather log and what to record.
- Comparing pressure readings at the same time of day, and treating only
  changes bigger than the daily swing as signals, from JetStream's
  description of the daily cycle.
- 0.06 inch of mercury an hour as about 2 millibars an hour (arithmetic),
  and looking at the forecast when the pressure falls that fast.
- The Northern Hemisphere rule for finding the low with the wind at your
  back, derived from JetStream's description of the winds.
- A veering wind with falling temperature and rising pressure as a cold
  front passing, summarised from the FAA handbook.
- Local winds mattering more than the weather map on calm days.
- Watching the tallest clouds on warm afternoons.
- The cloud sequence ahead of a warm front, put together from the FAA
  handbook and JetStream.
- A small spread on a clear, calm evening meaning fog by morning, and a
  cool gust on a humid afternoon meaning the storm is close, read from
  the FAA handbook.
- The halo as a possible sign of a front.
- The worked day, whose figures are invented for the example.
- Radar seeing storms you cannot, and using marine, mountain and aviation
  forecasts.
