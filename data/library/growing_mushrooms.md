# Growing Mushrooms

A mushroom is not the whole organism. It is the fruit. The living fungus
is the mycelium, a mass of fine white threads that grows through wood,
straw or compost and digests it. When the mycelium has eaten its way
through its food and the conditions around it change in the right way, it
pushes out mushrooms to shed spores. Growing mushrooms is therefore two
jobs done one after the other: first let the mycelium fill its food in
peace, then change its surroundings so it fruits.

This guide covers the three mushrooms most people start with, and the
simulation in this project grows: oyster, shiitake and the white button
mushroom (the same species sold brown as cremini and, opened up, as
portobello). They eat different things, want different conditions, and
suit different amounts of effort. The easiest start by a wide margin is a
ready-made fruiting block of oyster mushrooms, and most of this guide
explains why that works so you can move on to straw, logs or compost when
you want to.

Temperatures are given in degrees Fahrenheit with Celsius in brackets.

## Two rules before you start

**Grow only what you bought as a known culture, and eat only what grew
from it.** Commercial spawn is grown from stored, tested cultures rather
than from spores, because spores give new and unpredictable strains, and
Penn State describes making spawn as too complex to be practical for an
ordinary grower. So buy spawn or blocks from a supplier who names the
species and strain. Then keep your crop separate from anything picked
wild. A log, a straw bed or a block left outdoors can grow mushrooms you
did not plant. Cornell and the University of Vermont, in their guide for
log growers, tell every grower to pick only from logs they inoculated
themselves and to make a positive identification of every mushroom that
comes off them, and they name the look-alike they mean: Galerina, a small
brown mushroom that grows on wood, usually very decayed wood, and is very
poisonous. Its spore print is brown where shiitake's is white. If anything
on your substrate looks different from the crop you planted, do not eat
it, and ask a university mycologist if you want to know what it is.
Agromisa's growing manual makes the general point plainly: the way a
mushroom lives (on dead matter, on a living host, or in partnership with
tree roots) has nothing to do with whether it is edible, and edible and
poisonous species are found in all three groups. Telling wild mushrooms
apart is a separate skill with much higher stakes, and nothing in this
guide teaches it.

**Give the fruiting space fresh air, for your lungs as well as the
crop's.** Penn State puts the spore output of a single mushroom at up to
4 million spores an hour, and reports that inhaled spores can
cause an allergic reaction in some workers, with flu-like symptoms in
people who have become sensitised, which is why farm pickers wear masks
and why farms blow in extra fresh air an hour or two before harvest. The
medical literature records the same thing going further: a published
case report describes two workers growing oyster mushrooms indoors who
developed an allergic lung inflammation (extrinsic allergic alveolitis,
also called hypersensitivity pneumonitis; in mushroom workers it is
known as mushroom worker's lung), traced to the mushroom's spores
(Mori and colleagues, 1998). Those reports come from farms, not kitchens,
and a single block is a far smaller spore load. The home version of the
same advice, which is our reading rather than a published rule: do not
fruit oysters in a closed bedroom or a small unventilated room you spend
hours in, air the space before you pick, harvest before the caps flatten
and drop heavy spore deposits, and wear a dust mask if you are fruiting
many blocks at once. If you develop a cough, fever or breathlessness that
comes on after time in the fruiting space, stop, see a doctor, and tell
them you grow oyster mushrooms.

## The words growers use

- **Spawn**: mycelium already grown on sterilised grain or sawdust. It is
  the "seed" you mix into the food. Nobody plants spores.
- **Substrate**: the food the mycelium eats. Straw, sawdust, a hardwood
  log, or compost.
- **Spawn run** (also called colonising or incubation): the time the
  mycelium spends spreading through the substrate. The substrate turns
  white as it goes.
- **Pins** (also primordia): the tiny first mushrooms, a few millimetres
  across.
- **Flush** (also called a break): one wave of mushrooms. After a flush
  the mycelium rests and recovers, then fruits again.
- **Fruiting block**: substrate, usually hardwood sawdust with a nutrient
  supplement such as bran, packed into a plastic bag with a filter patch,
  sterilised, spawned, and left until the mycelium has filled it. It is
  sold ready to fruit.

Oysters are forgiving, fast and happy on many materials. Shiitake is the
patient one: a log takes about a year before its first mushroom and then
fruits for years. Button mushrooms are by far the largest mushroom crop
in the United States, over 90 percent of its value in the 2001 to 2002
figures Penn State reports, and also the hardest to start
from nothing at home, because their compost is made at commercial scale,
at temperatures and with a curing process a backyard pile is not built
for (see the button section below).

## The easiest start: an oyster fruiting block

When a block arrives, its mycelium has already finished the spawn run.
The sawdust inside should be solid white. Your job is only to trigger
fruiting and then keep the mushrooms comfortable.

1. **Open it.** The bag stays mostly on and you cut an opening in it,
   because the plastic keeps the block from drying out. Agromisa's manual
   says to open bags as soon as the mycelium has covered the substrate
   completely, to cut away only part of the plastic (less still if your
   air is dry), and not to cut so deep that you slice into the mycelium.
   The supplier's sheet will say where and how to cut; a slit or an X in
   one face is common. Mushrooms form around the edges of the opening
   (Penn State).
2. **Change its world.** Three things tell the mycelium it is time to
   fruit: fresh air (less carbon dioxide), light, and for some oyster
   strains a drop in temperature. Agromisa describes strains of the
   common oyster fruiting reliably after a cold shock, a drop of 9 to 18
   F (5 to 10 C), once colonising is done. Move the block out of wherever
   it was warm and dark and into a place with daylight or a lamp, moving
   air, and high humidity.
3. **Wait for pins.** They appear three or four days after opening
   (Agromisa).
4. **Hold the humidity high while they grow.** Agromisa gives 90 to 95
   percent while pins form, lowered to about 85 percent by bringing in
   fresh air once the pins are about 1 cm across. The University of
   Florida's home guide fruits oysters in a chamber held at 90 percent by
   a humidifier or fogger. Mist the air, the walls and the bag, not the
   mushrooms themselves once they are nearly ready: Agromisa explains that
   a mushroom grows by pulling water out of the mycelium and letting it
   evaporate from its own surface, so soaking a nearly ready mushroom or
   holding the air completely saturated spoils it and shortens its shelf
   life.
5. **Pick them young.** Cornell's growers pick when the gills show but the
   rim of the cap is still curled under. Agromisa puts harvest about five
   days on at 59 to 68 F (15 to 20 C), and two or three days in warmer air.
   Twist or gently pull the whole cluster off, taking as little substrate
   as you can.
6. **Rest it and go again.** The next flush comes from the same cut
   (Cornell), usually five to nine days later (Agromisa). Keep the
   humidity up in between.
7. **Know when it is finished.** Agromisa's rule: keep harvesting as long
   as the mycelium stays white and firm. Three or four flushes are
   typical. When the substrate goes soft and loses its colour, it is spent.

## A shiitake block is different

A shiitake block is not fruited through a slit in its bag. It is fruited
naked, and only after it has changed colour.

Shiitake mycelium starts white, then grows a brown, leathery skin over
the outside of the block. Penn State calls this the browning period and
Agromisa describes the brown coat as doing the job bark does on a log: it
keeps moisture in and contaminants out. Commercially, blocks brown for
about four weeks, at about 66 F (19 C), in air rich in carbon dioxide
(Penn State). A block that arrives still white needs that time. Your
supplier's instructions will say when to take the bag off; after that,
shiitake fruits from the bare block.

To trigger each flush, commercial growers soak the block in cool water,
about 54 F (12 C), for three or four hours, then stand it on a shelf.
Mushrooms are ready to pick seven to eleven days after soaking, and the
peak of one flush comes about 16 to 20 days after the peak of the last.
The first flush may not need a soak if the block browned in its bag,
because it still holds enough water; later flushes do, and each later
soak takes longer, up to 12 hours for the second and 18 for the third
(all Penn State, for commercial synthetic logs). Agromisa's schedule for
shiitake on sterilised substrate triggers fruiting at 50 to 68 F (10 to
20 C), with light and 85 to 95 percent humidity, and then lets the block
rest drier and warmer between flushes. It also notes that a short dry
spell after each harvest keeps contaminants from taking hold where the
mushrooms were picked. With a suitable strain, cool and fairly dry air
(60 to 70 percent) can make shiitake caps crack into a pattern; that is
not a defect, and Agromisa notes the cracked "flower" mushroom is the
most expensive grade in East Asia.

Pick shiitake while the edge of the cap is still slightly curled under,
before it flattens out (Cornell and the University of Vermont), and twist
them off by hand (Penn State).

## What each stage needs

| | Colonising (spawn run) | Triggering and pinning | Growing and picking |
|---|---|---|---|
| Light | Not needed by oyster or button. Shiitake does better with a little toward the end (Agromisa) | Needed for oyster and shiitake | Needed for oyster and shiitake. Button needs none |
| Air | Still air. High carbon dioxide actually helps the mycelium grow | Fresh air. Carbon dioxide must come down | Steady fresh air |
| Humidity | High enough that the surface does not dry | 90 to 95 percent | About 85 percent, never so wet the caps turn clammy or sticky |
| Temperature | Warm: ideally about 70 F (21 C), never above 80 F (27 C) (Cornell). Oyster bags at 75 to 77 F (24 to 25 C) (University of Florida) | A drop in temperature for some oyster strains, and for shiitake | The strain's fruiting range. Cooler air means slower growth |

Two points in that table surprise people. First, the mycelium likes
stale air while it colonises: Agromisa notes that a high carbon dioxide
level favours mycelial growth but not cropping. The same condition that
helps in the first stage ruins the second. Second, the conditions that
make a mushroom fruit are the ones that make mycelium grow worse, which
is why Agromisa says to change them only after the substrate is fully
colonised. Substrate the mycelium has not yet claimed is open to moulds
and bacteria (Penn State). Even stale air has its limit, though: for
shiitake, Agromisa reads misshapen mushrooms in the first flush as a sign
that the spawn run was too short or the carbon dioxide too high during
it.

How much light is enough? A rule of thumb some growers use, Agromisa
reports, is enough to read a newspaper anywhere in the growing space, and
Cornell's is enough to read a book comfortably. In Penn State's
commercial oyster rooms, from the end of the spawn run, 4 hours of light
a day at 50 to 300 lux may be given, which is the light of a dim room
rather than a grow lamp. Keep blocks out of direct sun, for the reason log growers
keep their logs in shade: sun and wind dry the substrate out (Cornell and
the University of Vermont).

## Fresh air, and why stale air ruins the crop

A colonised block breathes like an animal: it takes in oxygen and gives
out carbon dioxide, all day and all night. In a closed cupboard, a sealed
tub or a tent with no air exchange, that gas builds up, and mushrooms
growing in it come out wrong.

The symptom is specific and easy to read. Cornell's growers describe it
exactly: when carbon dioxide is high, oyster and other mushrooms form
long stems and little caps. Agromisa says the same of oysters short of
fresh air or light, and adds that in complete darkness oysters form no
caps at all, only branching stalks like coral.

The numbers growers work to:

- Outside air holds about 0.04 percent carbon dioxide, 400 parts per
  million (ppm) (Penn State).
- Cornell advises keeping fruiting rooms below 1,000 ppm for most species
  and below 800 ppm for oysters, which are fussier.
- Penn State's commercial oyster rooms bring in enough fresh air at
  pinning to get below 700 ppm. Button mushroom pins form once room air is
  brought down to 0.08 percent, 800 ppm, or lower depending on the strain.

How much yield stale air costs was measured in a controlled trial on a
close relative of the oyster mushroom (Pleurotus ferulae, grown in
bottles). Held at 1,000 ppm, the crop gave 102.4 g a bottle, the best of
the trial; held at 2,000 ppm, it gave 75.1 g, about a quarter less, and
took 23 days to grow where the crop at 500 ppm took 16 (Won and
colleagues, 2010). Other strains will differ, but the direction is not in
doubt.

The practical problem is that fresh air and humidity fight each other.
Every litre of fresh air you bring in is usually drier than the air it
replaces, so the more you ventilate, the more water you have to add.
Small growers solve this with a fruiting tent: a shelving unit wrapped in
clear plastic, with a small humidifier inside and a fan or open vents for
air exchange. The plastic holds the moisture, the vents let the carbon
dioxide out, and you balance the two by watching the mushrooms: long
stems and small caps mean more air; pins that dry out and shrivel mean
more water.

## Growing oysters on straw

Once a block has worked, straw is the cheap way to grow a lot more
oysters, and the method is simple enough to do in a kitchen.

1. **Chop and soak.** The University of Florida's home guide cuts wheat
   straw into pieces 4 to 5 inches long and soaks it overnight.
2. **Pasteurise it.** Straw carries moulds and bacteria that would
   outgrow the oyster, so it is heated enough to kill most of them without
   sterilising it. Agromisa holds straw in water at 158 F (70 C) for at
   least 15 minutes, and says 30 to 60 minutes is safer; cooler water or a
   shorter soak does not kill enough. The University of Florida uses 165 F
   (74 C) for one to two hours. Water that hot scalds. Lift the straw in a
   wire basket or mesh bag, drain it away from your body, and wear
   heat-proof gloves; see [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns) if it goes
   wrong.
3. **Drain and cool.** Let it drain for about an hour (University of
   Florida) and cool on a clean surface before spawning. Penn State notes
   that substrate above 95 F (35 C) injures spawn.
4. **Spawn it.** Mix in spawn at 5 to 10 percent of the straw by volume or
   weight (University of Florida). Penn State found that more spawn, up
   to 5 percent of the wet weight, gives higher yields and a faster spawn
   run, because every grain of spawn is a starting point and the faster
   the straw is claimed, the less time moulds have to get in.
5. **Bag it.** Pack it into plastic bags (Penn State's farms use clear or
   black ones) and punch holes
   every few inches for air.
6. **Colonise it.** Keep it dark and warm, 75 to 77 F (24 to 25 C), for two
   to three weeks until the whole bag is white (University of Florida).
7. **Fruit it** exactly like a block: light, fresh air, 90 percent
   humidity, about 68 F (20 C). Mushrooms come out of the holes.

A bag that turns green, black or orange in patches instead of white
points back to the steps above. Penn State notes that substrate which is
only pasteurised is more open to moulds than substrate given further
treatment, and that overheated substrate is taken over by competitors;
Agromisa calls spawning the moment contamination is most likely to get
in, and asks for clean clothes and quick work while bags are open.

## Growing shiitake on logs

Log growing is the old way, and the slow one. Its reward is a stack of
logs that fruits for years with little attention. Cornell and the
University of Vermont's best-practice guide for the northeastern United
States is the source for this section unless another is named.

- **Wood.** Only fresh-cut wood from living, healthy hardwood trees.
  Dead wood, old logs from the yard and firewood do not work, because
  other fungi have already moved in or the wood has dried out. Oak is the
  traditional choice; sugar maple, hophornbeam (ironwood) and beech also
  do well. Cut in winter or early spring, while the tree is dormant: the
  bark holds tighter then, and intact bark is what keeps the log moist
  and keeps competing fungi out.
- **Size.** Logs 4 to 6 inches (10 to 15 cm) across and 3 to 4 feet long
  are the standard. Thicker logs work but take longer to colonise and are
  heavy to handle.
- **Timing.** Inoculate between one day and three weeks after felling,
  and sooner is better. Waiting longer lets other fungi in and dries the
  wood.
- **Inoculating.** Drill holes 7/16 inch across and 1 1/4 inches deep, 3
  inches apart along the log, with rows 3 to 4 inches apart, and fill each
  with sawdust or plug spawn. Seal each hole with hot food-grade wax.
  Growers heat the wax until it just smokes, around 350 to 400 F (177 to
  204 C), and the
  guide warns that hot wax has a low flash point and can catch fire in
  very dry conditions or near sparks, so heat it only as hot as it needs
  to be.
- **Waiting.** Stack the logs in a "laying yard" under dense shade, 75 to
  100 percent (evergreens are best), out of sun and wind, for about a year
  while the mycelium colonises. Penn State gives 6 to 18 months depending
  on the tree, log size, strain, moisture and temperature. In long dry
  spells, soak or sprinkle the logs.
- **Fruiting.** A colonised log fruits by itself in spring and autumn,
  unpredictably. To force a flush, submerge it in water for 24 hours.
  Mushrooms are ready seven to ten days later. Then let the log rest six
  to eight weeks before forcing it again; two or three flushes a year is
  the usual aim.
- **Yield.** Logs produce most in their second and third years, about a
  quarter to half a pound each per flush then, and roughly 3 to 4 pounds
  of fresh mushrooms over about five years. Shiitake stops when the bark
  falls off (Penn State).

Slugs are a standing problem on log shiitake: growers in the guide check
for them morning and evening while mushrooms develop. In spring and fall,
when logs fruit on their own, walk the stacks every few days so no
mushrooms are missed.

## Button mushrooms: compost and casing

Button mushrooms do not eat wood. They eat compost made from straw-bedded
horse manure or from hay and straw, and the compost is the hard part.
Penn State's guide to commercial growing describes it in two phases.
First the pile is wetted and turned for one to two weeks, reaching 155 F
(68 C) and above and 170 to 180 F (77 to 82 C) at its hottest, with a
strong smell of ammonia. Then it is pasteurised to kill pests and
conditioned at 125 to 130 F (52 to 54 C) until the ammonia is gone, because ammonia above about 0.07
percent of the compost holds the spawn back. A home compost pile is not
made that way (see [Your First Compost](/library#your-first-compost) for what
one is), so at home, start from a kit of compost that has already been
made and spawned.

The stages, from Penn State:

1. **Spawn run.** The compost is kept at 75 to 80 F (24 to 27 C) with the
   surface kept moist, and the mycelium grows through it as a white to
   blue-white mass. This takes 13 to 20 days.
2. **Casing.** Spread a layer about 2 inches deep of casing over the
   colonised compost. The casing is peat moss mixed with ground limestone;
   it carries no food and serves as a water reservoir and a place for the
   mycelium to form the thick strands that mushrooms grow from. Penn State
   gives a proportion for scale: one 6 cubic foot bale of peat mixed with
   water and 40 pounds of limestone covers about 125 square feet 2 inches
   deep.
3. **Pinning.** Ventilate as little as possible until the mycelium shows
   at the surface of the casing, then bring in fresh air to bring carbon
   dioxide down. Air too soon and the mushrooms form under the casing and
   come up dirty. Harvestable mushrooms appear 18 to 21 days after casing.
4. **Cropping.** Hold the air at 57 to 62 F (14 to 17 C). Mushrooms come
   in flushes: a harvest of three to five days, then a few days of nothing,
   repeating roughly every seven to ten days. Farms usually pick for 35 to
   42 days. Pick buttons while the veil under the cap is still closed.
   Brown strains left to open are the ones sold as portobellos.

Water is the delicate skill with button mushrooms. The casing must stay
moist, but first-time growers usually overwater, and the surface seals
over and stops the gas exchange the pins need. Penn State's way to judge
it: a mushroom is about 90 percent water, so after each flush put back
roughly nine tenths of the weight you picked, as water. Button mushrooms
need no light; you only need enough to see what you are picking.

## How much you will get

A mushroom harvest is set by how much food you gave the fungus, not by
floor area. So yields are given per block, per log, or per square foot
of bed.

| Crop | Yield | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Oyster on a block or bag | About 20 percent of the wet weight of the substrate, over three or four flushes. A 5 lb (2.3 kg) block gives about 1 lb (0.45 kg) | Agromisa, commercial production |
| Shiitake on a block | 75 to 125 percent of the dry weight of the substrate. A block mixed at about 60 percent water, as Penn State's are, holds about 2 lb of dry material in 5 lb, so 1.5 to 2.5 lb | Penn State, synthetic logs |
| Shiitake on a block, lower estimate | 15 to 35 percent of the wet weight, so 0.75 to 1.75 lb from a 5 lb block | Agromisa |
| Shiitake on a log | A quarter to half a pound per flush in the best years; 3 to 4 lb over about five years | Cornell and University of Vermont |
| Button, cased compost | 5.10 to 5.89 lb per square foot per crop, the US averages for 2022 to 2025 (about 25 to 29 kg per square metre). Anywhere from 0 to 8 lb per square foot depending on the grower | USDA National Agricultural Statistics Service; Penn State |

Growers and researchers call the shiitake figure the biological
efficiency: the weight of fresh mushrooms divided by the dry weight of
the food. It can exceed 100 percent because a fresh mushroom is mostly
water.

These are commercial and research figures. A first home attempt may well
do less.

## Contamination: what to look for and what to throw out

A healthy colonised substrate is white (shiitake turns brown, and that is
healthy too). Anything else growing on it is competing for the same food.
The University of Florida's home guide lists the colours to watch for:
blue, green, black or yellow spots. The commonest is Trichoderma, green
mould, which spreads fast as a greenish growth; Penn State names it among
the competitors that take over substrate that has overheated, and
Agromisa calls green moulds the most common contaminant at spawning and
wherever a bag is cracked. An orange mould is usually Neurospora, which
researchers describe overrunning mushroom farms, often within a week, in
hot and humid weather. Bacteria tend to show as a creamy, shiny growth
(Agromisa, describing cultures), and on oyster mushrooms themselves, a
bacterial blotch turns the caps orange and brittle, worst when the
mushrooms stay wet (Penn State).

What to do:

- **Logs with some competing fungi**: expected. Cornell and the
  University of Vermont note that other wood fungi cannot be kept out of
  logs outdoors, and that shiitake can coexist with them and a log will
  usually still fruit.
- **A small spot on a block that is otherwise white and firm**: watch it.
  If it spreads, the block goes. The sources allow some room here. The
  University of Florida's guide says contaminated bags are best removed
  and discarded, especially highly contaminated ones, but that a partly
  contaminated bag may still give at least one flush. For green mould on
  shiitake blocks, Agromisa's advice is to wash it off with a strong spray
  of water after the harvest and keep the block drier between flushes.
- **A heavily contaminated block or bag**: throw it out. The University of
  Florida's advice is that contaminated bags, especially highly
  contaminated ones, are best removed and discarded. Agromisa's is to take
  contaminated bags and spent substrate out of the growing space
  immediately and put them well away, because the moulds and the flies
  they attract spread to fresh substrate. Take
  it out whole, in its bag, rather than breaking it open indoors: the
  colour of a mould is usually its spores (Cornell and the University of
  Vermont make this point about green mould).
- **Mushrooms from a badly contaminated block**: do not pick from it. None
  of the sources here suggests harvesting from a heavily contaminated bag;
  they discard it, and that is the rule this guide follows.
- **Prevention** is almost all of it: clean hands and tools, full
  pasteurisation of straw, spawning only after the substrate has cooled,
  plenty of spawn so the mycelium claims the food fast, and keeping blocks
  somewhat drier between flushes (Agromisa).

## After the last flush

Spent substrate still has value. Agromisa notes it makes a good soil
conditioner, and Penn State describes spent button mushroom compost as a
valued product in horticulture. Put it in the compost pile or dig it into
a bed well away from where you grow mushrooms, so its moulds and flies do
not find the next crop. See [Your First Compost](/library#your-first-compost).

## Troubleshooting

- **Long stems, small caps**: too much carbon dioxide, not enough light,
  or both. More fresh air first.
- **Coral-like stalks and no caps**: no light at all.
- **Pins form, then dry out and shrivel**: the air is too dry. Raise the
  humidity.
- **Cracked caps on shiitake**: cool air at 60 to 70 percent humidity,
  with a strain that does it. Harmless, and prized in some markets; raise the humidity if you would
  rather have smooth caps.
- **Nothing happens after a block is opened**: the usual causes are the
  missing triggers from the stage table: too little light, too little
  fresh air, or no drop in temperature for a strain that needs one.
- **Button mushrooms coming up dirty through the casing**: fresh air came
  in too early, or the casing was too dry.
- **Tiny flies around the blocks**: likely fungus gnats (sciarid flies),
  one of the insect pests Penn State names for oyster growers. Its advice
  is hygiene
  and getting spent substrate out promptly, because old substrate is where
  their numbers build up.

## How the game models it

The garden in this project grows all three mushrooms, and its numbers
come from the sources in this guide.

- A mushroom's "plant" is its unit of substrate: one 5 lb fruiting block
  for oyster and shiitake, one square foot of cased compost for button.
  Yields are the ones in the table above (`data/garden/yields.ron`,
  MUSHROOMS).
- Each mushroom rack stands in its own fruiting tent with a small
  humidifier holding 90 percent (`data/garden/humidity.ron`). A fungus
  kept in drier air than its window loses yield, on a curve taken from a
  measured humidity trial on king oyster.
- Every block breathes out carbon dioxide at a rate measured for oyster
  mushroom substrate, and each tent has a fan switched by a carbon dioxide
  controller that holds it at 900 ppm. Air above a crop's limit costs
  harvest at the rate Won and colleagues measured, scaled by the garden's
  Off, Gentle and Realistic setting
  ([Ship Life Support](https://github.com/Shaostoul/Humanity/blob/main/docs/design/ship-life-support.md)).
- The blocks, beds and mushrooms are drawn from what each species looks
  like at each stage (`data/plants_visual.ron`, `fungi`): an oyster block
  fruits from the cut in its bag, a shiitake block browns and fruits bare,
  a button bed shows its casing.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: every
flush is the same size (real later flushes usually vary), the shiitake
and button mushroom borrow the oyster's measured responses to humidity and
carbon dioxide, and a block colonises on its shelf rather than in a
separate warm, dark room.
