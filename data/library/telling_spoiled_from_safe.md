# Telling Spoiled From Safe

Take the container of Sunday's leftovers out of the refrigerator, lift
the corner of the lid, and smell it.

Everybody does this. It feels like a test. It feels like the moment where
you find out. What you have actually done is measure one thing and then
draw a conclusion about a different thing, and the gap between those two
things is what this document is about.

Your nose is a good instrument. It detects the waste products of
organisms that are busy breaking your food down. It is nearly useless at
detecting the organisms and poisons that will send you to a hospital,
because those mostly do not produce a smell, and in several important
cases the food they are in tastes perfectly normal right up until it
hurts you.

**This document will not give you a way to smell your way to safety,
because there is not one.** What it will give you is the shape of the
problem: what spoilage actually is, which of your senses are worth
trusting and for exactly what, the specific hazards that give no sensory
warning at all, what cooking can and cannot undo, and a decision
procedure you can run in front of an open refrigerator in ten seconds.

It will also be honest about the size of the other error. Throwing away
good food is not free. FDA puts it at "Between 30 and 40 percent of food
in the United States goes uneaten," which it describes as "as much as 20
pounds of food per person per month," adding up to "the equivalent of
$165 billion in food each year." A guide that just says "when in doubt,
throw it out" and stops has told you to solve one problem by making
another one worse. The useful skill is knowing which doubts are the real
ones.

## The central idea: spoiled and dangerous are two different axes

Here is the sentence that reorganises everything else, and it comes
straight from FDA:

> "You may be surprised to learn that food can make you very sick even
> when it doesn't look, smell, or taste spoiled. That's because foodborne
> illnesses are caused by pathogenic bacteria, which are different from
> the spoilage bacteria that make foods 'go bad.'"

Read that twice. **They are different organisms.** The bacteria, yeasts
and moulds that turn milk sour, make meat slimy and put fur on bread are
in the business of eating your food. They are good at it, they produce
smells and colours and textures while they work, and most of them have no
particular interest in you. The organisms that cause foodborne illness
are in a different line of work. They can multiply to an infectious dose
while leaving the food looking, smelling and tasting exactly as it did
when it was safe.

Penn State Extension states the consumer half of this plainly in its
material on spoilage signs: we cannot see, smell or taste pathogenic
microorganisms, though we can see or smell some signs of spoilage. Those
are two separate observations about two separate populations.

So there are two independent questions about any piece of food:

1. **Is it spoiled?** Has it been chemically and microbially degraded to
   the point where it is unpleasant or inedible? Your senses are
   reasonably good at this.
2. **Is it dangerous?** Does it carry enough pathogenic organisms or
   preformed toxin to make you ill? Your senses are close to useless at
   this.

The folk rule "trust your nose" quietly assumes these two questions are
the same question. They are not, and the direction of the error matters:
the rule fails safe in one direction and fails fatal in the other. If you
throw out food because it smells bad, you have probably wasted something
that would not have hurt you. If you eat food because it smells fine, you
have learned nothing about whether it will.

**Two things this argument does not say, and it is important not to hear
them.**

It does not say spoiled food is safe. Spoilage and contamination can and
do occur in the same piece of food, and one is a hint about the
conditions that produce the other. USDA's Food Safety and Inspection
Service makes this point directly about mould: "Foods that are moldy may
also have invisible bacteria growing along with the mold." Visible
spoilage is evidence that something has been growing in there under
conditions favourable to growth, and that is not a reassuring thing to
learn about something you were about to eat.

It also does not say your senses are worthless. They are the cheapest
instrument you own and they detect real conditions. It says they answer
question one and not question two, and that you must stop using an answer
to one as an answer to the other.

## What spoilage actually is: four processes, four signatures

"Going off" is not one process. It is at least four, they have different
causes, they produce different evidence, and they carry different
amounts of risk. A reader who can tell them apart makes better decisions
than one who lumps them together, because two of the four are almost
purely quality problems and one of them is where the danger lives.

### 1. Microbial growth

Bacteria, yeasts and moulds land on food and eat it. Breaking down its
carbohydrates, proteins and fats releases the by-products you detect:
sour, sulphurous, yeasty, ammoniac smells, gas, slime, visible colonies.

**This is the process that overlaps with danger,** because pathogens are
also microbes and grow under broadly similar conditions. It is also the
process we have the most control over, because microbial growth has
requirements, and if you remove one of them, growth stops or slows
dramatically. Iowa State University Extension's summary of those
requirements is the standard one: bacteria want warmth, moisture, a food
source, near-neutral acidity, time, and (for most, though not all) the
right oxygen conditions. Two of those thresholds are worth memorising
because so many other rules hang off them:

- **pH 4.6.** Above it, a food is "low-acid" and supports the growth of
  the organisms we worry about most. At or below, it is acid food. This
  is the line the whole discipline of home canning is built on, and it
  gets its own treatment in [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew).
- **Water activity about 0.85.** Iowa State cites FDA's use of water
  activity above 0.85 as the mark of a food able to support microbial
  growth. Water activity is not the same as wetness; it is how much of
  the water is chemically available. This is why honey, jam, dried beans
  and hard salami keep at room temperature: salt and sugar tie up the
  water. It is also why moulds beat bacteria in those foods, because
  moulds tolerate low water activity better.

### 2. Enzymatic change

Food is made of tissue that was recently alive, and it is full of enzymes
that were doing jobs. Those enzymes keep working after harvest or
slaughter. Penn State Extension describes them as a natural component of
food that, when active, change its colour, texture, flavour and
nutritional content.

This is the browning of a cut apple, the toughening of asparagus, the
overripening of a pear on the counter. It is a chemical process running
inside the food's own cells, not an infection, and it is why blanching
exists: a brief scald denatures the enzymes before freezing so they are
not still working slowly in the freezer for the next eight months. The
blanching procedure itself belongs to
[Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew).

**Enzymatic change is a quality process.** Left long enough it produces
conditions that invite microbes, but the browning itself is not the
hazard.

### 3. Oxidation

Fats react with oxygen and go rancid. The signature is unmistakable once
you have learned it: the flat, stale, crayon-and-old-nuts smell of an
opened bag of walnuts left in a warm cupboard, or cooking oil from the
back of the shelf. Light, heat and air drive it, which is why oils belong
in dark bottles in cool places and why nuts keep far longer in a freezer
than a pantry.

Rancidity is a real signal and worth acting on, but it is a signal about
palatability and nutritional degradation rather than about infection. **I
could not open an agency page stating whether eating rancid fat is
harmful in the short term, so this guide does not make a claim either
way.** Treat it as a strong reason not to eat something and not as
evidence of a pathogen.

### 4. Physical change

Freezer burn, bruising, wilting, crystallised honey, separated sauce.
Nothing is growing and nothing is oxidising in the fat; the food has been
physically damaged or dehydrated.

FDA is unusually direct that this class is not a safety class: "Freezer
burn does not mean food is unsafe. Freezer burn is a food-quality issue,
not a food safety issue. It appears as grayish-brown leathery spots on
frozen food." Penn State agrees, adding that you will not become ill from
eating a food with freezer burn, though the product is less desirable.

Physical damage does matter indirectly: a bruise is broken tissue with
its juices exposed, which is an excellent place for microbes and moulds
to start. That is precisely the mechanism behind patulin in apples,
below.

**The practical payoff of separating the four:** if you can name which
process you are looking at, you can usually name whether you are making a
quality decision or a safety decision. Grey leathery patches on frozen
beef is a quality decision. Fuzz on the bread is a different decision.
Sour cream that smells like acetone is a different decision again.

## What your senses genuinely tell you

A guide that says your senses are useless deserves to be ignored, because
they plainly are not. Be specific about where they work.

**Your senses are reliable for these.**

- **Visible mould is real information.** If you can see it, it is there,
  and there is more of it than you can see. FSIS: "you only see part of
  the mold on the surface of food," and "When a food shows heavy mold
  growth, 'root' threads have invaded it deeply."
- **Slime on meat, fish or vegetables** is a bacterial film. It means a
  population has had time and conditions to build.
- **Gas.** A bulging can, a jar with a domed lid, a vacuum pack that has
  inflated, a bottle of juice that fizzes when it should not. Something
  living has been producing gas inside a sealed container. Penn State's
  list of spoilage signs in preserved food is worth knowing by heart:
  unsealed, bulging or loose lids, rising air bubbles, leaking, foam,
  foul odour, unnatural colours, sliminess, dried food on the outside at
  the top of the jar, and cotton-like mould growth in white, blue, black
  or green.
- **Rancidity.** Your nose is genuinely sensitive to oxidised fat.
- **Obvious visual wrongness:** grey-green meat, pink or slimy patches on
  cooked chicken, a sauce that has separated and darkened.

FDA's consumer guidance treats these as actionable: "Anything that looks
or smells suspicious should be thrown out."

**Your senses are unreliable, in a way you cannot train away, for
these.** Each of the following can be present in food that looks, smells
and tastes completely normal:

- *Salmonella*, *Campylobacter*, *Listeria monocytogenes*, Shiga
  toxin-producing *E. coli* and the other bacterial pathogens.
- Staphylococcal enterotoxin.
- Botulinum toxin. CDC: "You cannot see, smell, or taste the toxin that
  causes botulism."
- Aflatoxin and other mycotoxins, which are invisible at the
  concentrations that matter.
- Histamine in fish. FDA's Bad Bug Book, on scombrotoxin: "The fish might
  not look or smell bad, but can cause illness," and, in the technical
  section, "Common sensory examination by the consumer cannot ensure the
  absence or presence of the toxin."
- Shellfish biotoxins. In most cases the toxin has no effect on the
  shellfish itself, so the animal looks and behaves normally.

**One more thing about the sniff test: it is not even free.** FSIS's
instruction on mouldy food is "Don't sniff the moldy item. This can cause
respiratory trouble." Deliberately inhaling from a colony of sporulating
mould is a bad idea on its own terms, quite apart from what it fails to
tell you about the food.

## The invisible things

Five hazards, five different mechanisms. They are grouped here because
they share the property that made this document necessary: **no sensory
warning.**

### Bacterial pathogens on food that looks fine

This is the ordinary case, the one behind most foodborne illness. The
food was contaminated at some point, sat at a temperature where bacteria
multiply, and reached a dose that makes you ill, all without changing
appearance.

**The Danger Zone.** CDC: "Bacteria can multiply rapidly if left at room
temperature or in the 'Danger Zone' between 40 degrees F and 140 degrees
F." FDA gives the rate: "at room temperature, the numbers of bacteria
that cause foodborne sickness can double every 20 minutes."

Sit with that doubling figure for a moment, because it explains why the
time limits are so short. Doubling every 20 minutes is eight-fold per
hour. It is exponential, so the last hour of a four-hour lapse does far
more damage than the first. This is also why the number is a rate and not
a promise: it is what fast-growing bacteria can do under favourable
conditions, not a guarantee of what any particular organism in any
particular food did.

**The two-hour rule, with its scope.** FDA: "Never allow meat, poultry,
seafood, eggs, or produce or other foods that require refrigeration to
sit at room temperature for more than two hours, one hour if the air
temperature is above 90 degrees F." CDC gives the same limits. Note what
the rule is scoped to: **perishable foods that require refrigeration.** It
is not a rule about bread, dry pasta, jam or an unopened tin. It also
applies to leftovers, restaurant doggie bags and take-out, and FDA notes
the same two hours applies to groceries in a car, which is usually hotter
than a room.

**Storage temperatures.** FDA: "Keep the refrigerator temperature at or
below 40 degrees F (4 degrees C). The freezer temperature should be 0
degrees F (minus 18 degrees C)." Buy an appliance thermometer; they cost
very little, and the dial on the door of a refrigerator is a setting, not
a measurement.

**Cold slows growth, it does not stop it, and one important pathogen
ignores the rule.** FDA notes that *Listeria* can grow at refrigerated
temperatures, which is why it singles out refrigerated ready-to-eat foods
such as luncheon meats: the longer they sit, the more chance *Listeria*
has to grow, "especially if the refrigerator temperature is above 40
degrees F." *Listeria* infection is rare, but CDC calls it "the fourth
leading cause of death from foodborne illness in the United States," and
it falls hardest on pregnant women, newborns, adults over 65 and people
with weakened immune systems. If you are in one of those groups, the
rules in this section are not conservative advice, they are the actual
risk boundary.

**How long leftovers keep.** The figure FSIS gives on its mould page is
to use leftovers within 3 to 4 days, and it gives that number for a
specific reason: "so mold doesn't have a chance to grow." That is the
scope I can source, and it is a good working number.

**The scale of the thing.** CDC: "CDC estimates that each year 48 million
people get sick from a foodborne illness, 128,000 are hospitalized, and
3,000 die." Most of those illnesses are a bad day or two on food that
looked fine. Some of them are not.

### Mycotoxins

Now the part that people get wrong most often, because the folk rule here
is not just unreliable, it is applied to exactly the foods where it is
most dangerous.

Moulds are fungi, and most of them are harmless nuisances. FSIS is
careful about this: "some molds cause allergic reactions and respiratory
problems. And a few molds, in the right conditions, produce
'mycotoxins,' poisonous substances that can make people sick." A few. In
the right conditions. Mould on food is usually a quality problem, and
FDA's consumer page says as much: "Mold is not a major health threat, but
it can make food unappetizing."

The trouble is that "usually" is doing real work in that sentence, you
cannot tell which mould you have by looking, and the toxins are not
confined to the fuzzy part you can see.

**Why cutting the mould off is not a general rule.** A mould is not a
stain on a surface. FSIS: "Molds have branches and roots that are like
very thin threads. The roots may be difficult to see when the mold is
growing on food and may be very deep in the food." And on where the
poison sits: "In dangerous molds, poisonous substances are often
contained in and around these threads. In some cases, toxins may have
spread throughout the food."

So whether you can cut it off depends entirely on whether the threads can
get through the food, which depends on how dense and how wet it is. FSIS
publishes this as a food-by-food table rather than a principle, and the
table is the authority. **Here is the actual rule, with the actual
depth.**

**Cut it off, and here is how:**

- **Hard cheese** (not cheese where mould is part of the process). FSIS:
  "Cut off at least 1 inch around and below the mold spot (keep the knife
  out of the mold itself so it will not cross-contaminate other parts of
  the cheese). After trimming off the mold, re-cover the cheese in fresh
  wrap." The reason given: "Mold generally cannot penetrate deep into the
  product."
- **Firm fruits and vegetables**, and FSIS gives cabbage, bell peppers
  and carrots as its examples. Same instruction: cut off at least 1 inch
  around and below the spot, keeping the knife out of the mould. The
  reason: "It's difficult for mold to penetrate dense foods."
- **Hard salami and dry-cured country hams**, where surface mould is
  normal for the product: scrub the mould off the surface.

**Discard, no trimming:**

- Luncheon meats, bacon, hot dogs.
- Cooked leftover meat and poultry, cooked casseroles, cooked grain and
  pasta.
- Soft cheeses such as cottage, cream cheese, Neufchatel and chevre, and
  **all** crumbled, shredded and sliced cheeses of any type, because the
  cutting instrument can have contaminated them.
- Yogurt and sour cream.
- Jams and jellies. FSIS's reason here is the sharpest sentence in the
  table: "The mold could be producing a mycotoxin. Microbiologists
  recommend against scooping out the mold and using the remaining
  condiment."
- Soft fruits and vegetables, with cucumbers, peaches and tomatoes given
  as examples.
- Bread and baked goods, because porous foods can be contaminated below
  the surface.
- **Peanut butter, legumes and nuts.**

The one-inch figure is a margin around a visible spot on a dense food,
not a universal permission. Note also what FSIS says to do with the rest
of the environment: clean the refrigerator or pantry where the food was
stored, and check nearby items the mouldy food might have touched,
because "Mold spreads quickly in fruits and vegetables."

**A note on a second, stricter rule you will meet.** Penn State
Extension's spoilage material says that because mould filaments grow down
into food, scraping surface mould does not eliminate mould or the toxins
it may have produced, and concludes: throw mouldy food away. That is
written inside a section about canning and preserved food in jars, where
discarding is unambiguously correct, and it is not in conflict with the
FSIS table once you keep each one's scope attached to it. If you want a
single rule to carry around, the FSIS table is the more specific
instrument and it is the one this guide follows. If you are looking at a
jar of anything home-preserved, both sources say the same thing: discard.

**Aflatoxin.** This is the mycotoxin that earns its reputation.

FSIS: "Aflatoxin is a cancer-causing poison produced by certain fungi in
or on foods and feeds, especially in field corn and peanuts." FDA's Bad
Bug Book is blunter: "AFB1 is the most potent known natural carcinogen
and is the most abundant of the AFs," and the International Agency for
Research on Cancer has classified AFB1 as a group 1 carcinogen. The US
National Toxicology Program's Report on Carcinogens lists aflatoxins as
"known to be human carcinogens based on sufficient evidence of
carcinogenicity from studies in humans," a listing revised upward from
"reasonably anticipated" in 1991.

It is produced mainly by *Aspergillus flavus* and *A. parasiticus*, and
the Bad Bug Book is specific about when: "In adverse weather or under
poor storage conditions." **Storage is the half you control.** In the US
the foods it is commonly found in are corn, sorghum, rice, cottonseed,
peanuts, tree nuts, copra, cocoa beans, figs, ginger and nutmeg.

Four properties make it different from a bacterial hazard.

*It is chronic before it is acute.* At the doses a US consumer is
realistically exposed to, aflatoxin is a cancer risk, not a poisoning.
The Bad Bug Book records that no human aflatoxicosis outbreak has been
reported in the United States, crediting strict regulation since 1971,
FDA monitoring, and a diverse diet. Acute outbreaks are real but they
happen where a single contaminated staple dominates the diet: 397
illnesses and 108 deaths in northwest India in 1974; and in rural Kenya
in 2004 and 2005, 317 people ill and 125 dead, from home-grown maize
averaging 354 ng/g, which is about eighteen times the US action level
below.

*It survives cooking.* WHO states that most mycotoxins are chemically
stable and survive food processing. For aflatoxin specifically, the
peer-reviewed review literature (Kutasi and colleagues, *International
Journal of Molecular Sciences*, 2021) reports that aflatoxins remain
stable above 100 degrees C and that significant decontamination was
reported only at heating or roasting temperatures of 150 degrees C (302
degrees F) or higher. Boiling is 100 degrees C. **Cooking a mouldy peanut
does not fix a mouldy peanut.** I did not find a federal page giving an
aflatoxin-specific temperature threshold, so treat the 150 degrees C
figure as the research literature's number and not as a US agency
position.

*It is wildly unevenly distributed, which is why sampling is hard and
looking is useless.* The Bad Bug Book: "There have been reports of AF
concentrations in excess of 1,000,000 ng/g for individual peanut kernels;
5,000,000 ng/g for cottonseed; and more than 4,000,000 ng/g in corn
kernels," and sampling variability "is the largest in the total testing
procedure." A single kernel can carry an enormous dose while the ones
beside it carry none. There is no version of eyeballing a bag of nuts
that samples it properly.

*It is regulated, and the regulation is doing the work.* FDA's action
levels are the limits "at or above which FDA will take legal action to
remove products from the market." For aflatoxin they are **20 ppb for
foods**, 20 ppb for peanuts and peanut products, and **0.5 ppb for
aflatoxin M1 in milk**, the lower figure because, as the Bad Bug Book
explains, milk makes up such a large part of the diet of infants and
children. The published action level list states that it is current as of
August 2000 and that it is the user's responsibility to keep up with
changes, so check FDA's current guidance before quoting these as today's
numbers.

WHO's practical advice for consumers is the part you can act on: inspect
whole grains and nuts for evidence of mould and discard any that look
mouldy, discoloured or shrivelled, and avoid damaging grain before,
during and after drying, since damaged grain is more prone to mould
invasion. Note that this is advice about visible mould and damage, which
is the only handle you have; it is not a claim that clean-looking nuts are
guaranteed clean.

*This guide's simulation data.* The toxin table carries a record
`aflatoxin_b1` in `data/chemistry/toxins.csv`, describing aflatoxin B1 as
the most potent natural carcinogen known, contaminating corn, peanuts and
tree nuts in storage, with liver damage, jaundice and hepatocellular
carcinoma as effects and no specific antidote. That matches the sources
above. Its two numeric fields should not be read as human figures. The
record carries a lethal dose of 0.5 mg per kg and an onset of 1,440
minutes, but FDA's Bad Bug Book says plainly that "The toxic level of AF
in humans is largely unknown," gives its dose figures as animal LD50
values ranging from 0.3 mg/kg in rabbits to 18 mg/kg in rats, and lists
onset for aflatoxin as "Not applicable," because the characteristic harm
is a cancer risk accumulated over years rather than an event with a
waiting time.

**Patulin**, the other mycotoxin you are likely to meet, is the one that
makes bruising matter. FDA's compliance programme for mycotoxins
describes it as "a toxic substance produced by Penicillium, Aspergillus,
and Byssochlamys molds that may grow on apples, and may be present if
rotten, moldy or damaged apples are used to make apple juice," and states
that "Patulin is not destroyed by heat processing, and can occur at high
levels in apple juice, including pasteurized apple juice." FDA's action
level is 50 micrograms per kilogram, that is 50 parts per billion,
measured on single strength apple juice. WHO adds that patulin turns up
in other mouldy fruits and grains as well, with apples and apple juice
the major dietary source.

The lesson for a home cider press or a bucket of windfalls is exact:
**the rot goes into the juice and the pasteuriser does not take it out
again.** Cut generously around damage on apples destined for juice, and do
not press fruit that is actually rotting.

### Botulinum toxin, in one paragraph

*Clostridium botulinum* grows without oxygen in low-acid food and
produces a neurotoxin that causes flaccid paralysis, starting with the
eyes and face and descending; when the diaphragm is involved, death is by
asphyxia. The Bad Bug Book gives onset as 4 hours to 8 days, usually 18
to 36 hours, and notes that "Often, there's no visible sign that a food
is contaminated, but sometimes a can is swollen." The spores are
heat-resistant and survive incorrect processing; the toxin itself is not,
and CDC's advice is to boil some foods for 10 minutes before eating them,
adding 1 minute for each additional 1,000 feet of elevation. CDC also
disposes of the idea that a cautious nibble is a test: "You cannot see,
smell, or taste the toxin that causes botulism. But taking even a small
taste of food containing the toxin can be deadly." Home-canned
low-acid food is the classic source, and CDC also flags two everyday
anaerobic traps: refrigerate homemade oils made with garlic or herbs and
throw them out after 4 days, and keep foil-baked potatoes at 140 degrees F
or hotter, or refrigerate them at 40 degrees F or colder with the foil
loosened. **The full treatment, including the pH 4.6 rule, why low-acid
food cannot be water-bath canned, and the disposal procedure for a
suspect jar, is in [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew), and
that is where to read it before you can anything.** Do not use the
ten-minute boil as a way to rescue food that was canned incorrectly.

### Scombroid, or why the fish that smells fine is the problem

Scombroid poisoning is the clearest illustration in this whole document
of the difference between an organism and the chemistry it leaves behind.

Here is the mechanism, from FDA's Bad Bug Book. Certain fish carry a lot
of the amino acid histidine in their flesh. Bacteria that normally
inhabit the fish, and which the Bad Bug Book pointedly notes "do not
sicken" it, make decarboxylase enzymes. If the fish is not chilled fast
enough after it is caught, those enzymes convert histidine into
histamine. Then you can cook the fish, can it, or freeze it. The bacteria
die. **The histamine does not care.**

FDA: "Cooking, canning, and freezing do not reduce the toxic effects."
This is not spoilage bacteria making you ill. It is a stable small
molecule sitting in the flesh, produced during an episode of poor
refrigeration that may have happened on a boat weeks before you bought
the fish.

- **The fish involved:** tuna, mahi-mahi, bluefish, sardines, mackerel,
  amberjack, anchovies, and others. Histamine can also form in other
  foods, and FDA names cheese and sauerkraut.
- **What it does:** tingling or burning in or around the mouth or throat,
  rash or hives, a drop in blood pressure, headache, dizziness, itching,
  nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, asthma-like constriction of the airway,
  heart palpitations and respiratory distress.
- **How fast:** onset is rapid, "ranging from minutes to a few hours
  after consumption." Symptoms commonly last several hours, occasionally
  several days.
- **How serious:** the Bad Bug Book records that no deaths have been
  confirmed from scombrotoxin poisoning, but severe cardiac and
  respiratory reactions do occur rarely, and people taking certain
  medicines, the anti-tuberculosis drug isoniazid named specifically, are
  at increased risk. It is treated with antihistamines by a medical
  professional. **If you or somebody you are with reacts this way after
  eating fish, say what was eaten and when: it helps the diagnosis.**
- **How common:** one of the most common forms of fish poisoning in the
  US, with 379 outbreaks involving 1,726 people reported to CDC between
  1990 and 2007, and FDA believes the true number is far greater.
- **The numbers FDA works to:** histamine at 50 ppm or greater indicates
  a fish in a state of decomposition; 500 ppm or greater is treated as a
  public health hazard. Those are regulatory guidelines for evaluating
  product, not something you can assess at home.

Two consequences worth holding onto. First, **the toxin is unevenly
distributed**, within a single fish and between cans in the same lot, so
one person at the table can be ill and another fine from the same
purchase. Second, since you cannot detect it and cooking will not remove
it, the entire defence is upstream: buy fish from somewhere that keeps it
properly cold, get it home cold, and keep it at 40 degrees F or lower
until you cook it. Prevention is the whole of the treatment.

### Shellfish biotoxins, in one paragraph

Filter-feeding shellfish concentrate toxins made by algae, and the
important property is the same one that runs through this section:
"In most cases, the toxin has no effect on the shellfish itself," so
contaminated shellfish look and smell entirely normal, and FDA's consumer
summary states that "These poisons aren't destroyed by cooking, freezing,
or other food preparation." Paralytic shellfish poisoning is the lethal
one, with onset generally within 30 minutes and death reported as soon as
3 to 4 hours after the meal. Because the hazard is in the water on a
given day rather than in the animal's appearance, the control is a
monitoring programme and a closure notice, not an inspection you perform
yourself. **This is the local case here, and it is covered properly in
[Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide), which carries Washington's
closure hotline and the rule that you check on the day you harvest, every
time.** Read that before you take anything off a beach.
[The Species Where You Live](/library#the-species-where-you-live) covers which
authority answers which question.

## What does and does not work as a rescue

The single most useful idea in food safety is this one:

> **Killing an organism and destroying the toxin it already made are
> different operations, and the second is much harder than the first.**

Almost every "can I save this?" question resolves once you ask which of
those two you need. Here is each common rescue, with what it does and
what it does not touch.

**Cooking.** Kills vegetative bacteria. CDC's safe internal temperatures
are 145 degrees F for beef, veal, lamb, pork and fish, with a 3 minute
rest for the meats, 160 degrees F for ground meats, and 165 degrees F for
poultry; a thermometer is the only way to know you got there, since
colour and texture do not tell you. What cooking does **not** do:
- It does not destroy staphylococcal enterotoxin. FDA: "Destruction of
  viable cells by heat does not destroy the biological activity of
  preformed staphylococcal enterotoxins. These toxins are highly heat
  stable and can remain biologically active." The consumer summary is
  even plainer: *S. aureus* "can make toxins (enterotoxins) that might not
  be destroyed by cooking, although the bacterium itself can be destroyed
  by heat." Onset is 1 to 7 hours, and because *Staph* lives on the skin
  and in the nose and throat of half or more of healthy people, the
  classic vehicles are heavily handled foods left warm: egg, tuna,
  chicken, potato and macaroni salads, cream-filled pastries, sandwich
  fillings.
- It does not destroy *Bacillus cereus* emetic toxin. That toxin,
  cereulide, is "pH-stable and heat- and protease-resistant" and remains
  stable "after heating at 121 degrees C for 30 minutes," which is about
  250 degrees F, hotter and longer than any kitchen cooking. This is the
  reheated-rice case: emetic illness starts 0.5 to 6 hours after eating,
  and the vehicle is typically rice or another starchy food held warm.
  FDA's own summary of the lesson: "Cooking may kill the bacteria, but it
  might not disable the toxin that causes the vomiting type of illness."
- It does not touch aflatoxin, patulin, histamine or shellfish biotoxin,
  for the reasons given above.
- It does not make mushroom poisoning safe. FSIS, on the same page as the
  mould table: "Most mushrooms that cause human poisoning cannot be made
  safe by cooking, canning, freezing, or any other processing." See
  [Identifying Wild Plants](/library#identifying-wild-plants) for why the
  identification, not the preparation, is the entire safety step.

**Reheating leftovers.** CDC gives 165 degrees F for leftovers. This kills
organisms that grew during storage. It does not undo toxin produced while
the food sat warm before it went into the refrigerator, which is exactly
why the two-hour rule matters more than the reheating temperature. **A
thorough reheat is not a reset button on a bad afternoon.**

**Freezing.** FDA: "While freezing does not kill most bacteria, it does
stop bacteria from growing," and "Though food will be safe indefinitely
at 0 degrees F, quality will decrease the longer the food is in the
freezer." Freezing is a pause, not a sterilisation. Whatever was in the
food goes into the freezer with it and wakes up on thawing, which is why
thawing happens in the refrigerator, in cold water or in the microwave
rather than on the counter.

For a thawed freezer after a power cut, FDA gives the one genuinely
useful test, and prefaces it with the sentence this whole document has
been arguing: "You can't rely on appearance or odor. If the food still
contains ice crystals or is 40 degrees F or below, it is safe to refreeze
or cook." For the refrigerator: food should be safe if the power was out
no more than four hours with the door kept shut, and you discard
perishables that have been above 40 degrees F for four hours or more.

**Cutting away.** Works only for the specific foods in the FSIS table
above, at 1 inch around and below, with the knife kept out of the mould.
It does not work for soft, porous, high-moisture or pre-cut foods,
because in those the threads and the toxins are already past the point
you would be cutting to.

**Skimming.** Taking the mould off the top of a jam, a sauce or a stock
and using what is underneath is the single most common version of this
mistake, and FSIS addresses jams and jellies by name: the mould could be
producing a mycotoxin, and microbiologists recommend against scooping it
out and using the rest. Skimming removes the evidence and leaves the
question.

**Re-canning or re-boiling a suspect jar.** No. A jar showing signs of
spoilage gets the disposal procedure, not the stove, and that procedure
is in [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew). Reprocessing
food that was canned by an untested method does not convert it into food
that was canned by a tested one.

**Washing.** Worth doing for produce, and it reduces surface
contamination, but it does not sterilise and it will not reach anything
that has got inside the tissue. Your hands are the more important
surface, and that belongs to
[Handwashing and Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene). If the water
itself is the uncertainty, see
[Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink) and
[Testing Water](/library#testing-water).

## The decision procedure

Run these in order. The order matters, because the first question is the
one that actually carries the information and the one everybody asks
last.

**1. What is its history?** Where has this been, at what temperature, for
how long? A container that went from the stove into a shallow dish into
the refrigerator within two hours and has been at 40 degrees F or below
for three days is in good standing regardless of what your nose reports.
A dish that sat out overnight is not, no matter how good it smells. **If
you cannot reconstruct the history, treat it as unknown, and unknown is
not the same as fine.**

**2. Is this a food class with a known invisible hazard?** Home-canned
low-acid food. Fish in the scombroid group. Shellfish. Nuts, grain,
peanut butter and dried figs. Rice or pasta held warm. Anything from a
bulging or damaged can, a jar with a compromised seal, or an inflated
vacuum pack. If yes, the class rule governs and your senses do not get a
vote.

**3. Now look and smell.** A positive result here is meaningful: mould,
slime, gas, rancidity, wrong colour. A negative result here is not
evidence of safety; it just means you have failed to detect the one
category of problem your senses can detect.

**4. If there is mould, which column is this food in?** Hard cheese and
firm vegetables get 1 inch cut around and below. The discard list gets
discarded. Then clean the spot where it was stored and check its
neighbours.

**5. Decide, and be honest about which decision you are making.** Is this
a quality judgement (freezer burn, a limp carrot, a stale cracker) or a
safety judgement? Quality judgements can be made with your own
preferences. Safety judgements are made with the rules.

**The standing rule: throw it out regardless of how it looks, if any of
these is true.**

- It is home-canned low-acid food that was not processed by a tested
  procedure, or the jar is bulging, leaking, spurting, mouldy or smells
  wrong.
- It is a perishable food that has been above 40 degrees F for more than
  four hours, or out at room temperature for more than two hours, or more
  than one hour above 90 degrees F.
- It is mouldy and on the discard list, particularly nuts, peanut butter,
  bread, soft cheese, jam, soft fruit or cooked leftovers.
- It is fish you have reason to think was warm at any point in its
  history.
- It is shellfish from an area you have not checked for closures on the
  day.
- You cannot account for where it has been.

**And one rule that changes the threshold rather than adding to the
list.** When you are feeding somebody in a higher-risk group, that is a
pregnant woman, a child under 5, an adult over 65, or anybody with a
weakened immune system, every marginal call above resolves toward
discarding. The same food is a different decision for a different eater,
and this is the single most important adjustment in the whole document.
CDC names those four groups as the ones more likely to get very sick from
foodborne germs, and *Listeria*, which grows in your refrigerator, falls
hardest on exactly them.

**The cost calculus, plainly.** On one side: the food. A container of
leftovers, a bag of nuts, a piece of fish. Call it a few dollars, and the
waste is genuine, which is why the earlier steps exist at all. On the
other side: an illness that at best costs you two days and at worst is
one of the 128,000 hospitalisations or 3,000 deaths CDC counts each year,
plus, for aflatoxin, a cancer risk that does not present a bill for
decades. **The asymmetry is enormous and it is not close.** No plate of
food is worth a hospital visit. The discipline is to make the earlier
steps good enough that you rarely reach a genuine coin-flip, because then
you are neither gambling nor throwing away food you did not need to.

## Dates and labels

Most of what people believe about the dates printed on food is wrong, and
in the United States the truth is startling once you see it stated.

**Almost no date on a food package is a safety date, and almost none of
them is required at all.** FDA: "Except for infant formula, manufacturers
are not required by Federal law or regulation to place quality-based date
labels on packaged food."

It gets looser. FDA: "There are no uniform or universally accepted
descriptions used on food labels for open dating (calendar dates) in the
United States. As a result, there are a wide variety of phrases used for
product dating." Sell By, Best By, Expires On and the rest are chosen by
manufacturers at their own discretion, and FDA says the most common
reason for applying one is to tell you and the retailer how long the food
can be expected to keep its intended quality and flavour.

FDA supports standardising on **"Best if Used By"** precisely because the
phrase says what it means: "the date when a product will be at its best
flavor and quality." It is not a safety date, and it is not a deadline.
FDA's own instruction for food past it is to examine it: if the product
has changed noticeably in colour, consistency or texture, you may want to
avoid eating it. That is a quality inspection, and, as everything above
this section has argued, a quality inspection is not a safety
verification.

The stakes of the confusion are real in the other direction too. FDA:
"Confusion over date labeling accounts for an estimated 20 percent of
consumer food waste."

**The one exception, and it is the answer to "which label IS a safety
date".** Infant formula. FDA: "The 'use by' date is required by FDA
regulations on each container of infant formula." And what it means is
specific: "The 'use by' date on infant formulas is a date, selected by
the manufacturer based on tests and other information, to inform
retailers and consumers about the quality of the infant formula. Until
that declared date, the infant formula will contain no less than the
amount of each nutrient declared on the product label and will otherwise
be of acceptable quality."

Be precise about what that does and does not say, because it is easy to
over-read. It is the only federally required date on food. It is a
guarantee that runs **until** the date: up to it, the formula holds the
nutrient levels on the label. Past it, the guarantee is gone, and for an
infant who is getting all of their nutrition from that tin, a shortfall in
declared nutrients is itself the harm. So it functions as a date you
obey, but its basis is nutrient content and quality rather than
contamination. **Use infant formula by the date on the tin.**

Two practical consequences for everything else.

First, **a date is a manufacturer's estimate of quality under proper
storage, and it knows nothing about your storage.** Milk that spent an
afternoon in a warm car is not made safe by the date on the carton, and
an unopened jar of something shelf-stable is not made dangerous by
passing it. History beats the printed date in both directions.

Second, if you want actual storage times, they exist and they are
specific to the food. FDA points to the FoodKeeper, developed with USDA,
Cornell University and the Food Marketing Institute, and to the
Refrigerator and Freezer Storage Chart. Those are tables, not principles,
and looking one up takes less time than arguing with yourself over a
carton.

## Where this is local

In Silverdale, on Dyes Inlet in Kitsap County, at about 47.65 degrees
north, two parts of this document stop being general and become specific.

**Shellfish.** You can gather them here, which means the biotoxin
paragraph above is not an abstraction but a thing to check before you
walk down to the water. Cooking and freezing do not touch paralytic
shellfish poison, the animal gives you no sign, and the only control is
the state health department's marine biotoxin programme and its closure
information. [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) carries the hotline
number and the procedure, and the standing instruction there is the right
one: check on the day you harvest, every time, not once a season.

**Damp.** This is a cool marine climate with a long wet season, and
mould grows on warmth and humidity. FSIS's advice to keep household
humidity below 40 percent is written for the general case and is a
demanding target in a Puget Sound winter, but the direction is right:
storage areas that stay damp will grow mould on food faster than a dry
pantry will, and the pantry, not the refrigerator, is where nuts, grain
and flour sit. Keeping the house dry is its own subject, in
[Ventilation, Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould). Note that
the household mould problem and the food mould problem are related by
mechanism but are not the same decision: a damp wall is a building
problem, a furred loaf is a food one.

For anything grown or preserved here, the storage conditions by crop,
the four preservation methods and the canning rules are in
[Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew).

## How you know you have this

- You can say which of the four processes you are looking at when
  something has gone wrong, and therefore whether you are making a
  quality or a safety decision.
- You reach for the history of a food before you reach for your nose.
- You know the two-hour rule, the 40 and 140 degrees F boundaries, and
  what they are scoped to.
- You know that cutting mould off is a rule for hard cheese and firm
  vegetables at 1 inch, and not a rule for bread, jam, nuts or soft
  fruit.
- You no longer believe that cooking fixes things, and you can name three
  toxins it does not touch.
- You have an appliance thermometer in the refrigerator and you have
  actually looked at it.
- Nothing in your house is being evaluated primarily by the date printed
  on it.
- You waste less food than you did, because you stopped throwing out
  perfectly good things on the strength of a date, and you stopped
  gambling on things whose history you could not account for.

## What in this document is a rule of thumb rather than a sourced fact

Kept separate on purpose, so you can tell which is which.

- **The four-process division** (microbial, enzymatic, oxidative,
  physical) is this guide's organising scheme. Each process is described
  by the sources cited, but the framing of them as four distinct
  signatures to be told apart is ours.
- **The five-step decision procedure and its ordering** is ours. Every
  individual rule inside it is sourced; putting history first and the
  sniff test third is a judgement about what carries information.
- **"Unknown history means treat as unsafe"** is a rule of thumb. No
  agency page opened for this guide states it in those words; it follows
  from the time-and-temperature rules, which do nothing for you if you
  cannot reconstruct the time or the temperature.
- **The claim that most obviously spoiled food would not have badly hurt
  you** is the weakest claim in this document and it is stated carefully
  above. What is sourced is the mechanism: spoilage organisms and
  pathogens are different populations, and FDA says mould is not a major
  health threat but makes food unappetising. What I could not source is
  any quantitative statement about what fraction of visibly spoiled food
  is harmless, so this guide does not make one, and it does not tell you
  to eat anything on that basis.
- **Whether rancid fat is harmful to eat** is not answered here, because
  I could not open a source that answers it.
- **The 150 degrees C aflatoxin figure** is from the peer-reviewed review
  literature, not from a US agency, and is labelled as such where it
  appears.
- **Household humidity in a marine climate.** FSIS's below-40-percent
  figure is sourced; the observation that this is hard to achieve in a
  Puget Sound winter is ours.
