# Fishing

A silver fish comes up on the line off Silverdale, out in the water of
Dyes Inlet. It is about the length of your forearm, bright, cold, and
already deciding what the rest of your day looks like. Before you decide
anything else, look at the top of its back, between the dorsal fin and
the tail, for a small fleshy lump about the size of a thumbnail.

If the lump is there, this fish is very probably wild. If there is a
healed scar where the lump should be, somebody cut that fin off when the
fish was a few inches long, in a hatchery, on purpose, so that you could
tell the difference from a boat five years later.

On many days in this water, that one missing fin is the whole difference
between a legal fish and a federal offence.

## What this guide is for, and what it will not do

This is not a guide to fishing technique. It will not teach you to cast,
to tie a knot, to read water, to pick a lure, or to find fish. Those are
real skills, they are worth learning, and you can learn them from almost
anybody who fishes, including badly written sources, because the feedback
is immediate: you either catch something or you do not.

The parts that do not give you immediate feedback are the parts this
guide covers, because those are the parts that go wrong quietly. Three
questions decide whether a day's fishing was legal and whether the fish
was safe to eat:

1. **Which fish is it**, including which population of that fish.
2. **What the rules are for that fish, in that exact water, on that exact
   date.**
3. **What happened to the fish between the water and the pan.**

You can be an excellent caster and get all three wrong. You can be a poor
caster and get all three right. Only one of those two people is doing
this properly.

A fourth thing sits underneath all of it, and it is not a technique
either: some of the fish you will hook here are protected, and how you
handle a fish you are required to release determines whether the
requirement accomplished anything at all.

**A warning about numbers, stated here because it governs the whole
document.** You will notice that this guide names very few seasons,
limits and sizes. That is deliberate and it is the most useful thing in
it. Those numbers change, sometimes in the middle of a season, and a
number printed in a document is a fossil the moment the agency revises
it. A reader who trusts a stale limit is worse off than a reader who
knows they have to look it up. So this guide teaches you the *shape* of
the rules and tells you where the live copy lives. The same principle is
set out at more length in [Taking Without Ruining It](/library#taking-without-ruining-it),
which is the Library's guide to harvest limits generally, and which owns
the licence and bag-limit material for shellfish.

## 1. Which fish is it, and why that is harder here than it sounds

### The same hour, the same line, two different answers

Start with the case that makes the point, because it is not hypothetical
and it is not far away. It is the water you can see from Silverdale.

Puget Sound Chinook salmon is listed as **threatened** under the federal
Endangered Species Act. NOAA Fisheries gives the listing as Threatened on
March 24, 1999, with updates on June 28, 2005 and April 14, 2014, and
defines the listed unit as including "naturally spawned Chinook salmon
originating from rivers flowing into Puget Sound from the Elwha River
(inclusive) eastward, including rivers in Hood Canal, South Sound, North
Sound and the Strait of Georgia." That description takes in every stream
that drains into Dyes Inlet. The local record is `chinook_salmon`.

Coho salmon, swimming in the same water, is **not listed at all**. NOAA
Fisheries lists four coho units under the Act and every one of them is
outside Puget Sound. The local record is `coho_salmon`.

So there are stretches of the year when a coho and a wild Chinook can
come over the same gunwale, on the same line, within the same hour, and
one of them may be kept and the other one must go back in the water. A
guide that tells you "salmon are edible" has told you nothing you can
act on. A guide that tells you "salmon are protected" has told you
something false often enough to be useless.

This is why identification is the first question and not a preliminary
to the real question. It **is** the real question.

### The fin that is not there

The adipose fin is the small, soft, rayless fin on a salmon's back,
behind the dorsal fin and in front of the tail. It has no obvious job.
Salmon appear to do fine without it. That is exactly why it was chosen.

Washington's Department of Fish and Wildlife removes that fin from
essentially all coho and Chinook salmon raised in Washington hatcheries,
including fish raised in federal and tribal facilities. The agency calls
this mass marking. A hatchery fish therefore carries a permanent,
visible, free-to-read mark that says "this fish was produced on purpose,
its parents were not part of the wild run, and taking it does not
subtract from the population the Act protects."

That mark is what makes a **mark-selective fishery** possible: a fishery
that stays open for marked fish while requiring that unmarked fish, which
are presumed wild, go back. Without the mark, the only way to protect the
listed wild fish would be to close the fishery to everybody. The clipped
fin is the reason there is a salmon season here at all.

The rules define it tightly. Washington's sport fishing rules define a
hatchery salmon as a Chinook or coho with a clipped adipose fin **and a
healed scar at the location of the fin**, and define wild Chinook and
coho as having an unclipped adipose fin. For steelhead and trout the
definition is wider: a clipped adipose **or ventral** fin with a healed
scar at the clip site.

Three things follow from the wording, and all three matter on the water:

- **The scar is part of the definition, not decoration.** A fish that is
  missing an adipose fin because a seal took a bite out of it, or because
  it was damaged, is not a hatchery fish. The healed scar at the fin site
  is what distinguishes a deliberate clip from an injury. If you cannot
  find a clean healed scar, you have not confirmed a hatchery fish.
- **The mark is about origin, not species.** It is a second axis. You can
  have wild coho and hatchery coho, wild Chinook and hatchery Chinook.
  Identifying the species tells you which rulebook page to read;
  identifying the mark tells you which line on that page applies.
- **"Virtually all" is not "all."** The agency's own description of the
  coverage is that virtually all hatchery coho and Chinook are marked.
  That word is doing work. It is not a guarantee that every unmarked fish
  is wild. It does not need to be, because the rule is written the other
  way round: an unmarked fish is treated as wild and released. The rule
  is deliberately biased toward the fish.

I could not find, on the agency page describing mass marking, any
guidance on telling a clipped fin from a regenerated or damaged one, and
no explanation of who performs the clipping or under what legal
requirement. Those are real gaps in the public explanation, not gaps I
have filled with a guess. What is published is the definition above, and
the definition is enough to act on: look for the scar.

### Wild and hatchery is a separate question again

It is worth being explicit, because people routinely collapse these into
one idea. There are at least three independent questions about any fish
you land here, and a different authority answers each:

| Question | Who answers it | What it decides |
|---|---|---|
| What species is this? | Your own identification | Which rules apply at all |
| Is this population listed? | NOAA Fisheries (marine and anadromous fish) | Whether federal protection is in play |
| Is this individual hatchery or wild? | The fin, read against the state rule | Whether this particular fish may be kept |

The middle row is the one people miss. Listing is not a property of a
species, it is a property of a **population** in a **place**. Chum salmon
makes the point neatly. Two chum units are ESA-listed as threatened, the
Columbia River unit and the Hood Canal summer-run unit, and neither of
them is the fall chum that runs into the Dyes Inlet creeks. The local
record `chum_salmon` says so plainly. A reader who heard "chum are
listed" and stopped there would be wrong here, and a reader who heard
"chum are not listed" and stopped there would be wrong one basin over in
Hood Canal. Both statements are incomplete in the same way.

For marine and anadromous species, NOAA Fisheries is the authority to
check, not the Fish and Wildlife Service. [The Species Where You Live](/library#the-species-where-you-live)
covers that division of labour and how to build a list for your own
place.

### What the local records say about each fish

The locale data carries eleven fish records for this place. Read them for
the current details; what follows is the shape of each, so you know what
kind of problem you are dealing with before you look it up.

- `chinook_salmon` (Chinook, king, blackmouth). ESA threatened here.
  Keepable only as a hatchery fish, in narrow open windows. Also the one
  local salmon with a contaminant advisory against it, which section 5
  covers.
- `coho_salmon` (coho, silver). Not listed. The species you are most
  often permitted to keep in this water.
- `chum_salmon` (chum, dog salmon, keta). The local run is not listed,
  but the local practical answer is usually to watch them rather than
  take them.
- `pink_salmon` (pink, humpy). Not listed. Runs in Washington occur only
  in odd-numbered years, which is a genuine calendar fact rather than a
  rule: the fish live a strict two-year cycle, so there are no
  one-year-old or three-year-old fish to seed a run in the other years.
  Planning an even-year pink trip here is planning to catch nothing.
- `steelhead`. ESA threatened here, and a Washington candidate species.
  The fish in the Silverdale creeks are the listed population. Treat this
  as a look-only fish locally.
- `coastal_cutthroat_trout`. Not listed anywhere, and yet the local
  fishing rule in both Silverdale creeks is release. This is a good
  reminder that "not protected" and "you may keep it" are different
  statements.
- `bull_trout`. ESA threatened, and closed outright. See below.
- `pacific_herring`, `surf_smelt`, `pacific_sand_lance`. Forage fish. Not
  listed and not closed, though the state identifies them as conservation
  priorities under planning and habitat-mapping programmes. Those
  designations steer shoreline permitting and funding. They are not a ban
  on taking fish, and it is worth knowing the difference so you neither
  break a rule that exists nor invent one that does not.
- `lingcod`. Not listed. Legal, and tightly bounded by short,
  area-specific seasons.

Why these fish matter to each other rather than only to you is the
subject of [How an Ecosystem Holds Together](/library#how-an-ecosystem-holds-together),
which explains why herring habitat sits upstream of nearly every other
animal in this dataset, and why the creek and the killer whale turn out
to be the same problem.

### The fish you must not lift out of the water at all

Bull trout, record `bull_trout`, is ESA threatened and closed to fishing
in every water around Silverdale. There is no lawful way to take one
here.

The state's instruction for an angler who hooks one by accident is
unusually specific and worth following exactly: a bull trout caught in a
water closed to bull trout fishing must not be taken out of the water at
all. It is to be unhooked in the water and released immediately. Not
lifted for a photograph. Not weighed. Not swung into the boat or netted
up onto the bank "just for a second."

That is a rule about handling, written as a rule about legality, and it
is the clearest example in the local data of the principle that section 3
is about: for a fish you must release, **how** you release it is the
entire content of the protection.

### Rockfish, and the case where identification decides a health rule

Rockfish deserve a paragraph because they show identification deciding
something other than legality.

Washington's Department of Health, in its Puget Sound fish consumption
advice, states that rockfish retention has been illegal and consumption
of rockfish from Marine Areas 6 through 13 prohibited since 2010, and
lists the consumption advice for all rockfish species in all Puget Sound
marine areas as no consumption, for conservation reasons rather than
contamination ones.

Two of the local rockfish are federally listed, and NOAA Fisheries states
both plainly. The Puget Sound and Georgia Basin population of **bocaccio**
is listed as endangered. The Puget Sound and Georgia Basin population of
**yelloweye rockfish** is listed as threatened, and NOAA's page describes
that listing as having been made in April 2010. The `lingcod` record
cites NOAA pages for both, because lingcod fishing is exactly where a
rockfish turns up uninvited.

That 2010 date is the same year the state health department gives for
rockfish retention becoming illegal, but I found no source connecting the
two, so treat the coincidence as a coincidence rather than a cause.

So a fish you did not mean to catch, in a fishery you were legally
conducting, can be a fish you may not keep and should not eat. Knowing
the difference between a lingcod and a rockfish is not pedantry. It is
the difference between the day being fine and the day being an offence.

### When you cannot tell

Sometimes you will not be able to identify a fish confidently, or you
will not be able to find the scar, or the light will be bad and the fish
will be thrashing and you will have about fifteen seconds.

The honest rule is the conservative one: **if you cannot confirm that a
fish may be kept, it may not be kept.** This is not caution for its own
sake. It is how the rules are built. A mark-selective fishery is
constructed so that uncertainty resolves in the fish's favour, and the
released fish is the default. If you find yourself reasoning toward
keeping a fish you are unsure about, notice that you have reversed the
burden of proof that the whole system rests on.

And the genuinely honest sentence, which reading cannot get around:
identifying fish quickly and correctly in poor light with cold hands is a
practical skill learned by doing it with somebody who already can. A
document can tell you which features matter. It cannot give you the
fifteen seconds of practice.

## 2. The rules are per water, per species, per date, and they change mid-season

### Why this section names almost no numbers

Every one of the following is a separate variable in Washington's sport
fishing rules: the species, the specific body of water, the date, the
gear you are using, the time of day, whether the fish is marked, and
sometimes the fish's length. A rule is the intersection of all of them.
There is no such thing as "the salmon limit." There is a limit for a
species, in an area, in a date range, under a set of gear rules, which
may have been amended last Thursday.

This is why the guide does not print the current answer. It prints the
structure, so that you can read the current answer correctly when you
look it up, which you must do every time.

### Marine areas and fresh water are two different rulebooks

Washington divides its saltwater into numbered marine areas, 1 through
13, with some subdivisions. Silverdale and Dyes Inlet sit in **Marine
Area 10**, which the state names the Seattle-Bremerton area. The state's
own marine areas index does not describe Area 10's boundaries or name
Dyes Inlet, Sinclair Inlet or Silverdale in the area descriptions; the
area name and the per-area rule pages are where the detail lives.

Fresh water is a different regime entirely, and the local default catches
people out. Statewide, fresh water is closed to salmon fishing unless a
specific water is opened by name. The Silverdale creeks, Chico Creek and
Clear Creek, are opened for trout and other game fish, not for salmon.
So a coho you could lawfully have kept in the saltwater of Marine Area 10
is not a fish you may take out of the creek it is swimming toward. The
records `coho_salmon`, `chum_salmon` and `pink_salmon` all carry this
point, because it is the single most common local misunderstanding.

The state also warns generally that many streams in the Puget Sound
region are closed to fishing unless the rules say otherwise. In fresh
water here, assume closed and look for the permission, rather than
assuming open and looking for the prohibition. That is the opposite of
most people's instinct.

### The regulation year is not the calendar year

Washington's Sport Fishing Seasons and Regulations pamphlet runs from
**July 1 to June 30**. The current edition covers July 1, 2026 through
June 30, 2027.

This catches people twice. First, a pamphlet you picked up "this year"
may be the previous edition if you picked it up in, say, May. Second,
seasons that feel like they belong to one year, such as a winter fishery,
can straddle two editions. Check which edition you are holding before you
trust a page in it.

Catch record card deadlines run on their own calendar again, which the
next subsection covers.

### Emergency rules, and why the pamphlet is not the last word

The pamphlet is the baseline, not the final answer. The state issues
emergency rule changes during the season, and its own framing is that the
rules in the pamphlet are in effect **unless otherwise noted** by a
listed change. An emergency rule can open a fishery, close one, or alter
a limit, between editions.

The state's emergency-rules page functions as a searchable list of
current changes rather than an explanation of them. It does not define
what an emergency rule is, name the issuing authority, say how quickly
changes take effect, or state that the angler is responsible for knowing
them. I looked for all five of those and none is on that page. What the
page does establish is the relationship: pamphlet unless amended.

The practical consequence is simple and it is the most important
procedural sentence in this guide. **Check the emergency rules for your
species and your area on the day you go, not the week before.** A fishery
that was open when you planned the trip can be closed when you arrive,
and the closure is not announced on the water.

The state also points anglers at its Fish Washington mobile app and at
telephone fishing hotlines as ways to stay current. Use whichever you
will actually use.

### What a licence covers, and what it does not

Everyone aged 16 and over needs a fishing licence in Washington. The
state names two narrow exceptions on its licensing page: fishing for
bullfrogs, and collecting relic shells.

Beyond that, the important structural fact is that **a licence is not one
thing**. There are separate licence types covering freshwater fishing,
saltwater fishing, a combination of both, and shellfish and seaweed, plus
endorsements purchased alongside a licence. The state's licensing page
refers to a table of all available licences, permits and endorsements
rather than reproducing it, and it does not explain on that page what any
particular endorsement covers.

Two consequences matter here:

- **A fishing licence does not automatically let you take shellfish.**
  Shellfish and seaweed is its own licence, and Puget Sound crab has its
  own endorsement on top. [Taking Without Ruining It](/library#taking-without-ruining-it)
  covers the shellfish licensing structure, including current fee
  figures, and [The Species Where You Live](/library#the-species-where-you-live)
  makes the sharp version of the point: the Puget Sound crab endorsement
  and Dungeness catch record card requirement can bind you even when the
  crab you actually keep is a red rock crab that needs no card of its
  own. None of that is findable by looking at the animal.
- **Buying the wrong licence is a quiet failure.** Nothing stops you
  fishing. You find out when somebody checks.

I did not find, on the state's main recreational fishing licence page,
any statement telling the buyer to check current regulations or warning
about emergency rule changes, and no statement of where the rules are
published. Those live on the regulations pages instead. It is worth
knowing that the licence purchase does not itself brief you.

### Catch record cards

A catch record card is a separate document from the licence, and it is
required when fishing for **salmon, steelhead, sturgeon, halibut, and
Puget Sound Dungeness crab**.

The timing requirement is the part people break without meaning to. After
you retain a fish or a Puget Sound Dungeness crab, you must record that
catch on the card **before continuing to fish**. For paper cards the
state states the requirement as recording retained catch immediately,
before fishing again or redeploying crabbing gear. You do not fill the
card in at the end of the day. You fill it in with wet hands, in the
boat, before the next cast.

Cards must be returned by their deadlines **even if you caught nothing**.
The state gives three: fish cards are due by April 30 following the end
of the licence year; summer crab cards by October 1; winter crab cards by
February 1.

That "even if you caught nothing" is not bureaucracy for its own sake.
The cards are a data source. A card returned blank is information about
effort and absence, which is exactly what a manager setting next year's
season needs and cannot get any other way. An unreturned card is a hole
in the dataset that the season is set from.

The state's page does not specify what information the card requires,
pointing instead to a separate instruction document with catch area
codes, and it names no penalty for failing to return a paper card beyond
a ten dollar fine attached to a particular crab endorsement survey. Read
the card's own instructions.

### Treaty rights are law, not a footnote

Who may fish here, and under what authority, is not answered entirely by
the state. Treaty-reserved fishing rights are law, and the fisheries are
co-managed rather than administered by the state alone.

This guide does not restate that material because the Library already
covers it properly: [Taking Without Ruining It](/library#taking-without-ruining-it)
carries the treaty text and the case law. If you are going to fish here
regularly, read it. It is not background colour. It explains why the
management structure looks the way it does.

### What the app's own law data does not have

This guide's topic declares `data/laws/laws.json` as its data layer. I
read that file in full, and I am reporting what is in it, because a
reader who goes looking will otherwise assume they missed something.

The file contains 170 rules across four jurisdictions: 9 general, 36
federal, 96 Washington state, and 29 Kitsap County. **None of them is
about fishing.** There is no rule about needing a licence to take
anything wild, no season, no bag limit, no size limit, no catch record
card, and no gear rule. The file's ten categories do not include one that
would hold them. Every one of the 40-odd licence references in the file
is a driver's licence, a business licence, a firearms dealer licence, or
a pet licence.

Four rules in it are genuinely adjacent and worth knowing:

- `usa-don-t-harm-protected-species-or-their-habitat` summarises the
  Endangered Species Act's prohibition on "taking" listed species, and
  names salmon and orcas in the Puget Sound region specifically. This is
  the legal backdrop to everything in section 1.
- `kitsap-kitsap-requires-buffers-around-wetlands-and-streams` and
  `wa-critical-areas-on-your-property-are-protected-wetlands-strea`
  protect fish-bearing streams through land-use buffers rather than
  through fishing rules.
- `kitsap-septic-systems-must-be-permitted-maintained-and-inspected`
  connects household wastewater to shellfish beds, which is a real
  mechanism and not a rhetorical link.

So the honest statement is this: **the app's law data cannot currently
answer a fishing question, and this guide does not pretend otherwise.**
It answers habitat and protected-species questions. For seasons, limits
and licences you must go to the live source, which is the point of the
next subsection anyway, since those are exactly the facts that a stored
copy would get wrong.

[Taking Without Ruining It](/library#taking-without-ruining-it) reported the
same gap from the shellfish side, so this is a known and repeated finding
rather than a new one.

### Where to check

For this place, the authorities are:

- **The state fish and wildlife agency** for seasons, limits, sizes, gear
  rules, licences and catch record cards, in the annual Sport Fishing
  Seasons and Regulations pamphlet, plus its emergency rule changes, plus
  its app or hotlines. In Washington this is the Department of Fish and
  Wildlife.
- **NOAA Fisheries** for whether a marine or anadromous population is
  federally listed.
- **The state health department** for whether a fish is safe to eat in
  the quantities you intend, which is section 5.
- **The state health department's biotoxin programme** for shellfish, on
  the day, which is section 6 and belongs to a different guide.

Check the first one every trip. Check the second one once per species and
then when something changes. Check the third when you change what you eat
or who is eating it. Check the fourth every single time, without
exception, before taking any shellfish.

## 3. Barbless hooks, selective fisheries, and why release technique is a rule

### The idea

Here is the logic that connects this section to the last one. If a
fishery requires you to release wild fish, then the conservation value of
that requirement is entirely determined by whether the released fish
lives. A rule that returns dead fish to the water accomplishes nothing
except paperwork.

So release technique is not etiquette, and it is not a courtesy to the
fish. It is the mechanism by which the rule works at all. In Washington
this is made explicit, because the handling requirements are written
**into the gear rules themselves**.

### What selective gear rules actually require

"Selective gear rules" is a defined term in Washington's sport fishing
rules, and both Silverdale creeks are open under it. The definition
requires all of the following at once:

- artificial flies or lures only, with **barbless single-point hooks**
- **bait is prohibited**
- up to three hooks
- **only knotless nets** may be used to land fish

Look at what that list is doing. Every element reduces damage to a fish
you are going to put back.

- **Barbless** hooks come out with far less tearing, and can often be
  backed out without handling the fish at all. The rules define barbless
  as a hook whose barbs were either absent when manufactured, filed off,
  or pinched down, so you do not need to buy special hooks; crimping the
  barb flat with pliers satisfies it.
- **Single-point** hooks mean one wound rather than the three that a
  treble can inflict, and no second hook swinging free to catch a gill or
  an eye while you work.
- **No bait** is the least obvious and possibly the most important. A
  fish takes a lure in the mouth and usually gets hooked there. A fish
  takes bait properly, swallows it, and gets hooked in the throat or the
  gut, which is the injury a released fish most often dies of. Banning
  bait in a release fishery is a direct attack on deep hooking.
- **Knotless nets.** A knotted mesh scrapes. This matters more than it
  sounds, for the reason in the next subsection.

The anti-snagging rule, also defined in the same rules, restricts
terminal gear to small single-point hooks attached at or below the lure
or bait and prohibits weights below the lure or within twelve inches
above it, with exceptions. Its purpose is the same family: it makes it
harder to foul-hook a fish in the body rather than catch it in the mouth.

There is also a defined night closure, which closes fishing from one hour
after official sunset to one hour before official sunrise, and a defined
daily limit, which is the maximum number or weight of a species or
combination of species a person may retain in a single day.

I looked for a definition of "release" itself in the rules definitions
and did not find one. The handling standard therefore does not come from
a single defined term; it comes from the gear rules above, from
species-specific instructions like the bull trout one in section 1, and
from the best-practice guidance below.

### Handling, from a source you can check

NOAA Fisheries publishes catch and release best practices. Because it is
a federal agency, its words are in the public domain and can be quoted
directly rather than paraphrased. The relevant instructions are:

- **"Handle the fish as little as possible and only with wet hands."**
- **"Keep air exposure to a minimum if you must remove a fish from the
  water. Less than 60 seconds is ideal."**
- **"Dehook the fish in the water whenever possible."**
- **"Cut the line as close to the hook as possible if a swallowed hook
  can't be easily removed."**
- **"Never fight a fish to exhaustion."**
- On nets: **"Wet, soft knotless mesh or a rubber landing net. These
  cause less damage to a fish's eyes, fins, scales, and protective mucus
  coating."**
- On hooks: **"Circle hooks, barbless hooks, or hooks with crimped barbs.
  These can increase survival rates and make hook removal easier."**
- On a fish that will not swim off: **"Resuscitate a sluggish fish by
  facing it into the current or pulling it head-first through the water
  until it regains strength and can swim away on its own."**

Take the wet hands instruction seriously, because the reason is
mechanical rather than sentimental. A fish is coated in mucus, and NOAA's
own wording names that coating as something a bad net damages. Dry hands,
a dry towel, dry gloves, or a dry deck strip it off in patches. The fish
swims away looking fine and is then open to infection through the bare
skin. Nothing you observe at the moment of release tells you whether this
happened. That is precisely why it has to be a habit rather than a
judgement call.

The 60-second figure is worth internalising as a budget rather than a
target. A fish out of water is suffocating, and if you are holding it up
while somebody finds a phone, unlocks it, and frames a photograph, you
will exceed 60 seconds without noticing. If you want a photograph, have
the camera ready before the fish leaves the water, or photograph it in
the net in the water.

**Cutting the line is a real instruction, not a last resort you should
feel bad about.** A hook left in a fish that swims away is a much better
outcome than a hook extracted from a fish that does not. If the hook is
swallowed, cut the line close and let it go.

Two things I looked for on that page and did not find: any mention of
photographs, and any discussion of water temperature affecting release
survival. Water temperature is known to matter for salmonids in the
general literature, but since I could not open a page stating it, this
guide does not give you a temperature threshold. If you are fishing warm
low water in late summer, treat handling as more critical rather than
less, and understand that I am telling you that as a conservative
inference and not as a sourced rule.

### The honest limit of this section

Catch-and-release mortality is not zero, and no technique makes it zero.
Every fish you hook and release has some chance of dying anyway, and that
chance rises with fight time, air exposure, handling, hook location and
water conditions. The techniques above reduce it. They do not eliminate
it.

This matters for how you plan a day rather than only how you handle one
fish. If you are fishing a water where you must release most of what you
catch, then catching a great many fish is not a harmless activity, and
"I put them all back" is not a complete account of your impact. The
number of fish you hook is itself a decision.

And again, the part reading cannot supply: unhooking a green fish in the
water, quickly, without lifting it, is a physical skill. The first few
times you will be slow and clumsy, and that is when fish get hurt. If you
can learn this alongside somebody who already does it, do that.

## 4. What happened to the fish between the water and the pan

This is the food-safety half, and it is where most home-caught fish goes
wrong. The failures here are quiet: the fish looks fine, smells fine, and
is fine to look at right up until it is not fine to eat.

### Cold is the whole job

A live fish holds bacteria on its gills, its skin and in its gut without
coming to any harm, because a living animal's defences keep them out of
the muscle. The moment the fish dies, those defences stop. From then on
the only thing standing between you and a problem is temperature.

The FDA publishes time limits for chilling fish after death, aimed at
commercial harvest vessels. They are the clearest sourced numbers
available for the question "how fast is fast enough," and they are worth
knowing even though your cooler is not a fishing boat:

- Fish exposed to air or water temperatures **above 83 degrees F (28.3
  degrees C)** should be placed in ice, refrigerated seawater, ice slurry
  or brine at **40 degrees F (4.4 degrees C) or less**, as soon as
  possible after harvest, and **not more than 6 hours** from the time of
  death.
- Fish exposed to air and water temperatures of **83 degrees F or less**
  get **not more than 9 hours** from the time of death, to the same 40
  degrees F or colder.
- Fish that are **gilled and gutted before chilling** get **not more than
  12 hours** from time of death.

Two things to take from that.

First, the target is not "cold," it is **40 degrees F or below, fast**. A
fish lying in the bottom of a boat in the sun, or in a bucket of
seawater, is not being chilled. Ice, or an ice slurry, or a proper cooler.
Bring more ice than you think you need, because ice you did not use costs
you nothing and ice you ran out of costs you the fish.

Second, notice that the clock starts **at death**, not when you get back
to the dock. If you are fishing over several hours, the first fish of the
day has been dead for hours by the time the last one is landed. It is the
first fish that is at risk, and it is the first fish that people forget.

### Why gutting buys you time

Look again at the third bullet: gilling and gutting before chilling
extends the limit from 9 hours to 12. That is a 3-hour gain, and it tells
you something about where the problem lives.

The gut and the gills are where the bacteria are. Removing them removes a
large part of the starting population, and it removes the gut enzymes
that digest the fish's own belly wall from the inside once it is dead.
Bleeding the fish promptly serves the eating quality as well, since blood
left in the flesh darkens it and carries flavours you do not want.

The FDA is careful about one thing here and so should you be. Removing
the viscera and gills **reduces but does not eliminate** the bacteria,
and if done badly it can make matters worse by spreading bacteria from
the gut cavity into the flesh. Practically: gut the fish cleanly, rinse
the cavity, do not saw through the intestine and then wipe it along the
fillet, and get it on ice. FDA also notes that packing the body cavity
with ice helps chill a large fish whose internal temperature is otherwise
slow to come down, which is the right technique for a big Chinook.

A caution worth stating plainly: gutting a fish is not automatically
better in all circumstances, because some fisheries require fish to be
kept whole or intact for identification or measurement. Check the rules
for your fishery before you start cutting. This is one of the places
where the food-safety answer and the legal answer can point in different
directions, and the legal one binds.

### Histamine, and why you are now the upstream

There is a hazard in some fish that is not spoilage in the ordinary
sense, cannot be detected by smell or taste, and cannot be cooked out.
Certain species carry a lot of the amino acid histidine, and bacteria
that live on the fish harmlessly convert it to histamine once the fish is
dead and warm. Then the histamine stays, through cooking, canning and
freezing.

The Library already carries this properly, with sources, in
[Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe). That guide
gives the mechanism, the affected species, the symptoms, the onset time,
the regulatory thresholds, and the FDA statement that cooking, canning
and freezing do not reduce the toxic effects. Go and read that section
rather than taking a summary from me.

What is worth adding **here**, and only here, is a change of position.
That guide's conclusion, correctly, is that for a fish you buy, the
entire defence is upstream of you: somebody else kept it cold, or did
not, before you ever saw it, and prevention is the whole of the
treatment.

**When you catch the fish yourself, you are the upstream.** The
refrigeration episode that creates histamine is the one happening in your
boat, in your cooler, on your tailgate. There is nobody further back to
blame and nobody else to get it right. That is the entire reason the
previous two subsections exist, and it is why "I'll deal with it when I
get home" is a decision rather than a delay.

### Parasites, and exactly what freezing does

Parasites in the larval stage, eaten in raw or undercooked seafood, are a
real hazard. FDA names the groups of most concern in seafood as the
nematodes or roundworms (Anisakis, Pseudoterranova, Eustrongylides and
Gnathostoma species), the cestodes or tapeworms (Diphyllobothrium
species), and the trematodes or flukes (including Nanophyetes salmincola,
which is a Pacific Northwest fluke). FDA states that most of these cause
mild to moderate illness but that severe symptoms can occur, and
describes roundworms embedding in the intestinal wall and sometimes
penetrating it.

**Cooking handles this.** FDA is unambiguous: "The process of heating raw
fish sufficiently to kill bacterial pathogens is also sufficient to kill
parasites." If you cook your fish properly, parasites are not your
problem. The cooking temperature itself is covered in
[Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe), which carries
the figure for finned fish along with the alternative test of cooking
until the flesh is opaque and separates easily with a fork. I am
deliberately not restating a temperature here, so that there is one
number in the Library rather than two that might drift apart.

**Freezing handles it too, but only at parameters most kitchens cannot
reach.** FDA gives three equivalent conditions, any one of which is
sufficient to kill parasites:

- freezing and storing at an ambient temperature of **minus 4 degrees F
  (minus 20 degrees C) or below for 7 days** total time; or
- freezing at an ambient temperature of **minus 31 degrees F (minus 35
  degrees C) or below until solid**, then storing at minus 31 degrees F
  or below **for 15 hours**; or
- freezing at **minus 31 degrees F or below until solid**, then storing
  at **minus 4 degrees F or below for 24 hours**.

Now the caveats, all of which are FDA's own and all of which matter more
to you than to the processor this guidance was written for:

- **FDA notes these conditions "may not be suitable for freezing
  particularly large fish (e.g., thicker than 6 inches)."** A whole
  Chinook salmon is thicker than 6 inches. So is a large lingcod. The
  published schedule does not claim to cover them, and FDA's own remedy
  is to run a study for your specific method, thickness, species and
  target parasite, which you are not going to do. Fillet it thin, or cook
  it.
- **A domestic freezer is typically set around 0 degrees F, not minus 4.**
  That is four degrees F warmer than the gentlest of the three schedules.
  Check your freezer with a thermometer before assuming it reaches minus
  4 degrees F and holds there for a week, and be aware that a freezer
  opened daily is not holding a steady ambient temperature.
- **Salting, brining, pickling and cold-smoking do not do this job.** FDA
  states that brining and pickling may reduce the parasite hazard but do
  not eliminate it or reduce it to an acceptable level, and gives the
  reason: nematode larvae have been shown to survive 28 days in an 80
  salinometer brine, which is 21 percent salt by weight. If you are
  making gravlax, ceviche, poke, sashimi or cold-smoked fish at home from
  fish you caught, the fish needs to have been properly frozen first.
  FDA's own list of dishes implicated in human parasite infection
  includes ceviche, lomi lomi, poisson cru, sashimi, sushi, herring roe,
  cold-smoked fish and undercooked grilled fish.
- **Not all parasites freeze alike.** FDA notes tapeworms are more
  susceptible to freezing than roundworms, and flukes appear more
  resistant than roundworms.
- **Farmed is not the same as wild here.** FDA states that species which
  normally have a parasite hazard from eating infected prey apparently do
  not have the same hazard when raised only on pelleted feed in
  aquaculture. This is one of the few genuine food-safety differences
  between farmed and wild fish, and it runs in the direction most people
  do not expect.

**What I could not source, stated plainly.** FDA's Table 3-2 lists
species-related hazards fish by fish, and it does distinguish wild salmon
from aquacultured salmon as separate rows. I opened that chapter, but the
hazard marks in the table are not extractable as text from the published
PDF, so I could not read which hazards FDA marks against Pacific salmon.
I am therefore **not** telling you what FDA's table says about salmon.

What I can tell you, because I read it, is that FDA's parasites chapter
lists among its references a study titled "Prevalence of larval Anisakis
simplex in pen-reared and wild-caught salmon (Salmonidae) from Puget
Sound, Washington." I have not read that study and I am not reporting
what it found. I mention it only to establish that the question of
whether the salmon in this exact water carry this exact worm is a
question somebody has studied by name, rather than something to wave
away.

And one thing that surprised me, which is why it is worth passing on: the
CDC page on anisakiasis names the most common carriers as predatory
species such as **herring, cod, mackerel and sculpin**, and does not name
salmon at all. Herring is a local species here, record `pacific_herring`,
and one that people eat pickled, which is exactly the preparation FDA
says does not eliminate the hazard. CDC's page gives no cooking or
freezing parameters, so the parameters above remain FDA's.

The safe summary, which requires none of the uncertainty above: **cook
it, or freeze it properly first. If you intend to eat any fish you caught
raw or nearly raw, the freezing schedule is not optional and your kitchen
freezer probably does not meet it.**

### What freezing does not do

This needs saying explicitly, because a reader could otherwise take the
last section to mean something it does not, and because it would appear
to contradict another Library guide.

[Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe) states that
freezing is a pause, not a sterilisation, and cites FDA for the fact that
freezing does not kill most bacteria, it stops them growing. That is
correct and it is not in conflict with anything above. The distinction is
by organism:

- **Parasites: freezing kills them**, at the parameters listed above.
  Parasites are multicellular animals and ice crystals destroy them.
- **Bacteria: freezing does not kill them.** It halts growth. They resume
  when the fish thaws.
- **Histamine: freezing does nothing at all.** It is already a chemical,
  not an organism, and it does not care about temperature.
- **Marine biotoxins in shellfish: freezing does nothing.** Covered in
  [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide).

So a fish that was left warm for six hours and then frozen hard is still
a fish that was left warm for six hours. Freezing it did not undo that.
It froze the consequences in place. This is the single most useful
sentence in this section: **freezing preserves the state the fish was in
when you froze it, including the bad states.**

## 5. Contaminants, which are invisible and are a per-water question

Everything above is about things you did or did not do. This section is
about something already in the fish before you caught it, which you
cannot see, smell, taste, wash off, or in one important case cook away.

### Mercury and PCBs are two different problems

Washington's Department of Health, in its Puget Sound fish consumption
advice, identifies two contaminants of concern for people eating certain
Puget Sound fish: **PCBs** and **mercury**, specifically methylmercury.
They behave differently and that difference decides what you can do about
them.

**PCBs** are industrial chemicals, once used widely in products such as
coolants and lubricants for transformers and banned in 1977, that persist
in the environment and accumulate in fat. Because they sit
in the fat, skin and organs, you can physically remove much of the
contaminated tissue. The department's preparation advice is to remove the
skin, fat and internal organs before cooking, and to grill, bake or broil
so that remaining fat drips away during cooking. It states that these
steps can reduce PCBs and similar fat-borne contaminants by up to 50
percent.

**Mercury cannot be handled this way and no preparation touches it.** The
department is explicit that mercury is stored in the muscle of the fish,
which is the part you eat, and that no cleaning method and no cooking
method reduces it. A lean, skinless, carefully trimmed fillet carries
essentially the mercury the fish had.

Both contaminants build up through the food web. The department's
explanation is that contaminants accumulate in the muscle and fat of fish
that eat smaller contaminated organisms, so the bigger and older a fish
is, the more it is likely to have eaten and the more it carries. This is
why its preparation advice includes choosing younger, smaller fish within
legal limits, and it is why a short-lived forage fish low in the food web
is generally a cleaner meal than a large, long-lived predator from the
same water.

### What Washington actually advises for these fish

Here I am going to give figures, which this guide has otherwise avoided,
because health advisories are a different kind of number from a bag
limit: they are revised rarely, they are not water-body-and-date
intersections, and withholding them would leave the section useless. But
read the date attached to them and check the current version.

The figures below come from the department's Puget Sound fish consumption
advice, publication 334-098, dated **September 2015**, covering Marine
Areas 6 through 13. Marine Area 10, the water off Silverdale, is inside
that range. The department's current Healthy Fish Guide independently
lists Puget Sound Chinook in its limit tier at one serving per week,
which agrees with the 2015 publication, so the headline figure appears to
have held. I could not open a more recent full advisory than the 2015
publication.

For salmon in all Puget Sound marine areas 6 through 13:

| Fish | Advice |
|---|---|
| Chinook | No more than 1 serving per week |
| Chinook, resident (blackmouth) | No more than 2 servings per month |
| Coho | Unrestricted |
| Chum, pink, sockeye | Unrestricted |

"Unrestricted" is a defined term in that document and it does not mean
unlimited. The department defines it as **2 to 3 servings per week**.

The blackmouth line is the interesting one, and it is the clearest
example in this whole guide of identification deciding a health question
rather than a legal one. Blackmouth are resident Chinook: fish that stay
in Puget Sound rather than migrating out to the ocean. The department's
explanation is exactly that, that most blackmouth remain in Puget Sound
rather than migrating, so they accumulate more contaminants, and that
resident Chinook therefore carry higher levels. Two servings a month is
less than half of one serving a week, so the same species in the same
water gets an advisory roughly twice as strict depending on how that
individual fish spent its life. The record `chinook_salmon` carries this.

The department also notes that chum, pink and sockeye were not sampled in
the monitoring programme behind this advice, and that its unrestricted
rating for them rests on data from other sources showing those species
tend to have low PCB levels. That is a weaker evidentiary basis than the
Chinook and coho figures, and it is labelled as such in the source rather
than by me.

**What a serving is.** The department defines a serving as 8 ounces of
uncooked fish for a 160 pound adult, adjusted by one ounce for each 20
pounds of body weight above or below that. So a 160 pound adult gets 8
ounces, an 80 pound child gets 4 ounces, and a 200 pound adult gets 10
ounces. It also gives the informal version: about the size and thickness
of your hand.

**The budget is shared.** The department states that if you eat the
recommended amount for a week, no other fish should be eaten that week.
The advice is not per-species-per-week stacked on top of each other. One
serving of Chinook is the week's fish.

### The local detail, which is more specific than the headline

The same publication carries area-by-area advice for English sole and
other flatfish, and this is where Silverdale readers should pay
attention, because Marine Area 10 is mostly unrestricted **with named
exceptions**, and two of the exceptions are close to home:

- Sinclair Inlet: no more than 1 serving per month.
- Port Orchard Waterway: no more than 1 serving per week.
- Eagle Harbor: no more than 1 serving per week.
- Elliott Bay: no more than 2 servings per month.
- Duwamish Waterway: no consumption.

That is a range from unrestricted to none at all, inside one marine area,
for the same species of fish. It is the single best illustration of the
section heading: contamination is a **per water body** question, and a
fish's address matters more than its species name. A flatfish taken a few
miles apart in the same marine area can carry two completely different
pieces of advice.

Rockfish, as noted in section 1, carry a no-consumption advice across all
Puget Sound marine areas 6 through 13, and that one is for conservation
reasons rather than contamination.

Lingcod is explicitly **outside** this advice. The publication states
that recommendations for other bottomfish such as lingcod are not
included. So for lingcod, record `lingcod`, there is no consumption
advisory in that document either way. Absence of advice is not advice of
absence, and I am not going to convert it into either reassurance or
alarm.

### The federal advice, which is a different instrument

The FDA and EPA jointly publish advice about eating fish aimed at people
who might become or are pregnant or breastfeeding, and children aged 1 to
11. It is a federal work, so it can be quoted directly.

Its structure is a three-tier list. Best Choices: "Eat 2 to 3 servings a
week from the 'Best Choices' list." Good Choices: one serving a week
instead. Choices to Avoid: do not eat, because of the highest mercury
levels. For children the advice is "Eat 2 servings a week from the 'Best
Choices' list."

Salmon is in the Best Choices tier. The Choices to Avoid list is king
mackerel, marlin, orange roughy, shark, swordfish, tilefish from the Gulf
of Mexico, and bigeye tuna, none of which you are going to catch in Dyes
Inlet.

Note that the federal serving size is defined differently from the state
one. FDA and EPA define one serving as 4 ounces for a pregnant or
breastfeeding adult, and for children give 1 ounce at age 1 to 3, 2
ounces at age 4 to 7, 3 ounces at age 8 to 10, and 4 ounces at age 11.
The state's 8-ounce-per-160-pound definition is a different measure for a
different purpose. Do not mix the two, and when following a piece of
advice, use the serving definition that came with it.

The most important sentence on the federal page for an angler is this
one, and it is quotable verbatim: **"Check for fish and shellfish
advisories to tell you how often you can safely eat those fish. If there
is no advisory, eat only one serving and no other fish that week."**

That is the default when you have no local information: one serving,
nothing else that week. It is a genuinely useful rule because it converts
"I do not know" into an action rather than a shrug.

### Who gets different advice, and why the difference is not a formality

Both the state and the federal advice single out the same groups: people
who are or might become pregnant, nursing mothers, and young children.

The reason is developmental. The state's material links both mercury and
PCB exposure in the womb to later learning and behaviour problems in
children, and notes PCBs can also affect the immune system. The exposure
that matters is exposure during development, which is why the advice is
framed around people who **might** become pregnant rather than only those
who currently are: the contaminant load builds over time and does not
clear on the timescale of noticing a pregnancy.

Both agencies are also clear that the answer is not to stop eating fish.
The state's framing is that removing fish from your diet does not
eliminate exposure to contaminants, since other foods carry them too,
but does eliminate the substantial benefits of eating fish. The point of
an advisory is to shift **which** fish and **how much**, not to replace
fish with something else.

### What I will not tell you

I am not going to give you a meal frequency for a fish, a water body, or
a person that I did not read on a real page. If you are fishing a water
that has no published advisory, the federal default above applies: one
serving, no other fish that week, until you find out more.

I am also not going to convert the 2015 figures into "the current rules."
They are the most recent complete Puget Sound advisory I could open, one
of the headline figures is corroborated by a current page, and that is
the honest description of their status. Check the department's advisories
by water body before relying on them, particularly if you are in one of
the named exception areas above or you are in one of the sensitive
groups.

## 6. Shellfish is a different regime entirely

Everything above is about finned fish. Shellfish is governed differently,
carries a different hazard, and is covered elsewhere in the Library. One
paragraph, then go and read the real treatment.

The difference that matters is that shellfish carry marine biotoxins,
which come from algae, are not detectable by look, smell or taste, are
not destroyed by cooking or freezing, and can be lethal. Because the
toxin comes and goes with algal conditions rather than with seasons, the
answer expires daily. A beach can be open for the season and closed for
biotoxins on the same afternoon. This is why shellfish harvest requires a
check on the day, every time, in a way that fishing does not.

[Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) is the owner of that material and
carries the biotoxin closure procedure and the recorded hotline number.
[The Species Where You Live](/library#the-species-where-you-live) covers the
three-authority test, which is the structure of who answers what: the
licence, the fish and wildlife agency's season and limits for that
specific beach, and the health department's biotoxin status for that
beach on the day. [Taking Without Ruining It](/library#taking-without-ruining-it)
adds the ownership question underneath all three, since most tideland
here is private, and carries the harvest limits and the digging technique
that determines whether the animals you put back survive.

Do not treat the fishing licence in your pocket as covering any of this.
Shellfish and seaweed is a separate licence, and Puget Sound crab carries
its own endorsement and catch record card on top.

## Where reading stops

This guide can give you the structure of the rules, the vocabulary to
read them, the reason the adipose fin matters, sourced numbers for
chilling and freezing, and an honest account of what the contaminant
advice says and when it was written.

It cannot identify a fish for you at dusk in a chop. It cannot teach you
to unhook a green salmon in the water without lifting it. It cannot tell
you what the season is today, and any version of this document that tried
would be wrong by the time you read it.

Three habits carry most of the value, and all three are cheap:

1. **Check the rules for your species, your area, and today, before you
   go.** Including emergency changes. Every trip.
2. **Look for the healed scar, not just the missing fin.** And if you
   cannot confirm a fish may be kept, it may not be kept.
3. **Get the fish cold immediately and keep it cold.** You are the
   upstream now, and cold is the only part of food safety that is
   entirely within your control on the water.

If you are going out on the water at all, read
[The Hazards Your Place Actually Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) for
the hazards of being out there, and [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide)
for cold water, currents and the fact that immersion here is dangerous
year round. More anglers are hurt by the water than by anything in this
guide.

- Washington Department of Fish and Wildlife, fishing regulations. The

  hotlines. https://wdfw.wa.gov/fishing/regulations

  numbered marine areas with subdivisions, and Marine Area 10 named as

  https://wdfw.wa.gov/fishing/locations/marine-areas
- Washington sport fishing rules, definitions. The definitions of
  hatchery salmon (clipped adipose fin plus healed scar), hatchery
  steelhead or trout (clipped adipose or ventral fin plus healed scar),
  wild Chinook and coho, wild steelhead and trout, selective gear rules,

  https://www.eregulations.com/washington/fishing/definitions

  Advice, publication 334-098, dated September 2015. PCBs and mercury as

- Washington State Department of Health, Healthy Fish Guide. Puget Sound
  Chinook in the limit tier at one serving per week, corroborating the
  2015 figure; the statement that no cooking method reduces mercury

  pesticides can be reduced by up to 50 percent by removing fat or
  letting it drip away; and the serving size definition. This page
  carries no blackmouth figure.
  https://doh.wa.gov/community-and-environment/food/fish/healthy-fish-guide
- Washington State Department of Health, fish consumption advisories by

### What could not be sourced

- **Which hazards FDA marks against Pacific salmon in Table 3-2.** The
  table's marks are not extractable from the published PDF. No claim is
  made.
- **A water temperature threshold above which catch-and-release handling
  becomes critical.** Not on the NOAA best-practices page, and I did not
  find another page I could open stating one. The guide flags the
  inference as an inference.
- **How to distinguish a clipped adipose fin from a damaged or
  regenerated one.** Not on the state's mass-marking page. The healed
  scar in the rules definition is what the guide relies on instead.
- **A Puget Sound fish consumption advisory more recent than the
  September 2015 publication.** The current Healthy Fish Guide
  corroborates the headline Chinook figure, but I could not open a newer
  complete advisory document.
- **A published reason why mass marking is done, expressed as a legal
  requirement.** The state page describes the practice and its purpose
  but cites no statute, so this guide does not attribute it to one.
