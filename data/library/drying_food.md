# Drying Food

Drying keeps food by taking away the water that moulds, yeasts and bacteria
need to grow. Done well, it turns a glut of fruit, vegetables, herbs or meat
into food that keeps at room temperature, weighs a fraction of what it did
fresh, and needs no power once it is dry. Done carelessly, it gives you fruit
that moulds in the jar, or jerky that still carries the bacteria the raw meat
did. This guide is about doing it well:

- which foods dry well, and how to choose and prepare them;
- the acid dips for fruit and the blanching for vegetables, and what each one
  is for;
- temperatures and times for fruit, vegetables, herbs and jerky;
- the one rule for meat that drying alone cannot replace;
- dehydrators, ovens, the sun and plain air, and when each one works;
- how to tell food is dry enough, conditioning, storing, and how long dried
  food keeps.

Temperatures are in degrees Fahrenheit with Celsius in brackets, because the
US sources this guide rests on are written in Fahrenheit. Lengths and weights
are in US units with metric alongside.

## Two rules before you start

**Meat has to be heated to a safe internal temperature, not only dried.**
The USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service (FSIS) tells home jerky makers to
"heat meat to 160°F and poultry to 165°F before the dehydrating process," and
gives the reason in the next sentence: "This step assures that any bacteria
present will be destroyed by wet heat." That is 71 C for meat and 74 C for
poultry. Wild game needs 165 F (74 C) as well, because of a parasite that
freezing may not kill (CDC; step 1 of the jerky method below). A dehydrator
running at 130 to 140 F (54 to 60 C) dries meat; it does
not reliably make it safe. The jerky section below explains why, and gives the
two tested ways to do it.

**Dry at a temperature you control, and dry outdoors only what can take it.**
The National Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) explains that fruit is
safe to dry outdoors in good weather because of its sugar and acid, but that
vegetables and meat are not recommended for it. Vegetables are low in both
sugar and acid, so they spoil more easily, and meat is rich in protein that
microbes grow on readily when nobody can control the heat and humidity. The
one outdoor exception it allows among vegetables is beans dried on the vine.
Everything else, meat and vegetables above all, is dried indoors in a
dehydrator or an oven.

## Why drying keeps food

Colorado State University Extension (CSU) puts it simply: drying preserves
food by taking out enough water to stop decay, and properly dried food holds
somewhere between 5 and 25 percent water, depending on the food. NCHFP gives
the two targets that matter most at home: home-dried fruit ends up with about
20 percent moisture, and vegetables with about 10 percent, dry enough to be
brittle.

Three things do the work, in CSU's account: enough heat to draw the moisture
out without cooking the food, dry air to take the moisture up, and moving air
to carry it away. Get the balance wrong at either end and it fails.

- **Too cool at the start**, CSU warns, and microorganisms can survive and
  even multiply before the food has dried.
- **Too hot**, and the outside of each piece hardens before the inside has
  dried, trapping moisture in the middle. Penn State Extension calls this
  case hardening. The piece looks dry and moulds later from the inside.

There is a deeper reason drying works, and a separate guide in this Library
covers it: [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains water
activity, the measure of how much of a food's water is free for microbes to
use, and why food that is properly dried cannot grow the organism behind
botulism. The practical lesson is the one that guide draws: "properly dried"
means dried by tested guidance, not by your guess at when a piece feels crisp,
and the usual way under-dried food fails is by moulding in its container.

Drying is a way to stop growth, not a way to sterilise. FSIS warns that
bacteria which survive the drying become harder to kill afterwards: "After
drying, bacteria become much more heat resistant." Utah State University
Extension (USU) makes the same point about fruit from the other side: fruit
dried to its target is safe from spoilage by microbes, with the exception of
mould. That exception is why the steps after drying, conditioning and
storage, matter as much as the drying itself.

## Which foods dry well

- **Fruit** is the easiest and most forgiving, because of its sugar and acid.
  CSU gives tested steps for apples, apricots, bananas, berries, cherries,
  citrus peel, figs, grapes and black currants, melons (cantaloupe dries
  better than watermelon), nectarines and peaches, pears (it recommends the
  Bartlett variety) and plums.
- **Vegetables** dry well once blanched. CSU's table covers asparagus, green
  beans, beets, broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, carrots and parsnips,
  cauliflower, celery, chili peppers, corn, eggplant, horseradish,
  mushrooms, okra, onions, parsley and other herbs, peas, sweet peppers,
  potatoes, spinach and other greens, summer and winter squash, and
  tomatoes.
- **Herbs** are the simplest of all to dry; NCHFP calls drying the easiest
  way to preserve them.
- **Lean meat** makes jerky. NCHFP lists beef, pork, venison and smoked turkey
  breast, says to trim off all the fat because fat goes rancid quickly, and
  notes that raw poultry is generally not recommended for jerky, for the
  texture and flavour of the result.
- **Dry beans** can be left to dry on the vine (see "Four ways to dry" below).

Choose well, because drying cannot fix poor produce. CSU says it plainly:
drying does not improve food quality. Pick fruit fresh and fully ripe;
unripe fruit lacks flavour and colour, and overripe fruit goes tough or
mushy. Pick vegetables at their peak, except sweet corn and peas, which are
better slightly immature, before their sugars turn to starch. Work quickly:
CSU notes that picking sets off enzyme changes in vegetables, so prepare them
at once. Throw out any piece with decay, bruises or mould, because, as CSU
warns, such defects can affect all the food being dried with it.

**Mushrooms carry a special warning.** CSU's vegetable table notes that the
poisons in poisonous mushrooms are not destroyed by drying or by cooking, and
that only an expert can tell poisonous from edible kinds. Dry only mushrooms
you bought or grew yourself from a known culture
([Growing Mushrooms](/library#growing-mushrooms)); telling wild ones apart is a
separate skill ([Identifying Wild Plants](/library#identifying-wild-plants)).

Expect food to shrink a great deal. CSU's yield tables give 12 pounds of
apples drying down to about 1 1/4 pounds (three pints), and 14 pounds of
tomatoes to about half a pound. FSIS says a pound of meat weighs about four
ounces once it is jerky.

## Getting fruit ready: the acid dip, and why it matters

CSU calls pretreating fruit before drying highly recommended, and gives three
reasons for it:

1. **Colour.** A dip keeps light-coloured fruit such as apples, pears,
   peaches and apricots from darkening while it dries and while it is stored.
   (Ascorbic acid is vitamin C, an antioxidant, which is what stops the
   browning.) CSU adds that dipping in ascorbic acid or lemon juice raises the
   fruit's vitamin C, though some of it is lost in drying.
2. **Safety.** CSU reports research showing that an acid dip, or a sodium
   metabisulfite dip, increases how many harmful bacteria die during drying,
   naming E. coli O157:H7, Salmonella and Listeria monocytogenes. Penn State
   Extension reports the same finding for acid dips. This is the reason not
   to skip the dip even if you do not mind brown fruit.
3. **Speed, for waxy-skinned fruit.** Grapes, prunes, small dark plums,
   cherries, figs and firm berries have skins with a waxy coating that hold
   moisture in. CSU's answer is to "crack" the skins before drying the fruit
   whole: dip it in briskly boiling water for 30 to 60 seconds, then in very
   cold water, and drain it on towels.

The dips, as CSU gives them. In each case, cut the peeled fruit straight into
the solution, soak it for 10 minutes, lift it out with a slotted spoon, drain
it well, and put it on the drying trays.

| Dip | How much, in cold water | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Ascorbic acid (pure crystals) | 2 1/2 tablespoons (34 g) per quart (1 litre); for a small batch, 3 3/4 teaspoons (17 g) per 2 cups | Six crushed 500 mg vitamin C tablets make 1 teaspoon. One quart of solution treats about 10 quarts of cut fruit. Commercial anti-browning mixes work less well. |
| Citric acid | 1 teaspoon (5 g) per quart (1 litre) | Usually sold near the canning supplies. |
| Lemon juice | Equal parts lemon juice and cold water | Penn State specifies bottled lemon juice. |
| Sodium metabisulfite | 1 tablespoon (21 g) per quart (1 litre) | Food grade (USP) or reagent grade only, never practical grade. Not for anyone sensitive to sulfites. |

Penn State's version of the acid dip, for comparison, is 3 3/4 teaspoons of
powdered ascorbic acid (or 20 crushed 500 mg tablets), or 1/2 teaspoon of
citric acid, in 2 cups of water, for the same 10 minutes.

**About sulfites.** CSU explains that sulfite compounds have been used for
centuries to keep dried fruit's colour and reduce spoilage, but that they can
set off asthma attacks in some people, especially people with asthma, and it
advises against pretreating with sodium metabisulfite if anyone who will eat
the fruit is sensitive to sulfites. It also says never to pretreat by burning
sulfur. Penn State leaves sulfiting out of its guide altogether, naming people
on restricted-sodium diets and people with asthma or other breathing
conditions, and says that sulfited food should be dried outdoors for safety.
The acid dips do the job for most home drying.

## Getting vegetables ready: blanch first

CSU recommends blanching vegetables, a short dip in boiling water, before
drying them, for four reasons: it slows or stops the enzymes that would
otherwise change their flavour and texture in storage; it relaxes the tissue
so pieces dry faster; it protects vitamins and colour; and it shortens the
soaking time before cooking. It also cites research showing that blanching,
in plain water or water with citric acid, increases how many harmful
bacteria (the same E. coli O157:H7, Salmonella and Listeria) die during
drying.

CSU's method:

1. Fill a large kettle half full of water and bring it to a boil. For an
   anti-browning boost, stir in 1/4 teaspoon (1 g) of citric acid per quart.
   CSU prefers boiling water over steam or a microwave, because the heat
   reaches the pieces more evenly.
2. Put no more than a quart of vegetable pieces in a cheesecloth or other
   mesh bag, so the water keeps boiling.
3. Drop the bag in, make sure the water covers it, and shake it so the heat
   reaches every piece. Start timing at once, and keep the water boiling.
4. Blanch for the time in the table: 4 minutes for most vegetables in CSU's
   list, 7 for potatoes, 4 to 6 for corn. Beets are cooked as usual instead,
   and green chili peppers, horseradish, mushrooms and tomatoes are not
   blanched (CSU dips the mushrooms and tomatoes in citric acid instead).
5. Cool the bag in very cold water for as long as it was blanched, and drain.

Read CSU's footnote before you use its times. They are written for 3,000 to
5,000 feet of elevation, so they run slightly shorter lower down and slightly
longer higher up or for large amounts. [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew)
makes the same point about Colorado's freezing tables.

## Temperatures and times

| Food | Temperature | Typical drying time | Dry when | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Fruit | 140 F (60 C). Penn State starts at 145 F (63 C) for the first hour while the surface is wet, then 135 to 140 F (57 to 60 C) | About 6 hours for thin apple slices to 36 hours for peach halves | Leathery and pliable, no moist spot in the middle when cut | CSU, Penn State |
| Vegetables | 140 F (60 C). In an oven, 140 to 150 F (60 to 66 C) | Carrots 6 to 10 hours, green beans 8 to 14, peas 8 to 10, onions 6 to 10, tomatoes 6 to 24 | Brittle or crisp | CSU, NCHFP |
| Herbs | 95 to 115 F (35 to 46 C); up to 125 F (52 C) where the air is humid | 1 to 4 hours in a dehydrator | Leaves crumble, stems snap when bent | NCHFP |
| Jerky (after the heating step below) | 140 F (60 C) in a dehydrator or oven; FSIS asks for at least 130 to 140 F (54 to 60 C) held throughout | 10 to 24 hours for strips not heated in marinade; less for heated strips | A test piece cracks but does not break when bent | NCHFP, FSIS |

A few rules of thumb from the same sources apply to all of it:

- Thinner slices and smaller pieces dry faster (CSU). Lay pieces in a single
  layer, not touching or overlapping (CSU, Penn State).
- Turn large pieces and stir small ones every few hours if you can (CSU
  suggests every 3 to 4 hours for fruit), and rotate trays, because in many
  dehydrators the trays nearest the heat dry first.
- Fruit scorches easily near the end. CSU's advice is to switch off the heat
  when drying is nearly done and leave the door wide open for about another
  hour before taking the pieces out.
- Test pieces only after they have cooled to room temperature. Warm fruit
  always feels softer and moister than it really is (CSU).

## Jerky: the rule for meat

This is the part of home drying where a mistake makes people ill, so it gets
the most space.

### Why drying alone is not enough

FSIS starts its jerky guidance with the problem: when raw meat or poultry is
dried at home for jerky that will sit on a shelf, disease-causing bacteria
are likely to survive the dry heat of a warm oven, and especially the 130 to
140 F of a food dehydrator. Its explanation is worth reading in its own
words, because it is the whole reason for the rule:

> "Within a dehydrator or low-temperature oven, evaporating moisture absorbs
> most of the heat. Thus, the meat itself does not begin to rise in
> temperature until most of the moisture has evaporated. Therefore, when the
> dried meat temperature finally begins to rise, the bacteria have become
> more heat resistant and are more likely to survive."

In plain words: while the meat is wet, the evaporating water keeps it cool,
the way sweat cools skin. By the time the meat is dry enough to heat up, the
bacteria on it have dried out too, and dry bacteria are far harder to kill.
So the drying never gets hot enough, early enough, to do the killing. The
heating step does the killing while the meat is still wet, and the drying
afterwards does the preserving.

FSIS summarises the research behind this, from food scientists at the
University of Georgia and Colorado State University, published in the Journal
of Food Protection in 2004:

- Marinating does not make raw meat safe. The study concluded:
  "Marination alone did not result in significant reduction of the pathogen
  compared with whole beef slices that were not marinated."
- Some samples lost all their bacteria in drying and others did not,
  especially jerky made from ground beef.
- In venison deliberately contaminated in the laboratory, disease-causing
  E. coli could survive drying times of up to 10 hours and temperatures of up
  to 145 F (63 C).

An earlier University of Georgia study on ground beef jerky, which FSIS also
reports, found that a curing mix containing salt and sodium nitrite killed
more bacteria than none, and that cured meat heated before drying did best of
all. Its conclusion: "For ground beef jerky prepared at home, safety concerns
related to E. coli O157:H7 are minimized if the meat is precooked to 160°F
prior to drying."

You will still find jerky recipes that dry raw strips with no heating step.
The research above is the reason not to follow them.

### The rule, and the two ways to meet it

FSIS's own instruction to home jerky makers reads: "Steam or roast meat to
160°F and poultry to 165°F, as measured with a food thermometer, before
dehydrating it." And for the drying: "Dry meats in a food dehydrator that
has an adjustable temperature dial and will maintain a temperature of at
least 130°F to 140°F throughout the drying process."

NCHFP's jerky page, revised by two of the University of Georgia authors of
that 2004 study, gives two ways to bring the meat to 160 F (71 C) without
case hardening it (a hard, dry surface sealing moisture into the middle):

- **Heat before drying.** At the end of marinating, bring the strips and
  their marinade to a boil and boil them for 5 minutes, then drain and dry
  them. Strips thicker than 1/4 inch may need longer. If you can, check
  several strips with a metal stem thermometer to confirm they reached
  160 F. These strips dry faster, and look and chew differently from
  traditional jerky.
- **Heat after drying.** Dry the raw marinated strips first, then lay them on
  a baking sheet, close together but not touching, and heat them for 10
  minutes in an oven preheated to 275 F (135 C). That time is for strips cut
  1/4 inch thick or thinner; thicker strips may need longer to reach 160 F.

### Making it, step by step

From NCHFP's tested method, with FSIS's handling rules:

1. **If the meat is farmed pork, freeze it first. If it is wild game,
   freezing is not enough.** Pork and wild game can carry *Trichinella*,
   the worm that causes trichinellosis (trichinosis), and drying does not
   deal with it. The CDC: "Curing (salting), drying, smoking, or
   microwaving meat alone does not consistently kill the worms." For farmed
   pork, NCHFP's treatment is to freeze a piece 6 inches (15 cm) thick or
   less at 0 F (-18 C) or below for at least 30 days before slicing and
   marinating; it warns that freezing does not get rid of bacteria. Wild
   game, wild boar and feral pigs included, is different. The CDC:
   "Freezing wild game meats may not effectively kill all worms because
   some worms that infect wild game are freeze-resistant." It names bear,
   wild boar, wildcat, fox, wolf, seal and walrus among the meats at risk;
   its outbreak reports say wild game has to reach 165 F (74 C) inside,
   checked with a meat thermometer, and one of them adds that strict
   meat-eaters such as cougar and wolverine carry the worm even more often
   than black bears. That is hotter than the 160 F the jerky methods below
   are built to reach. In 2024 a North Carolina outbreak came from bear
   jerky made by marinating and drying alone, with no heating step, and
   jerky made from bear and from cougar had caused outbreaks before (CDC,
   in its journal *Emerging Infectious Diseases*, 2026). This guide is
   stricter than the CDC here: its reports do not say never to make jerky
   from these meats, only that the meat has to pass 165 F. But bear, wild
   boar or feral pig, and any meat-eating wild animal, are the meats most
   likely to carry the worm, sometimes in great numbers (the bear meat in
   the 2022 outbreak held more than 800 live larvae in each gram, CDC), and
   a thin strip is hard to check with a thermometer. So do not make jerky
   from them; cook them to 165 F instead. For other wild game, venison
   included, bring the strips to 165 F, not 160 F, by heating them before
   drying, the way you can check (step 5; this guide's reading).
2. **Keep the raw meat cold and clean.** FSIS: keep meat and poultry at 40 F
   (4 C) or slightly below; use or freeze ground beef and poultry within 2
   days and whole red meats within 3 to 5 days; thaw frozen meat in the
   refrigerator, not on the counter; wash your hands with soap and water
   before and after handling it, and use clean equipment.
3. **Slice thin.** Partly freezing the meat makes it easier to cut. Slice it
   no thicker than 1/4 inch (6 mm): NCHFP notes that the safety of its
   methods depends on the thickness. Trim off all fat. Slicing with the grain
   gives chewy jerky; across the grain gives more tender, brittle jerky.
4. **Marinate in the refrigerator**, for 1 to 2 hours or overnight. Never
   reuse the marinade.
5. **Heat the strips to 160 F**, or 165 F for wild game (step 1), by one of
   the two ways above. For wild game, use the first way, heating before
   drying (boiling in the marinade, or the steaming or roasting FSIS
   describes), because it is the one you can check: take several strips
   straight from the heat and make sure the thermometer reads 165 F.
   NCHFP's 10 minutes in the oven after drying is its time for 160 F, and
   a dried strip barely takes a thermometer probe (this guide's reading).
6. **Dry.** Drain the strips on clean towels, lay them on dehydrator trays or
   on racks set over baking sheets, close together but not touching, and dry
   at 140 F (60 C). Start checking after 3 hours. The jerky is done when a
   test piece cracks but does not break when you bend it.
7. **Finish and pack.** Pat off any beads of oil with clean paper towels, let
   the strips cool, and pack them in glass jars or heavy food storage bags.
   Vacuum packing works well too.

**Ground meat jerky** needs more care, not less. NCHFP explains that
disease-causing organisms are harder to get rid of in ground meat than in whole
strips, and that an internal temperature of 160 F is needed to kill bacteria
such as E. coli O157:H7 if they are present (for wild game, 165 F; step 1).
Kansas State University's version is to heat ground meat jerky for 10 minutes
in an oven preheated to 275 F after it has dried, and NCHFP's heats it at the
end of drying by the dehydrator maker's directions. A thermometer check is
hard at that stage, so this guide's reading is to cook ground wild game in a
dish rather than make jerky of it. And whatever you grind, clean the grinder
thoroughly after each use, which the CDC says helps stop trichinellosis and
other foodborne illness spreading.

**Wild game** carries its own risk. FSIS quotes researchers warning that
venison can be heavily contaminated with bacteria from the animal's gut,
depending on the hunter's skill and where the animal was hit, and that deer
carcasses are usually left at air temperature where beef would be chilled
quickly. NCHFP's advice: if the gut contents touched the meat or the hunter's
hands during dressing, do not make jerky from that meat, and use it only in
dishes where it will be cooked thoroughly; and chill deer carcasses quickly.
And for *Trichinella*, go back to step 1: freezing is not enough, and wild
game has to reach 165 F (CDC). Keep raw wild game and its juices away from
other food as well: in the 2022 bear meat outbreak, two of the six people who
fell ill had eaten only the vegetables cooked with the meat (CDC).

**Never sun-dry meat.** FSIS: "Sun drying is not recommended for making meat
jerky due to a lack of a steady heat source and the potential for
contamination from animals, insects, dust and bacteria."

**How long jerky keeps.** FSIS: "Commercially packaged jerky can be kept 12
months; home-dried jerky can be stored 1 to 2 months." NCHFP is more cautious
about the shelf: properly dried jerky keeps two weeks at room temperature in a
sealed container, and should go in the refrigerator or freezer to keep longer.
Kansas State likewise says home-made jerky is best used within 1 to 2 months,
refrigerated or frozen for longer storage. Our reading of the three together:
two weeks in the cupboard, and the refrigerator for anything you mean to keep
beyond that.

## Four ways to dry, and when each works

### A food dehydrator

A dehydrator is a small electric box with a heating element, a fan and vents,
designed to dry food quickly at 140 F (NCHFP). It is the most controllable
choice and the one CSU recommends for home drying. NCHFP's list of what to
look for includes a thermostat that covers 85 to 160 F (29 to 71 C), an
enclosed heating element, a fan, four to ten open mesh trays that are easy to
wash, and a timer, because drying often finishes in the night and a timer
stops the food scorching. It advises against wooden dehydrators, which are a
fire hazard and hard to clean.

NCHFP describes two designs. With the fan and element at the **side**
(horizontal air flow), every tray gets the same heat, flavours mix less, so
you can dry different foods at once, and juices cannot drip onto the element.
With them in the **base** (vertical air flow), flavours can mix and liquids
can drip into the heater. The main drawback of any dehydrator is its size:
NCHFP puts it at about half a bushel of produce for every 12 square feet of
tray space. CSU adds that a dehydrator should run indoors, in a dry,
well-ventilated room.

### An oven

An oven works only if it can hold a genuinely low temperature. Penn State's
test is whether you can set it to 140 to 150 F (60 to 66 C), and CSU warns
that ordinary ovens may not hold a steady temperature at settings that low.
The method, from Penn State and CSU:

- Prop the door open 2 to 3 inches (5 to 7.5 cm) so the moist air can escape
  (Penn State). A convection oven, which has a fan, works well.
- Put a calibrated oven thermometer on the rack and check it every two hours,
  because ovens often drift at low settings (CSU).
- Leave room for air: trays about 1 1/2 inches smaller than the oven each
  way, at least 3 inches clear at the top and bottom of the oven, and 2 1/2
  inches between trays. Swap the trays top to bottom and front to back every
  half hour (CSU).
- Watch closely near the end, because oven drying scorches easily (CSU).

It is slow and costly: USU finds oven drying typically takes two to three
times as long as a dehydrator and uses more energy.

### The sun

Sun drying suits **fruit**, and dry beans finished on the vine. It depends
entirely on the weather where you live. NCHFP's conditions:

- Hot, dry, breezy days, at 85 F (29 C) at the very least, and hotter is
  better.
- Humidity below 60 percent. NCHFP names humidity as the problem in the
  American South, and Penn State says Pennsylvania's humidity makes sun
  drying impractical there. California's raisin country, with warm days, low
  humidity and a steady breeze, is the example of where it works.
- Several days of it. Cover the fruit or bring it under shelter every night,
  because cool night air condenses on it and puts moisture back.

Set racks or screens up on blocks so air moves underneath, ideally over a
concrete driveway or a sheet of aluminium or tin, which reflects extra heat
onto the food. NCHFP is specific about screens: stainless steel,
Teflon-coated fibreglass or plastic are safe; "hardware cloth" (galvanised
mesh coated with cadmium or zinc) can leave harmful residues on the food;
copper destroys vitamin C; and aluminium discolours and corrodes. Birds and
insects love drying fruit, so use two screens, one as the shelf and one as a
cover, or drape cheesecloth over the top.

Food dried outdoors needs one more step before it is stored: treating it to
kill insects and their eggs (see "Pasteurising against insects" below).

### Air drying indoors

Plain room air works for **herbs**, and for little else. Penn State notes that
room drying needs enough heat, low enough humidity and enough moving air,
and that an air-conditioned house may be too cool to dry food fast enough.

NCHFP's herb methods:

- **Pick at the right moment.** Harvest just before the flowers open, in the
  early morning once the dew has dried. Rinse in cool water, shake off the
  excess, and discard bruised or damaged leaves.
- **Sturdy herbs** (rosemary, sage, thyme, summer savory, parsley): tie in
  small bundles and hang them to dry. Outdoors can work, but colour and
  flavour usually keep better indoors.
- **Tender herbs** (basil, oregano, tarragon, lemon balm, the mints) hold so
  much water that they mould if they do not dry quickly. Hang a small bunch
  inside a paper bag with holes torn in its sides, closed at the top, where
  air moves around it. Large bunches mould.
- **Single leaves**, useful for mint, sage or bay and in humid places: lay
  leaves on a paper towel without touching, cover with another towel and
  another layer, up to five layers, and leave them overnight in an oven warmed
  only by its light bulb or a gas pilot light.
- **Not in the sun.** NCHFP does not recommend sun drying herbs, because they
  lose flavour and colour.

Dried herbs are three to four times stronger than fresh, so use a quarter to
a third as much in a recipe (NCHFP).

### Dry beans on the vine

For dry beans such as navy, kidney, great northern, lima, lentils and soy,
NCHFP's method is to leave the pods on the plant until the beans rattle
inside and the vines and pods are dry and shrivelled, then pick and shell
them. They need no pretreatment. Beans that are still moist are not done and
will mould; finish them in the sun, an oven or a dehydrator. Like sun-dried
fruit, they then need treating against insects.

### Pasteurising against insects

Food dried outdoors, and unpeeled or uncovered fruit, can carry insect eggs
(NCHFP, Penn State). Two treatments, from both sources:

- **Freezer:** seal the food in freezer bags and hold it at 0 F (-18 C) or
  below for at least 48 hours.
- **Oven:** spread it in a single layer and heat it at 160 F (71 C) for 30
  minutes.

## How to tell it is dry enough

Always test pieces that have cooled; warm food feels softer and wetter than
it is (CSU, USU).

- **Fruit** should be leathery and pliable, with no moist spot in the middle
  when you cut a piece. CSU's squeeze test: take a handful and squeeze it; if
  your hand stays dry and the pieces spring apart when you let go, they are
  dry. Penn State's version is that no beads of moisture form when a piece is
  pressed between your fingers. Very sugary fruit, such as figs and cherries,
  stays a little sticky (USU).
- **Vegetables** should be brittle or crisp; NCHFP notes that some will
  shatter if hit with a hammer. If you are unsure, USU's advice is to lower
  the heat and give them longer, since there is little harm in extra time.
- **Herbs** are ready when the leaves crumble easily between your fingers and
  the stems break when bent (NCHFP).
- **Jerky** is ready when a cooled test piece cracks but does not snap when you
  bend it (NCHFP).

**Checking by weight.** Feel is a judgement. USU gives a way to check by
arithmetic instead, based on how much of the fresh food is solid matter
rather than water:

1. Weigh the fresh, prepared food (subtract the weight of the tray or bowl).
2. Multiply by the food's percentage of solids from the table below, and
   divide by the target: 80 percent solids for fruit (never less), 90 percent
   for vegetables.
3. That is the weight the dried food should come down to.

USU's example: 40 ounces of fresh cherries at 14 percent solids, dried to 80
percent solids, should weigh 40 x 14 / 80 = 7 ounces. In metric, a kilogram of
apple slices at 16 percent solids should dry down to about 200 grams.

| Fruit | Solids | Vegetable | Solids |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apples | 16% | Beans | 10% |
| Apricots | 14% | Cabbage | 8% |
| Bananas | 26% | Carrots | 12% |
| Cherries, sour | 14% | Corn | 24% |
| Cherries, sweet | 20% | Onion | 9% |
| Grapes | 19% | Peppers, bell | 7% |
| Peaches | 12% | Potato | 21% |
| Pears | 16% | Squash | 6% |
| Strawberries | 9% | Tomatoes | 6% |

(Selected from USU's table, which it takes from the USDA's food composition
handbooks.)

## Conditioning: evening out the last moisture

However carefully you dry, some pieces come out moister than others because
of their size or where they sat. Conditioning spreads that last moisture
evenly so a few damp pieces do not mould the whole jar.

NCHFP's method for fruit: pack the cooled, dried fruit loosely into plastic or
glass jars, seal them, and let them stand for 7 to 10 days, shaking them every
day to separate the pieces and watching for condensation. CSU gives 4 to 10
days, with the containers about two-thirds full, lightly covered, in a warm,
dry, airy place. Both give the same rule: **if moisture beads form inside
the jar, the fruit goes back in the dryer**, and then through conditioning
again.

Vegetables dried to brittle usually do not need conditioning, because they
are so much drier (NCHFP, CSU).

## Packing and storing

From NCHFP unless noted:

- **Cool it completely first.** Warm food sweats in its container, and that
  moisture is enough for mould.
- **Pack it straight away**, into clean, dry, insect-proof containers, as
  tightly as you can without crushing: canning jars, freezer containers with
  tight lids, or freezer bags. Vacuum packing is a good option.
- **Pack in amounts you will use at once.** Every opening lets in air and
  damp.
- **Use glass where you can**, because you can see any moisture that
  collects inside. CSU prefers dark glass.
- **Keep sulfured fruit off metal.** Put it in a plastic bag first; the
  sulfur reacts with metal and changes the fruit's colour.
- **Label it** with what it is, the date, and how it was pretreated and dried
  (CSU).
- **Store it cool, dry and dark, and check it often.**

### How long dried food keeps

| Food | How long | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Dried food in general | 4 months to 1 year, depending on the food and the storage temperature | NCHFP |
| Most dried fruit | About 1 year at 60 F (16 C); about 6 months at 80 F (27 C) | NCHFP |
| Dried fruit, properly stored | 6 to 12 months | CSU |
| Dried vegetables | About half as long as fruit | NCHFP |
| Dried vegetables, properly stored | 6 to 12 months | CSU |
| Home-dried jerky | 1 to 2 months | FSIS |
| Home-dried jerky at room temperature, sealed | Two weeks; refrigerate or freeze it to keep longer | NCHFP |
| Commercially packaged jerky | 12 months | FSIS |

The two sources differ on vegetables, and the cautious reading is the
shorter one. NCHFP sets these times by quality, because heat lowers it. The rule behind every number in that table is NCHFP's: the warmer the
store, the shorter the time. Dried fruit lasts longer still in the
refrigerator or freezer (Penn State), and CSU lists both as storage places.
For the most nutrition, CSU suggests eating dried food within a year.

## When dried food goes wrong

- **Condensation in the jar while conditioning:** not dry enough. Back into
  the dryer (NCHFP, CSU).
- **Food that has picked up moisture in storage but shows no spoilage:** use
  it straight away, or dry it again and repack it (NCHFP).
- **Mould, or an off smell:** throw it out (NCHFP, CSU). Do not cut the mould
  away and keep the rest: [Telling Spoiled From Safe](/library#telling-spoiled-from-safe)
  explains why cutting mould off is a rule for only a few firm foods.
- **Hard outside, damp inside:** case hardening, from too much heat (Penn
  State). Cut a piece open to check before you store a batch.
- **Scorched edges:** too hot near the end. Turn the heat off for the last
  hour next time (CSU).
- **Brown fruit:** no acid dip, or too short a soak.
- **Insects:** pasteurise outdoor-dried food, and store everything in
  insect-proof containers.
- **Dried herbs or garlic put into oil.** This can turn a safe dried food
  into a botulism risk, because the oil seals low-acid food away from air. The CDC's rule for home-made flavoured oils: "Refrigerate homemade
  oils made with garlic or herbs. Throw the oils away after 4 days." See
  [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) for the reasoning.

## How the game models it

The simulation has dried food, but not yet the drying.

- **Dried fruit** is made by the "Dry Fruit" recipe, which turns three apples
  and two bananas into four portions of dried fruit in an oven, with no skill
  required but a utility knife in the backpack (`data/recipes.csv`,
  `cook_dried_fruit`; `data/crafting/tools.ron`).
- **Jerky and dried meat** are made at the home's stove by "Make Jerky" (two
  raw mutton, one salt and one spice mix into four jerky, with a utility
  knife in the backpack) and "Dry Meat" (two raw mutton and one salt into
  four dried meat, no tool), both level-1 cooking recipes, which anyone can
  make (`data/recipes.csv`, `cook_jerky` and `cook_dried_meat`;
  `data/crafting/tools.ron`). Make Jerky's description names the FSIS step,
  "heat to 160 F then dry", but the craft has no heating step. The food
  vendor also sells dried meat (`data/npcs.ron`), and it is listed as a
  trade good (`data/trade_goods.ron`).
- **How long they keep** comes from the USDA's FoodKeeper figures, taken at
  the low end: at room temperature, jerky and dried meat keep 30 days
  (FoodKeeper gives home-made jerky 1 to 2 months), and dried fruit 30 days
  (FoodKeeper's figure for an opened package) (`data/food_system.ron`, the
  `jerky` and `dried_fruit` rows). Spoiled food makes the player ill when
  eaten; fresh jerky and dried fruit never do.
- **Drying as a method** is written down in `data/food_system.ron` (a
  preservation method that multiplies shelf life twenty times and needs the
  cooking skill at level 2, and a "dehydrated" cooking method), but the file
  itself notes that no code acts on those rows yet.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: there is no
dehydrator, drying rack or sun drying, and no temperature, humidity or time to
get right; nothing can be under-dried, case-hardened or mouldy; there is no
acid dip or blanching; the jerky and Dry Meat recipes start from raw mutton
and their descriptions name the heating step, but nothing in the craft heats
the meat or checks a temperature, so the rule this guide is built around
exists in the game only as a line of text; every store aboard but the home's
Freezer, which keeps food twenty times as long, is at room temperature, and
dryness and darkness count for nothing, so the cool, dry, dark store this
guide asks for makes no difference; and dried fruit spoils on the
opened-package clock, where properly stored dried fruit really keeps six
months to a year.
