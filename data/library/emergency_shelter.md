# Emergency Shelter

Most nights that go wrong start ordinarily. The power goes out in a
December storm and the house cools hour by hour. The car slides into a
ditch on a snowy hill road. A day walk runs into the dark. A flood
warning says to leave. Each time the question is the same: where will
you and the people with you spend tonight, and how will you all be warm,
dry and alive in the morning?

This guide answers it in the order you will meet it: what kills people
on the first night; whether to stay where you are or go; how to make a
cold house, a stuck car or a public shelter work; and how to make a
shelter outdoors from what you have, starting with the ground under you.
The worked example is Kitsap County, Washington, where the simulation is
set.

It leans on other guides and does not repeat them. [Cold and
Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) covers what cold does to a body,
how to spot hypothermia and what to do for it. [What to Do When You Are
Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost) covers being lost outdoors,
calling for help and where to wait. [The Hazards Where You
Live](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) covers which disasters a place
actually has and what to keep in the house for them. [Heating a Home
Safely](/library#heating-a-home-safely), [Choosing and Running a
Generator](/library#choosing-and-running-a-generator),
[Firewood](/library#firewood) and [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould) cover the heat sources and the
carbon monoxide that comes with them. [Heat and How It
Moves](/library#heat-and-how-it-moves) and [Insulation and Heat
Loss](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss) explain the physics.

Its sources are the National Weather Service (NWS) and NOAA's storm
records, the Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and its Ready.gov
site, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the National
Institutes of Health's Safe to Sleep campaign, the Consumer Product
Safety Commission (CPSC), the US Fire Administration (USFA), the National
Park Service and the Army's survival manual, all US federal and in the
public domain; and, restated in our own words, Kitsap County's emergency
management pages and the project's dated findings on Washington's fire
code. Where something is general practice or our own reading, the text
says so.

## Tonight, right now

1. **Get out of the wind and the wet.** Wet clothes and wind are what
   turn a cool night into a dangerous one (below).
2. **Decide whether to stay or go, and decide early.** Usually stay: in
   the house, in the car, by the trail. Go when staying is the danger, or
   when you are told to (below).
3. **Put on every layer you have, loose, and get something between you
   and the ground.**
4. **Make the space small.** One room, one car, one small shelter. A
   baby still sleeps on its own firm, flat surface, not in the shared
   bed (below).
5. **Heat only with things made to be used indoors, and never anything
   that burns fuel without a flue, in a closed space.** Carbon monoxide
   kills sleeping people without waking them (below).
6. **Light with flashlights, not candles.**
7. **Tell someone where you are, and check on the people near you.**

The rest of this guide is the reasoning behind those seven, and the
detail.

## What kills people on the first night

Ready.gov lists the dangers a winter storm brings: "Winter storms create
a higher risk of car accidents, hypothermia, frostbite, carbon monoxide
poisoning, and heart attacks from overexertion." Here is each one, and
the rest that this guide is written against.

**Cold, wet and wind together.** The National Weather Service:
"Hypothermia occurs when the body loses heat faster than it can produce
it." The CDC says it can happen above 40 F if a person is wet from rain,
sweat or cold water and becomes chilled, so a wet night just above
freezing is enough. [Cold and Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) has
the signs and what to do; this guide is about not getting there.

**Carbon monoxide, from whatever was used for heat or power.** NOAA's
storm record for the windstorm of December 2006 in western Washington
records eight indirect deaths from carbon monoxide in the zone covering
Seattle and Bremerton, from barbecues or generators used improperly or
indoors, and 275 people treated for it in hospitals. The CDC's list of
what never to use indoors: charcoal, a portable gas camp stove, portable
flameless chemical heaters, and a gas oven to heat the house. The same
page warns: "People who are sleeping, drunk, or under the influence of
other substances can die from CO poisoning before they have symptoms."

**Fire, from candles and heaters.** In the same 2006 storm, a man in
Spanaway died in a house fire that NOAA's record says was apparently
started by a candle used for light. The US Fire Administration: "Home
portable heater fires were only 3% of all home heating fires annually for
2017-2019, but they accounted for 41% of fatal heating fires in homes."

**Driving.** The National Weather Service: "Each year, on average, more
than 5,000 people are killed and more than 418,000 are injured due to
weather-related vehicle crashes." And flood water: the Weather Service
says it is never safe to drive or walk into flood waters, and that
according to the CDC over half of flood-related drownings happen when a
vehicle is driven into hazardous flood water.

**Falling trees and fallen lines.** The 2006 record also has a man in
McCleary killed when a tree fell onto the trailer he was sleeping in, and
a man in Gig Harbor electrocuted while walking near a downed power line.
Keep away from any line that is down and anything touching it; [Where Your
Own Electrical Work Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops) explains
why (our summary).

**Overexertion.** Shovelling heavy snow, pushing a car and walking in deep
snow strain a cold heart. The Weather Service says the strain from the
cold and the hard labour may cause a heart attack, and that sweating
could lead to a chill and hypothermia.

Read that list again and notice how many of the deaths come not from the
cold itself but from what people do about the cold, the dark and the
journey (our reading).

## Stay or go

**Usually, stay where you are.** Ready.gov's winter advice: "Stay off
roads if at all possible. If trapped in your car, then stay inside." Its
winter page says, in capitals, that if you are under a winter storm
warning you should find shelter right away. A warm house you can keep
warm, or a car on a road where people will find it, is usually safer
than a journey through the storm (our reading). The lost person's rule is
the same, for the same reasons: [What to Do When You Are
Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost).

**Go when staying is the danger:**

- **When you are told to.** Ready.gov: "Always follow the instructions of
  local officials". The Weather Service, on floods: "If told to evacuate,
  do so immediately." Floods, wildfires and tsunamis are hazards you
  leave, not ones you shelter in place from; [The Hazards Where You
  Live](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) has which your place has (our
  summary).
- **When water is rising.** The Weather Service: "If you live in a flood
  prone area or are camping in a low lying area, get to higher ground
  immediately." And: "If you are trapped by moving water, move to the
  highest possible point and call 911 if possible."
- **When you smell or hear gas.** The CPSC: "If you smell or hear gas
  leaking, leave your home immediately and contact local gas authorities
  from outside the home." It adds that you should not operate any
  electronics, such as lights or phones, before you leave.
- **When a carbon monoxide alarm sounds,** or several people feel ill at
  once: outside, then 911 (the CPSC; see [Choosing and Running a
  Generator](/library#choosing-and-running-a-generator)).
- **When someone depends on a powered medical device or refrigerated
  medicine,** and the outage will outlast its battery or its cooler: go
  early, to somewhere with power. Ready.gov: "Have alternate plans for
  refrigerating medicines or using power-dependent medical devices." It
  also says that if the power is out for more than a day, medicine that
  should be refrigerated is thrown away unless its label says otherwise,
  and to ask a doctor or pharmacist at once for a new supply.
- **When the house cannot be kept warm enough** for the people in it,
  above all for babies, the old and the sick (below). FEMA's power outage
  sheet: "Consider spending the coldest parts of the day in a location
  with heat."

**If you go, go early and leave word.** Ready.gov: "Leave early enough to
avoid being trapped by severe weather." And: "Leave a note telling others
when you left and where you are going." The Weather Service, for winter
driving: let someone know where you are going and what route you will
take, so that "If something happens, this person will know where to start
a search."

## Where to go

**Family, friends, a motel.** Ready.gov's evacuation advice is to
identify several places you could go "such as a friend's home in another
town or a motel", in different directions, so that you have a choice on
the day.

**A warming centre, a cooling centre, or a community building with
power.** Ready.gov's power outage page: "Check with local officials about
heating and cooling locations open near you." And: "Go to a community
location with power if heat or cold is extreme." Some open only for part
of the day, so ask when yours closes and where to go after it (general
practice).

**A public shelter.** Ready.gov: "Mass care shelters provide life
sustaining services to disaster survivors." It is honest about what they
are like: "Mass care sheltering can involve living with many people in a
confined space, which can be difficult and unpleasant." Even though they
often have water, food, medicine and toilets, it says to bring your own
emergency supply kit, and soap, hand sanitiser and cleaning wipes too.

**Finding one.** Ready.gov: "Search for open shelters by texting SHELTER
and your ZIP code to 43362." It also points to the FEMA app for a list of
open shelters during a disaster. Listen too: Ready.gov says to sign up
for your community's warning system and that NOAA Weather Radio also
gives emergency alerts, and the Weather Service says to keep listening to
radio and television, NOAA Weather Radio included, during a flood.

In Kitsap County the community warning system is KitsapALERT, run by the
county's Department of Emergency Management: anyone who lives or works
in the county can sign up for its messages by text, phone call or email,
and it is used only when there is an immediate threat to life and
property. The same department's website is where it announces its severe
weather shelters, which give overnight beds during severe weather
between 1 November and 31 March, to single adults, families with
children and teenagers (both pages read 5 October 2026).

**Pets.** Ready.gov: "All shelters accept service animals, but many
public shelters and hotels do not allow pets inside." Its evacuation page
says most public shelters allow only service animals, so find a place
that takes your animals before you need one.

## A cold house

When the heat goes out and you are staying, the job is to keep a small
space warm with the body heat and the safe heat you have.

**Shrink the house.** The National Weather Service's list for when your
heat goes out:

- "Close off unneeded rooms to avoid wasting heat."
- "Stuff towels or rags in cracks under doors."
- "Close blinds or curtains to keep in some heat."
- "Eat and drink. Food provides the body with energy for producing its
  own heat."
- "Wear layers of loose-fitting, lightweight, warm clothing. Remove layers
  to avoid overheating, perspiration and subsequent chill."

Choose one room that everyone shares, small, on the side of the house out
of the wind, with few outside walls and windows (general practice; why
walls and windows lose heat is in [Insulation and Heat
Loss](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss)). The adults and older children sleep
together in it, on mattresses or pads rather than on a cold floor, with
hats on (general practice).

**A baby sleeps on its own surface, even on a cold night.** A baby does
not share the mattress, the sleeping bags or the hats. The safe-sleep
advice of the CDC and of the National Institutes of Health's Safe to
Sleep campaign is that a baby sleeps on its back, on its own firm, flat
surface, such as a crib, a bassinet or a portable play yard (a travel
cot), in the same room as the adults but not in their bed, with nothing
soft in it. The CDC: "Keep soft bedding out of your baby's sleep area.
This includes blankets, pillows, bumper pads, and soft toys." And: "Do
not cover your baby's head or allow your baby to get too hot." Safe to
Sleep says a baby's hat comes off indoors, and puts the warmth on the
baby instead: "Dress baby in a wearable blanket or an extra layer of
clothing to keep them warm without adding items to the sleep area." The
CDC counts babies sleeping in cold rooms among the people at high risk of
hypothermia, so a room too cold for a baby dressed that way is a room
too cold to stay in (our reading; the reasons to go are above).

**Heat it only with things made for it.**

- **Never a gas oven or stove.** Ready.gov: "Do not use a gas stove or
  oven to heat your home." It adds that this increases the risk of carbon
  monoxide poisoning, fires and burns.
- **Never charcoal, a camp stove or a flameless chemical heater indoors**
  (the CDC, above), and never a generator anywhere near the house (see
  [Choosing and Running a
  Generator](/library#choosing-and-running-a-generator)).
- **A fireplace or wood stove only if it was built for the job,** with its
  flue swept and working; [Firewood](/library#firewood) covers it. The Weather
  Service: "When using heat from a fire place, wood stove, space heater,
  etc., use fire safeguards and properly ventilate."
- **Portable heaters, by the rules.** The CPSC: "Keep all sides of the
  portable heater at least 3 feet from beds, clothes, curtains, papers,
  sofas and other items that can catch fire." On a stable, level surface,
  plugged straight into a wall outlet, never a power strip, with children
  and pets kept back. The US Fire Administration: "Turn space heaters off
  when leaving the room or going to bed."
- **Kerosene and propane space heaters** have no flue, so whatever they
  burn stays in the room (our reading). In Washington the fire code lets
  a household run a portable heater of that kind indoors only if it is
  listed to UL 647, the standard for unvented kerosene heaters, only in a
  house or a duplex, and never in a bedroom, a bathroom, a toilet room or
  a storage closet; as we read it, a portable propane heater is not
  covered at all ([the project's dated findings on the
  rule](https://github.com/Shaostoul/Humanity/blob/main/docs/reference/findings/2026-10-04-portable-unvented-heaters-washington.md),
  researched 4 October 2026). The one room everyone is sleeping in, shut
  up against the cold, is no place for one either (our reading), and the
  CPSC says: "NEVER leave a portable heater running unattended in a
  confined space". How to run a kerosene heater where one is allowed is
  in [Heating a Home Safely](/library#heating-a-home-safely).
- **Carbon monoxide alarms, working, with batteries.** The CPSC: on each
  level and outside separate sleeping areas, tested monthly; and clear the
  snow from the outside vents of the furnace and other fuel-burning
  appliances so that carbon monoxide cannot build up inside. Placement is
  in [Ventilation, Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould).

**Light with flashlights.** The CPSC: "Use flashlights or battery-operated
candles instead." Ready.gov's outage page says to have a flashlight for
every household member.

**Watch the people, not the thermometer.** Babies, older people and
anyone ill or drinking are at higher risk and may not show it or say it;
[Cold and Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) lists who is at risk and
the signs (our summary of it).
FEMA's winter storm sheet: "Check on neighbors. Older adults and young
children are more at risk in extreme cold."

**A car is not a heater in a garage.** FEMA's outage sheet: "Don't leave a
vehicle running inside a garage, even if the garage door is left open. If
you use your vehicle as a source of power or warmth, make sure to run it
in a well-ventilated place outside." The CDC says the same of a garage
attached to the house.

## A car

### In snow and cold: stay with it

The National Weather Service's instructions if your car gets stuck in a
winter storm:

- "Stay in the vehicle!" Its reason: "If you leave your vehicle, you will
  become disoriented quickly in wind-driven snow and cold."
- "Run the motor about 10 minutes each hour for heat."
- "While running the motor, open the window a little for fresh air to
  avoid carbon monoxide poisoning."
- "Clear snow from the exhaust pipe to avoid gas poisoning."
- Be visible: "Turn on the dome light at night when running the engine."
  "Tie a bright colored cloth, preferably red, to your antenna or door."
  "After snow stops falling, raise the hood to indicate you need help."

The exhaust pipe is the one that kills. Snow drifting over it while the
engine runs fills a closed car with carbon monoxide, which is why the
window and the pipe come together in that list (our reading). Check the
pipe each time before you start the engine, and switch the engine off
before anyone falls asleep (general practice). The CDC adds that a small
leak in a car's exhaust system can let CO build up inside, and says to
have the exhaust checked every year.

Keep moving a little: the Weather Service's advice for anyone caught
outside in a winter storm, which holds in a car too (our reading), is to
move arms, legs, fingers and toes vigorously from time to time to keep
blood circulating and stay warm, without working up a sweat. Use the
blankets, sit on something, huddle together, and keep your phone warm and
mostly off; [What to Do When You Are Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost)
covers calling for help and saving the battery (general practice).

**Carry what a night in the car needs.** Ready.gov's car kit: "jumper
cables, sand, a flashlight, warm clothes, blankets, bottled water and
non-perishable snacks", and "Keep a full tank of gas." The Weather
Service's winter car kit adds, among other things, a mobile phone and
charger, sleeping bags, a first aid kit, high-calorie food, extra
clothing to keep dry, a shovel, a tow rope, booster cables and a water
container.

### In water: never drive in

The National Weather Service: "It is NEVER safe to drive or walk into
flood waters." Ready.gov: "Do not drive into flooded areas." Turn round
before the water, every time. The Weather Service's advice for anyone
trapped by moving water is to move to the highest possible point and call
911 if possible; that is its general flood advice, not a method for
getting out of a car that is already in the water, which this guide does
not cover (our note).

### In heat: never stay in a parked car

A car that keeps you alive in a snowstorm can kill you on a mild sunny
day. The Weather Service: "Hyperthermia can occur even on a mild day with
temperatures in the 70s." It adds that "Leaving the windows slightly open
does not significantly decrease the heating rate", and that adults die
too: its page records a man who died of heatstroke after falling asleep
in his car with the windows rolled up. Ready.gov: "Never leave people or
pets in a closed car on a warm day." If your home has no air
conditioning in a heat wave, it says to find a cooling centre.

## Outdoors, with no building and no car

This is the case [What to Do When You Are
Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost) prepares you for. That guide covers
choosing where to wait, signalling, staying put and lightning; this one
adds how to make the shelter itself.

**Carry one.** The National Park Service's ten essentials include
emergency shelter, and it names the options: "A tent, tarp, bivy sack, or
emergency space blanket are all lightweight options for emergency
shelter." (A bivy sack is a light waterproof bag to sleep in, or to go
over a sleeping bag.) It also says to pack an extra layer for the worst
conditions you could meet.

**When there is no shelter nearby,** the National Weather Service's
winter advice: "Build a lean-to, windbreak or snow cave for protection
from the wind. Build a fire for heat and to attract attention. Place rocks
around the fire to absorb and reflect heat." And a caution from the
Army's survival manual about those rocks: "Do not use wet or porous rocks
as they may explode when heated." Why they do is in [Pressure in Water,
Air and Steam](/library#pressure-in-water-air-and-steam).

### Where to put it

The survival manual's site rules that matter for a shelter (the rest are
in the lost guide):

- out of flash flood areas, and above the high water mark near water;
- clear of dead trees and branches that could fall;
- away from avalanche and rockslide slopes;
- level and big enough to lie down in.

**Back to the wind.** For its simplest shelter, a poncho or tarp lean-to,
the manual says to check the wind direction first and set the lean-to with
its back into the wind (restated). Brush, a pack or other gear piled at
the open sides adds protection from wind and rain, it says.

### Small

The survival manual: "The most common error in making a shelter is to
make it too large. A shelter must be large enough to protect you. It must
also be small enough to contain your body heat, especially in cold
climates." In its cold weather chapter it is blunter: "A fatal error in
cold weather shelter construction is making the shelter so large that it
steals body heat rather than saving it. Keep shelter space small." And:
"Always block a shelter's entrance, if possible, to keep the heat in and
the wind out."

### Off the ground

The ground is the cold people forget: blankets piled on top do nothing
about the heat going out underneath, as [Cold and
Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) puts it for a casualty (our
reading). The survival manual:
"Never sleep directly on the ground. Lay down some pine boughs, grass, or
other insulating material to keep the ground from absorbing your body
heat." For a lean-to it says to put leaves or pine needles inside to
reduce the heat lost to the ground. The National Park Service's advice
for a person who is already hypothermic is the same rule from the other
side: insulate the body from the ground with whatever is available; see
[Cold and Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia).

Why it works: heat leaves a body pressed against cold ground by
conduction, and loose dry material works by trapping still air, which
carries heat badly; [Heat and How It Moves](/library#heat-and-how-it-moves)
explains both. Pile it thick, because it squashes flat under you, and
keep it dry, because wet material stops trapping air (general practice).
A foam pad, a pack, a coil of rope, a car mat or a pile of dry bracken all
beat bare ground (general practice).

### A debris hut

When you have time and no gear, the survival manual says that, for
warmth and ease of construction, the debris hut is one of the best
shelters. In outline: a ridgepole from a sturdy base or a tripod down to
the ground, sticks leaned along both sides, steep enough to shed rain and
wide enough for your body, finer sticks across them, then dry leaves,
grass and needles piled over the lot at least a metre thick, a layer of
about 30 centimetres inside to lie on, more material to drag into the
entrance once you are in, and branches on top so the wind cannot strip it
(restated from the manual). It takes hours and a great deal of material:
start long before dark (general practice).

### Snow

A snow cave or snow trench insulates well, but the manual warns that a
snow cave takes time and energy to build and that you will get wet
building it, and it calls for a ventilation shaft. For most people
caught out without training, a lean-to or windbreak with a good bed of
insulation is the safer first choice (our reading). The Weather Service
adds: "Eating unmelted snow will lower your body temperature." Melt it
first.

### Fire, and the air in a small space

A fire warms you and helps searchers find you, and every fire comes with
two dangers: setting the land alight, and carbon monoxide. Check for
burn bans and fire restrictions, and never light a fire in fire season or
dry weather ([Firewood](/library#firewood) and [What to Do When You Are
Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost)). In a small closed shelter, the
survival manual says to "ventilate an enclosed shelter, especially if you
intend to build a fire in it", and: "Never fall asleep without turning
out your stove or lamp. Carbon monoxide poisoning can result from a fire
burning in an unventilated shelter." It adds why it is so dangerous:
"Usually, there are no symptoms. Unconsciousness and death can occur
without warning."

### Dry clothes are shelter too

Wet cotton against the skin is a shelter with the roof off. [Cold and
Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) explains why cotton fails and wool
and synthetics do not. Change out of wet clothes before you settle for the
night, and do not work so hard building the shelter that you soak
yourself in sweat: the Weather Service says sweating could lead to a chill
and hypothermia.

## Keeping each other alive overnight

- **Share warmth.** Adults and older children lying close together, out
  of the wind and off the ground, lose less heat than people apart
  (general practice); never a baby, who sleeps on its own surface (above).
  For someone already too cold, [Cold and
  Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) gives the CDC's skin-to-skin
  method; for a baby, that means an adult who stays awake holding the
  baby, not an adult and a baby asleep together (our reading of the
  safe-sleep advice above).
- **Watch the quiet ones.** Hypothermia makes people deny it; the signs
  to watch for, the "umbles", are in that guide.
- **Take turns awake** if there is a fire, a stove or an engine running,
  so that someone is watching the flame, the exhaust and the people (our
  reading of the survival manual's carbon monoxide warning).
- **Eat and drink.** Food is fuel for the body's own heat, as the Weather
  Service's list says, and cold air is dry: it says to drink plenty of
  water and other drinks without caffeine or alcohol. Alcohol makes the
  cold more dangerous, not less; [Cold and
  Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) explains why.

## Worked example: a December storm near Silverdale

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on.

A windstorm takes the power out on the Kitsap Peninsula on a December
afternoon. It is raining, about 4 C outside, and the forecast says the
power may be out for days; outages measured in days follow storms here
(see [The Hazards Where You Live](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live)). The
household is two adults, a baby and a grandmother; the house is heated
by a gas furnace, which needs electricity for its fan.

1. **The first evening.** They stay. The kitchen is the warmest room, on
   the sheltered side, with one window: they hang a blanket over the
   doorway, roll a towel against the outside door, close the curtains,
   and bring mattresses and sleeping bags in for the adults, and the
   baby's travel cot. The baby sleeps in it on her back, in an extra
   layer and a wearable blanket, with her head uncovered and nothing else
   in the cot; the adults and the grandmother wear hats. Light is
   flashlights and a battery lantern; the candles stay in the drawer. The
   CO alarms have batteries, and someone tests them.
2. **What they do not do.** They do not light the gas oven, bring the
   barbecue in out of the rain, or run the car in the attached garage to
   warm up the baby. Barbecues and generators used indoors or improperly
   are what NOAA's record says killed eight people in the Seattle and
   Bremerton zone after the 2006 storm, and the oven and the car make the
   same gas (the CDC, above).
3. **The second morning.** The kitchen is down to about 13 C and still
   falling, and the power may be out for days. Nobody is ill yet, and
   that is the time to go: with a baby and a grandmother in the house,
   waiting for signs of cold means waiting too long (our reading of
   FEMA's sheet and of [Cold and Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia)).
   Had the baby's skin been bright red and cold, or her energy very low,
   or had the grandmother been confused, drowsy or no longer shivering,
   they would have called 911 at once instead, because those are signs
   of hypothermia that guide lists, and warmed the baby skin to skin
   under loose blankets while they waited, held by an adult who stays
   awake.
4. **Where to go.** They text SHELTER and their ZIP code to 43362,
   listen to local radio, and check the website of the county's
   Department of Emergency Management, which is where it announces its
   severe weather shelters. In this example a warming centre is open in
   Silverdale for the day. They leave a note on the door saying where
   they have gone, check on the neighbour next door, who is alone, and
   take her with them.
5. **The drive.** It is half a mile on roads they know, with no water
   across them. Had a road been flooded, they would have turned back.
6. **That night.** A friend in another part of the county still has
   power, and they spend the night there rather than going home to a cold
   house. Had there been no friend to go to, they would have checked
   whether one of the county's severe weather shelters was opening for
   the night.

## Know where your own effort stops

- **Someone confused, drowsy or no longer shivering in the cold, or a
  baby with bright red, cold skin or very low energy:** call 911 ([Cold
  and Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia)).
- **Someone whose powered medical device or refrigerated medicine will
  run out:** go early to somewhere with power, and ask their doctor or
  pharmacist (Ready.gov).
- **A carbon monoxide alarm, or several people ill at once:** everyone
  outside, then 911.
- **The smell or sound of gas:** leave, then call the gas company from
  outside.
- **A downed power line:** keep away, and call 911 and the utility.
- **An order to evacuate, or rising water:** go.
- **A fire you cannot put out with a lid or an extinguisher in seconds:**
  get everyone out and call 911 from outside (general practice).

Yours to do: deciding early, shrinking the space, the layers and the
ground, safe heat and light, checking on others, and leaving word.

## Traps

- **"Just for a few minutes, the oven will warm the kitchen."** Ready.gov
  and the CDC both say never.
- **"The barbecue's out of the rain in the garage."** Charcoal indoors,
  including an open garage, makes enough carbon monoxide to kill.
- **"A candle is enough light."** Use a flashlight.
- **"I'll walk for help from the car."** In wind-driven snow you will be
  disoriented quickly.
- **"The car's warm with the engine running."** Not with snow over the
  exhaust pipe or the window shut.
- **"The water's only a few inches deep."** Six inches of moving water
  can knock an adult over ([What to Do When You Are
  Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost)), and it is never safe to drive
  into flood water.
- **"A big shelter's more comfortable."** It is colder.
- **"I'll lie down on the tarp."** A tarp keeps the rain off; it does not
  keep the ground's cold off. Pile insulation under you.
- **"The baby's asleep, so she's fine."** Babies show cold differently;
  see [Cold and Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia).
- **"The baby will be warmest in the bed between us, with a hat on."** A
  baby sleeps on its own firm, flat surface, head uncovered, warmed by a
  wearable blanket or an extra layer (CDC; Safe to Sleep).
- **"We'll wait and see if the baby gets cold."** With a baby or an old
  person in a cooling house, go before anyone shows signs of cold.

## How the game models it

The game models the body's heat and a built shelter, and leaves out most
of what kills people on a real first night.

- **Weather on the body.** On a planet's surface, with fly mode off, your
  body heat comes from the weather (the air temperature, wind, rain or
  snow, humidity, the sun and, on a clear night, the cold sky overhead),
  your clothes, what you are doing and any shelter you have built. Wet
  clothes lose heat faster. While fly mode is on, the weather does not
  reach your body at all. The Body heat setting in Settings > Gameplay
  decides how hard the cold hits; [What to Do When You Are
  Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost) explains its Forgiving default
  (`src/systems/body_heat.rs`, `src/engine/survival_env.rs`).
- **A built shelter.** The walls and the Wood Roof in
  `data/blueprints/basic.ron` count as shelter. A roof overhead keeps the
  rain and snow off and the cold of a clear night sky away. Under a roof,
  walls keep the wind off on the sides they stand: three walls with the
  open side turned away from the wind keep it all off, the same back to
  the wind as the survival manual's lean-to, and the open side turned into
  the wind lets it straight in. The HUD's Shelter line says "Sheltered",
  "Out of the rain and the wind", or how much of the wind still gets in
  (`src/systems/construction/uses.rs`, `src/gui/pages/hud.rs`). Finishing
  a structure trains Shelter Building.
- **Only what you build.** Trees, hollows and a parked vehicle give no
  shelter in the game; only built pieces count.
- **A fire in the open.** A campfire you build (the Campfire ability)
  warms you while its logs burn, the nearer the more (see [Making and
  Controlling Fire](/library#making-and-controlling-fire)). The game will not
  build one under a roof, finished or still going up, and will not build
  a roof over one, so it stands in the open beside your shelter, never
  inside it. Its warmth reaches you only in a straight line: a wall or a
  roof between you and the fire stops all of it, so a fire in front of
  the open side of a three-walled shelter warms the person in it, and a
  fire behind its walls does not (`src/systems/construction/fires.rs`,
  `src/systems/construction/mod.rs`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no ground to lose heat to, so lying on cold earth costs nothing; no
carbon monoxide from fires, stoves, heaters or engines; nothing in a home
catches fire, a candle included; no gas leaks, flood water or falling
trees; and no warming centres or public shelters to go to. In the game a
roof and three walls are the whole answer. On a real night they are the
start of it.

## You own this when

- You can name what kills people on a first night, and how much of it is
  what people do about the cold.
- You decide early whether to stay or go, and you know the reasons to go:
  an order, rising water, gas, a carbon monoxide alarm, a medical device
  or a medicine that will run out before the power comes back, a house
  too cold for the people in it.
- You would put a baby to sleep on its own firm, flat surface, on its
  back, with nothing soft in it and its head uncovered, warmed by a
  wearable blanket or an extra layer, and you would leave before a baby
  or an old person showed signs of cold.
- You know how to find a warming centre or a public shelter, and that most
  will not take pets, and you have signed up for your county's warning
  system.
- You can shrink a cold house to one room and keep it warm without a gas
  oven, charcoal, a camp stove or a generator nearby.
- You light with flashlights and keep heaters 3 feet from anything that
  burns.
- You would stay with a car stuck in snow, run the engine 10 minutes an
  hour with a window open and the exhaust pipe clear, and never drive into
  flood water or leave anyone in a parked car on a warm day.
- You can build a small shelter outdoors with its back to the wind, and
  you always put insulation between yourself and the ground.
- You would ventilate a shelter with a flame in it and put the flame out
  before you sleep.
- You check on the old, the young and the people living alone.
