# Talking Across Space

## Purpose

People want to live on other worlds one day.
Messages between worlds can travel no faster than light,
and that changes how talking, sharing, and deciding have to work.

Designing for that now is not just dreaming.
Almost every rule that space forces on a chat app
also makes it better here on Earth today.

This page explains those rules in plain words.
HumanityOS already follows some of them and is being built toward the rest;
the technical version says which: [interplanetary.md](/library#interplanetary).

---

## How Far Is Far

Light is the fastest anything can go. Even so:

- **The Moon** is about 1.3 seconds away.
  Talking works, like a bad phone line.
- **Mars** is between about 3 and 22 minutes away, each way,
  depending on where both planets are in their orbits.
  A question and its answer can take 44 minutes.
- **Every 26 months or so, the Sun sits between Earth and Mars** for about two weeks,
  and no message gets through at all.

No live calls, no "loading...", no instant replies.
Space agencies already send data this way:
each message travels as a sealed package that every stop along the way
holds until the next link opens.
The idea is called delay-tolerant networking,
and NASA has tested it on the International Space Station.

## Six Rules, and What Each One Gives Earth Today

### 1. Every message is a sealed, signed package

It carries who wrote it and proof that they did,
so it can travel any way at all and be checked on arrival.

*On Earth today:* the same package can go by internet, by a home network,
by radio, or on a USB stick carried across a town where the internet is down.

### 2. Nothing needs a back-and-forth

At 44 minutes per exchange, "hello, prove who you are, here you go"
would take hours.
Everything has to prove itself in one trip.

*On Earth today:* fewer round trips means faster messages on phones,
satellite links and patchy connections.

### 3. There is no single "now"

Clocks on different devices disagree,
and a message written later can arrive first.
So the order of things comes from what each person had already seen
when they wrote, not from whose clock says what.

*On Earth today:* your phone and your computer can both be offline for a while,
and when they reconnect, the choices you made on each come together the right way.

### 4. Read what is near you

Mars keeps its own copies of what people on Mars read,
and the two worlds swap updates across the gap.

*On Earth today:* your private messages live on your own devices first.
They open instantly, stay private, and still work when the internet does not.

### 5. Send only what is missing, the most important first

Over a narrow link, two sides compare what they already have
and send just the difference.
Words and votes go before pictures, pictures before full videos,
and emergencies before everything.

*On Earth today:* less mobile data, faster catching up after being offline,
and urgent messages getting out first in a disaster.

### 6. Say honestly where a message is

Not just "sent" or "failed", but each step:
written, left your device, reached your server, reached theirs, delivered, read.
Between worlds: "arrives about 10:42 Mars time".

*On Earth today:* you stop wondering whether a message got through,
which is most of what makes chat feel unreliable.

## What Changes Between Worlds

- **Calls become video messages**, and live streams become broadcasts that arrive late,
  watched when they arrive.
- **Every world governs itself.** A vote across worlds needs a window
  long enough for the farthest voter,
  and most decisions belong to the people they affect.
  [Interstellar Civilization Governance](/library#interstellar-civilization-governance)
  takes this all the way out to other stars.
- **The game can teach it.** HumanityOS already simulates space,
  so messages between ships and worlds can travel at the speed of light,
  and players learn the limit by living with it.
