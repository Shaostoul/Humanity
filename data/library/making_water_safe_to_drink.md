# Making Water Safe to Drink

There is water in front of you and you do not know whether it is safe.
It might be a stream, a well after a flood, a rain barrel, a tap under a
boil-water advisory, or a jug that has been in the garage since you
cannot remember when. This guide is about that moment: how to decide what
is wrong with the water, which treatment fixes that particular thing, and
which problems no home treatment fixes at all.

Its companion, Storing Water Safely, covers the calmer half of the
subject: how much to store, what containers to use, how to sanitize and
label them, and how to rotate the supply. If you are filling containers
from a working, treated tap, that guide is the one you want and this one
is not needed. Come here when the source is questionable. The two guides
overlap deliberately on boiling and bleach, because those two facts are
worth knowing twice.

One honest thing first. "Purifying water" is not one job, it is three
separate jobs, and no single method does all three:

1. **Germs** (bacteria, viruses, parasites). These are what make people
   acutely ill within days, and they are what most treatment addresses.
2. **Dirt** (silt, leaves, cloudiness). Mostly a nuisance, but it
   interferes with the methods that handle germs.
3. **Chemicals** (fuel, pesticides, industrial spills, lead, nitrate,
   arsenic). These are the ones home treatment largely cannot touch, and
   the ones people most often assume boiling handles.

Sort out which one you actually have before you do anything.

## Start by choosing the best source you can

Treatment is a last resort, not a first move. Before treating, look for
better water. A covered well is better than an open pond. Water upstream
of a settlement is better than water downstream of it. Rain caught in a
clean container is better than runoff. Clear is better than cloudy.
Flowing is better than standing.

And the one absolute rule, in CDC's words: "You cannot make water
containing fuel, toxic chemicals, or radioactive materials safe by
boiling or disinfecting it." If the water smells of fuel or solvent, or
if you know an industrial or agricultural spill has reached it, no
technique in this guide applies. Find another source.

## Step 1: clear it first (this is not treatment)

Cloudy water defeats chemical disinfection, because the disinfectant is
used up on the particles, and germs shelter inside clumps of sediment.

CDC's method: let the water settle, then filter it through a clean cloth,
paper towel, or coffee filter, and draw off the clear part.

That does not make it safe. Nothing that removes visible dirt makes water
safe. It makes the actual treatment work.

## Step 2: boiling, the one that always works

If you can boil, boil. It handles all three classes of germ, it needs no
special equipment, and it cannot be done wrong except by not doing it
long enough.

- CDC: "Bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute (at elevations
  above 6,500 feet, boil for 3 minutes)."
- EPA puts the altitude cutoff lower, above 5,000 feet. The agencies
  differ, so above 5,000 feet the safe choice is the full 3 minutes.
- WHO, whose guidance covers the whole world rather than one country,
  states that "heating water to a rolling boil, as recommended in the WHO
  Guidelines for Drinking-water Quality, is sufficient to inactivate
  pathogenic bacteria, viruses and protozoa."

Note the WHO wording carefully. It is the *rolling boil* that is the
signal, not the number of minutes. The visible rolling boil tells you the
water reached a disinfecting temperature; you do not need a thermometer,
which is exactly why the method is so robust. CDC's travel guidance notes
that lower temperatures also work with longer contact, with temperatures
as low as 60 C (140 F) effective over 30 minutes, but at home you do not
have a way to verify 60 C, and you can see a boil from across the room.

Let it cool covered. Boiled water tastes flat because the dissolved air
has been driven off; pouring it back and forth between two clean
containers puts the air back.

**What boiling does not do:** it does not remove chemicals. Worse, it
concentrates them, because the water leaves as steam and the chemical
stays behind. CDC states it plainly for lead: "Boiling this water will
not reduce the amount of lead in your water."

## Step 3: filters, and the pore size that decides everything

This is where most confusion lives. A filter is a sieve, so whether it
works depends entirely on whether its holes are smaller than the thing
you are trying to catch. Germs differ enormously in size, and no single
number covers them.

CDC's travel health guidance gives the sizes:

- **Viruses** are about 0.03 micrometers across, and need a pore size of
  about 0.01 to catch them, which means an ultrafilter.
- **Bacteria** such as E. coli are 0.5 by 2 to 8 micrometers, and need a
  pore size of 0.2 to 0.4 or smaller.
- ***Cryptosporidium***, a parasite, is 4 to 6 micrometers, and needs a
  pore size of 1 or smaller.
- ***Giardia***, a parasite, is 8 by 19 micrometers, and needs a pore
  size of 3 to 5 or smaller.

A micrometer, also called a micron, is a thousandth of a millimeter. The
practical consequence of those sizes is this ladder:

- **Microfilters**, pores under 1 micron: the ordinary hiking and
  camping filter. Removes bacteria and parasites. CDC: microfilters "do
  not reliably remove enteric viruses." Good for a clean wilderness
  stream. Not good enough on its own where human sewage may have reached
  the water.
- **Ultrafilters**, about 0.01 micron: CDC describes these as "effective
  for removing viruses, bacteria, and parasites." This is the level at
  which a filter alone handles all three classes of germ.
- **Nanofilters and reverse osmosis:** remove viruses and, unlike
  everything above, also remove chemical contaminants. This is the only
  filtration class in ordinary reach that touches problem 3.
- **Activated carbon** (the black granules in most household filter
  pitchers): CDC is precise here, carbon filters "trap, but do not kill,
  microorganisms." What they do well is remove chlorine, iodine, heavy
  metals, and bad taste. A carbon pitcher filter is not a treatment for
  unsafe water and was never sold as one.

CDC's emergency guidance for choosing a portable filter: it needs "an
absolute pore size of 1 micron or smaller to remove parasites (such as
*Giardia* or *Cryptosporidium*)," and such a filter "will not remove
viruses."

**How to read a filter's label.** Two things matter. First, look for
**absolute** pore size, not nominal; a nominal rating is an average, and
an average pore size of 1 micron means some pores are larger. Second,
look for what it is certified to remove. A product marketed in the United
States as a water **purifier** has to demonstrate, under EPA's guidance,
removal of "at least 99.9999% of bacteria, 99.99% of viruses, and 99.9%
of *Cryptosporidium* oocysts or *Giardia* cysts." A product marketed as a
**filter** makes no such claim about viruses.

If you buy one thing after reading this guide, buy the filter whose label
says what it removes, and keep the label.

## Step 4: chemical disinfection

Use this when you cannot boil: no fuel, no power, or you need to treat a
large volume.

**Household bleach.** Plain and unscented, labeled as sodium
hypochlorite, with nothing else added. Not scented, not "splashless," not
color-safe, not a bleach with detergent or cleaners mixed in, and not
pool chlorine. If the label lists anything beyond sodium hypochlorite and
water, do not put it in drinking water. CDC's amounts for 5 to 9 percent
bleach:

- **1 quart or 1 liter of water:** 2 drops of bleach.
- **1 gallon:** 8 drops, a little less than 1/8 teaspoon.
- **5 gallons:** 40 drops, about 2.5 mL.

Double the amount if the water is cloudy, colored or very cold. Stir,
cover, and let it stand at least 30 minutes before drinking. The water
should have a slight chlorine smell afterward; if it does not, repeat the
dose and wait another 15 minutes. EPA's table is equivalent, differing
only in that it gives 6 drops per gallon for 8.25 percent bleach and 8
drops per gallon for 6 percent. Storing Water Safely has more on
selecting and storing the bleach itself, including the fact that bleach
loses strength as it ages and should be replaced along with your stored
water.

**Iodine.** EPA: add 5 drops of 2 percent tincture of iodine per quart or
liter, or 10 drops if the water is cloudy or colored, then stir and let
it stand at least 30 minutes. Iodine has medical limits that chlorine
does not; read the product label, and if you are pregnant, have a thyroid
condition, or have an iodine sensitivity, ask a clinician before using
it. It is also not meant for continuous long-term use.

**Chlorine dioxide tablets.** These matter for one specific reason, in
the next section. Follow the product label exactly; strengths and contact
times differ between products, and the contact time is often much longer
than for bleach.

**The gap you have to know about.** Chlorine and iodine do not reliably
kill *Cryptosporidium*. CDC states it directly: "Iodine, tablets with
iodine, or chlorine tablets kill most germs, but will not kill
Cryptosporidium germs." CDC's travel guidance explains why: the parasite
is "poorly inactivated by chlorine- or iodine-based disinfection at
practical concentrations."

So a jug treated with bleach is not automatically safe from everything.
Two things do handle *Cryptosporidium*: boiling, and chlorine dioxide,
which CDC says "kills waterborne pathogens, including *Cryptosporidium*
oocysts, at practical doses and contact times." A filter of 1 micron
absolute or smaller catches it physically.

## The combination that covers the most ground

Match the method to what you are worried about:

- **Bacteria:** boiling yes, microfilter yes, bleach or iodine yes,
  chlorine dioxide yes.
- **Viruses:** boiling yes, microfilter **no**, bleach or iodine yes,
  chlorine dioxide yes.
- ***Giardia*:** boiling yes, microfilter yes, bleach or iodine **less
  reliable**, chlorine dioxide yes.
- ***Cryptosporidium*:** boiling yes, microfilter yes, bleach or iodine
  **no**, chlorine dioxide yes.
- **Chemicals:** boiling **no**, microfilter **no**, bleach or iodine
  **no**, chlorine dioxide **no**.

("Microfilter" here means a filter with an absolute pore size under 1
micron, the ordinary camping kind.)

On the *Giardia* line, the two CDC sources differ in emphasis and the
cautious reading is the one above. CDC's travel guidance treats
chlorine and iodine as effective against *Giardia* given adequate contact
time; CDC's emergency guidance says more plainly that disinfectants "do
not work as well as boiling for killing some germs, including the
parasites *Cryptosporidium* and *Giardia*." Treat bleach as an imperfect
answer for both parasites and use boiling, a filter, or chlorine dioxide
where you can.

Reading down that list, boiling is the only single method that covers
every germ line, which is why it stays the first recommendation whenever fuel exists.
When it does not, the strong pairing is **filter, then disinfect**: a
microfilter takes out the parasites and the cloudiness that would
otherwise consume the chemical, and the chemical then handles the viruses
the filter let through. CDC's own phrasing on filters is the warning
label for doing only half of this: "Filtered water might need additional
treatment to be safe."

Note the entirely empty bottom line. That is the point of the next
section.

## What nothing here fixes

No method in this guide removes chemical contamination. Not boiling, not
a camping filter, not bleach, not a carbon pitcher.

That covers fuel and solvent spills, pesticides, industrial discharge,
and also the quieter ones that have no smell at all: lead from old
plumbing, nitrate from fertilizer runoff, arsenic in some groundwater.
For lead specifically, CDC recommends a point-of-use filter certified by
an independent testing organization, one whose label shows "NSF/ANSI
standard 53 for lead removal and NSF/ANSI standard 42 for particulate
removal," and notes that you should draw water for drinking and cooking
only from the cold tap, because warm or hot tap water "can have higher
levels of lead."

The general answers to chemical contamination are reverse osmosis,
certain certified filters, or a different source. The general answer to
"is there a chemical problem in my water" is a laboratory test, not a
taste or a smell. If you are on a private well, your local health
department or extension office can tell you what to test for and where.

Two methods that are worth knowing exist but that this guide deliberately
does not teach, because doing them properly requires more than a
paragraph:

- **Solar disinfection (SODIS)**, which uses sunlight in clear plastic
  bottles and matters greatly where fuel is scarce or expensive. WHO
  evaluates household water treatment technologies including this one;
  start from their drinking-water quality guidance rather than from a
  summary.
- **Distillation**, which is the one home method that separates water
  from dissolved chemicals and salts. It is slow, fuel-hungry, and has
  its own procedure.

## After treatment: do not put it back where it came from

Treated water in a dirty container is untreated water again. Pour it into
a container that has been washed and sanitized, cap it tightly, label it
with the date, and keep it cool and dark. Storing Water Safely covers
sanitizing containers step by step, which containers never to use (milk
jugs, and anything that held a chemical), and the six-month rotation
rule.

If you treated the water because of a boil-water advisory, keep treating
until your water utility says the advisory is lifted, and follow their
instructions over anything written here. They know what happened to the
system; a general guide does not.

## What can go wrong

- **The water still tastes and smells of chlorine.** Normal after
  disinfection, and a slight chlorine smell is the confirmation that the
  dose was enough. Let it stand uncovered for a while, or pour it between
  clean containers, or run it through a carbon filter, which is the one
  job a carbon pitcher does very well.
- **You treated cloudy water and someone got sick anyway.** The classic
  chemical-disinfection failure. The particles consumed the chlorine and
  sheltered the germs. Always settle and pre-filter first, and double the
  dose for cloudy water.
- **You used a hiking filter on water downstream of a town.** A
  microfilter does not remove viruses. Boil it, or add a chemical step,
  or use a filter rated as a purifier.
- **The filter clogged or slowed to a trickle.** That is what a working
  filter does as it fills up. Backflush or replace the element per the
  instructions, and pre-filter cloudy water to make the element last.
  Never scrape or "clean" a filter element in a way the maker does not
  describe; you can open channels straight through it.
- **You boiled water that had a chemical smell.** Do not drink it. You
  concentrated the problem. Discard it and find another source.
- **A jug tastes like the container.** It may not be food grade. Retire
  it from water duty. Storing Water Safely lists containers to never use.
- **You cannot remember whether that jug was treated.** Then it was not.
  Label everything, always, at the moment you fill it.

## How you know it worked

- You can name which of the three problems you were solving, and the
  method you used is one that actually addresses it.
- Water that started cloudy was settled and pre-filtered before any
  chemical went in.
- If you used a filter, you know its absolute pore size and what its
  label says it removes.
- If you used bleach, the water had a faint chlorine smell after 30
  minutes.
- The treated water is now in a clean, sanitized, labeled, dated,
  tightly capped container, stored cool and dark.
- If there is any chance of chemical contamination, you did not treat it
  at all. You went and found different water.

That last one is the most important line in this guide. Almost everything
about drinking water is a solvable problem with a known procedure. The
one that is not solvable at home is the one worth recognizing, and
recognizing it is a skill in itself.
