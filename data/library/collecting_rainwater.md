# Collecting Rainwater

Rain that lands on your roof runs into the gutters, down a pipe called a
downspout, and away into the ground. A rain barrel is simply a container
parked under that downspout to catch some of the water before it leaves.
The reward is free water for your garden, less mud around your house in
storms, and a small buffer of non-drinking water for emergencies.

This is one of the easiest projects in this series. The hard parts are
not the plumbing; they are the safety details around weight, mosquitoes,
and where the extra water goes. This guide covers all of them.

## First: check your local rules

Rainwater law is not the same everywhere. In most of the United States
you may collect freely, but a few states regulate or limit it because of
how water rights work there. Colorado, for example, allows single-family
homes (and multifamily buildings of four or fewer units) only two
barrels holding 110 gallons combined, with sealable lids, used outdoors
on the same property (see the Colorado State University Extension page
in Sources). Other countries have their own rules. Before
you buy anything, search your state or local government website for
"rain barrel" or "rainwater harvesting," or ask your local university
extension office. This takes ten minutes and can save you a fine.

## Second: this water is not for drinking

Roof water picks up bird droppings, dust, insect remains, and traces of
the roof itself. University extension guidance is consistent: water from
a rain barrel is non-potable, which means not safe to drink, and should
not be used for drinking, cooking, or brushing teeth. This guide is for
watering ornamental plants, washing tools and muddy boots, and other
non-drinking uses. For actual drinking water storage, use the Storing
Water Safely guide in this series; do not try to make roof water
drinkable.

One honest disagreement to know about: extension services differ on
using rain barrel water on food gardens. Nebraska Extension recommends
keeping it off fruits and vegetables entirely. The cautious path, and
the one this guide takes: use rain barrel water on flowers, shrubs,
trees, and lawn, and keep tap water for anything you will eat.

## How much water a roof really makes

The math is simple and the answer surprises everyone. One inch of rain
falling on one square foot of roof yields about 0.6 gallons of water
(0.623 to be exact).

Worked example: a modest 1,200 square foot roof in a one inch rainstorm
sheds about 750 gallons (1,200 x 1 x 0.623). If one downspout drains a
quarter of that roof, about 300 square feet, it delivers nearly 190
gallons per inch of rain, which means about a third of an inch of rain
fills a standard 55 gallon barrel. A barrel fills much faster than
intuition says, which is why the overflow section below matters.

## What you need

Cheapest options first.

- A barrel, 50 to 55 gallons is the standard size. A recycled
  food-grade barrel (one that held food or drink ingredients, sold
  cheaply by feed stores, soda bottlers, and farmers, or found on
  local marketplaces; avoid barrels that held meat products) works as
  well as a bought rain barrel. Never use
  a barrel that held chemicals, soaps, or anything unknown; residue
  can poison your soil and you cannot rinse away what you cannot
  identify. Dark, opaque barrels are best because sunlight through
  light-colored plastic grows algae; a light barrel can be painted a
  dark color.
- A spigot (faucet) kit for the bottom of the barrel, or a purpose-made
  rain barrel that already has one, plus an overflow fitting near the
  top. Hardware stores sell simple kits.
- Fiberglass window screen, enough to cover every opening.
- Concrete blocks or a purpose-built stand for a raised, level base.
- A hacksaw for shortening the downspout, and a drill if your barrel
  needs holes cut. Safety glasses and gloves for the cutting.
- Optional but worthwhile: a first flush diverter (explained below) and
  a flexible downspout elbow to aim water into the barrel.

## Check your roof before you start

Nebraska Extension's list of roof materials that can shed chemicals
into runoff includes asphalt roofing, redwood or cedar shakes, treated
wood shingles, roofing containing asbestos, lead solder or lead-based
paint, zinc from galvanized flashing, and moss-killer treatments. That
list includes the most common roof in America, so here is the honest
practical reading: extension rain-barrel programs across the country
assume ordinary asphalt-shingle roofs, because for the
ornamental-and-garden watering this guide allows, shingle runoff is
broadly treated as acceptable. The list is why the no-drinking rule
exists, and why the riskier materials below deserve real caution. If your roof is old enough that asbestos is possible, or
it has been treated with moss killer, or you simply do not know what
it is made of, do not collect from it until you find out. Zinc
anti-moss strips near the ridge are a common quiet offender; look for
thin metal strips running along the roof peak.

## Build the base like it matters, because it does

Water weighs almost 8.5 pounds per gallon. A full 55 gallon barrel
weighs about 460 pounds. That is not a thing you can catch if it tips,
and a falling barrel can seriously injure a child or pet standing next
to it. So:

1. Choose ground that is firm and can be made level. Soft soil under
   460 pounds will settle unevenly and lean.
2. Build a platform from solid concrete blocks (not stacked bricks
   that can shift) one or two courses high. Raising the barrel makes
   room for a watering can under the spigot and improves the gravity
   flow.
3. Check the platform with a level side to side and front to back
   before the barrel goes on. Fix it now; you cannot fix it full.
4. Never let children climb on or hang from the barrel, and do not
   stack anything on top.

## Setting it up

1. Set the empty barrel on its finished base, directly under or beside
   the downspout.
2. With the barrel in place, hold the downspout elbow where it will
   sit, aimed at the barrel inlet, and mark the downspout about two
   inches below the top of the elbow (the Rutgers Extension method:
   measure with the elbow held in position, never against the bare
   inlet, because the elbow itself adds several inches of drop). Take
   the barrel away and cut the downspout at the mark with the hacksaw.
   Wear safety glasses; cut metal edges are sharp, so file or tape
   them. If the cut requires a ladder or the gutters need repair, hire
   a gutter company rather than balance a saw overhead. Ladder falls
   are the serious injury in this project.
3. Squeeze the freshly cut end slightly with pliers if needed and push
   the elbow onto it, secured with short downspout screws, so water
   aims into the barrel inlet. Keep the removed section; you will want
   it back in winter.
4. Fit the spigot near the bottom and the overflow fitting near the
   top, following the kit instructions.
5. Cover the inlet and every other opening with window screen pulled
   tight and secured. No gaps, ever. This is your mosquito defense.
   Some jurisdictions add their own container rules on top (Colorado,
   for example, requires barrels to have a sealable lid).
6. Attach a hose to the overflow fitting, at least 8 to 10 feet long,
   and run it where you want storm water to go (next section).

## First flush: the first water off the roof is the dirtiest

Between rains, droppings and dust build up on the roof, and the first
minutes of a storm rinse almost all of it off. A first flush diverter
is a simple standpipe on the downspout that fills with that first
dirty water and then routes the rest, cleaner, to your barrel. Texas
A&M recommends diverting about 1 to 2 gallons for every 100 square
feet of roof feeding your barrel, and emptying the diverter chamber
after each rain so it is ready for the next one. A diverter is
optional for a garden barrel, but it noticeably improves water
clarity and smell for a few dollars of PVC pipe.

## Plan the overflow before the first storm

Remember the arithmetic: a third of an inch of rain fills the barrel,
and a real storm keeps going. Hundreds of gallons will pass through
the overflow, and water dumped beside your house finds its way into
basements and crawl spaces. Rutgers Extension says to direct overflow
at least 6 feet from the foundation if you have a basement, at least
2 feet if you have a crawl space; farther is better, and it should
flow downhill away from the house, into a garden bed, a lawn, or the
base of a tree. Watch it work during the first heavy rain and adjust.

## Using the water

Honesty about pressure: a rain barrel is gravity-fed, and gravity a
few feet up delivers far less pressure than a house spigot. Sprinklers
will not run from a barrel. What works: filling watering cans (the
simplest and best method, per Nebraska Extension), drip or soaker
hoses designed for rain barrels, and letting a hose trickle at the
base of a plant. Raising the barrel on its base helps. Use the water
within a few days after rain; that empties the barrel for the next
storm and denies mosquitoes a nursery.

## Winter care

Where it freezes, water left in the barrel will expand and crack it.
Before hard freezes: drain the barrel completely, disconnect it,
reattach the downspout piece you saved (or add an extension) so winter
melt still drains away from the foundation, and store the barrel
upside down, covered, or in a shed with the spigot open.

## Cleaning

Once a year, usually when you shut down for winter, scrub the inside
with a long-handled brush and rinse. For lingering smells, Alabama
Extension suggests 2 teaspoons of castile soap plus 2 teaspoons of
lemon juice or vinegar per gallon of wash water, and advises against
bleach or harsh commercial cleaners because the residue ends up in
your soil. Rinse until the water runs clear.

## What can go wrong

- Wiggling little larvae in the water: mosquitoes got past a gap. In
  warm weather they can go from egg to adult in as little as 5 to 7
  days, so act the day you see them. Find and fix the screen gap,
  and use or dump the water. If water routinely sits more than about
  72 hours, check for larvae; a Bti mosquito dunk (a bacterial larva
  killer sold at garden stores, labeled for rain barrels) is a safe
  backstop, but tight screens are the real fix.
- Green water or green film: algae, fed by sunlight through the
  barrel wall. Move the barrel to shade or paint it a dark color,
  and scrub it out at the next cleaning.
- Rotten-egg or swampy smell: water sat too long with debris in it,
  often leaves that got past the inlet screen. Drain it onto the
  lawn, clean the barrel, add or repair the inlet screen, and
  consider a first flush diverter.
- Barrel always empty: your downspout may drain a smaller roof
  section than you thought, or the elbow is missing the inlet in
  wind-blown rain. Watch it during a storm. If it truly fills and
  empties fast, that is success; add a second barrel in series (its
  inlet fed by the first barrel's overflow hose) if your rules allow.
- Barrel leaning: drain it immediately and fix the base. Never try
  to shim a full barrel.

## How you know it worked

After a storm, the barrel is full, the overflow ran away from the
house, and nothing leans. A week later the water is clear-ish, smells
like nothing much, and has no wigglers in it. Your watering cans fill
themselves for free all summer, and when you tip the barrel over in
late fall, cleaning it takes ten minutes. That is the whole win: a
quiet system that mostly tends itself.
