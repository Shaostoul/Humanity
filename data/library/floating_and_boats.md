# Floating and Boats

A steel ship weighing thousands of tonnes floats, and a pebble sinks. A
canoe carries two adults and their gear across a lake, and tips them all
into it when one of them stands up to stretch. Both facts come from the
same two pieces of physics: what makes a thing float, and what keeps a
floating thing the right way up. This guide teaches both, because they
are the reasons behind every rule for being safe in a small boat.

Those rules are not optional extras. In 2024 the US Coast Guard counted
556 deaths in recreational boating. Where the cause of death was known,
76 percent of the people who died drowned, and of those who drowned,
where it was known whether they wore a life jacket, 87 percent were not
wearing one. (Of all 556 deaths, where it was known, 80 percent were not
wearing one: our arithmetic from the same report's table of deaths by
life jacket wear.) The Coast Guard's guide to the federal requirements
for recreational boats puts it in one line: "Drowning is the #1 cause of
boating fatalities, and it is in many cases preventable."

This guide sits beside [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide), which
covers currents, being cut off on the shore and what cold water does to a
body in its first minutes, and [Cold and
Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia). [Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](/library#force-levers-and-mechanical-advantage) explains forces and
moments, which is what stability is made of. [Navigating Without
Instruments](/library#navigating-without-instruments) covers finding your way
on the water, and [What to Do When You Are
Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost) covers calling for help.

Its sources are the US Coast Guard, the Code of Federal Regulations, the
National Weather Service, the US Geological Survey, NOAA, the Forest
Service's *Wood Handbook* and military training material approved for
public release, all public domain; and, for the worked example,
Washington State law, restated in our own words. Where something is
general practice, our reading or arithmetic, the text says so.

## First: what keeps people alive on the water

Read this part even if you skip the physics.

1. **Wear the life jacket, do not just carry it.** The Coast Guard: "A
   wearable PFD can save your life, but only when you wear it." It also
   warns that "most boating related drownings happen on nice days", the
   days people leave the jacket off. Fasten it: the Coast Guard says a
   foam jacket worn correctly fits snugly and does not ride up above the
   chin or ears. Clip a whistle to each one; the Coast Guard: "Some items
   that are not required but are a good idea to have with your wearable
   PFD are a whistle and an emergency light." Federal rules require a
   wearable life jacket on board for every person (33 CFR 175.15), and a
   child under 13 must wear one while the boat is under way, unless
   below deck or in an enclosed cabin, wherever a state has not set its
   own rule (33 CFR 175.15 and 175.25). Washington's rule is in the
   worked example below.
2. **Treat all water as cold until you know otherwise.** The National
   Weather Service says cold shock can be just as severe in water of 50
   to 60 F as in water of 35 F, and that the gasping it causes can be
   triggered by water as warm as 77 F (25 C). Its advice: "Always dress
   for water temperature, not for air temperature." The examples it gives
   are a wetsuit, a drysuit, an immersion suit, a survival suit and
   exposure coveralls. [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) has the
   numbers for Puget Sound and the 1-10-1 rule.
3. **Do not overload, and sit down.** The Coast Guard: "Too many people
   and/or too much gear can cause the boat to become unstable." And:
   "Standing in a boat raises the center of gravity and risks capsizing
   the boat." The sections below explain why.
4. **Boat sober.** In the Coast Guard's 2024 statistics, alcohol was the
   leading known contributing factor in fatal boating incidents, and the
   leading factor in 20 percent of deaths where the main cause was known.
5. **Check the weather, get off the water at the first thunder, leave a
   float plan, and carry a way to call.** The Coast Guard: "You should
   never leave the dock without first checking the local weather
   forecast." The National Weather Service says most lightning deaths
   and injuries on boats happen on small boats with no cabin, and that if
   thunderstorms are forecast, you should not go out. Its rule for
   thunder: "If you hear thunder, lightning is close enough to strike
   you." It says to move at once to a substantial building or a
   hard-topped vehicle, and to anyone caught outside: "Immediately get
   out and away from ponds, lakes and other bodies of water". On a small
   boat that means heading for shore at the first rumble, then getting
   out of the boat and away from the water. If you cannot get back to
   land, it says to drop anchor and get as low as possible; anchor from
   the bow, never the stern (the Coast Guard's rule, explained below).
   Tell a responsible person where you are going and when you will be
   back, as the Coast Guard advises. It also recommends always carrying a
   means of sending a distress alert, most commonly a VHF marine radio,
   and says a mobile phone is not a substitute for one, though it can be
   an added measure of safety. The Weather Service's cold water advice is
   the same: carry a beacon or a VHF radio. A personal locator beacon
   calls for help by satellite, and the Coast Guard says such beacons
   alert rescuers and guide them to you "even when all other
   communications fail"; they must be registered with NOAA. That advice
   is for every trip; beyond the near shore, treat it as a rule and do
   not go without a VHF radio or a beacon (our reading of both). Keep a
   phone in a waterproof case as well (general practice; [What to Do
   When You Are Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost)).
6. **If you go in, stay with the boat.** The Coast Guard says a
   capsized vessel will likely float on or just below the surface, and
   advises: "To reduce the effects of hypothermia, get in or on the
   boat." Keep your life jacket on. Leave the boat only when the shore is
   close and you are sure of reaching it (the National Weather Service:
   "If you can swim to safety, stay calm and do so"), or when the boat is
   drifting into rocks, a weir or fast water (general practice).
7. **If someone else goes in, throw, do not go.** The Army's survival
   manual: "The best technique for rescuing personnel from the water is
   to throw them a life preserver attached to a line." And: "A rescuer
   should not underestimate the strength of a panic-stricken person in
   the water." [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) puts it as throw,
   reach, row, call.

## Why things float

Push a ball under water and you feel the water push back. That push is
buoyancy, and Archimedes worked out its size more than 2,200 years ago: an
object in water is pushed up with a force equal to the weight of the
water it pushes out of the way (the principle, as every physics text
states it). A floating object sinks until the water it displaces weighs
exactly as much as the object does, and stops there. If it can displace
its whole weight of water before it is completely under, it floats; if it
cannot, it sinks.

So what matters is weight compared with volume, which is density. The US
Geological Survey: "The density of water is roughly 1 gram per milliliter
but, this changes with temperature or if there are substances dissolved
in it." That makes the arithmetic easy: a litre of water weighs about a
kilogram, and a cubic foot weighs about 62.4 pounds (the USGS's table
gives 62.4 near freezing and 62.3 at 70 F). Every litre of boat pushed
below the waterline holds up about a kilogram; every cubic foot holds up
about 62 pounds (arithmetic).

**Why steel floats.** A cube of steel sinks: it is far denser than water.
The same steel rolled into a hull floats, because the hull encloses a
great volume of air, and the boat as a whole (steel plus air) is less
dense than water. A US Navy damage control lesson makes exactly this
comparison: a ton of steel as a block sinks, and a ton of steel as a hull
floats, sinking only to the draft where buoyancy and gravity balance. The
weight of water displaced by the underwater part of the hull equals the
weight of the ship; that weight is what ships call their displacement.

**How much shows above water.** The share of a floating object under
water is its density divided by the water's (arithmetic). The USGS gives
the example everyone has seen: ice is about 90 percent as dense as
water, "That means that about 10 percent of an ice cube (or iceberg) will
be above the water line."

**Salt water floats you higher.** The USGS: "ocean water, which contains
dissolved salt, is denser than pure water." Denser water means less of it
has to be pushed aside to carry the same weight, so the same boat floats
a little higher in the sea than in a lake (our reading). Cargo ships
carry separate load lines for fresh and salt water partly for this
reason, as the Navy lesson explains: the minimum freeboard they must
keep varies with the density of the water and the severity of the
weather.

**People nearly float on their own.** A human body is close to the
density of water, which is why a life jacket can be so small. The Coast
Guard: "Most adults only need 7 to 12 pounds of buoyancy (31 to 53
Newton) to keep their heads above water." That is about 3 to 5
kilograms of lift for someone who weighs 70 or 80 on land (arithmetic).
Life jackets labelled with a performance Level state their minimum
buoyancy in newtons: "70 Newtons equals 15.74 pounds of buoyancy." The
Army's survival manual says that in sea water the body's natural
buoyancy keeps at least the top of the head above water, but that some
movement is needed to keep the face out. That small margin is all that
separates floating from drowning, and cold water's gasping and loss of
muscle control take it away; a life jacket gives it back (our reading).

**Clothes do not sink you straight away.** The Coast Guard: "It is a
common belief that persons dressed in heavy clothing or waders will sink
immediately if they fall overboard. This is not true." It explains that
air trapped in clothing provides buoyancy, and that bending your knees
traps air in waders. Its advice is not to try to take clothing or
footwear off in the water: thrashing wears you out and lets the air
escape.

## What a boat can carry, and why the law leaves a margin

Every boat has a limit set by the same arithmetic: the most it can
displace before water comes over the side, less its own weight, is the
most it could possibly carry in flat calm. Federal rules turn that into
the capacity plate.

**The plate.** Monohull boats under 20 feet long must carry a capacity
plate showing the maximum number of persons, the maximum persons weight
in pounds, the maximum total weight of persons, motor and gear, and the
maximum horsepower, or a statement that the boat is not rated for a
motor; the plate must be where the operator can see it when getting
under way (33 CFR 183.21 to 183.25). Sailboats, canoes, kayaks and
inflatable boats are not covered by these rules, so do not expect a
federal plate on them (33 CFR 183.21); for those, look for the maker's
rating in the manual or on the hull (general practice).

**How the numbers are set.** The regulations define a boat's maximum
displacement as the weight of the water it displaces at its deepest level
immersion in calm water without water coming aboard. Then:

- for a boat rated for oars or paddles, or for a motor of 2 horsepower or
  less, the maximum weight capacity may not be more than three tenths of
  the difference between that maximum displacement and the boat's own
  weight (33 CFR 183.37);
- for an outboard boat, no more than one fifth of it (33 CFR 183.35);
- for a boat rated for manual propulsion, the persons weight may be no
  more than 90 percent of the maximum weight capacity, and the number of
  persons is that figure plus 32 pounds, divided by 141 and rounded to
  the nearest whole person (33 CFR 183.43).

**A worked example** with made-up numbers (arithmetic on the rules
above). A 12-foot rowboat weighs 150 pounds, and loaded level until
water would come over the side it displaces 1,700 pounds of water.

1. The most it could ever carry in calm water is 1,700 - 150 = 1,550
   pounds.
2. The rules allow three tenths of that: 465 pounds of people and gear.
3. Persons capacity: 90 percent of 465 is about 418 pounds; (418 + 32) /
   141 = 3.2, so the plate says 3 persons or 418 pounds.

The law keeps seven tenths of that rowboat's reserve unused. That margin
is for everything that real water does and a calm test tank does not:
waves slopping in, people shifting their weight, someone leaning out to
reach a line, rain filling the bottom (our reading of why the fraction is
so small). Inside the limit, the boat has room for mistakes. At the limit,
it has none left.

Note also that the 141 pounds per person is a small adult. If the plate
says 3 persons or 418 pounds and your three people weigh 600, the pounds
are the limit that counts (our reading).

**No plate?** The Coast Guard gives a rule of thumb for a boat without
one, based on a 150-pound person: "Maximum Number of Persons = Boat
Length X Boat Width/15." It warns that the rule does not account for the
boat's displacement or volume, and it says that where there is a plate,
do not exceed it. For a 12-foot boat 4.5 feet wide, the rule gives 3.6,
so no more than three people (arithmetic, rounding down).

**Loading.** The Coast Guard's loading rules: spread the load evenly from
bow to stern and from side to side, keep it low in the boat, keep
passengers seated, and secure gear so that it cannot shift.

## Why boats tip, and why they come back

Floating is half the story. A boat also has to stay the right way up,
and that depends on two points.

- **The centre of gravity** is the point where the whole weight of the
  boat and everything in it can be taken to act, straight down. It moves
  when weight moves: toward weight added, away from weight removed, and in
  the same direction as weight shifted.
- **The centre of buoyancy** is the centre of the underwater part of the
  hull, where the water's upward push can be taken to act. It moves when
  the shape of the underwater part changes.

Both definitions, and the rules for how the points move, are from the
Navy's damage control lesson on stability.

When a boat heels over, the side going down pushes more hull into the
water and the side coming up lifts some out, so the centre of buoyancy
moves toward the low side. If the centre of gravity stays low and near
the middle, the buoyancy now pushes up on the low side while the weight
pulls down nearer the middle, and the two together turn the boat back
upright. The Navy lesson calls the distance between those two lines of
force the righting arm. If the centre of gravity is too high, the pair
of forces turns the boat further over instead, and it capsizes; the
lesson calls that negative stability. Weight moved to one side tips a
stable boat toward that side and uses up the height of that side above
the water, so a smaller wave comes over it (our explanation).

Everything in the loading rules follows from those two points (our
reading):

- **Standing up** lifts what is often the heaviest thing in a small boat,
  you, and raises the centre of gravity, which is why the Coast Guard says
  standing "risks capsizing the boat." In a canoe it is the classic way
  to end up swimming.
- **Leaning out** over the side, to grab a mooring line, land a fish or
  pull someone in, moves the centre of gravity toward the low side just
  as that side goes down. Move to the middle and low first (general
  practice).
- **Loose gear and water in the bottom** slide toward whichever side is
  lower, moving the centre of gravity exactly the wrong way. The Coast
  Guard's list for bad weather includes keeping the bilges free of water
  and seating passengers on the bottom of the boat near the centre line.
- **Narrow, round or flat-bottomed boats** have less to work with. The
  Coast Guard names semi-V hulls, flat-bottom jon boats, canoes, kayaks
  and stand-up paddleboards as tending to be less stable and easily
  capsized.

Two more rules from the Coast Guard that are physics underneath:

- "Do not anchor from the stern as this could swamp the boat." The stern
  of a small boat is usually the lowest and squarest part, and a taut
  anchor line holds it down while the current or waves pile against it
  (our explanation).
- In rough water, "Head the boat into the waves at a 45-degree angle."
  Waves striking the side roll the boat; waves met on the bow at an
  angle are climbed (our explanation).

**The words people use.** The Coast Guard's statistics define capsizing
as the overturning of a vessel; swamping as water coming in over the
top of the sides, transom or decking; flooding as water coming in through
a hole, crack, fitting or drain plug; and sinking as the vessel losing
enough buoyancy after any of these to go entirely under. Among the 2024
statistics' top five primary incident types, swamping accounted for 262
incidents and 57 deaths, and a person falling overboard for 239
incidents and 138 deaths.

## If you capsize or fall in

The Coast Guard's advice for staying afloat, in order:

- Remain calm. Do not thrash, and do not try to take off clothing or
  footwear; thrashing wears you out and lets out the air that keeps you
  up.
- "If you are wearing a PFD, keep it on."
- Keep your knees bent, float on your back and paddle slowly to safety.

Then the cold. The Coast Guard describes cold water survival in three
phases: cold shock, a deep sudden gasp and fast breathing that passes in
about a minute; cold incapacitation, in which "in the next 10 minutes you
will lose the effective use of your fingers, arms, and legs for any
meaningful movement"; and hypothermia, with loss of consciousness in as
little as an hour depending on the water. The National Weather Service
gives the gasping and rapid breathing the first 2 to 3 minutes or more,
so do not count on one minute. The first minutes are for getting your
breathing under control with your face clear of the water, the next ten
or so are for getting yourself out or onto something, and after that it
is a matter of staying afloat and keeping heat in until help comes (our
reading of the phases together).

**Stay with the boat.** It is bigger and easier to see than a head in the
water (our reading), and it floats. The Coast Guard says a capsized vessel will likely
float on or just below the surface, and that "Smaller outboard powered
vessels, built after 1978, are designed to support you even if full of
water or capsized." Federal rules require monohull outboard boats under
20 feet, including those rated for oars or very small motors, to pass
flotation and stability tests while swamped in fresh, calm water (33 CFR
183, subparts G and H). Those rules do not cover canoes, kayaks,
sailboats or inflatable boats (33 CFR 183.201 and 183.301), so check how
yours behaves when full of water before you rely on it (general
practice).

**If you cannot get out,** the National Weather Service's guidance,
from the Coast Guard: "If you cannot swim to safety, conserve energy and
heat and await rescue." Take up the Heat Escape Lessening Position: "Draw
your knees to your chin and keep your legs together. Press both arms
against your side and keep your head out of the water." With others, "If
possible, form a huddle in the water with others to conserve body heat."

**Getting back in.** The Coast Guard asks whether you could climb back
into your boat if you fell out with all your gear on, and whether you
have practised. Practise in warm, shallow water near shore, wearing your
life jacket, with someone to help; a canoe or kayak that is full of
water is much harder to empty and climb back into than it looks
(general practice).

## Logs, driftwood and rafts: when wood floats and when it sinks

A log is the simplest thing that floats a person, and how well wood
floats depends on how much water is in it. The Forest Service's *Wood
Handbook* gives the tools.

The density of wood including its water is the density of water times
the wood's specific gravity times one plus its moisture content as a
fraction (equation 4-14). Above about 30 percent moisture the wood stops
shrinking, so for wet wood the specific gravity to use is the one based
on green volume. The handbook also gives the moisture content at which
wood will sink: 100 times (1 minus the green specific gravity), divided by
the green specific gravity (equation 4-4).

Using the handbook's figures for three Pacific Northwest woods (its
Tables 4-1 and 5-3b), the arithmetic is ours:

| Wood | Seasoned, 12% moisture | Green heartwood | Green sapwood |
|---|---|---|---|
| Douglas-fir (Coast) | about 540 kg per cubic metre | about 620 (37% moisture) | about 970 (115%) |
| Western redcedar | about 360 | about 490 (58%) | about 1,080 (249%): sinks |
| Red alder | about 460 | (no figure given) | about 730 (97%) |

Fresh water is about 1,000 kilograms per cubic metre (USGS). So:

- **Seasoned wood floats high.** A cubic metre of seasoned redcedar can
  hold up about 640 kilograms before it is completely under, and seasoned
  Douglas-fir about 460 (arithmetic).
- **Green wood floats low, and some sinks.** Green Douglas-fir sapwood is
  so close to the density of water that it barely floats. Green
  western redcedar sapwood, at its average moisture, is denser than water
  and sinks; the handbook's sinking point for redcedar is about 223
  percent moisture, and its green sapwood averages 249 (arithmetic).
- **Wood soaks up water, so floating logs can sink in time.** The
  handbook says that as wood takes up liquid water above fibre
  saturation, the air in its cells is replaced by water, and this can
  continue to the maximum moisture content. For all three woods above,
  the maximum moisture content from its equation 4-3 is higher than the
  moisture at which they sink (arithmetic), so a log left in the water
  long enough can settle to the bottom (our reading).

The handbook warns that moisture varies considerably within and between
trees, and even within a single board cut from one tree, so treat these
numbers as a guide, not a promise.

**What that means for a raft.** A raft is a floor held up by its own
buoyancy, with no sides. As an illustration (arithmetic): six seasoned
cedar logs, each 3 metres long and 20 centimetres across, hold about 0.57
cubic metres of wood weighing about 200 kilograms, and can hold up
roughly 360 kilograms more before they are pushed completely under. Two
adults and 20 kilograms of gear, 180 kilograms, would push the logs about
two thirds of the way under, leaving about 7 centimetres of each log
above flat water: the people aboard would be wet in the first wave. A
raft is slow, hard to steer, has nothing to stop you rolling off, and is
dangerous in current, wind or cold water; if you build one, use it only
on calm, shallow, warm water close to shore, with life jackets on
(general practice).

## Worked example: a canoe on Dyes Inlet

The project's canonical place is Silverdale, at the head of Dyes Inlet on
Puget Sound (see [Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa)).
Two adults want to paddle a canoe along the inlet's shore on a sunny
August afternoon, with a 10-year-old.

**The water.** Puget Sound does not get warm. NOAA's water temperature
record at Tacoma, the one [Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide) uses
for this basin, ran from 7.7 C (45.9 F) in February 2025 to 15.6 C (60.1
F) in July, and the monthly averages ran from 9.0 to 13.7 C (48 to 57
F). That is at or below the 50 to 60 F the National Weather Service says
can bring cold shock as severe as water at 35 F. A hot day makes no
difference to the water. The tide guide also explains that Tacoma is not
Dyes Inlet, and why that matters.

**The current.** Dyes Inlet fills and empties through Port Washington
Narrows, where NOAA predicts peak currents of about 4.5 knots for 2026
([Reading the Tide](/library#reading-the-tide)). Stay out of the narrows and
check the tide and current before setting out.

**The law, restated from Washington's statutes.** Every person on a
vessel in Washington must have a life jacket aboard that is serviceable,
the right size and readily accessible (RCW 79A.60.160). A child twelve
or under on a vessel under 19 feet long must wear one whenever the vessel
is under way, with narrow exceptions for an enclosed cabin and a few
other cases, so the 10-year-old wears one; under the federal rules, the
state's child rule replaces the federal one on its waters (33 CFR
175.25). Washington requires a boater education card to operate a
motor-driven boat of 15 horsepower or more (RCW 79A.60.640), so a canoe
needs none, but the Coast Guard's statistics are a good reason to take a
course anyway: where it was known, 69 percent of 2024's boating deaths
happened on boats whose operator had no boating safety instruction.

**The load.** A canoe carries no federal capacity plate, so use the
maker's rating. Two adults of 80 kilograms, a 35-kilogram child, 15
kilograms of gear and a 30-kilogram canoe come to 240 kilograms, so the
canoe settles until it displaces about 240 litres of water: a little
less in the salt water of the inlet (arithmetic and USGS). Put the
heaviest paddler in the stern, the child on the floor near the middle,
the gear low and tied in, and nobody stands (general practice, following
the Coast Guard's rules for a low, central, secured load).

**Wearing it.** Everyone wears a life jacket, not just the child. The
Coast Guard says to choose one by the user weight and chest size on the
label, that adult sizes may not work for children, and to try it on in
the water to make sure your airway stays clear. It sizes life jackets by
weight: a 35-kilogram child, about 77 pounds, is in its youth size, over
55 and up to 88 pounds (arithmetic). It also lists inflatable life
jackets in adult sizes only and says they are not appropriate for weak
swimmers or non-swimmers, so the child wears an inherently buoyant
(foam) jacket.

**Dressed for the water.** For water of 13 to 16 C, that means a wetsuit
or a drysuit, the first two of the National Weather Service's examples
of dressing for the water. At the very least, wear clothing that still
insulates when wet, not cotton (general practice; [Cold and
Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) explains why cotton is the
problem).

**The plan.** Check the forecast first, and if thunderstorms are
forecast, stay ashore; if you hear thunder while you are out, head for
the shore at once and get away from the water (the National Weather
Service, item 5 above). Tell someone ashore where you are paddling and
when you will be back. Stay close enough to shore to reach it quickly
(general practice). Clip a whistle to each life jacket, as the Coast
Guard suggests. Carry a phone in a waterproof bag and a handheld VHF
radio: the Coast Guard advises always carrying a way to send a distress
alert, and names the VHF radio as the most common. A paddle beyond the
near shore is not one to make without a VHF radio or a personal locator
beacon (item 5).

**If you tip over.** Keep your life jackets on, get hold of the canoe and
of each other, and keep your faces clear of the water until the gasping
passes, which can take a few minutes. Get as much of your bodies as you
can onto or into the canoe, call for help, and do not let anyone swim off
for shore alone.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Rescue** of anyone in the water is a call to 911, or on the water to
  the Coast Guard on VHF channel 16. Throw something that floats, but do
  not swim out ([What to Do When You Are
  Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost)).
- **Repairs to a hull, its flotation or its transom,** and any change to
  the motor or the load a boat was rated for, change the numbers on the
  plate. For a boat with no plate, the Coast Guard's own advice includes
  asking the manufacturer or hiring a naval architect or marine engineer
  (general practice beyond that).
- **Engines and fuel** bring fire and carbon monoxide; the Coast Guard's
  guide warns that carbon monoxide from engines and generators can kill
  people near the back of a boat and on the swim platform. That is beyond
  this guide.

## Traps

- **"I'm a strong swimmer."** The National Weather Service: cold shock
  creates a drowning risk "even for confident swimmers in calm waters."
- **"The life jackets are right here under the seat."** That is where
  they are when you drown. Wear them.
- **"It's a hot day."** The air is not the water. Dress for the water.
- **"Heavy clothes will drag me down."** They will not sink you straight
  away; air trapped in them helps you float while you keep still and do
  not thrash.
- **Standing up** to land a fish, to stretch, to change places. Stay low
  and move to the middle first.
- **"One more will fit."** The plate's limit already keeps most of the
  hull's reserve for waves and mistakes. Do not spend it.
- **Anchoring from the stern.** It can pull the stern under.
- **Swimming for shore.** Unless the shore is close and you are sure of
  reaching it, stay with the boat; it floats and it can be seen.
- **Going in after a dog or a dropped paddle.** Throw something that
  floats instead.

## How the game models it

The game has water you can stand on and boats you can build, but no
buoyancy and no boating.

- **The sea is a floor.** On the game's Earth, over the ocean, you stand
  on the sea surface, which rises and falls with the waves under your
  feet; you go under only by holding the sprint key (Shift by default).
  Under water you move where you look, at up to about 2.5 metres a second
  at the normal speed gear with fly mode off, and your depth holds steady
  until you swim up or down. You reach the Earth, and so its sea,
  through the Dev page's travel or Land on surface buttons, and both
  leave fly mode on, and the line under the compass says so ("FLY x1 -
  gravity on [F9 to hover]"). Until you press F9 twice, or untick Fly
  mode on the Dev page, you swim at 5 metres a second.
  Nothing about your own density,
  clothing or a life jacket changes how you float. The game's code calls
  this riding of the waves its buoyancy float, but it is a floor that
  follows the drawn waves, not a calculation of what floats.
- **No drowning and no cold water.** Your oxygen does not fall under
  water, so you cannot drown. With fly mode off, your body heat model
  counts rain and snow wetting your clothes; with it on, the weather does
  not reach your body at all. Either way, going into the sea does not wet
  your clothes or chill you.
- **Boats are items you cannot launch.** The item list has a Raft (20
  kilograms), a Canoe (25), a Rowboat (40), a Sailboat (200), a
  Motorboat (400) and a Submarine (5,000). The last two have no recipe.
  The raft is lashed by hand from six logs and four ropes. The canoe,
  rowboat and sailboat are built at a workbench, the canoe and rowboat at
  Shelter Building level 3 and the sailboat at level 4, and like other
  assembly at the workbench they need an Adjustable Wrench and a Flat
  Screwdriver in your backpack; tools in home storage do not count. A new
  character carries both tools but starts with no skill levels. Under the
  default Dev play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode) a craft uses up
  none of its parts and wears no tool, but the Craft button still wants
  the parts in your backpack or home storage and the tools in your
  backpack, and the skill levels still apply. The default home's store
  holds 60 planks and 10 ropes: enough for a canoe's or a rowboat's
  planks and rope, short of the sailboat's 72 and 12, and no logs, glue,
  nails, paint, bolts, cloth or steel. During development the Dev
  buttons "Dev: stock all materials" on the Crafting page and "Dev: max
  skills" on the Profile page supply the parts and the skill levels, but
  not the tools. The stock button fills your backpack, and the home's
  sawmill, which saws logs into planks by itself, takes its logs from
  home storage only, never from your backpack, so the stocked logs wait
  for the raft. A boat is too big for your backpack, so it goes into your
  home's storage when it is made, and the game makes one only aboard,
  with your own home on this ship; on a planet your home's storage is in
  orbit and does not count. No boat can be put on the water or sat in:
  none of the vehicles you can drive is a boat.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches, so a
  boat you made, and any skill levels you earned, are gone the next time
  you start the game.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: weight
and displacement, capacity, stability, capsizing, swamping, cold shock,
lightning and drowning. All of the lessons in this guide have to be
learned on real water, carefully.

## You own this when

- You wear a life jacket on the water, fitted and fastened, every time,
  and so does everyone with you.
- You can explain why a steel ship floats and a steel block sinks.
- You can work out roughly how much water a loaded boat displaces, and
  why the same boat floats higher in salt water.
- You can read a capacity plate, and you know how the law sets its
  numbers and why it leaves a margin.
- You can say what the centre of gravity and the centre of buoyancy are,
  and why standing up, leaning out and water in the bottom tip a boat.
- You load low, central and secured, and you never anchor from the stern.
- You dress for the water's temperature, not the air's.
- You head for shore at the first thunder, and you carry a whistle on
  each life jacket and a way to call for help that is not only a phone.
- If you capsize, you would keep your life jacket on, stay with the boat
  and get as much of yourself out of the water as you can.
- You would throw something that floats rather than go in after someone.
- You know why seasoned wood floats high and green wood floats low.
