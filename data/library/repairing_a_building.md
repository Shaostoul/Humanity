# Repairing a Building

A building rarely fails all at once. A seal cracks, a gutter clogs, a
pipe weeps behind a cupboard, and for months nothing seems wrong. Then
the floor goes soft, the ceiling stains, or a door stops closing, and
the repair is far bigger than it would have been. FEMA's guide to
snow on roofs says it in one sentence: "Small disrepairs can propagate
into much larger issues." The EPA's moisture guidance says the same of
roofs and ceilings: failing to correct their problems quickly can cause
additional damage, inside and out.

This guide is about catching those small failures, working out what
caused them, and fixing them in the right order, so that the repair
holds. Most of it is about water, because water is behind so much of
the damage a house suffers. It also covers the two hidden hazards that
make old-house repair different from new work, lead paint and asbestos,
and where your own work should stop.

It takes its rules from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the
Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the Federal
Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) and its Ready.gov site, and the
Forest Service's Wood Handbook. Those are United States government
publications, in the public domain. It also draws facts, restated in
our own words, from an inspection guide written for the Department of
Housing and Urban Development (HUD) by a private institute in 2000.
Where something is general practice or our own reasoning rather than a
published rule, the text says so.

Several guides sit beside this one. [Roofs and Keeping Water
Out](/library#roofs-and-keeping-water-out) is where most water problems
start. [Ventilation, Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould)
covers mould and cleaning it. [Rust, Rot and
Decay](/library#rust-rot-and-decay) explains rot and rust in depth. [Working
Out Why Something Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke) is the
method for diagnosis, and [Keeping Things
Working](/library#keeping-things-working) is the maintenance that keeps
repairs rare.

## First: the ways repair work hurts people

### Gas, electricity and water together

Some building failures are emergencies before they are repairs.
Ready.gov, FEMA's preparedness site, gives the rules for the three
utilities every household should be able to shut off:

- **Gas.** If you smell gas or hear a blowing or hissing noise, open a
  window and get everyone out quickly; turn off the gas at the outside
  main valve if you can, and call the gas company from a neighbour's
  home. If the gas is turned off for any reason, "only a qualified
  professional can turn it back on", and Ready.gov says never to try it
  yourself. Ask your gas company how your own meter shuts off before
  you need to know, but do not practise by actually turning it off.
- **Water.** Find the main shut-off valve where the water line enters
  the house, tag it, and make sure everyone knows where it is. Check
  that it shuts off completely; if it is rusted open or will not close
  all the way, Ready.gov says to replace it. The street valve in the box
  at the kerb is not the one to use: Ready.gov says it is extremely hard
  to turn and needs a special tool.
- **Electricity.** Know where the circuit panel is. Ready.gov's advice
  for a full shut-off is to switch off the individual circuits before
  the main. It also warns that electrical sparks can ignite leaking
  natural gas, which is why, if you smell gas, you leave rather than
  flipping switches or using anything electrical on the way out
  (general practice).

The danger to watch for in any leak is water reaching electricity.
Ready.gov's flood advice: do not touch electrical equipment if it is
wet or if you are standing in water, and turn off the electricity only
if it is safe to do so. If reaching the panel means walking through
water, do not; call the electric utility or the fire service (general
practice).

### Hidden wires and pipes

Every nail, screw, saw cut or drill hole into a wall, floor or ceiling
can meet something you cannot see. OSHA's construction rule (29 CFR
1926.416(a)(3)) requires that before work begins, it is found out by
asking, by looking, or by instruments whether any energised circuit,
"exposed or concealed", is where the work could touch it. At home that
means, before cutting or drilling into any wall, floor or ceiling:
finding the cables and pipes behind it, gas pipes included, with a
detector and by working out where they must run from the outlets,
switches and fixtures nearby, and switching off and testing the
circuits in that area. Not only near outlets and switches: cables run between outlets, switches
and fittings anywhere in a wall or ceiling, and one box can be fed by
more than one circuit, so test every wire you might touch, not just the
one you switched off (general practice). The detail, and the tools
for finding what is behind a surface, are in [Nails, Screws and
Bolts](/library#nails-screws-and-bolts); proving a circuit dead is in
[Working Out Why Something Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke).

### Lead paint in older homes

The EPA says homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint, "the
older your home, the more likely", and that any renovation, repair or
painting in such a home can easily create dangerous lead dust. HUD's
inspection guide puts rough dates on it: most homes built before 1940
used heavily leaded paint, and homes built after 1978 should be nearly
free of it. Children are the main concern, because they crawl, play on
floors and put things in their mouths.

The EPA's advice:

- **Hire, if you can.** For any renovation, repair or painting in a
  pre-1978 home, the EPA recommends a lead-safe certified contractor.
  Federal rules require any contractor paid for that work to be lead-safe
  certified, and they apply to you as well if you rent out all or part of
  the home, run child care in it, or renovate homes to sell.
- **If you do it yourself, assume lead** when you cannot find out the
  age of the house, and use the EPA's lead-safe practices: keep everyone
  else, especially children, out of the work area; take out or seal up
  furniture and belongings; cover the floor with plastic; turn off
  forced-air heating and cooling and cover its vents; mist surfaces
  before you scrape, sand, drill or cut, to keep dust down; consider a
  disposable N-100 respirator, coveralls, eye protection and gloves, as
  the EPA suggests; wash your hands and face whenever you stop; wash
  work clothes apart from the family's;
  never eat, drink or smoke in the work area; clean with a HEPA vacuum
  and wet wiping, every day and at the end.
- **The practices that are banned for contractors** (40 CFR 745.85) are
  the ones to avoid at home too (our reading): burning or torching
  painted surfaces; power sanding, grinding, planing or blasting
  without a shroud and a HEPA vacuum attachment; and a heat gun at 1,100
  degrees Fahrenheit or more.

### Asbestos

The EPA lists where asbestos may be found in a home: vermiculite attic
and wall insulation, vinyl floor tiles and the backing of sheet vinyl,
roofing and siding shingles, textured paint and patching compounds on
walls and ceilings, the walls and floors around wood stoves, pipes
covered with asbestos blanket or tape, and furnace and door gaskets.
You generally cannot tell by looking. The EPA's advice is to leave
material that is in good condition and will not be disturbed alone; if
in doubt, treat it as asbestos. Its don'ts: do not dust, sweep or
vacuum debris that may contain asbestos; do not saw, sand, scrape or
drill it; do not sand or level asbestos flooring; do not track it
through the house. Removal, major repair, and even sampling are for a
trained and accredited professional. **So before you cut, sand or
scrape anything in an older home, ask whether it could be on that
list** (general practice).

### Mould, dust and the ordinary hazards

Cleaning up mould safely, including the bleach rules, is in
[Ventilation, Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould). The EPA's
brief guide recommends an N-95 respirator, long gloves and goggles
without vent holes when cleaning mould, and says to see a health
professional first if you have health concerns. Ladders, power tools
and isolating machines are covered in [Keeping Things
Working](/library#keeping-things-working).

## Fix things in the right order

Many repairs fail because they were done in the wrong order:
the stain painted over while the leak goes on, the rotten board
replaced while the water still reaches it. This order is our
arrangement; each step rests on the sources named.

1. **Make it safe.** Gas, electricity near water, anything that looks
   like it might fall (the rules above).
2. **Stop the water.** Shut the valve, put a bucket under the drip,
   cover the hole. Until the water stops, everything else is wasted.
3. **Dry it, fast.** The EPA says that if wet materials are dried within
   24 to 48 hours of a leak, in most cases mould will not grow. Move
   things out, open up, run fans and a dehumidifier (general practice).
4. **Find the cause.** Not the place the damage shows, but where the
   water came from. The method is below.
5. **Fix the cause.** The EPA's rule for mould is the rule for all of
   it: clean up and fix the water problem, because if you clean up but
   do not fix the water problem, the mould will most likely come back.
6. **Repair the damage.** Only now, and with the right material.
7. **Write it down.** What failed, when, why and what you did. [Keeping
   Records](/library#keeping-records) gives the shape of a log.

## Finding the cause

### Where water in a house comes from

Water reaches the inside of a house in a few ways, and each has its
sign (our grouping, with the sources for each):

- **Rain through the roof or walls.** Stains after rain, worse after
  wind-driven rain. The EPA's moisture guide says rain is the largest
  source of water in buildings in most climates. Missing flashing at
  roof-wall joints and above windows is a common route; see [Roofs and
  Keeping Water Out](/library#roofs-and-keeping-water-out).
- **Ground water at the foundation.** A wet basement or crawlspace.
  HUD's guide calls a wet basement one of the most common problems in
  small houses, says most basement moisture comes from poor site
  drainage, and suggests rechecking the gutters and the grading first:
  the cause can be as simple as a clogged gutter.
- **Plumbing.** Leaks that do not care about the weather. The Wood
  Handbook warns that plumbing leaks can cause serious decay inside
  buildings, particularly when nobody finds them for a long time.
- **Condensation.** Water from the household's own air, on cold
  surfaces: windows, cold pipes, the corner behind a wardrobe. The EPA
  names it, and the cure is ventilation and humidity control; see
  [Ventilation, Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould).

Remember the EPA's rule that water runs along the undersides of joists
and pipes before it drips. The damage shows where the water falls, not
where it gets in, so follow the trail back and uphill.

### A leak you cannot see

The EPA's WaterSense programme gives two simple tests that find hidden
plumbing leaks:

- **The meter test.** Read the water meter, use no water at all for two
  hours, then read it again. If it has changed at all, there is
  probably a leak.
- **The toilet dye test.** Put a few drops of food colouring in the
  toilet tank and do not flush. If any colour shows up in the bowl after
  10 minutes, the tank is leaking into the bowl. Flush afterwards so the
  colour does not stain.

WaterSense also gives the scale of it: an average household's leaks can
waste more than 9,300 gallons a year, and a tap dripping once a second
wastes more than 3,000 gallons a year.

### Reading cracks

Cracks frighten people, and most are not dangerous; a few are. HUD's
inspection guide gives the distinctions, restated here:

- **Most wall and ceiling cracks** come from the building settling,
  framing bending or warping, or the small seasonal movements that come
  with changes in temperature and humidity.
- **Seasonal cracks open and close** through the year. They can be
  filled with a flexible, paintable sealant, but HUD's guide says they
  cannot be repaired effectively. In masonry, filling a cyclical crack
  with mortar holds it open and causes cracking somewhere else, so it
  takes a flexible sealant instead.
- **Settlement cracks** can be repaired once the movement has stopped,
  which is often the case.
- **A clean crack suggests recent movement**; a dirty or previously
  filled crack may be old and inactive.
- **Signs of more serious movement:** cracks that taper, wide at one end
  and hairline at the other, often running diagonally from corners of
  doors and windows; sloping or springy floors; bulging walls; doors and
  windows that start to stick. HUD's guide names these, and for severe
  settlement says to seek a structural or soils engineer.

To tell a moving crack from a still one, mark each end of it in pencil
with the date and look again over the following months (general
practice; HUD's guide notes that hand measurements of a crack are
possible but less precise than a gauge). A crack that keeps growing is
a reason to call an engineer (general practice).

FEMA's snow load guide lists several of the same signs, including doors
and windows that can no longer be opened or closed, cracking noises and
cracks in walls, as warnings that a roof is overloaded, and its answer
is to get everyone out and call a professional engineer. Signs that appear
suddenly, under a load like snow, are an emergency, not a repair
(our reading).

### Soft wood, insects and rot

The Wood Handbook explains why rot is a water problem first. Decay
fungi need the wood to be wetter than it gets from humid air: serious
decay needs a moisture content above about 30 percent, reached only
when rain, condensation or wet ground wets the wood. Air-dry wood is
usually under 20 percent, and "wood will not decay if it is kept air
dry, and decay already present from prior infection will not
progress." It also warns that decay weakens wood long before you can
see it, and that wood with visible decay should be assumed to have lost
a great deal of its strength.

To check suspect wood, push an awl or a small screwdriver into it:
sound wood resists, rotten wood lets the point sink in or splinters
across the grain in short pieces (general practice). The Handbook
suggests probing with a pick or a knife where termites are suspected,
because their galleries follow the grain and leave a sound-looking
shell. Its signs of subterranean termites are mud tubes, the shelter
tubes they build to travel through, and swarms of winged termites or
their shed wings, usually in early spring or autumn. Some carpenter
ants prefer wood that is already decaying, and the decay a leak causes
makes wood more attractive to them, which is why the Handbook says leaks
in porch roofs in particular should be repaired.

## Repairing, and what lasts

### Rot

The Wood Handbook's rule for repairs is the order of this whole guide in
one paragraph: in any repair made necessary by decay, correct the
moisture condition that caused it. If the source of moisture is
entirely removed, the weakened wood only needs replacing with dry
lumber. If the moisture cannot be corrected, replace all the infected
wood with preservative-treated wood or with heartwood of a naturally
decay-resistant species. Reading treated wood, and choosing it, is in
[Rust, Rot and Decay](/library#rust-rot-and-decay).

### Mould on what you are repairing

From the EPA's brief guide: scrub mould off hard surfaces with detergent
and water and dry them completely; porous materials such as ceiling
tiles and carpet may have to be thrown away; and "Do not paint or caulk
moldy surfaces." Paint over mould is likely to peel. If the mouldy area
is larger than about 10 square feet (roughly 3 feet by 3 feet), or there
has been a lot of water damage, the EPA points to its guidance for
larger clean-ups; if the water was sewage or otherwise contaminated,
call a professional experienced with that.

### Drips, runs and seals

The EPA's WaterSense advice for the commonest plumbing repairs:

- **A running or silently leaking toilet** is often the rubber flapper
  at the bottom of the tank, one of the commonest household leaks. It
  wears out, should be checked now and then, and should be
  replaced at least every five years. Take the old one, or a photo of
  it, to the hardware store so the new one fits.
- **A dripping tap** is often a worn washer or gasket.
- **A leaking showerhead** can sometimes be fixed by making the
  connection tight, with thread seal tape on the pipe's threads; a
  shower valve that drips when it is off is a job for an experienced
  handyperson or a
  licensed plumber.

Before taking anything apart, shut off the water to it, at its own
valve under the fixture if it has one, otherwise at the main (general
practice). Put the plug in the sink so small parts cannot go down the
drain (general practice).

## Worked example: the stain under the bathroom

A brown stain appears on the kitchen ceiling, under the upstairs
bathroom. It has not rained in a week.

1. **Safe?** The stain is dry to the touch and nowhere near the light
   fitting. If it were dripping on a fitting, the circuit would go off
   first (see above).
2. **Weather or not?** No rain, so think plumbing or condensation
   first.
3. **Test.** The meter creeps over two hours, so something is leaking.
   The toilet dye test shows colour in the bowl after ten minutes: the
   flapper leaks, which explains the meter, but that water goes into the
   bowl, not onto the ceiling. Then you notice the stain grows after
   showers.
4. **Look.** The caulk where the tub meets the tiles has a gap, and the
   grout below it is dark. Every shower sends water through that gap
   and down the side of the tub (a common pattern; general practice).
5. **Fix the cause.** Cut out the old caulk, clean, let the joint dry
   completely, and re-caulk it. Replace the flapper while you are
   there.
6. **Dry and check.** Leave the stained ceiling alone for a few weeks
   and watch it. If it stays dry, the cause is fixed.
7. **Repair the damage.** If the stain is only a stain, seal and paint
   it once it is dry. If the ceiling is soft, sagging or mouldy, it
   needs opening up and the wet material replacing. In a pre-1978 house,
   or with a textured ceiling that might contain asbestos, cutting it is
   the moment to stop and check (the lead and asbestos rules above).
8. **Write it down,** with the date of the new caulk.

The order matters most at step 7: painting the ceiling first would have
hidden the evidence while the leak went on.

## Worked example: the soft window sill

The bottom corner of a wooden window sill is soft; an awl sinks in a
quarter of an inch.

1. **Probe the extent.** Find where sound wood starts in every
   direction, including the trim below and the framing behind if you can
   reach it.
2. **Find the water.** Look above the window for head flashing (the
   metal or plastic lip that should shed water over the top of the
   frame; the Building America guide described in [Roofs and Keeping
   Water Out](/library#roofs-and-keeping-water-out) lists it among the things
   to look for), at the paint and caulk on the sill, and at whether the
   sill slopes outward so water runs off. Look at the gutter above too,
   and check for a missing kick-out flashing where a roof meets the wall
   above.
3. **Check the paint.** In a pre-1978 house, assume lead before you
   scrape (above).
4. **Fix the water first,** then repair: small, shallow decay can be cut
   out and filled; a sill rotted through is replaced with dry,
   decay-resistant or treated wood, primed on every face including the
   ends before it goes in (general practice, following the Wood
   Handbook's rule above).
5. **If rot reaches the framing** under the window, the job is bigger
   than a sill, and it is worth having a carpenter look before opening
   the wall (general practice).

## Worked example: a crack over the door

A diagonal crack runs up from the corner of a door frame in a plastered
wall.

1. **Look at it closely.** Is it clean or dirty? Is it the same width
   all along, or wider at one end?
2. **Mark it.** A pencil line across each end, with the date.
3. **Watch the door.** Does it start to stick? Is the floor near it
   sloping more than it used to?
4. **Decide.** If the crack does not grow over a year of seasons, it is
   probably settlement or seasonal movement, and can be filled; a
   flexible filler suits a crack that opens and closes (HUD's
   distinctions above). If it grows, tapers, or comes with a sticking
   door and a sloping floor, call a structural engineer before you
   patch it.

## If you rent

The EPA's brief guide on mould tells renters to report all plumbing
leaks and moisture problems to the building owner or manager
immediately, and says that if persistent water problems are not
addressed you may contact local, state or federal health or housing
authorities. The EPA also says that a landlord or property manager
doing work that disturbs paint in a pre-1978 home must follow the lead
renovation rule. Keep a dated record of what you reported (general
practice).

## Know where your own work stops

Many repairs are yours. Published advice draws the line in these places:

- **Gas:** if you shut it off, only a qualified professional turns it
  back on (Ready.gov). Gas piping and appliances are not DIY work
  (general practice).
- **Electrical panels and wiring:** see [Working Out Why Something
  Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke); beyond replacing a like
  device on a proven-dead circuit, call a licensed electrician (general
  practice).
- **Structure:** tapering or growing cracks, sloping floors, bulging
  walls, severe settlement: a structural or soils engineer (HUD's
  guide). An overloaded roof: leave the building and call an engineer
  (FEMA).
- **Asbestos:** sampling, repair and removal by a trained and accredited
  professional (EPA).
- **Lead:** in a pre-1978 home, the EPA recommends a lead-safe certified
  contractor for any renovation, repair or painting, and requires one
  for paid work.
- **Mould:** more than about 10 square feet, much water damage, or
  sewage (EPA).
- **Termites:** a pest control professional; the Wood Handbook says
  fumigation is only for licensed professional fumigators.
- **Roofs:** anything that needs walking on the roof (see [Roofs and
  Keeping Water Out](/library#roofs-and-keeping-water-out)).
- **Permits:** electrical, plumbing, gas, structural and roofing work
  often needs a permit and an inspection from your local building
  department. Ask before you start (general practice).

FEMA's snow guide makes a point that applies to all of these: an
attempt to repair something vital that is beyond your ability "can
cause additional damage". Knowing where to stop is part of the skill.

## Traps

- **Paint first, fix later.** Paint over a stain or over mould hides the
  evidence while the cause goes on.
- **Repairing where the damage shows.** Water travels; the stain is the
  end of the trail.
- **Replacing the rotten board and not the leak.** The new board rots
  too, unless the water stops or the new wood is chosen to resist it.
- **Sanding an old windowsill on a sunny afternoon.** In a pre-1978
  house that is lead dust, in the air and on the floor.
- **Mortar in a moving crack.** In masonry, mortar in a crack that opens
  and closes holds it open, and the wall cracks somewhere else (HUD).
- **"It has always done that."** A drip, a stain or a sticking door that
  has gone on for years is still telling you something.

## How the game models it

The game models almost none of this.

- **Nothing in a built home breaks.** Built pieces have a health
  figure in their data (a Wood Wall 150, a Stone Wall 350), but nothing
  in the current build lowers it: no weather, decay, leak, fire or
  disaster damages a wall, roof or floor, so there is nothing to repair.
  The figures are in `data/blueprints/basic.ron`; nothing in
  `src/systems/construction/` reduces them.
- **The only fix is to rebuild.** While holding a piece to place from
  the Crafting page, the Swing tool key (F by default) takes down the
  finished piece you are looking at, within 8 m, and returns all of its
  materials to your pack, and what does not fit there to storage, with
  a message saying so; a chest has to be emptied first. You can then
  build it again (`src/engine/build_place.rs`).
- **Building trains you.** Finishing a piece gives experience in the
  Shelter Building skill (`src/systems/construction/mod.rs`).
- **The rest of the home does not wear either,** as [Keeping Things
  Working](/library#keeping-things-working) and [Rust, Rot and
  Decay](/library#rust-rot-and-decay) describe: machines need no servicing,
  wood does not rot and metal does not rust.

**One setting changes what you keep.** While Settings > Gameplay >
"Start every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
during development, only your character (name, look and outfit) carries
between launches, so anything you built or took down starts the next
session as the default home has it. Turn the setting off to keep your
changes.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there
are no leaks, no stains, no cracks, no rot, no lead paint and no
asbestos. In a real house the repair work is
mostly diagnosis, finding where water came from and what it has done,
and the dangers are the ones you cannot see. The game has none of that
yet.

## You own this when

- You know where your gas, water and electricity shut off, you have
  tagged the water main, and you know what to do when you smell gas.
- You never touch electrical equipment that is wet or while standing
  in water.
- You find the cables and pipes behind a wall, floor or ceiling, and
  switch off and test the circuits there, before you cut or drill into
  it.
- You know your house's age, and in a pre-1978 house you treat old paint
  as lead paint and work lead-safe, or hire a lead-safe certified
  contractor.
- You leave anything that might be asbestos alone, and never sand, saw
  or scrape it.
- You fix things in order: safe, water stopped, dried within a day or
  two, cause found and fixed, then the damage, then the record.
- You can run the meter test and the toilet dye test.
- You can tell a seasonal crack from a moving one, and you know which
  signs mean an engineer.
- You check suspect wood with an awl, and you never replace rotten wood
  without first stopping the water that rotted it or, where you cannot,
  using treated or decay-resistant wood.
- You never paint or caulk over mould.
- You know which jobs need a professional or a permit, and you get them.
