# Making and Controlling Fire

Fire is the oldest tool people have and the one that turns on them most
easily. The Army's survival manual opens its chapter on firecraft with
the reason to learn it: "In many survival situations, the ability to
start a fire can make the difference between living and dying." Fire
warms you, cooks food, boils water and signals for help. The same
chapter lists what it does when it gets away from you: it can start
forest fires, destroy equipment, burn you, and poison you with carbon
monoxide when it is lit inside a shelter. And the Olympic National
Forest, in western Washington, puts the risk plainly: "Most wildfires in
Washington are caused by humans."

This guide is about lighting a small fire on purpose, keeping it small,
and putting it out so completely that it cannot come back. It covers what
a fire needs, whether you may light one today, choosing the spot, building
and lighting it, keeping it under control, putting it out until it is
cold, what to do when a fire gets away from you outdoors or indoors,
candles and other small flames at home, and burning yard waste. The
worked example is a back-yard fire pit in Silverdale, Washington, where
the simulation is set, and that example is where most of the local rules
are.

Several guides carry parts of this subject. What wood to burn and how dry
it must be, and felling trees, are in [Firewood](/library#firewood). Fire inside
a heater, stove or fireplace is in [Heating a Home
Safely](/library#heating-a-home-safely). Liquid fuels, and why none of them
belongs on a fire, are in [Fuels and Their
Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards) and [Fire Performance
Fuels](/library#fire-performance-fuels). Pan fires and oven fires are
in [Heat and How It Moves](/library#heat-and-how-it-moves), clothing that
catches fire in [Fibre, Cord and Textiles](/library#fibre-cord-and-textiles),
and burns in [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns). What must never be
burned is in [What Not to Compost, Burn or Pour
Away](/library#what-not-to-compost-burn-or-pour-away), and signal fires when you
are lost are in [What to Do When You Are Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost).

Its sources are the Army's survival manual, the US Forest Service, the US
Fire Administration (USFA), the Consumer Product Safety Commission
(CPSC), the National Weather Service, the Federal Emergency Management
Agency's Ready.gov and the Federal Register, all US federal and in the
public domain; and, for the local rules, the Puget Sound Clean Air
Agency, Kitsap County, Central Kitsap Fire & Rescue, the Washington State
Department of Natural Resources and one newspaper report, restated in our
own words. Where something is general practice or our own reading rather
than a published rule, the text says so.

## First: before you strike a match

1. **Is any fire allowed today?** There are two kinds of burn ban, and
   either can stop you. The Puget Sound Clean Air Agency, which covers
   Kitsap, King, Pierce and Snohomish counties, explains the difference:
   air quality burn bans, which it declares, usually come in autumn and
   winter when calm, cold weather traps smoke; fire safety burn bans come
   from the fire marshal, usually in summer, when dry weather makes
   wildfires likely, and can last for months (restated). On forest land,
   the land manager's restrictions apply too. If the National Weather
   Service has issued a Red Flag Warning, its advice is: "Be extremely
   careful with open flames." The CPSC's advice for back-yard fires is
   simply not to burn when the risk of wildfire is high.
2. **Is a fire allowed here?** Some places ban some kinds of outdoor fire
   all year, and every place limits what you may burn. The worked example
   below gives Silverdale's rules; find your own from your fire district
   and your clean air agency.
3. **Have the means to put it out before you light it.** The Forest
   Service: "Keep plenty of water and a shovel nearby for throwing dirt on
   the fire if it gets out of control." Central Kitsap Fire & Rescue's
   conditions ask for a shovel and either a garden hose connected to water
   or a bucket holding at least 5 gallons (restated). The CPSC adds a hose
   or a fire extinguisher.
4. **Someone stays with it.** The Forest Service: "Never leave a campfire
   unattended! Even a small breeze could quickly cause the fire to
   spread. Make sure a responsible adult is always in attendance."
5. **Small, and well clear of everything.** The USFA: "Build campfires at
   least 25 feet away from tents, shrubs and anything that can burn," and
   use outdoor fireplaces and fire pits "at least 10 feet away from your
   home or anything that can burn." Your local rule may be stricter, as
   Silverdale's is (below).
6. **No liquid fuel to start or revive it.** The CPSC says never to use
   gasoline to start or restart a fire, even if you think it has not
   caught, to use proper fire starters only before the fire is lit, and
   never to add liquid fuel after you have tried to light one (restated
   from a page summary). Why is in "Lighting it" below.
7. **Children watched, and lighters put away.** The USFA: "Store matches
   and lighters out of children's sight and reach," and "Closely watch
   children when the fire pit is in use." Ready.gov's advice is to teach
   children that fire is a tool, not a toy.

## What a fire needs

The survival manual explains what is actually burning. "Fuel (in a
nongaseous state) does not burn directly. When you apply heat to a fuel,
it produces a gas. This gas, combined with oxygen in the air, burns." A
log does not burn; the gas that heat drives out of it does. That is why
damp wood is hard to light (its heat goes into boiling water first, as
[Firewood](/library#firewood) explains) and why a thin shaving lights where a
thick log will not (our reading).

So a fire needs three things at once, which the manual calls the fire
triangle: "The three sides of the triangle represent air, heat, and fuel.
If you remove any of these, the fire will go out."

Every way of putting a fire out takes away one side (our reading of the
triangle):

- **Heat:** water cools what is burning below the point where it gives
  off gas. This is why a campfire is drowned and stirred, not just
  covered.
- **Air:** a lid on a pan, soil thrown on embers, a damper closed.
- **Fuel:** let the fire burn down; clear what is around it so it has
  nothing to spread to.

The side you choose matters. Water on wood and paper is right. Water on
a pan of burning fat or on burning liquid fuel spreads it: [Heat and How
It Moves](/library#heat-and-how-it-moves) explains the pan fire, and the CPSC's
kerosene heater warning says not to put water on that fire either. Nor
does water go on anything electrical that still has power: water
conducts electricity, which is why the extinguishers for plugged-in
equipment use an agent that does not (our reading of the USFA's classes
below), and why the CPSC keeps electric heaters away from water "To
prevent electrical shocks and electrocutions". For a burning appliance,
use an extinguisher only if the USFA's checklist (below, under "When it
gets away") allows it; otherwise get out and call 911.

Fire extinguishers are labelled for the kind of fire they suit. The USFA
lists five kinds: class A for ordinary materials like cloth, wood and
paper; class B for flammable liquids such as grease, gasoline, oil and
oil-based paint; class C for electrical equipment, using an agent that
does not conduct electricity; class D for burning metals; and class K
for cooking oils and fats in commercial kitchens. A multipurpose
extinguisher marked A-B-C covers most home fires.

## Choosing the spot

The survival manual: look for a dry spot protected from the wind, and in
woods or brush, "Clear a circle at least 1 meter in diameter so there is
little chance of the fire spreading." The Forest Service's list for a
campfire:

- use an existing fire ring rather than making a new one, and away from a
  campground, build your fire inside a ring of rocks;
- clear all vegetation from around the ring, including needles, leaves
  and sticks;
- choose "an open level spot away from trees, logs, stumps, overhanging
  branches, dense dry grass, and forest litter";
- keep the fire small.

One caution about the rocks, from the survival manual: "Do not use wet or
porous rocks as they may explode when heated." River stones and soaked
rocks are the ones to leave alone (our reading).

At home, a fire pit or outdoor fireplace goes on bare ground, gravel or
stone, well away from the house, the fence, the woodpile and anything
overhead (general practice, with the USFA's distances above). Keep the
woodpile itself away from the house, as [Firewood](/library#firewood) explains,
and away from the fire.

## Tinder, kindling and fuel

The survival manual names three materials, and a fire needs all three in
order:

- **Tinder** is "dry material that ignites with little heat". It must be
  absolutely dry, because it has to catch from a spark or a match: dry
  grass, shredded bark, wood shavings, paper (examples ours).
- **Kindling** is "readily combustible material that you add to the
  burning tinder": small dry sticks, from matchstick thickness up to about
  thumb thickness (general practice). It raises the fire's temperature so
  that it can light bigger wood.
- **Fuel** is "less combustible material that burns slowly and steadily
  once ignited": split, dry firewood, which [Firewood](/library#firewood)
  explains how to choose and season.

Burn only dry wood or charcoal on a fire for warmth or cooking. Never
garbage, plastic, or painted or treated wood, which [What Not to Compost,
Burn or Pour Away](/library#what-not-to-compost-burn-or-pour-away) explains,
and in Silverdale not even clean scrap lumber (below). On public land,
take only what the rules there allow; on the Caribou-Targhee National
Forest, for example, the Forest Service asks for dead and downed wood only
and never branches cut from living trees.

## Laying and lighting it

**Lay it before you light it.** Two lays from the survival manual cover
most needs:

- **Tepee:** tinder in the middle, kindling leaned over it in a cone.
  "Light the center." As it burns, the outer sticks fall inward and feed
  it, and the manual says it "burns well even with wet wood."
- **Lean-to:** a green stick pushed into the ground at about 30 degrees,
  its end pointed "in the direction of the wind" as the manual puts it,
  tinder deep underneath, kindling leaned against the stick.

Have more kindling and fuel within reach before you strike anything, so
you are not leaving a new flame to fetch it (general practice).

**Light it from the upwind side,** so the wind blows the flame into the
tinder and not into you (the survival manual says to light from upwind;
the reason is our reading).

**What to light it with.** Most of these are from the survival manual's
list; the lighter is our addition.

- **Matches,** waterproof, kept in a waterproof container with a striker.
- **A lighter,** the easiest of all to carry and use (general knowledge).
- **A metal match** (a ferrocerium rod): put a dry leaf under the tinder,
  hold the rod against it, and scrape a knife along the rod so the sparks
  fall into the tinder.
- **A lens,** only on bright, sunny days: hold the focused spot of
  sunlight on the tinder until it smoulders, then blow it gently into a
  flame.
- **Flint and steel:** strike a hard, sharp-edged rock with carbon steel;
  the manual notes that "stainless steel will not produce a good spark."
- **Friction,** the fire-plow and the bow and drill. The manual is honest
  about them: they take hard work and practice to succeed. Learn them on
  a dry day at home, not when you are cold and wet (general practice).

When tinder starts to smoulder, blow on it gently and steadily, then add
kindling a little at a time, smallest first (general practice).

**Never with gasoline or other liquid fuel.** Gasoline gives off vapour
that can ignite far below freezing ([Fuels and Their
Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards)), so pouring it on a fire, or on wood
that "didn't catch" but is still hot, puts a cloud of vapour next to a
flame. And pouring from a can over a flame invites what the CPSC calls
flame jetting: the flame lights the vapour inside the container, which
throws burning vapour and liquid out of its mouth. As the CPSC explained
in the Federal Register, "Flame jetting typically injures people other
than the person holding the container." Portable fuel containers made
after 12 July 2023 for sale in the United States must have a flame
mitigation device, such as a flame arrestor screen, to stop it (88 FR
2206), but no screen makes a fuel can a fire starter.

**Charcoal.** Charcoal lighter fluid goes on before the fire is lit and
never afterwards (CPSC, restated). Charcoal is for outdoors only, as
[Heating a Home Safely](/library#heating-a-home-safely) explains: it makes
carbon monoxide.

## Keeping it under control

- **Small.** Feed it a few pieces at a time; a fire is easier to keep
  than to rebuild, and easier to put out when it is small (general
  practice).
- **Watched, always,** by an adult who can put it out (Forest Service).
- **Sparks.** The USFA: "Use a metal screen over wood-burning fires to
  keep sparks from floating out". Watch where the smoke and sparks drift.
- **Wind.** If the wind picks up, let the fire die down and put it out
  (general practice); Central Kitsap Fire & Rescue's burn permits can be
  revoked for high winds (restated).
- **Clothes.** Many synthetic fabrics melt when they burn. The Library's
  fire performance guides advise snug natural fibres or fire-resistant
  clothing near flame, never synthetics; [Clothing for Fire
  Performance](/library#fire-performance-clothing) explains which
  fabrics melt and which do not.
- **People.** Keep children and pets back; the USFA asks for a 3 foot
  safe zone around a grill. Anyone drinking alcohol is not the person in
  charge of the fire (general practice).

## Putting it out, until it is cold

This is the part people rush. The Caribou-Targhee National Forest says
many of its wildfires start from campfires that were thought to be out
but were not. The Forest Service's Colville
National Forest, in Washington, gives the full procedure:

1. "First, drown the campfire with water!"
2. Mix the ashes and embers with soil, and scrape the hot embers off any
   partly burned sticks and logs.
3. Stir the embers once they are covered with water, and make sure
   everything is wet.
4. Feel the coals, embers and partly burned wood. "Everything (including
   the rock fire ring) should be cool to the touch." Feel under the rocks
   too.
5. When you think you are done, take an extra minute and add more water.
6. Check the whole campsite for sparks or embers, "because it only takes
   one to start a forest fire."

Its rule of thumb: "if it is too hot to touch, it is too hot to leave."

Feel for heat safely. The Caribou-Targhee page says to hold your hand
close to the ashes, not in them, first; if you feel any warmth, add water
and stir again. Touch the ashes only once no heat comes off them (our
reading of the two forests' advice together). The same page explains why
this matters: an ember can smoulder for hours and flare up when the wind
picks up.

Fire can also travel where you cannot see it. The survival manual, in
its chapter on cold regions, warns that "Fires have been known to burn
underground, resurfacing nearby." Roots and deep layers of needles and
rotted wood can carry it (our reading), which is one more reason for bare
mineral soil under a fire and a long, thorough soaking after it.

**Coals and ashes from a grill or fire pit.** The USFA: "Place the coals
from your grill in a metal can with a lid once they have cooled." Ash from
a stove or fireplace goes in a lidded metal can outside, at least 10 feet
from the house, as [Firewood](/library#firewood) explains.

## When it gets away

### Outdoors

Call 911 at once, from somewhere safe. The Forest Service's Pacific
Northwest Region: "If you see smoke, fire, or suspicious activities, note
the location as best you can and report it to authorities. Call 911." A
fire that is bigger than your water and your shovel can handle is not
yours to fight: get people away from it, upwind and out of its path, and
let the fire service come (general practice).

There is a cost too. Washington's Department of Natural Resources says
that if your fire escapes, state law makes you responsible for paying for
the people and equipment that fight it, and that breaking a burn
restriction is a crime (restated).

### Indoors

Indoors, the clock is the danger.

- **Fire is fast.** The USFA: "Residents could have less than 2 minutes
  to escape a home fire once the smoke alarm sounds." Ready.gov: "In less
  than 30 seconds a small flame can turn into a major fire."
- **Smoke kills before flames do.** The USFA: "Smoke and toxic gases kill
  more people than flames do." Ready.gov says to drop to the floor and
  crawl low under the smoke to your exit, because heavy smoke and
  poisonous gases collect first along the ceiling.
- **Use an extinguisher only if all of these are true.** The USFA's
  checklist: you have alerted others in the building; someone has called
  the fire department; you are physically able to use it ("Young children
  and older adults should not use fire extinguishers."); the fire is
  small and contained in a single object or surface, like a pan or a
  wastebasket; you are safe from the smoke; and you have a clear escape
  route. Otherwise, the USFA says, "alert others, leave the building, and
  call 911 from a mobile or neighbor's phone."
- **How to use one: PASS.** The USFA: pull the pin, with the nozzle
  pointing away from you; aim low, at the base of the fire; squeeze the
  lever slowly and evenly; sweep the nozzle from side to side. Your fire
  department can train you, the USFA notes, and practising with a real
  extinguisher before you need it is worth the trouble (our reading).
- **Doors.** Ready.gov: before opening a door, feel the doorknob and the
  door; if either is hot, or smoke is coming around it, leave it closed
  and use your second way out. Sleep with the bedroom door closed. If you
  cannot get out, close the door, cover the cracks and vents, call 911,
  and signal at a window with a light-coloured cloth or a flashlight.
- **Once out, stay out.** The USFA's escape pictograph says to get out
  and stay out, and go to your outside meeting place: "Never go back
  inside." Ready.gov: "If you can't get to someone needing assistance,
  leave the home and call 9-1-1 or the fire department. Tell the
  emergency operator where the person is located." If pets are trapped
  inside, tell firefighters right away (Ready.gov), and tell the fire
  department if anyone is still inside (the USFA's escape plan page).
  Firefighters have the gear to go in and you do not (our reading).
  After the fire, Ready.gov says to check with the fire department that
  your home is safe to enter, and not to reconnect the utilities
  yourself.
- **A plan before you need one.** The USFA: draw a map of your home with
  all doors and windows, find two ways out of every room, choose an
  outside meeting place in front of the home, and practise the drill with
  everyone, starting it by pushing the smoke alarm's test button.

### If clothes catch fire

Ready.gov: "stop, drop and roll", stopping at once, dropping to the
ground, covering your face with your hands, and rolling over and over
until the fire is out; if someone cannot, smother the flames with a
blanket or towel. Then get the burn under cool running water and get help,
as [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns) explains. [Fibre, Cord and
Textiles](/library#fibre-cord-and-textiles) has the CPSC's version, and its
warning to keep sleeves and dangling clothing away from the cooktop.

## Small flames at home

- **Candles.** The USFA: keep candles at least 12 inches from anything
  that burns, in stable holders where they cannot be knocked over; blow
  them out when you leave a room or go to bed; do not use lit candles in
  bedrooms, bathrooms or sleeping areas; use flashlights, not candles, in
  a power outage. It suggests battery-operated flameless candles instead.
- **Matches and lighters,** out of children's sight and reach (USFA), and
  Ready.gov adds, preferably in a locked cabinet.
- **Cooking.** Stand by the pan, and keep a lid at hand for a pan fire:
  [Heat and How It Moves](/library#heat-and-how-it-moves).
- **Grills.** The USFA: propane, charcoal and wood pellet grills only
  outdoors, well away from siding and deck railings and out from under
  eaves and branches, never on a porch or balcony, and never left alone
  while lit. The CPSC's gas grill fact sheet puts a number on it: at
  least 10 feet from the house or any building.
- **Fireplaces and stoves:** [Heating a Home
  Safely](/library#heating-a-home-safely) and [Firewood](/library#firewood).

## Burning yard waste and brush

As of October 2026, around Puget Sound this is the exception, not the
rule. Central Kitsap Fire & Rescue explains the history: the Department
of Ecology adopted the state's outdoor burning rule (chapter 173-425 of
the Washington Administrative Code) on 13 April 2000, the state's
revised Clean Air Act ended outdoor burning in urban growth areas and
other high-density areas after 31 December 2000, and land clearing
burning has not been allowed in Kitsap County since 1 September 2009
(all restated, read 4 October 2026). The Puget Sound Clean Air Agency
says that burning yard waste is never allowed in the urbanized areas of
its four counties, that land-clearing fires are banned across all four,
and that burning trash, using a burn barrel and smoking out your
neighbour are always illegal; it puts fines for illegal fires at
typically $1,000 or more, plus the cost of the fire department's
response (restated).

Where residential burning is still allowed, it needs a permit from the
local fire district, and comes with conditions. Central Kitsap Fire &
Rescue's, for its area outside the no-burn zone, include (restated, as
read on 4 October 2026): only
natural vegetation grown on that property, such as leaves, clippings and
prunings; a small pile no bigger than 4 by 4 by 3 feet, or a large one
no bigger than 10 by 10 by 7 feet; the pile at least 10 times its own
diameter from any structure (for a large pile, up to 50 feet) and at
least 25 feet from anything combustible; lit no earlier than sunrise and
not fed after sunset; one pile at a time; and the permit kept on site.

That may change. The Puget Sound Clean Air Agency is drafting a rule on
residential yard waste burning across its four counties: state law says
that where people have reasonable alternatives, such as yard waste
collection or a drop-off site, the burning must be prohibited, and the
agency has found such alternatives available across the whole region.
If the prohibition is adopted, residential yard waste burning would no
longer be allowed; until then the current rules stay in force (read 4
October 2026, restated). Check the agency's page before you plan a
permitted burn.

Where you may not burn, the alternatives the clean air agency names are
curbside yard waste collection, drop-off sites, and composting, brush
piles or mulching at home (restated); [Your First
Compost](/library#your-first-compost) covers composting.

## Worked example: a back-yard fire pit in Silverdale

The project's canonical place is Silverdale, on the Kitsap Peninsula (see
[Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa)). The details of the
evening are made up; the rules are real and were read on 4 October 2026.

**The summer.** On 16 June 2026 the Kitsap Daily News reported that the
county fire marshal's Stage 1 outdoor burn ban would start on 22 June:
burn permits suspended, and only cooking fires and small recreational
fires allowed, no more than 3 feet across, of charcoal or firewood, at
least 25 feet from structures and anything combustible, attended by an
adult with water or a shovel at hand, and soaked until cool to the touch
before being left (restated from the newspaper's report of the fire
marshal's announcement).

**The autumn.** On 29 September 2026 Kitsap County announced that the
fire marshal, with the county's fire chiefs, would lift the Stage 1 ban at
8 a.m. on 1 October, after rain, cooler weather and higher humidity; the
fire marshal still urged caution because heavier fuels remained dry
(restated).

**The household.** On a Saturday evening in mid October, a family in
Silverdale wants a fire in the back yard.

1. **Is it allowed today?** They check the county's burn ban status and
   the Puget Sound Clean Air Agency's air quality burn ban status: neither
   ban is in force. Either one would have ended the plan.
2. **Is it allowed here?** Central Kitsap Fire & Rescue's area includes a
   no-burn zone that covers all of Silverdale, where burn permits are not
   issued, so the pile of hedge clippings by the fence cannot be burned
   at all. Recreational fires need no permit and are allowed in that zone:
   cooking, camp and bonfires no bigger than 3 feet, at least 25 feet from
   any structure, of charcoal or dry firewood only, in a designated fire
   pit, and no milled lumber, treated or untreated (all restated). The
   clean air agency adds a height limit of 2 feet. Its list of fuels also
   includes manufactured firelogs, but Central Kitsap Fire & Rescue's
   list for this zone does not, so here it is charcoal or dry firewood
   only (our reading: follow the narrower rule).
3. **The spot.** Their steel fire pit sits on gravel 30 feet from the
   house, the shed and the fence, under open sky. The woodpile is on the
   far side of the yard.
4. **Ready to put it out.** A garden hose connected and turned on at the
   tap, a 5 gallon bucket of water and a shovel beside the pit, as Central
   Kitsap Fire & Rescue asks for outdoor fires.
5. **Fuel.** Dry split firewood from their covered stack, cedar kindling
   and a handful of shavings. The old pallet by the garage stays where it
   is: it is milled lumber.
6. **Lighting.** A small tepee, lit with a match from the upwind side. No
   lighter fluid, no gasoline.
7. **The evening.** One adult stays with it the whole time, adds a few
   pieces at a time, and keeps the children behind a line on the gravel.
   A spark screen sits over the pit.
8. **Out.** At ten, they let it burn down, drown it, stir, drown again,
   and feel with the back of a hand above the ashes, then the ashes
   themselves, until everything is cold. In the morning they check it once
   more (general practice).

## Where your own work stops

- **Any fire bigger than your water and your shovel:** 911, and get
  people away (general practice, with the Forest Service's call 911).
- **Any fire indoors that fails one item on the USFA's checklist:** leave,
  and call 911 from outside.
- **A chimney that roars or throws flames or sparks from the top:** get
  everyone out and call 911 (general practice).
- **Wildfire smoke or flames near you:** report it, and leave when you are
  told to; [The Hazards Your Place Actually
  Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) covers wildfire where the simulation
  is set.
- **Burning brush or yard waste:** only where it is allowed and with the
  permit; land clearing debris, never in Kitsap County.
- **Fireworks.** The United States Fire Administration: "The best way to
  stay safe from fireworks is to not use them." The Olympic National
  Forest says fireworks and explosives are always prohibited on national
  forest lands.

## Traps

- **"It's out, the smoke stopped."** Embers smoulder for hours under ash.
  Drown, stir, feel.
- **"A splash of gas will get it going."** It will, all at once, and
  flame jetting most often burns the people standing near the can, not
  the one holding it (CPSC).
- **"I'll only be gone a minute."** The Forest Service: never leave it.
- **"Those river rocks will make a nice ring."** Wet and porous rocks can
  burst when heated.
- **"It rained last week."** Kitsap's fire marshal lifted the 2026 ban and
  still warned that the heavier fuels were dry.
- **"A burn barrel keeps it contained."** In the Puget Sound Clean Air
  Agency's counties a burn barrel is always illegal.
- **"It's only scrap wood."** Pallets and offcuts are milled lumber, and
  painted or treated wood gives off poisons.
- **"Throw water on it."** Not on a pan of burning fat, not on burning
  fuel, and not on anything electrical that still has power.
- **"I'll just run back in for the dog."** Once out, stay out: tell the
  firefighters where the pet is (Ready.gov, USFA).
- **"I'll get the extinguisher" when the fire is already bigger than a
  wastebasket.** Get out and call 911.

## How the game models it

The game does not model fire yet, and it is worth saying exactly how far
that goes.

- **A skill nothing trains.** The skill list has Fire Making, up to level
  15, but nothing you do in the game earns it experience: no recipe,
  ability or quest names it (`data/skills/skills.csv`; a search of the
  game's data and code finds it nowhere else that counts). Only the
  Profile page's "Dev: max skills" button, shown only in the Dev play
  mode and not in Normal, where the game starts, raises it, along with
  every other skill (`src/gui/pages/profile.rs`,
  `src/systems/skills/mod.rs`).
- **A campfire you build.** The Abilities list has a Campfire ability,
  and for a new character it sits in the first slot of the hotbar, so
  the 1 key casts it. Casting it builds a campfire on the ground in front
  of you: a ring of 6 Raw Stone with 3 Wood Logs in it, taken from your
  backpack, for 15 energy. The Crafting page's structures list has the
  same Campfire to place by hand. It is built only outdoors, on a
  planet's ground: never aboard the ship, never under a roof you built
  or one still going up, and never where there is no air for it to
  burn, such as the Moon. Nor will the game build a roof over one,
  burning, gone out or still going up: it refuses the roof with the
  campfire's own reason, that under a roof its smoke would fill the
  shelter. Refused, it says why and uses nothing up. In the Normal play mode,
  where the game starts, you never leave the ship, so there it is always
  refused; only the Dev play mode's travel tools take you down to a
  planet's ground (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). Finished, it is lit, and its
  three logs are its fuel: each Wood Log burns 40 minutes of game time,
  the rate at which a campground fire ring the US Forest Service tested
  burned its wood in ordinary use (11 kg in 55 minutes). Press E at it
  to put another log from your backpack on; the ring holds four at a
  time. A log put on a fire that has gone out lights it again, with no
  match or tinder, which no real fire allows. Taken down, it gives back
  its stones and only the whole logs it has not burned. With Settings >
  Gameplay > "Keep growing while away" on, as it is at first, a campfire
  burns on while the game is closed: one left burning has burned down by
  the time you come back, and one still going up when you quit is
  finished while you are away and has burned from then on. It heals
  nothing (`data/abilities.csv`, `data/blueprints/basic.ron`,
  `src/systems/abilities.rs`, `src/systems/construction/fires.rs`,
  `src/save_load.rs`). The
  item list's Campfire Kit, "Tinder flint and starter bundle", and its
  decorative Candles and Handheld Torches do nothing (`data/items.csv`).
- **Charcoal without a fire.** The Make Charcoal recipe turns three wood
  logs into four Coal (the game's charcoal) in a kiln, and the home has a
  kiln in its forge room; recipes at the smelter, kiln and forge list
  charcoal among their ingredients (`data/recipes.csv`,
  `data/machines/home.ron`). In the Normal play mode, where the game
  starts, a craft from the Crafting page uses those ingredients up; in
  Creative, and in Dev if you choose it, the page still asks for them but
  uses none of them up. The home's smelter is different: it runs Smelt
  Iron by itself, and a machine that runs by itself always uses up real
  ingredients, its
  charcoal included, taken from home storage and never from your
  backpack (`src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`). No flame or smoke is involved
  either way.
- **Nothing catches fire, and nothing spreads.** A campfire burns its
  own logs and nothing more: it lights nothing near it, throws no sparks
  and makes no smoke, so nothing in the game catches fire or has to be
  put out. The home's backup generator burns its own fuel too, from its
  drum while it runs, but with no flame, smoke or exhaust ([Choosing and
  Running a Generator](/library#choosing-and-running-a-generator)). The code
  has a fire system that would let fires spread and do harm
  (`src/systems/fire.rs`), but nothing registers it. The Crafting page
  lists a Fire Extinguisher and a Smoke Detector, but once made neither
  does anything.
- **No burn bans, smoke or wind-blown sparks.** There are no fire
  restrictions, no smoke and no wildfire.
- **Warmth from a fire.** While a campfire burns, your body feels its
  heat the way the body heat model feels the sun's: as warmer
  surroundings, strongest close in and falling with the square of the
  distance. Standing 1.5 m from its centre on a clear, calm night at
  0 C, your surroundings feel like about 16 C instead of minus 11, and
  at 20 m the fire makes no difference. Half a metre from the ring, a
  person in everyday clothes keeps a normal core temperature through
  that freezing night and shivers too little for the game to show it;
  at 1.5 m they are shivering by morning, and far from it they shiver
  hard. A fire that has gone out warms nothing. Its warmth reaches you
  only in a straight line, as radiant heat does: anything built that
  stands between you and the fire, a wall, a roof, a shut door, even a
  window, which the game counts as a wall, stops all of it, and an open
  doorway lets it through. A real fire warms only the side of you that faces it;
  the game's body has one skin, so it spreads that warmth all round
  (`src/systems/construction/fires.rs`, `src/engine/survival_env.rs`,
  `src/systems/body_heat.rs`). Built shelter keeps wind and rain off your
  body, and a roof hides the cold night sky; [Heating a Home
  Safely](/library#heating-a-home-safely) describes the body heat model and its
  default settings.
- **No light.** A campfire gives no light at night: the game's lamps
  light the inside of the ship, and nothing yet lights a planet's ground.
  That is a known gap, recorded as BUG-153.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: wet
tinder, matches that blow out, sparks in dry grass, embers that wake up
overnight, smoke that blinds, burns, and a fire that does not stop where
you meant it to.

## You own this when

- You check both kinds of burn ban, and your local rules, before every
  fire.
- Water and a shovel are at the fire before the match is struck, and an
  adult stays with it until it is out.
- You can name the three sides of the fire triangle, and which one water,
  a lid and a cleared ring each take away.
- You can lay a tepee or a lean-to, light it from upwind, and feed it from
  tinder to kindling to fuel.
- You would never use gasoline or any liquid fuel to start or revive a
  fire, and you know what flame jetting is.
- You put a fire out by drowning, stirring and feeling until it is cold,
  and you test with your hand above the ashes before touching them.
- You know the USFA's extinguisher checklist and PASS, and that the
  default answer indoors is to get out and call 911.
- Your household has practised its fire escape plan, with two ways out of
  every room and a meeting place outside, and everyone knows that once
  out, nobody goes back in.
- You never put water on burning fat, burning fuel or anything electrical
  that still has power.
- You know what you may and may not burn where you live, and who issues
  permits there.
