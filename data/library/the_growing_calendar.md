# The Growing Calendar Where You Live

Every seed packet asks you the same question, and it is always the same
question: when do I start this.

Nobody can answer it for you from a zone map or a national chart,
because the answer is arithmetic done on three numbers that belong to
your own ground. Those numbers are the date your spring frosts stop,
the date your autumn frosts start, and how much daylight you get in
between. Get those three, and every planting date you will ever need
falls out of them by counting.

This guide teaches the counting. Silverdale, Washington is the worked
example throughout, because there is a real, sourced climate file
behind it, but the method is the point and the method is the same
everywhere. Swap in your own three numbers at the end and redo the
sums.

Two companion guides sit either side of this one.
[Starting Seeds](/library#starting-seeds) is how to raise a transplant once
you know when to sow it. [Your First Tomato](/library#your-first-tomato) is
how to grow the plant once it is in the ground. This guide is only
about when.

## The number most people plant to is a coin flip

Here is the single most valuable idea in this document, and it is worth
reading twice.

**The average last frost date is not a deadline. It is a coin flip.**

It is the 50th percentile: the date by which the last spring frost has
finished in half of all years. In the other half of years, frost comes
after it. Plant a tomato on your average last frost date every year for
a decade and, on the long-run odds, you lose the plant in five of those
ten years.

Silverdale's average last spring frost is **March 29**. Its
90th-percentile date is **April 24**, and that is the useful one: past
April 24, only about one year in ten still delivers a frost.

The average is the date nearly every planting chart prints. The
90th-percentile date is the one you plant to.

Here is the same idea as a table, for both ends of the year.

| Last spring frost, 32 F | Date | Read it as |
|---|---|---|
| Earliest tenth of years | March 5 | Spring frost is finished by now in 1 year in 10 |
| Median | March 29 | Finished by now in half of years, still coming in the other half |
| Latest tenth | April 24 | Finished by now in 9 years in 10 |

| First autumn frost, 32 F | Date | Read it as |
|---|---|---|
| Earliest tenth of years | October 26 | Frost has already hit by now in 1 year in 10 |
| Median | November 14 | Has hit by now in half of years |
| Latest tenth | December 1 | Has hit by now in 9 years in 10 |

Both tables use the same rule, applied to opposite ends of the season.
In spring you want the LATE date, because you are waiting for a danger
to finish. In autumn you want the EARLY date, because you are racing a
danger that is coming. Using the average at either end is betting your
crop on a coin.

So on the autumn side, October 26 is when your row cover needs to exist
and be reachable in the dark. Not November 14.

## Two thresholds, and why 32 F is the optimistic one

All the dates above are measured at 32 F, the freezing point of water,
which is the threshold weather services publish.

It is not the threshold your seedlings experience.

Official air temperature is measured in a shaded, ventilated
instrument shelter about head height above the ground. On a clear, calm
night, the ground and everything lying on it radiate heat to the open
sky and get colder than that shelter does, while cold air, being dense,
drains downhill and pools in low ground. A reading of 36 F at the
station can be a frozen seedling at soil level.

NOAA publishes a 36 F threshold for exactly this reason, and for
Silverdale it moves the calendar a long way:

| Threshold | Spring date, 1-in-10 risk | Autumn counterpart |
|---|---|---|
| 32 F | April 24 | October 26 |
| 36 F | May 13 | October 4 |

**May 13 is six weeks later than the March 29 average.** That gap is
the whole reason people lose tomatoes in May and blame themselves.

Now the three season lengths, which are the same climate described
three ways:

| Measured how | Length |
|---|---|
| Growing season as NOAA publishes it (32 F, 50th percentile) | 227 days |
| April 24 to October 26 (32 F, one-in-ten risk at each end) | 185 days |
| May 13 to October 4 (36 F, one-in-ten risk at each end) | 144 days |

The headline number is 227 days. The season you can plant frost-tender
crops into without gambling is 144. Plan to 227 and your autumn
tomatoes are a hope, not a plan.

In practice most growers work between the two middle dates: put tender
crops out from about April 24 with cover ready and used, or from about
May 13 with nothing. Both are defensible. Planting to March 29 is not.

## Counting backwards: the spring side

You now have an anchor date. Everything else is subtraction.

There are only two shapes of crop, and they count differently.

**Transplanted crops** (tomato, pepper, eggplant, broccoli, cabbage,
cauliflower, Brussels sprouts, melons) are raised indoors and moved
out. The anchor is the plant-out date, and you subtract the indoor
weeks to find the sowing date.

**Direct-sown crops** (beans, carrots, peas, radish, lettuce, spinach,
squash, corn) go into the ground as seed. Their anchor is not a frost
date at all. It is the day your soil reaches the temperature that crop
will germinate at, which is a different section below.

The general arithmetic, which is what you take away from this guide:

```
Transplanted crop:
    sow indoors   = plant-out date minus weeks indoors
    plant out     = your chosen frost-risk date
    first harvest = plant-out date plus days to maturity

Direct-sown crop:
    sow           = the day your soil reaches the crop's minimum
    first harvest = sowing date plus days to maturity
```

The indoor weeks per crop are listed in
[Starting Seeds](/library#starting-seeds) and are not repeated here.

## What "days to maturity" actually measures

This is the step where most people's arithmetic quietly goes wrong,
because the number on the packet does not mean what it looks like it
means.

For a set of crops it is counted **from the day you transplant**, not
from the day you sowed the seed. Maryland Extension names that set:
tomatoes, peppers, eggplants, muskmelons, watermelons, broccoli,
cabbage, cauliflower and Brussels sprouts. For every other annual crop
the number counts from direct seeding.

So a tomato packet reading "75 days" does not mean 75 days from the day
you sowed. It means 75 days after the transplant went into the ground,
and the six weeks you spent raising that transplant sit in front of it
and are not in the number at all. Real elapsed time from seed is closer
to 120 days.

Two sourced anchors to calibrate against: Maryland Extension gives
tomatoes 65 to 90 days from transplant, and sweet and hot peppers 70 to
85 days from transplanting, with bell peppers reaching mature green at
70 to 80 days and needing a further 2 to 3 weeks to colour up. Utah
State notes that a transplanted tomato matures roughly four weeks
before a tomato direct-seeded on the same ground, which is exactly the
head start you bought with the indoor weeks.

When a packet does not say which way it counts, assume transplanted
crops count from transplant and everything else counts from sowing.

## Soil temperature is what actually governs germination

A seed does not know the air temperature. It knows the temperature of
the soil touching it.

This is why planting by the calendar fails in a cold spring and why a
warm week in March does not mean anything. Below its minimum, a seed
does not germinate slowly. It sits, absorbs water, and rots. Maryland
Extension is blunt about the common casualties: beans, cucumbers and
melons need soil of at least 60 F to germinate.

A selection from the germination table published by university
extension services, in Fahrenheit:

| Crop | Minimum soil temp | Optimum |
|---|---|---|
| Lettuce | 35 | 75 |
| Onion | 35 | 75 |
| Spinach | 35 | 70 |
| Beet | 40 | 85 |
| Cabbage | 40 | 85 |
| Carrot | 40 | 80 |
| Pea | 40 | 75 |
| Radish | 40 | 85 |
| Corn | 50 | 95 |
| Tomato | 50 | 85 |
| Bean, snap | 60 | 80 |
| Cucumber | 60 | 95 |
| Pepper | 60 | 85 |
| Squash | 60 | 95 |

Read the minimum column as "will eventually come up" and the optimum
column as "comes up fast and evenly." The gap between them is large and
it is where most germination disappointment lives. Lettuce sown at
40 F does germinate; it just takes weeks, and weeks in cold wet soil is
plenty of time for something else to eat it.

The tool is a soil thermometer, pushed to seed depth, read in the
morning before the sun has warmed the surface, on several consecutive
days rather than one. **Rule of thumb:** soil lags air by weeks, and it
lags most where the ground is wet, shaded, north-facing or heavy, which
describes a great deal of Silverdale. Half the land here is Alderwood,
a thin gravelly mantle over dense glacial till that stays saturated
through the wet season (see the
[Silverdale gazetteer](/library#silverdale-wa) for the soils). Wet
soil warms slowly, because the water has to be heated too.

Do not guess this number. Measure it. It is the cheapest instrument in
gardening and it settles arguments the calendar cannot.

## Day length: the limit that does not care how warm the afternoon was

Temperature is the limit everyone watches. Daylight is the limit that
quietly decides what is possible, and it is completely immune to
weather. A February warm spell changes the temperature. It does not
change the fact that February has February's light.

Silverdale runs from 8.4 hours of daylight in midwinter to 16.0 hours
at midsummer, a swing of nearly eight hours:

| Month | Daylight | Month | Daylight |
|---|---|---|---|
| January | 8.9 h | July | 15.6 h |
| February | 10.3 h | August | 14.3 h |
| March | 11.9 h | September | 12.6 h |
| April | 13.7 h | October | 10.9 h |
| May | 15.2 h | November | 9.3 h |
| June | 16.0 h | December | 8.4 h |

Light is the plant's energy budget. A February seedling under 10 hours
of weak, low-angle light has roughly two thirds of the day length a May
seedling gets, at a much lower sun angle, and it grows accordingly: a
warm February week produces a pale, stretched plant rather than an
early one. This is the honest reason that starting too early does not
work, and it is why [Starting Seeds](/library#starting-seeds) calls starting
too early the beginner's classic mistake.

**Rule of thumb, widely used by winter growers and not something anyone
has published a measurement for:** below about 10 hours of daylight,
most vegetables effectively stop making new growth and simply hold
whatever size they had reached. Checked against the Naval Observatory's
sunrise and sunset tables for this point, Silverdale drops below ten
hours around **October 31** and climbs back above it around
**February 10**. That is about 102 days, a little over fourteen weeks,
in which a plant in the ground is stored, not growing.

That single fact reorganises the whole autumn plan, which is the next
section.

## The autumn side, which people plan worse

Almost everyone plans spring carefully and autumn by feel, and then
wonders why the late sowing never finished.

The reason is that spring and autumn are not symmetrical. A spring crop
grows into lengthening days and warming soil, so every week is better
than the last. An autumn crop grows into shortening days and cooling
soil, so every week is worse than the last. A crop sown late does not
simply finish late. It slows down, then holds, then sits there through
the winter at whatever size it reached.

Virginia Cooperative Extension publishes the standard correction for
this. Count backwards from your first frost date, and add up:

```
days from seed to transplant   (if you are raising your own)
+ days from transplant to harvest
+ the average harvest period
+ about 14 days  "fall factor", for slower growth in cooler, shorter days
+ about 14 days  "frost-tender factor", if frost kills this crop
= the number of days to count back from your first frost date
```

The fall factor is the piece people leave out. A 50-day crop sown in
August is not a 50-day crop; it is closer to a 64-day crop, and if it is
frost-tender you want it finished a fortnight before the frost rather
than on the day of it.

Which crops need the tender factor is not a judgement call. Virginia
Extension sorts them: tomato, summer squash, melon, eggplant, cucumber,
pepper and okra cannot withstand frost at all. Cabbage, cauliflower,
broccoli and Brussels sprouts withstand some cold and several taste
better after a frost. Kale, spinach, evergreen bunching onion, lettuce,
parsley, parsnip, carrots and salsify may survive all winter in the
ground.

That last group is the reason the autumn plan is worth doing well. Get
them to full size before daylight falls under ten hours, and the garden
becomes a refrigerator you harvest from all winter rather than a bed
you clear in October.

## A worked Silverdale calendar

Everything below is arithmetic on the numbers already given. The
working is shown in the last column so you can redo it with your own
dates. Days-to-maturity figures are from the extension sources listed
at the end; the number on your own packet takes priority over any of
them.

### Spring

Anchor for frost-tender crops: **May 13** (the 36 F one-in-ten date).
If you will cover plants on cold nights, you may use April 24 instead
and pull everything forward by 19 days.

| Crop | Sow | Plant out | First harvest | The arithmetic |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pea | March 1, direct | n/a | May 5 to May 20 | Soil min 40 F; 65 to 80 days from seed |
| Lettuce, leaf | March 15, direct | n/a | April 24 to May 24 | Soil min 35 F; 40 to 70 days from seed |
| Broccoli | Feb 25, indoors | April 1 | May 31 to June 10 | 5 weeks indoors; hardy, so the March 29 median is an acceptable plant-out; 60 to 70 days from transplant |
| Carrot | April 15, direct | n/a | June 14 to June 24 | Soil min 40 F; 60 to 70 days from seed |
| Tomato | April 1, indoors | May 13 | July 17 to Aug 11 | 6 weeks indoors: May 13 minus 42 days; 65 to 90 days from transplant |
| Pepper | March 11, indoors | May 13 | July 22 to Aug 6 | 9 weeks indoors: May 13 minus 63 days; 70 to 85 days from transplant |
| Bean, snap | When soil hits 60 F | n/a | 50 to 60 days later | Soil-gated, not date-gated. Measure it |
| Squash | When soil hits 60 F | n/a | per packet | Same gate as beans |

Note what the broccoli row does differently. Broccoli survives frost,
so it does not need the 36 F anchor and can go out around the median
date, weeks before the tomatoes. Frost tolerance, not the calendar, is
what decides which anchor a crop uses.

### Autumn

Anchor: **October 26**, the date by which one year in ten has already
frosted. The harvest period below is set at 14 days, which is a choice
rather than a published figure; lengthen it for a crop you pick from
over several weeks.

| Crop | Last useful sowing | The arithmetic |
|---|---|---|
| Lettuce, leaf | about July 30 | 60 days to maturity + 14 harvest + 14 fall factor = 88; Oct 26 minus 88 |
| Bean, snap | about July 21 | 55 + 14 + 14 + 14 tender factor = 97; Oct 26 minus 97 |
| Spinach, to overwinter | early September | Sized up before daylight drops under ten hours on Oct 31, then held |
| Garlic | late October to Nov 10 | Planted in autumn, harvested the following summer |

Those computed dates are worth checking against somebody who has
already done the counting for this region. WSU Extension's western
Washington guidance puts the last bush bean sowing at late July and the
last leaf lettuce at mid-August, and gives late October to November 10
for garlic and September for overwintering spinach. Our July 21 for
beans lands inside their late-July window, and our July 30 for lettuce
is a comfortable fortnight ahead of their last possible date. The
formula and the local expertise agree, which is the outcome you want
before trusting either.

Silverdale's autumn has one more constraint the frost dates do not
show. October brings 131 mm of rain and November 225 mm, against 21 mm
in July. A crop that needs to dry down, cure or ripen in the field is
fighting the rain long before it is fighting the frost.

## Season extension, briefly

Row cover, cloches, cold frames, low tunnels and greenhouses all do the
same thing: they trap heat and block wind, buying you a few degrees on
a cold night and a few weeks at each end of the season. Practically,
they let you use the April 24 anchor instead of the May 13 one in
spring, and they keep hardy crops in usable condition well past the
October 26 date in autumn.

What none of them can do is add daylight. A covered bed in December is
a warmer bed in December, not a growing one, which is why winter
growing is about holding crops that are already full-sized rather than
growing new ones. Build the calendar first; add cover to stretch it.
Cover is a separate skill and a separate guide.

## How to find YOUR dates

The Silverdale numbers above are nobody else's. Here is how to get your
own.

**1. Get your frost dates from NOAA.** The US Climate Normals are the
authority: a suite of products covering thousands of US stations,
currently the 1991 to 2020 period, regenerated every ten years. Use the
Climate Normals Quick Access tool at
`https://www.ncei.noaa.gov/access/us-climate-normals/`, pick a station
near you, and choose the **annual/seasonal** normals. The variables you
want are named like this:

| Variable | What it gives |
|---|---|
| `ANN-TMIN-PRBLST-T32FP10/50/90` | Last spring frost, 32 F, at the 10th, 50th and 90th percentiles |
| `ANN-TMIN-PRBFST-T32FP10/50/90` | First autumn frost, 32 F, same three percentiles |
| `ANN-TMIN-PRBGSL-T32FP50` | Growing season length |

Swap `T32F` for `T36F` to get the honest seedling threshold.

**2. Look at how far away your station actually is.** This is the step
people skip, and it is why Silverdale's own climate file names its
station in full rather than hiding it. The nearest station with a
complete temperature and freeze record is at Bremerton, 8.5 km
(5.3 miles) due south of Silverdale at 33.5 m elevation. About fifteen
stations sit closer, and every one of them is a precipitation-only site
with no temperature data at all, so they cannot answer a frost
question. A station a few miles away, at a different elevation, on a
different side of a hill, is a genuinely different climate.

The same file also records that its normals carry NOAA's
"Representative" completeness flag rather than "Standard": 20 of the 30
years have a near-complete daily record and four years have substantial
gaps that were filled with estimates. That does not make the numbers
wrong. It makes them a good estimate rather than an unbroken
measurement, and knowing which one you are holding is part of using it
well.

**3. Get your day length from the Naval Observatory.** Sunrise and
sunset for any point and date, free, at `https://aa.usno.navy.mil/`.
Ask for a fixed time zone offset with daylight saving disabled, or the
spring and autumn clock changes will corrupt your interval arithmetic.

**4. Then correct all of it for your own yard.** Published normals
describe a point in an instrument shelter, not your garden. How much
your ground differs is not small. Silverdale itself sits on a hardiness
zone boundary: the ZIP codes for Silverdale, west Bremerton and Poulsbo
are 8b, while two ZIPs nearer the Bremerton waterfront are 9a, and
shoreline sites on Dyes Inlet run about half a zone warmer than sites a
few hundred metres inland or uphill. Half a zone, over a few hundred
metres.

**Rule of thumb for reading your own ground:** cold air is dense and
flows downhill, so hollows and the uphill side of a solid fence collect
frost while a slope sheds it. Large water bodies moderate the sites
next to them. A south-facing wall radiates stored heat at night. A
minimum-maximum thermometer left in the bed you actually plant, read
for a couple of seasons, will beat any published table for that bed,
and it costs less than one tray of transplants.

## What the hardiness zone does not tell you

Silverdale is USDA hardiness zone 8b, and that fact is less useful for
this guide than it looks.

The USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map shows the average annual extreme
minimum winter temperature, drawn as 10 F zones and 5 F half zones, and
the 2023 edition uses the 1991 to 2020 period. What it answers is
whether a perennial will survive your winter. It is one number about
the coldest night of an average year.

It is not a statement about when your spring frosts end, when your
autumn frosts begin, how long your season is, or how warm your summer
gets. Two places can share a zone and have last-frost dates a month
apart. Use the zone to choose fruit trees and perennials; use the
frost percentiles to choose planting dates. USDA also notes that zone
boundaries shifted in 2023 partly because of better data and improved
interpolation rather than temperature change alone, which is another
reason not to load more meaning onto the zone than it carries.

## How you know it worked

- You can say your own 90th-percentile last frost date, not just your
  average, and you plant tender crops to the former.
- You own a soil thermometer and you use it before sowing beans.
- You know which of your crops count days to maturity from transplant
  and which count from sowing, and your sowing dates reflect the
  difference.
- Your autumn sowings include the fall factor, and the hardy ones
  reach full size before your daylight drops under ten hours.
- Your row cover exists and is reachable in the dark before your
  one-in-ten autumn frost date, not before your average one.

When those five are true you are no longer planting on faith or on
somebody else's regional chart. You are planting on arithmetic done
with your own numbers, and that arithmetic transfers unchanged to any
crop you ever grow and any place you ever move to.
