# Life in Soil

A spadeful of garden soil is mostly ground-up rock and empty space, as
[What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is) explains. What makes it fertile rather
than merely dirt is the part you cannot see: bacteria, fungi, tiny
animals that graze on them, the insects and mites that shred dead
leaves, and the earthworms that drag those leaves underground. Between
them they turn last year's dead plants into this year's plant food,
glue loose grains into crumbs that let air and water in, and hold
nutrients where roots can reach them.

You do not have to know their names to look after them. You do have to
know what they eat, what they need, and what kills them, because most
of the ordinary things a gardener does (digging, leaving ground bare,
spreading sawdust, spraying) either feeds that life or starves it. This
guide covers who lives in soil, what each group does for you, how to
see them at work with a spade and a jar, how to garden so they thrive,
and the small number of soil organisms that can make a person ill.

It takes its facts from the US Department of Agriculture: the Natural
Resources Conservation Service (NRCS), its Soil Quality Institute and
the Agricultural Research Service, and the Forest Service; its health
advice from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC); and
one rule from the federal organic regulations. All are United States
government publications, in the public domain. Where something is
general practice or our own reasoning rather than a published finding,
the text says so.

## Who lives there

NRCS describes the living part of soil as "an incredible diversity of
organisms": microscopic ones such as bacteria, fungi and algae, and
larger ones such as mites, springtails, spiders and beetles. Its
information sheet on rangeland soil says tons of them can live in a
single acre, and that they are concentrated in plant litter, the upper
few inches of soil, and along roots. They eat each other and each
other's leftovers, and together they make up what is called the soil
food web.

An NRCS soil technical note sorts them by the job they do, which is
the most useful way to think about them in a garden.

**Decomposers.** Bacteria, actinomycetes and fungi break down plant
residue, organic compounds and some pesticides. Bacteria usually work
on the easily broken-down compounds; fungi break down the more complex,
longer carbon chains; actinomycetes, which are filamentous bacteria,
sit somewhere between. NRCS's rangeland sheet adds that bacteria and
fungi make up the bulk of the living things in soil, and that they
finish the work of decomposition, storing the energy and nutrients of
what they eat in their own cells.

**Grazers and predators.** Protozoa and nematodes (microscopic worms)
graze on bacteria and fungi, which keeps both in check, and they
release plant-available nutrients as they eat. Larger predators,
including centipedes, spiders, some mites, ants and beetles, keep the
smaller animals in check in turn.

**Litter transformers.** Insects, spiders, mites, springtails,
centipedes and millipedes shred and eat dead leaves and other organic
matter. The technical note explains why this matters: shredding greatly
increases the surface area the decomposers can attack, and the material
in the animals' droppings is easier for microbes to get at. Ants,
termites and earthworms go further and change the soil itself by
chewing and burrowing through it.

**Mutualists.** Some fungi and bacteria live in partnership with plant
roots: mycorrhizal fungi, which act as an extension of the root system,
and nitrogen-fixing bacteria, which live in nodules on the roots of
peas, beans, clovers and their relatives. Both get paid in sugar from
the plant. They have their own sections below.

For a sense of numbers, NRCS's soil biology classroom activities (a
draft dated February 2001) say a single square yard of soil may contain
from 500 to 200,000 individual arthropods, the jointed-legged animals
such as mites, springtails and beetles, and that most of them live in
the top few centimetres.

## What they do for you

### They turn dead things into plant food

When a plant or an animal dies, the nutrients locked in its body are
no use to the next plant until something takes the body apart. NRCS
puts the alternative plainly: without the soil food web, the remains of
dead plants and animals would pile up on the surface and the nutrients
in them would stay out of reach.

Two words describe what happens next, and both are worth knowing
because they explain a common gardening surprise.

- **Mineralisation** is the release. As microbes digest organic matter
  they turn the nutrients in it into simple inorganic forms that roots
  can take up: ammonium, for instance, is the first form organic
  nitrogen is turned into (NRCS, Agricultural Waste Management Field
  Handbook). When bacteria and fungi die or are eaten, their nutrients
  are released too.
- **Immobilisation** is the borrowing. Microbes need nutrients to build
  their own bodies, and while they are feeding they hold those nutrients
  in their cells. NRCS's rangeland sheet points out the good side:
  nutrients such as nitrogen held in microbes are not washed out of the
  root zone. The bad side shows up when you feed them something rich in
  carbon and poor in nitrogen.

That bad side is the surprise. NRCS's field handbook explains that a
fresh load of organic material such as manure stimulates the soil's
microbes so much that they can consume more of the available nitrogen
than the decomposition releases, leaving less than the plants need for a
while.
Its soil biology activities put the chemistry in one line: material low
in nitrogen relative to carbon (a high carbon to nitrogen ratio) limits
the microbes, and woody material contains lignin and other compounds
that are hard to break down.

In the garden, that is why sawdust or wood chips dug into a vegetable
bed can leave the crop yellow and hungry for a season: the microbes
working on the wood are taking the nitrogen first. Keeping woody
material on the surface, on paths and around shrubs, or adding extra
nitrogen with it, avoids the problem (general practice; [Your First
Compost](/library#your-first-compost) explains how to balance high-carbon
"browns" with nitrogen-rich "greens").

### They build the crumbs

Good topsoil falls apart into rounded crumbs, and [What Soil
Is](/library#what-soil-is) explains why those crumbs matter for air, water and
roots. Soil life is what makes and keeps them.

The Soil Quality Test Kit Guide, published by the Agricultural Research
Service and NRCS in July 2001, lists what holds the larger crumbs
together: fungal threads (hyphae), fibrous roots, and sticky substances
called polysaccharides. It also says such crumbs form readily under
pasture and grass, with their dense, fibrous roots, and where organic
residues have been added. And it gives the
reason the work never ends: "Binding of soil particles must constantly
be renewed by biological processes." Stop feeding the life and the
crumbs slowly fall apart.

Earthworms add to it. The same guide says their burrowing improves how
fast water soaks in and their casts (droppings) improve the crumb
structure, though a fresh cast is soft and easily washed away; it
becomes stable as it ages, helped by organic matter, wetting and drying,
and fungal threads.

### They feed roots directly

Mycorrhizal fungi grow into or around plant roots and spread their
threads out into the soil. NRCS's rangeland sheet says they act like an
extended root system, improve a plant's ability to compete for water
and nutrients, and can connect the roots of different plants, passing
nutrients, especially nitrogen and phosphorus, between them. [How
Plants Work](/library#how-plants-work) describes the partnership from the
plant's side: the plant pays in sugar, the fungus pays in reach.

You cannot see mycorrhizae without a microscope, and you do not need
to. What matters is that they live on living roots. That is one reason
NRCS keeps returning to the same advice: keep something growing in the
soil for as much of the year as you can (more on that below).

### They pull nitrogen out of the air

Air is about four-fifths nitrogen gas, but plants cannot use nitrogen
in that form (common knowledge). A few kinds of bacteria can. NRCS
explains that nitrogen-fixing bacteria infect the roots of clover and
other legumes and form visible nodules, convert nitrogen from the air
in the soil into a form the plant can use, and that when the legume's
leaves and roots later die and decompose, the nitrogen level of the
surrounding soil rises and other plants grow better.

The partnership is choosy. An NRCS reference sheet on inoculating
legumes says it directly: "Specific species of Rhizobium bacteria are
required for specific species of legumes." It sorts the common forage
and cover-crop legumes into groups that share a bacterium: an alfalfa
group (alfalfa, black medic, the sweetclovers), a clover group
(crimson, red, white and alsike clover), a cowpea group (cowpea,
peanut, lespedeza), and a pea and vetch group (common vetch, hairy
vetch, winter pea and other true peas), with soybean, birdsfoot trefoil
and a few others needing their own. NRCS's Bismarck Plant Materials
Center adds the practical consequence: when you plant a legume in
ground for the first time, it is questionable whether the right
bacteria are already there, so an inoculant, a packet of the right
live bacteria, is often recommended.

The same NRCS sheet lists what reduces nitrogen fixation even when the
right bacteria are present: nutrient shortages in the soil, heavy
nitrogen fertilizer (it gives "generally over 20 lbs/acre"), weeds or
grass shading the legume, and low soil pH, drought or extreme heat. The
pH point is confirmed in NRCS's soil quality sheet on pH, which says
nitrogen fixation is inhibited by low pH.

**Worked example: peas in a new bed.** You are sowing garden peas where
no peas have grown in living memory.

1. Buy an inoculant whose label lists peas. NRCS's sheet says to check
   the label for the legume listing and the expiry date, and to use
   fresh inoculant.
2. Keep it cool and out of the sun until you use it.
3. On the day you sow, dampen the seed slightly so the powder sticks
   (NRCS suggests a commercial sticker or a little syrup or soft drink
   in water), mix in the amount the packet says, and sow straight away.
   If more than a day passes before sowing, inoculate again. Do not
   mix inoculated seed with fertilizer.
4. A month or two later, dig up one plant gently, wash the roots, and
   slice a nodule open. NRCS's sheet shows clover nodules and says "The
   large pink color of these clover root nodules indicates good nitrogen
   fixation." (Checking around flowering time is general practice.)
5. Do not feed the peas much nitrogen. Heavy nitrogen fertilizer is on
   NRCS's list of things that reduce fixation.

Garden beans of the common kind (kidney, pinto, navy, green beans) are
not on NRCS's list; buy an inoculant that names them on the label
rather than assuming another group's will work (our advice, following
NRCS's rule that the bacterium must match the legume).

### They clean up

NRCS's rangeland sheet notes that many soil organisms can break down
pesticides and other pollutants, and its classroom activities add that
soil organisms decompose would-be pollutants before they reach ground
water or surface water. That is not a licence to pour things on the
ground, but it is part of why living soil filters water better than
dead soil.

## What they need

Soil organisms want much the same things you would expect: food, water,
air, warmth, and a tolerable pH.

**Food.** Dead plant material is the obvious food, but NRCS's soil
health guidance stresses the living kind. Living roots maintain a zone
of intense microbial activity around them; in NRCS's words, "Microbial
food is exuded by plant roots to attract and feed microbes" that in turn
supply nutrients to the plant. Living roots, recently dead roots, crop
residues and soil organic matter all feed the food web. For earthworms,
the Soil Quality Test Kit Guide says the main food in most places is
litter on the surface, with dead roots and root exudates also
important.

**Water, but not too much.** NRCS's rangeland sheet says bacteria need
films of water in the soil's pores to be active, while fungi can work
in drier conditions; when soil is too dry, bacteria and fungi slow down
or shut down and protozoa form dormant cysts. The Test Kit Guide puts
the best balance at about 60 percent of the pore space filled with
water. Wetter than that, and air runs short: in waterlogged soil,
bacteria that work without oxygen can turn nitrate into nitrous oxide or
nitrogen gas, which escapes to the air (NRCS field handbook), so the
nitrogen is lost.

**Warmth.** The Test Kit Guide gives a general rule that biological
activity doubles with each 10 degrees Celsius rise in temperature (it
uses a steeper factor for soil colder than 15 C). NRCS's field handbook
says microbial activity almost stops below 41 F (5 C). NRCS's soil
biology activities put bacteria at their most active between 70 and
100 F, and an NRCS technical note gives 68 to 104 F. That is why cold spring soil releases so little
nitrogen from compost and organic matter, and why the same fertilizer
seems to do nothing until the soil warms ([How Plants
Work](/library#how-plants-work) has the plant's side of this).

**A tolerable pH.** NRCS's soil pH sheet gives ranges for the main
groups, adapted from published research:

| Group | Range it lives in | Best at |
|---|---|---|
| Bacteria | pH 5 to 9 | about 7 |
| Actinomycetes | pH 6.5 to 9.5 | about 8 |
| Fungi | pH 2 to 7 | about 5 |
| Protozoa | pH 5 to 8 | above 7 |

It adds that bacterial numbers and activity fall at low pH while fungi
cope with a wide range, and that at very acid or very alkaline pH the
breakdown of organic matter slows or stops. Earthworms are similar: the
Test Kit Guide says they are usually absent below pH 3.5, scarce
between 3.5 and 4.5, and that most live between pH 5.0 and 7.4. So
correcting a very acid soil ([Testing and Correcting
Soil](/library#testing-and-correcting-soil)) helps the soil life as well as
the crop.

## What harms them

NRCS sums up what soil health research has found in four principles
for farmland, and every one of them scales down to a garden bed:

1. Minimize disturbance.
2. Maximize soil cover.
3. Maximize biodiversity.
4. Maximize continuous living roots.

Here is what sits behind each, and what it means at home.

### Digging and tilling

NRCS: "Tillage can destroy soil organic matter and structure along with
the habitat that soil organisms need." The Test Kit Guide explains part
of the mechanism: loosening the soil lets in more oxygen, which speeds
up the breakdown of organic matter and releases its carbon as carbon
dioxide. The faster decomposition releases nutrients for a while,
which can look like a gain, but the organic matter is being spent (our
reading of those two statements).

Earthworms show the pattern clearly. An NRCS technical note says a
single tillage pass could kill as many as 25 percent of the earthworms,
that the population may recover within a few months as the survivors
breed, but that tillage weakens the soil's structure over time and
reduces the surface residue fungi and earthworms live on. The Test
Kit Guide agrees that earthworm numbers are often higher under no-till
than under conventional tillage, and that populations recover fairly
quickly, within a few years, if the disturbance is not repeated.

At home: dig when you must (to open a new bed, to bury something, to
lift potatoes), not by habit every spring. Loosen with a fork rather
than turning the soil over, and add compost on top for the worms to
take down (general practice, following NRCS's principle).

### Compaction

NRCS's rangeland sheet says compaction squeezes out the larger pores,
which removes habitat for nematodes and the larger soil animals, and
can make the soil airless, increasing the loss of nitrogen to the air.
[What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is) covers compaction in detail. The two
free rules are the same here: do not work soil when it is wet, and do
not walk on your growing beds.

### Bare ground

Bare soil is soil with no food coming in and nothing protecting it.
NRCS's rangeland sheet points out that erosion takes the surface layer
first, and that layer holds the greatest concentration of soil
organisms, organic matter and plant nutrients. NRCS's cropland guidance
lists the ways to keep soil covered: cover crops, annual and perennial
crops, crop residue and living mulches.

Cover crops earn their place twice. NRCS says they add living roots in
months when nothing else is growing, and that they trap excess nitrogen
that would otherwise wash into ground water or run off, releasing it
later to the next crop. Its examples include rye, wheat, oats, clovers
and other legumes, turnips and radishes.

### Chemicals

The NRCS rangeland sheet ranks the effects. Pesticides that kill
insects above ground can also kill beneficial insects in the soil;
herbicides and foliar insecticides used at the label rate have a
smaller effect on soil organisms; fungicides and soil fumigants have a
much greater one. The Test Kit Guide lists several groups of
fungicides, insecticides and nematicides as toxic to earthworms, and
adds that regular use of some nitrogen fertilizers (ammonium sulfate,
anhydrous ammonia and sulfate-coated urea) has been shown to reduce
earthworm numbers.

At home: use any pesticide only for a named problem, at the label rate,
and only where it is needed ([Pests and Disease](/library#pests-and-disease)
has the order to try things in, and the label rules).

### Fire and heat

Burning crop waste on a bed costs the life in it. NRCS's rangeland
sheet says fire can kill some soil organisms and reduce their food
supply while making some nutrients more available.
[What Not to Compost, Burn or Pour
Away](/library#what-not-to-compost-burn-or-pour-away) covers what should not
be burned at all.

## See it for yourself

You do not need a laboratory to watch soil life at work. A few simple
observations, most of them adapted from NRCS and Agricultural Research
Service procedures, will tell you more about your own ground than any
general guide.

### Count the earthworms

The Soil Quality Test Kit Guide's earthworm test, as written:

1. Do it in spring or autumn, when earthworms are most active.
2. Mark out a square one foot on each side and dig it out 12 inches
   deep. Dig the hole first and sort afterwards, and cut as few times as
   you can so as not to injure the worms.
3. Sort the soil on a pale sheet or tarp so the worms show up, and
   count them.
4. Optionally, to bring up the deep-burrowing kinds, level the bottom
   of the hole and pour in a mustard solution: 2 tablespoons of mustard
   powder in 2 litres of tap water. Deep burrowers should come up within
   five minutes. The guide says the solution should not harm the worms,
   and to rinse them in water before returning them to the soil.
5. Put the soil and the worms back.

How to read it: the guide says about 10 earthworms per square foot (100
per square metre) is generally considered a good population in
farmland, that cultivated ground rarely has more than 20 per square
foot, and that grassland can reach about 50. It also gives three
cautions worth keeping. Counts are patchy and change with the season,
so count several times in a season and compare averages year to year.
Avoid spots next to mulch or compost piles, where numbers may not be
typical of the bed. And hand digging misses some deep-burrowing and
fast-moving kinds.

Above all, a low count is not automatically bad news. The guide notes
that not all soils support earthworms, either because they were never
introduced or because conditions do not suit them. In the parts of the
northern United States that the ice-age glaciers covered, as a later
section explains, that is the natural state of the land.

### Bury a strip of cotton

From NRCS's soil biology classroom activities ("How fast does it rot?"):

1. Cut identical pieces of plain cotton fabric, or identical index
   cards.
2. Bury them a few inches deep, all at the same depth, in the places
   you want to compare: a vegetable bed, the lawn, a path, under mulch,
   a spot that is always bare. NRCS suggests a loose mesh bag around
   each piece so it is easy to find and lift. Mark each spot.
3. Dig them up after two or three weeks and compare how much is left.
   If you cannot see a difference, rebury them for another couple of
   weeks.

The fastest rot is where the soil life is busiest. NRCS's advice for
making the comparison fair: change one thing at a time (compare two
spots that differ only in moisture, or only in whether they are dug),
and do not use kitchen scraps, which attract animals.

### Drop a dry crumb into water

The Test Kit Guide's slake test measures how well soil crumbs hold
together when suddenly wetted, which it describes as a qualitative
indicator of soil biological activity. Its version uses a small sieve
basket in a tray of water and 16 crumbs; this is a simpler home version
of the same idea (our adaptation):

1. Take a few crumbs, each about 1 cm across, from the top inch of
   soil, and let them air-dry for a day.
2. Put one crumb on a small kitchen sieve or a piece of window screen
   and lower it gently into a bowl of water at about the same
   temperature as the soil.
3. Watch for five minutes. Then lift it out and lower it back four more
   times, taking about a second to lift it clear and a second to lower
   it (five dips in all, counting the first).

The guide rates crumbs from 0 to 6: a crumb that loses half its shape
within five seconds rates 1; one that keeps a quarter to three quarters
of its soil on the mesh after the five dips rates 5, and one that keeps
three quarters or more rates 6. It calls classes 0 to 3 relatively
unstable, class 4 somewhat stable but weak, and 5 and 6 relatively
stable.

Do it side by side with a crumb from a dug bed and one from under old
grass, and the difference the soil life makes is usually plain to see.

### Look at the roots and the surface

Two more observations need nothing but your eyes. Dig up a spent
legume and look for nodules on its roots, pink inside if they were
working. And look at the soil surface after rain: a hard crust that
seals over, with puddles, is a soil short of stable crumbs (general
practice; the Test Kit Guide treats crusts as a sign of reduced soil
function).

## Earthworms are not good everywhere

In a garden, earthworms are allies. In some forests they are invaders,
and a gardener can spread them without knowing.

The US Forest Service explains why. Native earthworms are absent from
the northern part of the United States because during the last ice age
the glaciers froze them all, and earthworms travel so slowly that they
have not recolonised the land the ice covered. NRCS's classroom
activities add that many of the earthworm species now common in the
United States, including the night crawler, were introduced from
Europe, and that in some deciduous forests with no earthworms the leaf
litter piles up, with some forest seeds needing that thick layer to
germinate.

The newest arrival is the Asian jumping worm. The National Invasive
Species Information Center (part of the US Department of Agriculture)
says it is native to east-central Asia, has been in the country for
more than a century (the Information Center says since the late 1800s,
the Forest Service that it began arriving in potted plants in the early
1900s), and has recently been invading natural habitats in the
Northeast and Midwest, possibly arriving through the plant trade or
with anglers' bait. The Forest Service describes the damage: the
worms eat the leaf litter that forms the top layer of forest soil,
which is home to a vast number of tiny animals and which many plants
need. And it says how they travel: in mulch, potting mixes and potted
plants; raking or blowing leaves can move the worms or their egg cases;
and leaves collected by a town and returned as compost can spread them.

The Forest Service's advice is short: "The best way to prevent future
invasions is to avoid moving earthworms around." In practice (general
practice, following that advice):

- Do not empty leftover bait worms onto the ground or into the woods.
- Buy compost, mulch and potted plants from sources you trust, and do
  not move soil or mulch from a garden known to have jumping worms.
- Keep a worm bin's worms in the bin.

## The few soil organisms that can hurt you

Almost everything living in garden soil is harmless to people. A few
things are not, and a beginner should know them, because the
precautions are cheap.

### Tetanus

CDC: "Spores of Clostridium tetani are common in the environment,
including soil, dust, and manure." The spores enter the body through
broken skin, usually through an injury, and are more likely to infect
puncture wounds (from a nail or a needle, for instance), wounds
contaminated with dirt, feces or saliva, and injuries with dead tissue
such as burns, crush injuries and frostbite. CDC calls tetanus an
uncommon but very serious disease that requires immediate treatment in
a hospital, and says about 1 in 10 cases in the United States are
fatal.

What to do:

- **Keep your tetanus shots up to date.** CDC recommends tetanus
  vaccination for everyone and says people who are not up to date with
  their 10-year booster are at increased risk. If you do not know when
  your last one was, ask your doctor or clinic before the gardening
  season, not after an injury.
- **Look after small wounds.** CDC's advice is to give first aid even
  to minor wounds and wash your hands with soap and water. [Bleeding
  and Wounds](/library#bleeding-and-wounds) covers cleaning a wound.
- **Get a puncture or a dirty wound seen.** CDC says to consult a
  healthcare provider if a wound is a puncture or is deep (stepping on
  a nail is its example), or contains dirt, soil, feces or saliva.
  Medicine and a vaccination may be needed after some wounds to prevent
  infection.
- **Wear gloves, cover cuts, and wash your hands after working soil**
  (general practice).

The first sign of tetanus is usually spasms of the jaw muscles, which
is where the name lockjaw comes from (CDC). That is an emergency.

### Fresh manure

Manure is full of life, and some of it can make people ill. The federal
rule for certified organic farms (7 CFR 205.203(c)) says plant and
animal materials must be managed so they do not contaminate crops,
soil or water with, among other things, "pathogenic organisms", that
is, germs. It allows raw manure on land growing food only if it is
worked into the soil at least 120 days before harvesting a crop whose
edible part touches the soil, or at least 90 days before harvesting one
whose edible part does not; otherwise the manure must be composted to a
set temperature first. Home gardens are not bound by that rule, but it
is a sensible benchmark (our suggestion). [Chickens and
Eggs](/library#chickens-and-eggs) gives the composting temperatures it sets.

### Fungal spores in dusty soil

Two fungi that live in soil can cause lung infections when their spores
are breathed in with dust.

- **Histoplasma.** CDC says it lives in soil, spreads in bird and bat
  droppings that mix into soil, and in the United States mainly lives
  in the central and eastern states. Most people who breathe the spores
  do not get sick. Activities CDC lists as raising the risk include
  cleaning chicken coops, gardening and yard work, and tearing down old
  buildings, and it says large amounts of bird or bat droppings should
  be cleaned up by professional companies that specialise in hazardous
  waste removal.
- **Coccidioides, which causes Valley fever.** CDC says it lives in
  soil in some areas of the southwestern United States and south-central
  Washington State, and that activities that disturb soil and dust, such
  as construction and windstorms, raise the risk. Its prevention advice
  includes avoiding very dusty areas, wearing a fitted N95 respirator if
  you cannot, staying inside with the windows closed during dust storms,
  and cleaning skin injuries well with soap and water. Its list also
  includes avoiding activities involving contact with soil, such as
  gardening, and using air filtration indoors.

Damping down dusty soil before you dig it, and wearing a well-fitted
mask when you cannot avoid dust, are general practice. CDC also says
both infections are often mistaken for ordinary pneumonia: if you
develop a cough and fever after a dusty job in an area where these
fungi live, tell your doctor what you were doing.

## A year of looking after soil life, in an ordinary vegetable garden

Put the principles together and the calendar is simple. Every step
below is NRCS's four principles applied to one bed; the specific
choices are general practice.

- **Autumn.** As each crop finishes, sow a cover crop (a winter cereal
  such as rye, or a mix with a legume) or cover the bed with a few
  inches of leaves or straw. Living roots over winter catch nitrogen
  that the autumn rain would otherwise wash down, and feed the soil life
  through the cool months (NRCS).
- **Winter.** Stay off the beds when they are wet.
- **Spring.** Cut the cover crop and leave it on the surface, or turn
  in only the top few inches. Spread finished compost on top. Count
  earthworms in one spot and write it down ([Keeping
  Records](/library#keeping-records)).
- **Planting.** Inoculate legumes going into new ground. Disturb only
  the soil you need to for each row or planting hole.
- **Summer.** Keep the soil covered between plants with mulch, and
  water so the soil stays moist but not soggy.
- **Every year.** Rotate crops so the same family is not in the same
  bed year after year, and keep a legume somewhere in the rotation
  (NRCS's biodiversity principle).

## Where your work stops

- **Laboratory tests.** NRCS and research laboratories measure soil
  respiration, microbial biomass and other biological indicators with
  equipment a household does not have. If you want numbers rather than
  observations, ask your state's cooperative extension service which
  soil laboratory offers them ([Testing and Correcting
  Soil](/library#testing-and-correcting-soil) explains how to choose a lab).
- **Large accumulations of bird or bat droppings,** in a barn, attic or
  under a roost: professional cleanup (CDC).
- **A deep, dirty or puncture wound, or any sign of tetanus:** a
  healthcare provider, at once (CDC).
- **Soil fumigants and pesticides you are unsure about:** the product
  label and your extension service before use.

## Traps

- **"Turning the soil is good for it."** It feels productive, and the
  flush of nutrients looks like a gain, but tillage destroys structure
  and habitat (NRCS). Dig when there is a reason.
- **"Wood chips are free fertilizer."** Dug into a bed, they borrow
  nitrogen from the crop while they rot (NRCS field handbook). Keep them
  on top, on paths, or under shrubs.
- **"No worms means dead soil."** Not in ground that never had them
  (Forest Service), and not in a dry or sandy soil where they do not
  thrive. Use the cotton strip and the slake test too.
- **"More earthworms are always better."** In a garden, generally yes.
  Released into a northern forest, they are an invasive species.
- **"Inoculant is optional."** For a legume new to that ground, NRCS
  says the right bacteria may well be absent. A packet is cheap.
- **Bare beds all winter.** No roots, no food, and the surface layer,
  where most of the life is, is the first to wash away.

## How the game models it

The game does not simulate soil organisms as such. It models some of
what they do, as numbers in the garden's soil.

- **Legumes fix their own nitrogen.** Nine crops in `data/plants.csv`
  carry a share of their nitrogen that they take from the air (the
  `n_fixed_pct` column): fava bean 67 percent, soybean 55, pea 54,
  lentil 54, chickpea 52, and the four common beans (bean, kidney bean,
  pinto bean and navy bean) 26. Such a crop draws only the rest of its
  nitrogen from its bed, and when it leaves the bed it leaves the fixed
  nitrogen in its roots and stems behind for the next crop, scaled to
  how much of its season it actually grew (`data/garden/nutrients.ron`).
- **Compost's nitrogen comes back slowly, the way decomposition
  releases it.** Seven percent of a bag of compost's nitrogen is
  available in its first season. The rest is banked in that bed as
  organic nitrogen and released at 3.5 percent of what remains in its
  second year and 2 percent a year after that, and only while a crop is
  growing there, so a bed composted year after year builds up
  (`data/garden/nutrients.ron`).
- **Woody mulch borrows nitrogen.** Sawdust and bark mulches each take
  20 grams of nitrogen from the bed under them for every kilogram of
  mulch, spread over the mulch's life (150 garden days for sawdust,
  1,095 for bark), and bank it to come back as the mulch rots, on
  compost's schedule (`data/garden/weeds.ron`). That is the
  immobilisation this guide describes. It runs while Settings >
  Gameplay > "Garden pests, diseases and weeds" is not Off; its default
  is Gentle.
- **One soil bacterium is a tool.** The pest controls include Bt, which
  the game describes as a soil bacterium that harms only the
  caterpillars that eat it (`data/garden/pests.ron`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there
are no earthworms, fungi, mycorrhizae, nematodes or protozoa; a legume
always fixes its full share, with no inoculant to buy and no effect
from the soil's pH; digging, walking on beds, bare soil and pesticides
do the soil no harm; nitrogen is never lost to leaching or
denitrification (the nutrient file says so); and nobody catches
tetanus.

**Two settings change what you see.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play
  mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), and in Dev, as in Creative,
  materials are free: compost, fertilizer, lime, sulfur and mulch cost
  nothing from your pack. Switch Play mode to Normal to garden with
  what you actually have.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay >
  "Start every session from the default home" is on, which is also the
  default during development, only your character (name, look and
  outfit) carries between launches. Your beds, and the slow nitrogen
  banked in them, start each session as the default home has them, so
  the years-long build-up from compost cannot be seen. Turn the setting
  off to keep your garden from one session to the next.

## You own this when

- You can name the four jobs soil life does (decomposing, grazing,
  shredding, partnering with roots) and give an example of each.
- You know why wood dug into a bed leaves a crop short of nitrogen, and
  where woody mulch belongs instead.
- You inoculate legumes going into new ground, and you can find and
  read a nodule.
- You dig only when there is a reason, stay off wet beds, and keep the
  soil covered and growing for as much of the year as you can.
- You have counted earthworms, buried a cotton strip and dropped a dry
  crumb in water, and you know what each result means and what it
  cannot tell you.
- You never release bait worms, and you know why earthworms are a
  problem in northern forests.
- Your tetanus booster is up to date, you garden in gloves, and you get
  a puncture or dirty wound seen.
- You treat fresh manure as something to compost or to keep well away
  from food you will soon harvest.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- That air is about four-fifths nitrogen and plants cannot use the gas
  directly is general knowledge.
- Keeping woody material on the surface or adding extra nitrogen with
  it, forking rather than turning soil, and adding compost on top are
  general practice following NRCS's findings.
- Checking nodules around flowering time, and buying an inoculant that
  names common beans rather than using another group's, are our advice.
- The home slake test with a kitchen sieve or window screen and a glass
  of water is our simplification of the Test Kit Guide's procedure.
- Reading a hard surface crust as a sign of weak crumbs is general
  practice; the Test Kit Guide discusses crusts reducing soil function.
- The household steps for not spreading jumping worms are our
  application of the Forest Service's advice.
- Gloves, covering cuts, washing hands after soil work, damping dusty
  soil and wearing a mask are general practice.
- Using the organic rule's 120 and 90 day manure intervals as a home
  benchmark is our suggestion; the rule binds only certified organic
  producers.
- The year's calendar is our arrangement of NRCS's four principles.
