# Helping Your Neighbours After a Disaster

The ground has stopped shaking, or the wind has dropped, or the water has
gone down. The power is out, the phones are busy, and the sirens are
somewhere else. In the first hours after a large disaster, the people who
can reach your street are mostly the people who already live on it.

FEMA's training for volunteers starts from that fact. Its Community
Emergency Response Team (CERT) manual says that damaged roads and broken
communications can keep emergency services out of the worst-hit areas,
so that for the first period after a disaster, often up to three days or
longer, households and neighbourhoods may have to rely on their own
resources for food, water and shelter. Washington's emergency management
division puts the same thing more bluntly: in a disaster, 911 and the fire,
police, ambulance and utility crews will be overwhelmed and unable to help
individuals straight away, and neighbours will likely be each other's
first responders. It also asks for more than three days: communities may
have to rely on each other for days to weeks, so every household should
keep at least two weeks of supplies at home (restated).

That is the good news and the warning together. The CERT manual tells why
the programme exists: in the response to the 1985 Mexico City earthquake,
which killed more than 10,000 people, "untrained volunteers saved 700
lives, but unfortunately, 100 volunteers died in the process." Neighbours
helping neighbours saves people. Neighbours rushing into collapsed
buildings die with them. This guide is about the difference: what a
street can safely do for itself in the first hours, in what order, and
where it must stop and wait for trained crews.

Most of it comes from FEMA's CERT Basic Training and its Ready.gov pages,
the CDC, the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration
(PHMSA) and the Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration
(SAMHSA), all United States government works in the public domain. The
worked example uses Washington State's neighbourhood guidance and Kitsap
County's CERT programme, which are restated in our own words. Where
something is general practice or our own reading, the text says so.

This guide builds on others. [The Hazards Your Place Actually
Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) is the one to read first: it covers
which disasters your place gets, how much warning each gives, getting a
household ready, and drop, cover and hold on. [First Aid Until Help
Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives) covers caring for one hurt
person; this guide is what changes when there are several and no ambulance
is coming. [Organising Shared Work](/library#organising-shared-work) explains the
incident command ideas a neighbourhood team borrows. Water is in [Storing
Water Safely](/library#storing-water-safely) and [Making Water Safe to
Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink), keeping clean is in [Handwashing and
Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene), and electrical dangers are in [Where
Your Electrical Work Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops).

## What this guide is, and what it is not

It is a plan for the first hours, for a street of ordinary people: what to
do in your own house first, how to meet and organise, how to check on
everyone, and how to deal with the gas, the water, small fires and the
injured until help arrives. It will help you be one of the people who make
things better.

It will not train you. Search and rescue, fire suppression and disaster
medicine are physical skills, and the place to learn them is CERT Basic
Training, which FEMA describes as teaching fire safety, light search and
rescue, team organisation and disaster medical operations. FEMA says CERT
became a national programme in 1993 and that there are now more than 3,200
local programmes and more than 600,000 people trained. Its free online
course IS-317, Introduction to CERT, is open to anyone. The CERT manual
adds that its course does not teach CPR, and encourages everyone taking it
to learn first aid and CPR as well.

Everything in this guide sits under one rule, which the CERT manual states
in its first unit and comes back to, in other words, in the units on
medical work, fire and rescue: "Remember that, at all times, a CERT
volunteer's first job is to stay safe."

## First, your own household

The CERT manual says volunteers should look after their own needs and those
of the people immediately around them first. That is not selfishness. A
household that has its own water, light, warmth and medicine is a
household that does not need rescuing, and its adults are free to help
(our reading; [The Hazards Your Place Actually
Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) makes the same point and lists what to
store).

When it happens:

1. **Your own people first.** Check everyone in the house, give first aid
   where needed, and if you are near the shore after an earthquake, go
   uphill on foot at once ([The Hazards Your Place Actually
   Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) explains why).
2. **Protect your head, hands and feet.** Broken glass, fallen brick and
   splintered wood are everywhere after a disaster. The CERT manual lists
   the protective equipment its volunteers must wear: a helmet, goggles,
   an N95 mask, work gloves and non-latex exam gloves, and sturdy shoes or
   boots. Keep shoes, gloves and a torch by the bed (general practice;
   Washington's earlier neighbourhood programme, below, suggested keeping
   sturdy shoes, a hard hat and leather gloves under the bed).
3. **Check the utilities, carefully.** If you smell gas or hear it
   hissing, get everyone out first, then shut the gas at the outside meter
   if you can do it safely, and call the gas company from a neighbour's
   house; nobody works a light switch or the electrical panel. Shut the
   water main to save the water already in the house. Switch the
   electricity off at the panel only in an electrical emergency, with no
   smell of gas, and never by wading through water. The next section has
   the detail.
4. **Light without flames.** Torches, not candles, in a house that may
   have a gas leak (the CERT manual says to use a flashlight, not a candle,
   when finding the gas valve).
5. **Tell the street how you are.** Washington's emergency management
   division offers a printable OK/HELP card, and its earlier neighbourhood
   programme said to put it on the front door or in a front window, so that
   neighbours can see from the street who needs help.
6. **Then go and help,** with a friend, and bring your first aid kit.

## The utilities

### Gas

Broken gas lines start fires after disasters: FEMA's Ready.gov says that
natural gas leaks and explosions cause a significant number of them.

**When to turn it off.** Ready.gov: "If you smell gas or hear a blowing or
hissing noise, open a window and get everyone out quickly. Turn off the
gas using the outside main valve if you can and call the gas company from
a neighbor's home." The CERT manual adds a third sign, the dials on the
meter showing gas flowing when every appliance is off.

**When not to.** Every source here makes the shutoff conditional on one of
those signs, so do not turn the gas off as a precaution when nothing is
wrong (our reading), and know why: the CERT manual says that once natural
gas has been shut off, only a trained technician can restore it, and
Ready.gov says never to try to turn it back on yourself. A street that
shuts every meter just in case may wait a long time for heat and hot water
(our reading).

**How.** The CERT manual: on a meter outside, the shutoff valve is on the
pipe coming out of the ground; turn it a quarter turn with a non-sparking
wrench. Washington's earlier neighbourhood programme adds the check: when
it is off, the valve lies across the pipe instead of along it. If the
meter is inside the building, the CERT manual says to shut the gas off
only when local authorities tell you to; if you smell gas inside,
everyone leaves and you
call 911 from outside, and you never try to reach an indoor meter while
gas may be in the air. Some meters have an automatic earthquake shutoff
fitted; only a qualified professional should reset one. Never go into the
basement of a burning building to turn off a utility.

Find your meter and its valve now, keep a wrench by it (the earlier
Washington programme recommended one right by the meter), and make sure
everyone in the house knows how; Ready.gov says to practise the procedure
without actually turning the gas off, and to ask your gas company for the
right procedure for your meter.

**If you smell gas in the street or in a building,** PHMSA's advice for a
pipeline leak applies (applying it to a house is our reading): leave on
foot at once and send others away, upwind if you can; do not try to find
the source; do not light anything or work any switch, and do not start a
car; do not use a phone near the leak, and do not ring doorbells to warn
people: knock with your hand instead; call 911 from a safe distance; and
never try to put out a gas fire.

### Water

Ready.gov says cracked lines may let polluted water into the house, and
that gravity can drain the water in the water heater and toilet tanks
unless you trap it by shutting the main valve where the water enters the
house. That is the house valve, not the street valve in the box at the
kerb, which Ready.gov says is very hard to turn and needs a special tool.
Find yours and label it. Keep the water off until the authorities say it is
safe to drink, and treat any water you use for drinking ([Making Water Safe
to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink)).

### Electricity

The CERT manual: switch off the individual breakers, or unscrew the fuses,
first, then the main switch; when the power comes back, the main switch
first, then the smaller circuits one at a time. Ready.gov gives one reason
for knowing how: electrical sparks can ignite leaking gas. But if you can
already smell gas, do not go to the panel at all. Working a breaker can
spark, PHMSA says not to work any switch near a leak, and the CERT manual
says not to go after the gas inside a building while gas may be in the
air: get out, and let the gas company and the fire department deal with
it (our reading of the three together). Never go into a flooded basement
or wade through water to reach the panel, because the water conducts
electricity (the CERT manual). Fallen power lines, and
anyone touching one, are in [Where Your Electrical Work
Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops): keep well away and call the
utility.

## Fire

The fire service may not come quickly to a street fire after a large
disaster, so a small fire put out early matters more than usual. It is
also where untrained people get hurt.

**Before you fight one, answer five questions.** The CERT manual: Are
there two ways out of the area if I try? Do I have the right extinguisher
for this fire? Is it big enough for the fire? Is the area free of other
dangers, such as hazardous materials or falling debris? Am I trained in
using an extinguisher? A "no" to any of them means you leave, closing doors
behind you to slow the fire, and call the fire department. Ready.gov notes
the US Fire Administration's advice to use an extinguisher only if you have
been trained, and says a household should have at least an ABC
extinguisher.

**The five-second rule.** The CERT manual: "As a rule, if you cannot put out
a fire in five seconds, it is already too big to handle, and you should
leave the premises immediately." A typical dry chemical extinguisher, it
says, empties in about 10 to 20 seconds and reaches 8 to 12 feet.

**How.** Test the extinguisher first, then P.A.S.S.: pull the pin, aim at
the base of the fire, squeeze the handle, and sweep across the base of the
fire until it is out (the CERT manual). Work in pairs: one person on the
extinguisher, the other behind with a hand on their shoulder, watching for
danger, and when you back out, never turn your back on the fire.

**The rest of the CERT manual's fire rules:**

- Never fight a fire alone.
- Look at a door before you touch it. If air is being sucked in under it,
  or smoke is seeping out at the top, do not touch it or open it.
- Feel a closed door with the back of your hand, from the bottom up, before
  you touch the handle. A hot door means fire behind it: do not open it.
- Stay low. The manual calls smoke inhalation the leading cause of
  fire-related deaths.
- Do not go into smoke-filled areas at all, and do not try a fire bigger
  than your extinguishers can handle.
- Afterwards, make sure it is really out, and tell the fire department
  even when you have put it out yourself.

**Hazardous materials.** The CERT manual says to treat a warning placard on
a building or vehicle as a stop sign. The only thing a volunteer should do
is move people who are downwind to a place uphill or upwind, and not go
into the building to get anyone out.

## Meet, and organise

After a large disaster, CERT volunteers who find no need around their own
homes go to an agreed staging area, and, the manual says, the first one to
arrive becomes the initial team leader, who can hand over to someone else
as others arrive. A street can do the same thing without any training at
all (our reading):

- **An agreed gathering place.** Somewhere covered and easy to see, chosen
  in advance. Washington's emergency management division suggests every
  community designate a meeting spot to share and distribute supplies.
- **One person in charge,** who stays at the gathering place, keeps track
  of who is out doing what, and does not wander off to dig. The CERT manual
  makes the command post the team leader's place, and says that a leader
  who must leave hands the role to someone there.
- **Small teams, always in pairs.** The CERT manual: each person takes
  direction from only one person; one leader for about five people, with
  an acceptable spread of two to seven; teams of two who buddy up, plus a
  runner to carry messages back.
- **Write it down.** The CERT manual: "The most important thing to do is
  to write down what happened." Who went where, what they found, who was
  hurt and where they are now, and the time. It is what you will hand to
  the first fire crew or police officer who arrives.

**The first jobs to hand out** (our list, following the CERT manual's
priorities and Washington's earlier neighbourhood programme):

1. **Check on the people most likely to need help:** neighbours who are
   old, disabled, living alone, or children who may be home on their own.
   This is why the list you make in advance matters.
2. **Walk the street** to every house showing HELP and every house showing
   no sign at all, because the people inside may be unable to put one up.
3. **Check the gas** at any house where it smells, hisses or nobody is
   home to say.
4. **Listen to the radio** for official information, with a battery or
   wind-up set ([The Hazards Your Place Actually
   Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) has NOAA Weather Radio).
5. **Report back** to the gathering place, and decide what to do next with
   what everyone has found.

**Size it up, again and again.** The CERT manual's nine steps of size-up
are worth carrying in your head: gather the facts; assess and communicate
the damage; consider what is likely to happen next; assess your own
situation (are you in danger, are you trained, do you have the tools);
establish priorities, with life safety first; make decisions; make a plan;
take action; and evaluate progress, then start again. Its point is that
size-up never stops: an aftershock, a change in the wind or a fire two
streets away changes the answer.

## Damaged buildings: the line you do not cross

This is where neighbours die trying to help. The CERT manual: "Previous
disasters have shown that the first response to trapped survivors
immediately after almost every disaster is by spontaneous, untrained, and
well-intentioned persons who rush to the site of a collapse in an attempt
to free the survivors." And: "Often, these spontaneous rescue efforts
result in serious injuries and compounded problems."

The CERT manual sorts damage into three levels, from a walk all the way
round the outside of the building:

- **Light damage:** superficial damage, broken windows, cracked or fallen
  plaster, minor damage to the contents. Volunteers may go in to find
  people, check them, treat airway problems, major bleeding and low body
  temperature, and keep assessing.
- **Moderate damage:** visible damage, decorative work fallen, many cracks
  in the walls, major damage to the contents, but the building still on its
  foundation. Volunteers go in only to find people and remove them quickly,
  limiting care on the spot to breathing, major bleeding and keeping them
  warm, with as few rescuers inside, for as short a time, as possible.
- **Heavy damage:** a partial or total collapse, tilting, obvious
  instability, the building off its foundation, heavy smoke or fire, a gas
  leak or hazardous materials inside, or rising or moving water. "CERT
  volunteers are not to enter a building with heavy damage under any
  circumstances." Their job there is to keep others out and warn them.

Those are the rules for trained CERT volunteers, wearing the helmet,
goggles, mask, gloves and boots listed above and working in pairs.
Without that training and equipment, treat anything worse than light
damage as a building you do not enter (our reading).

Three more rules from the same manual:

- **When in doubt, it is heavy.** If you are unsure whether damage is
  moderate or heavy, assume heavy.
- **Listen to the building.** A building that creaks or groans is unstable;
  do not go in. If you see collapsed floors or walls inside, leave at once.
- **Do not dig into a collapse.** If you hear someone trapped in a
  collapsed space, report it at once and mark the place for the
  professional rescuers, but do not try the rescue yourself: without the
  right equipment, you can bring down the rest of the collapse on them.

The manual gives the reason so much caution is needed: "The most frequent
causes of rescuer deaths are disorientation and secondary collapse." It
adds that the main problem for rescuers after working in a collapse is
breathing dust, so a dust mask is essential, and: "You should never attempt
to search an area where water is rising."

It also admits a hard limit: volunteers have no legal authority to stop
someone who insists on entering a dangerous building. They can only warn
them, and tell the team leader at once if people are known to be inside.

**Where rescue is safe,** the CERT manual's order is: get the walking
wounded and the people who can move out first, then the lightly trapped,
and keep rescuers and survivors safe throughout. It is usually best to let
someone who can walk get themselves out, with help. Moving debris is done
by a human chain passing it hand to hand, and lifting anything heavy off a
person uses a lever and cribbing, raised a little at a time with blocks
packed in as it rises: "Lift an inch; crib an inch." That is a skill to
practise in CERT training, not to work out on the day ([Force, Levers and
Mechanical Advantage](/library#force-levers-and-mechanical-advantage) explains
the lever).

## When several people are hurt

First aid for one person is in [First Aid Until Help
Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives). With several hurt and no
ambulance coming, the CERT manual changes how the work is done.

**The killers come first, for everyone, before anything else for anyone.**
The CERT manual names three life-threatening conditions after an injury:
an airway that is blocked, severe bleeding and low body temperature. Its
first priority in medical operations is to control bleeding and to position
people so that they can breathe. Check everyone quickly for those three,
and treat them, before spending long on any one person (our summary of the
CERT approach of doing the greatest good for the greatest number).

**Organise the care.** The CERT manual describes setting up a treatment
area at a safe distance from the damage, easy for vehicles to reach and with
room to grow, with injured people kept there until responders can take
them on; a separate area for anyone who has died; and someone recording
each person brought in and their condition. It adds that volunteers should
never pronounce anyone dead or move a body: note where they are and tell
the medical responders. It also asks that, once the threats to life are
treated, every injured person gets a careful head-to-toe check, even those
who seem fine, with anyone found unconscious or under heavy debris treated
as having a possible neck or back injury.

**Ask first.** The CERT manual: tell people who you are, and ask
permission before you treat someone who is awake (a parent's, for a child,
if you can). Respect their privacy about their injuries, and their
culture.

**Keep it clean.** The CERT manual's public health rules for a treatment
area: wash hands often, for at least 15 to 20 seconds, or use a sanitiser
with at least 60 percent alcohol; change gloves between patients; keep
dressings sealed until they are used; bag used gloves and dressings
separately from other rubbish; and bury human waste away from the area and
mark the spot. Why each of these matters is in [Handwashing and
Hygiene](/library#handwashing-and-hygiene).

**Keep calling 911.** Lines may be jammed for hours, but a call that gets
through brings help to the worst injuries first (general practice). FEMA's
Ready.gov notes that text messages need less bandwidth than calls, so they
"are able to be transmitted more reliably during situations when many people
are trying to use their mobile phones at the same time." Texting 911, where
it works, is covered in [What to Do When You Are
Lost](/library#what-to-do-when-you-are-lost). The CERT manual says that a team in
a badly hit area should send runners to its staging area to fetch help.

## People who need more help

Washington's emergency management division asks every community to make a
contact sheet and a list of the skills and resources its members are
willing to share, and its earlier Map Your Neighborhood programme had
neighbours mark on a street map every gas meter and propane tank and every
person who might need extra help: older people, people with a disability,
and children who might be home alone at certain times of day. Do it before
anything happens; the earlier programme had neighbours meet again after
about a year to update the map and the list.

Ready.gov's advice to people with disabilities and to older adults is the
other side of the same list: build a support network of family, friends
and neighbours, make sure at least one person in it has a key and knows
where your emergency supplies are, and, if you use powered medical
equipment, ask your power company about its list for priority restoration.

For an older person who is frightened and alone, the earlier Washington
programme suggested a neighbourhood care centre: one house in the street,
with few steps to climb, where people who need company and watching can be
brought rather than sent to a distant shelter (restated).

## The minds of the people around you, and your own

The CERT manual's unit on disaster psychology says that a disaster brings
out a wide range of reactions in survivors and helpers alike, from numbness
and disbelief to exhilaration at having survived, from trouble making
decisions to nightmares and loss of appetite, and that these are normal
reactions. It warns volunteers never to assume they know what someone is
thinking or feeling, and not to take a survivor's anger personally.

What helps, from the same unit:

- **Listen, protect, connect.** Listen to what people say and what they
  need, without pushing them to talk; protect them from further stress and
  help them feel safe; and connect them to their own family, friends or
  clergy.
- **Give the unhurt something to do.** The manual says that a focused job,
  such as organising supplies, helps people cope, and works especially well
  for people who are being disruptive.
- **Look after the helpers.** Rotate people between hard jobs and easy
  ones, make them rest, eat and drink, encourage them to talk with each
  other, and do not send someone home straight from a harrowing job: give
  them something quiet to do first so they can wind down.

SAMHSA's Disaster Distress Helpline, 1-800-985-5990, takes calls and texts
around the clock, in many languages, from anyone in emotional distress
after a disaster: survivors, their families, responders and volunteers.
For a crisis, [How Your Body Works](/library#how-your-body-works) has the 988
line.

## The days after

- **Generators and stoves.** After a power cut, some of the deaths come
  not from the disaster but from the fuel people burn to cope, as [The
  Hazards Your Place Actually Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) shows
  from a local windstorm. The CDC:
  generators only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents,
  never inside a home or garage even with the doors and windows open; never
  charcoal or a portable camp stove indoors; never a gas oven to heat the
  house.
- **Water and food.** [Storing Water Safely](/library#storing-water-safely),
  [Making Water Safe to Drink](/library#making-water-safe-to-drink), and the
  refrigerator and freezer rules in [The Hazards Your Place Actually
  Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live).
- **Do not head for someone else's disaster on your own.** FEMA: "Do not
  self-deploy to disaster areas." It says that showing up without
  coordinating with a trusted organisation can make things harder for
  responders and put you at risk, and that such organisations know where
  volunteers are needed and can train them. Recovery takes months or years,
  and volunteers are needed long after the news has moved on.
- **Give money, not things.** FEMA: cash to trusted relief organisations is
  usually the fastest and most effective help; give goods only when a
  relief organisation has asked for them and someone is ready to receive
  them.

## Worked example: a street in Silverdale at 3:30 in the morning

A made-up street of sixteen houses on the hill above Silverdale, and a deep
earthquake in February: the kind that [The Hazards Your Place Actually
Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) calls the one most likely in an
ordinary lifetime here. The street is well up from the water, so nobody
needs to run uphill; on the shore, that would come first.

**The first ten minutes, in each house.** The shaking stops. Power is out.
At number 3 you check your family, put on the shoes and gloves you keep by
the bed, take the torch, and go round the house. No gas smell; the meter
outside is still. You shut the water at the house valve in the garage. You
put the OK side of your card in the front window.

**The gathering place.** The street agreed on the Lees' carport last
spring, and you are the first there, so you are in charge until someone
better arrives. You write the time on the clipboard you keep in your
emergency bag. Within twenty minutes there are nine adults, you among
them. You send three pairs out: one to the four people on the list (the
man at number 11 who uses oxygen, the widow at number 7, the couple at
number 14 with a newborn, and the boy at number 2 whose mother works
nights); one to the gas meters; one to every house with a HELP sign or no
sign at all. One person stays with the radio and the clipboard, and one is
kept back as a runner.

**What comes back.**

- *Number 9:* the gas pair heard hissing at the meter. They got the family
  out of the house first and across the street, upwind; then one of them
  turned the valve a quarter turn so it lay across the pipe, and listened
  until the hissing stopped. Nobody went back in or touched a switch. They
  called the gas company and 911 from well up the street, away from the
  meter, kept everyone back, and wrote it down. Only the gas company will
  turn the gas back on.
- *Number 11:* his oxygen concentrator stopped with the power. He has a
  cylinder for about a day. He is on the power company's priority list;
  the radio person adds him to the message for the fire station.
- *Number 2:* the boy, eleven, is frightened but unhurt. He comes to the
  Lees' house, which the street chose as its care centre because it has
  no steps, and someone tries his mother's number by text.
- *Number 6:* the chimney has fallen through the roof and the front wall
  is cracked from end to end. The owner, cut on the forearm and bleeding
  heavily, is on the lawn. One of the pair presses hard on the wound with a
  folded towel and does not let go; the other fetches a kit. He is helped
  to the carport with the pressure kept on all the way, then kept flat and
  wrapped in blankets, and given nothing to drink ([First Aid Until Help
  Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives)).
- *Number 12:* the house has shifted on its foundation and the back half
  has dropped. A voice answers from inside. That is heavy damage: nobody
  goes in. The pair stay outside at a safe distance, call to her so she
  knows help is coming, keep everyone else back, and a runner goes for the
  fire station at the bottom of the hill, because 911 is busy.

**The next hour.** You keep the list: who is where, what was done, when.
The first fire engine reaches the street at 5:40, and you hand the captain
the clipboard: number 12 first, the cut arm second, number 11's oxygen
third, the gas off at number 9. Then you do what the captain asks.

The details of this street are invented. The steps are the ones in the
sections above.

## Where your own help stops

- **Any building with heavy damage,** whoever is inside. Keep people out,
  keep talking to anyone trapped, and send for the professionals.
- **Any collapsed space,** even in a building that looks only moderately
  damaged.
- **Fires bigger than five seconds and an extinguisher,** smoke-filled
  rooms, gas fires, and anything with a hazardous materials placard.
- **Turning the gas back on,** ever.
- **Fallen power lines,** and wading through water to reach a panel.
- **Medical care beyond first aid.** Stop the bleeding, open the airway,
  keep people warm, and get them to the professionals.
- **Other people's disaster areas,** unless a trusted organisation has
  asked for you.
- **Anything an official tells you not to do.** When the first fire crew
  or police officer arrives, they are in charge; the CERT manual has its
  team leaders take direction from the first fire or law enforcement
  official on the scene.

## Getting trained, locally

Kitsap County's Department of Emergency Management runs CERT Basic Training
free for anyone in the county over 18, with advance registration and
limited places; its autumn 2026 course ran on Thursday evenings and
Saturdays over just over three weeks, from 17 September to 3 October, and
every session had to be attended to finish. The county's CERT page is where
its courses are announced, and it says that members must register with
the county as volunteer emergency workers, before a disaster rather than
during one (restated). Washington's emergency
management division points to CERT for anyone who wants to help at the
state level, and lists neighbourhood programmes nearby such as Bainbridge
Prepares. If you live elsewhere, your county or city emergency management
office is the place to ask (general practice).

Washington's emergency management division no longer provides workbooks
for its earlier neighbourhood programme, Map Your Neighborhood, and points
instead to its own community planning resources: a printable brochure, a
skills and resources list, a contacts sheet, grid paper for a street map
and the OK/HELP card. The older programme's idea remains a good one: one
street at a time, small enough to walk round in an hour.

## Traps

- **"There's someone in there, we have to get them out."** Not through a
  heavily damaged building. Keep them talking and send for help.
- **"Turn off everyone's gas, just in case."** Only where it smells, hisses
  or the meter is running; it cannot be turned back on by you.
- **"I'll put it out myself."** Not alone, not without two ways out, and
  not if it is not out in five seconds.
- **"Everyone just start helping."** Without a gathering place, a leader,
  pairs and a list, the old man at number 7 is the one nobody checks.
- **"Light a candle."** Not where there may be gas.
- **"Run the generator in the garage."** Never, doors open or not.
- **"Let's drive down and help in the worst-hit town."** FEMA's rule is not
  to self-deploy.
- **"I'm fine."** Helpers have reactions too. Take your turn resting.

## How the game models it

The game does not model a disaster or a neighbourhood yet.

- **No disasters happen.** The game has a disaster system that lists
  earthquakes, floods, tsunamis, heat waves and more
  (`src/systems/disasters.rs`), but nothing registers it with the game, so
  it never runs.
- **The storms do no damage of their own.** The weather can bring a
  Thunderstorm, a Blizzard, a Tornado or a Meteor Shower
  (`data/weather/events.ron`), and near a Tornado the HUD can tell you to
  take cover, but the damage these events list is not applied by the
  game's code, so none of them hurts you or your home directly
  (`src/lib.rs`, `src/systems/weather_events.rs`). Only their wind reaches
  your body: a Thunderstorm or a Blizzard adds gusts to the wind your body
  heat feels, so a Blizzard can still chill you outdoors. The rain and snow
  they show are drawn on the screen and nothing more; it is the ordinary
  weather's rain and snow that wet you (`src/systems/weather.rs`,
  `src/systems/precipitation.rs`, `src/engine/survival_env.rs`).
- **No neighbours to check on.** Nobody in the game gets hurt, trapped or
  frightened in a way you can help with. The Leadership skill, which this
  topic belongs to, appears in the skill list (`data/skills/skills.csv`),
  but nothing in the game awards it experience; only the Profile page's
  "Dev: max skills" button, shown only in the Dev play mode and not in
  Normal, where the game starts, raises it, along with every other skill
  (`src/gui/pages/profile.rs`,
  `src/systems/skills/mod.rs`). The Medicine skill rises by crafting
  medical supplies, not by helping anyone ([First Aid Until Help
  Arrives](/library#first-aid-until-help-arrives) has the detail).
- **No gas, no fire to fight.** There are no gas meters to shut off and no
  buildings damaged by a disaster to judge. The game has a fire system
  that would spread fire and let it be put out (`src/systems/fire.rs`),
  but nothing registers it either. A Fire Extinguisher appears in the item
  list and in some recipes (`data/items.csv`, `data/recipes.csv`), but the
  Inventory's Use button does nothing with it (`src/gui/pages/inventory.rs`).
- **Starting from the default home.** Settings > Gameplay > "Start every
  session from the default home" is off by default, so your progress is
  kept between launches. With the setting on, only your character carries
  over between launches.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the
broken street, the phones that do not get through, the people who need
checking, and the plain fact that the first help after a disaster comes
from whoever lives next door. That part is practice you can only do in
your own street.

## You own this when

- Your household could manage on its own for two weeks, so that you are
  free to help.
- You keep shoes, gloves and a torch by the bed, and you would put your own
  people and protective gear first.
- You know where your gas meter and valve, water main and electrical panel
  are, and when, and when not, to use each.
- You know PHMSA's steps for a gas smell: leave on foot, no switches or
  flames, knock rather than ring, call from a safe distance.
- You would only fight a small fire with a buddy, two ways out and the
  right extinguisher, and leave if it is not out in five seconds.
- Your street has an agreed gathering place, a list of the people who may
  need extra help, and a map of its gas meters and propane tanks.
- You would go out only in pairs, and write down who went where, what they
  found and when.
- You know the three levels of building damage, and you would never enter
  a heavily damaged building or dig into a collapse.
- You would check everyone for a blocked airway, severe bleeding and cold
  before spending long on anyone, and you would ask before treating.
- You know the Disaster Distress Helpline, 1-800-985-5990, and that the
  helpers need looking after too.
- You would not self-deploy to someone else's disaster, and you would give
  money rather than goods.
- You have taken CERT training, or know where your county offers it.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

  from the CERT manual and the Washington programme.

  clipboard.

  the shutoff conditional; that shutting every meter leaves a street

  from Ready.gov, PHMSA and the CERT manual together.

  anything worse than light damage.
