# Pressure Canning

All fresh vegetables except most tomatoes, and every piece of meat,
poultry and fish, are low-acid foods in the USDA's list, and unless a
tested recipe has pickled or acidified them, a pressure canner is the
only tested way to can any of them at home. The CDC's page on home-canned
food gives the reason in one sentence: "Home-canned vegetables, which are
low-acid foods, are the most common cause of botulism outbreaks in the
United States."

This guide is about the pressure canner: which foods need it and why the
line falls where it does, what a pressure canner is (and what is not
one), how to follow a tested recipe step by step, why altitude and the
gauge matter, and what to do with a jar that did not seal or looks wrong.
The worked example is a canner load of green beans in Silverdale,
Washington, where the simulation is set.

Read [Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning) first. Jars, lids,
headspace, testing the seals, storing the jars and the signs of a bad jar
work the same way here, and this guide does not repeat them except where
pressure canning differs. [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains why low-acid food needs a
higher temperature than boiling water, why boiling for longer is no
substitute, and who gets botulism and from what. [Keeping What You
Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) has the step-by-step disposal of a bad
jar. Fish starts in [Fishing](/library#fishing), which covers keeping it cold
from the moment it is caught, and smoked fish is in [Salting, Curing and
Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking).

Its sources are the US Department of Agriculture's Complete Guide to Home
Canning and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), both US
federal and public domain, and, restated in our own words, the National
Center for Home Food Preservation (NCHFP) at the University of Georgia
and Washington State University Extension in Kitsap County. Where
something is general practice or our reading, the text says so.

## First, where pressure canning can hurt you

### A jar that was not heated enough, and looks fine

Most of what goes wrong in pressure canning leaves no mark on the jar.
The USDA guide lists the usual ways: "The food may spoil if you fail to
select the proper process times for specific altitudes, fail to exhaust
canners properly, process at lower pressure than specified, process for
fewer minutes than specified, or cool the canner with water." Add to that
list a dial gauge that reads high, which the USDA guide says causes
under-processing, and a recipe nobody tested. Each one can leave food in
which botulism can develop, and the jar looks exactly like a good one
([Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)). Every step below
closes one of those gaps.

### A pot of steam under pressure

A pressure canner holds steam well above the temperature of boiling
water. The USDA's rules for living with it:

- Let it cool down by itself. The USDA guide warns that forced cooling
  may result in unsafe food, and that cooling it with cold running water
  or opening the vent port before it is fully depressurized makes jars
  lose liquid and seals fail.
- When it has cooled, first make sure the pressure is gone: on a newer
  canner the vent lock (a small pin in the lid that rises with the
  pressure and locks the cover) has dropped, and on a dial-gauge canner
  the dial reads zero (USDA, NCHFP). Even then, tilt the weight slightly
  before lifting it off, and if steam still comes out, wait; never force
  the lid while the cover lock has not released (NCHFP). Then take off the
  weight, or open the petcock (the small valve that opens the vent on
  some older canners, USDA), wait 10 minutes, and unfasten the lid. In the
  USDA's words: "Lift the lid away from you so that the steam does not
  burn your face."
- Look after the gasket, the rubber ring that seals the lid. The USDA
  guide says nicked or dried gaskets let steam leak; clean them as the
  canner maker directs.
- Do not pick at or scratch the safety fuse, the metal insert or rubber
  plug in the lid that lets the pressure out if it climbs too high
  (USDA).
- Use only a canner with Underwriters Laboratories (UL) approval, and
  the USDA guide does not recommend pressures above 15 pounds per square
  inch with new canning equipment.
- Keep it on a burner it can take. NCHFP warns that a burner putting out
  too much heat can damage a canner, advises against an outdoor LP gas
  burner or a gas range burner over 12,000 BTU in general, and says to
  check the canner maker's directions. (BTU, British thermal units, is the
  burner's heat rating; the range's manual or maker can give you yours,
  general practice.)

Read your own canner's manual before you use it, and keep children and
pets out of the kitchen while it is on (general practice).

### A cooker that is not a canner

Electric multicookers and electric pressure cookers are not canners,
even when the front panel has a canning button. NCHFP does not recommend
using USDA canning processes in them: no USDA testing has tracked the
temperature inside jars in one, temperature rather than pressure is what
matters, and the food in one could cool too quickly and be
under-processed. The CDC says the same, button or no button.

Small stovetop pressure saucepans are not canners either. The USDA's
smallest canner is one that holds four quart jars standing upright on its
rack, and the USDA guide does not recommend pressure saucepans with
smaller capacities. The CDC's advice repeats it: "Use a recommended
pressure canner that holds at least four 1-quart jars sitting upright on
the rack."

## Which foods need a pressure canner

The USDA's list of low-acid foods, those with a pH above 4.6, is red
meats, seafood, poultry, milk, and all fresh vegetables except most
tomatoes; [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) quotes it and
explains it. So the pressure canner is for:

- vegetables: green beans, peas, corn, carrots, beets, potatoes, sweet
  potatoes, pumpkin and winter squash, greens, asparagus, mushrooms,
  peppers and the rest of the USDA's vegetable guide (a vegetable goes in
  a water bath only by a tested water-bath recipe, such as a pickle or
  the USDA's stewed rhubarb);
- all meat, poultry, fish and seafood;
- soups and other mixtures that contain any of them, unless a tested
  recipe has acidified the whole mixture.

Not every low-acid food has a tested process, and where there is no
tested process the answer is not to can it:

- **Purees and mashes.** The USDA: "Do not attempt to can pureed
  vegetables, red meats, or poultry meats, because proper processing times
  for pureed foods have not been determined for home use." Can them in
  pieces and puree them when you serve them; the USDA then has you bring
  the puree to a boil, simmer it for 10 minutes, cool and serve it, and
  keep what is left in the refrigerator, using it within 2 days for best
  quality. Pumpkin and winter squash go in as 1-inch cubes, with the
  guide's own caution: "Do not mash or
  puree."
- **Thickened food.** The USDA's caution for soups: "Do not add noodles or
  other pasta, rice, flour, cream, milk or other thickening agents to home
  canned soups." Dried beans or peas in a soup must be fully rehydrated
  first.
- **Milk and other dairy.** The USDA lists milk as a low-acid food, and the
  guide's index of foods has no process for it.
- **Anything not in a tested source**: the USDA guide, NCHFP, or your
  extension service.

Tomatoes are the special case. They can go in a pressure canner by a
tested recipe, but they still get their added acid ([Keeping What You
Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew), [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)). Acid foods can also be pressure
canned by tested times, but the USDA guide recommends the boiling-water
canner for them because it is faster.

## Why temperature, not pressure

The USDA guide puts it plainly: "Pressure does not destroy
microorganisms, but high temperatures applied for an adequate period of
time do kill microorganisms." Pressure is only how a canner gets its
water hotter than it can boil in an open pot. The USDA's target for
low-acid foods is 240 to 250 F, reached in a pressure canner at 10 to 15
pounds of gauge pressure; at sea level, a canner at 10.5 pounds gives
240 F inside. [Why Canning Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains
why that temperature matters, and why the same job in boiling water would
take 7 to 11 hours.

The USDA guide names two serious errors that lower the temperature in a
canner without changing what the gauge says:

1. **Altitude.** The temperature inside a canner is lower at higher
   altitudes, so canners there must run at the higher pressures the
   guide gives for each altitude range.
2. **Trapped air.** Air left in a canner lowers the temperature reached
   at a given pressure. The most air is trapped with raw-packed food in
   dial-gauge canners. Hence the rule: "To be safe, all types of pressure
   canners must be vented 10 minutes before they are pressurized."

That is why venting is not optional, and why the pressure in a recipe
depends on where you live.

## What you need

### The canner

A modern pressure canner, in the USDA's description, is a light,
thin-walled kettle, usually with a turn-on lid, a jar rack, a gasket, a
dial or weighted gauge, an automatic vent and cover lock, a vent port
closed by a counterweight or weighted gauge, and a safety fuse. Canners
of 16 to 23 quarts are common, and most hold seven quart jars or eight to
nine pints; the smallest that counts as a canner holds four quart jars
(USDA).

### Dial gauge or weighted gauge

- **A weighted gauge** is a weight that sits on the vent and rocks or
  jiggles as the canner holds its pressure. The USDA guide says it
  controls pressure precisely and needs neither watching nor checking for
  accuracy. Its one drawback is that it comes in fixed steps, so above
  1,000 feet it must be run at 15 pounds instead of 10 (or 10 instead of
  5).
- **A dial gauge** is a needle you watch, adjusting the heat to keep it
  where the recipe says. The USDA: "Check dial gauges for accuracy before
  use each year." A gauge that reads high causes under-processing and may
  leave the food unsafe; one that reads up to 2 pounds high or low can be
  corrected for, and the USDA's rule for the rest is: "Replace gauges
  that differ by more than 2 pounds." Many county extension offices test
  gauges. WSU Extension Kitsap County, the office for Silverdale, tests
  dial gauges at its office in Bremerton by appointment (360-228-7300),
  for $10 a canner according to its page for a free testing event on 15
  August 2026, read 4 October 2026; it says dial gauges should be tested
  every year, including before a new gauge is first used, and that
  weighted gauges do not need testing.

### Jars and lids

The same as for a water bath ([Water-Bath
Canning](/library#water-bath-canning)), with one difference: the USDA guide
does not recommend mayonnaise-type jars in a pressure canner, because so
many break. Jars for the pressure canner do not need sterilising, only
washing and keeping hot (USDA). Use new flat lids every time.

## Step by step

This is the USDA's pressure-canner procedure from Guide 1, in order. The
recipe you follow adds its own preparation, headspace, time and
pressure.

1. **Choose the recipe and read all of it.** Find your jar size and pack,
   the processing time, and the pressure for your type of gauge in your
   altitude column.
2. **Check the canner.** The gasket clean and not nicked or dried out, the
   safety fuse intact, the vent port clear, and a dial gauge tested this
   year (the USDA's gasket, fuse and gauge rules; a clear vent port,
   NCHFP).
3. **Wash the jars and keep them hot**, as for a water bath. Prepare the
   lids as the lid maker directs.
4. **Put 2 to 3 inches of hot water in the canner.** Some recipes call for
   more; follow the recipe when it does (USDA). NCHFP adds that for a hot
   pack the water can be brought to 180 F beforehand, without letting it
   boil, and for a raw pack to 140 F.
5. **Prepare and pack the food exactly as the recipe says.** The USDA's
   usual headspace for low-acid foods in a pressure canner is 1 to 1-1/4
   inches; use the recipe's figure. Free the air bubbles, check the
   headspace, wipe the rims and put on the lids and bands, as in
   [Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning).
6. **Load the canner.** Set the jars on the rack with a jar lifter, kept
   upright, and fasten the canner lid securely (USDA).
7. **Heat with the vent open.** Leave the weight off the vent port, or
   open the petcock, and heat at the highest setting until steam flows
   freely from it (USDA).
8. **Vent for 10 minutes.** Keep the heat high and let the steam flow
   continuously for 10 minutes. Then put the weight on the vent port, or
   close the petcock. The canner will come up to pressure over the next 3
   to 5 minutes (USDA).
9. **Start timing at pressure.** Start the timer when the dial gauge shows
   the recipe's pressure, or when the weighted gauge begins to jiggle or
   rock the way the canner maker describes (USDA).
10. **Hold the pressure.** Adjust the heat to keep the pressure steady at
    or slightly above the recipe's; quick, large swings make jars lose
    liquid (USDA). The rule the USDA marks IMPORTANT: if at any time the
    pressure goes below the recommended amount, bring the canner back up
    to pressure and start the timing over from the beginning, using the
    total original time.
11. **Let it cool by itself.** When the time is up, turn off the heat,
    take the canner off the burner if you can, and let it depressurize.
    The USDA: "Do not force-cool the canner." An older heavy-walled
    canner without a dial gauge takes about 30 minutes with pints and 45
    with quarts; a newer canner is depressurized when its vent lock
    drops back to its normal position (USDA).
12. **Open it.** First make sure the pressure is gone: the vent lock has
    dropped, and on a dial-gauge canner the dial reads zero. Tilt the
    weight slightly before lifting it off, and if steam still comes out,
    wait; never force the lid while the cover lock has not released
    (NCHFP). Then take off the weight or open the petcock, wait 10
    minutes, unfasten the lid, and lift it away from you (USDA).
13. **Take out the jars** with the jar lifter, upright, onto a towel with
    at least an inch between them, and leave them for 12 to 24 hours at
    room temperature (USDA).
14. **Test the seals, then store the jars**, as in [Water-Bath
    Canning](/library#water-bath-canning).

### Altitude and pressure

Each USDA recipe has two pressure tables, one for each kind of gauge,
with a column for each range of altitude. For the green beans in the
worked example below, the USDA's tables read:

| Gauge | Pints | Quarts | Pressure by altitude |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weighted | 20 minutes | 25 minutes | 10 pounds from 0 to 1,000 feet; 15 pounds above 1,000 feet |
| Dial | 20 minutes | 25 minutes | 11 pounds from 0 to 2,000 feet; 12 pounds from 2,001 to 4,000; 13 pounds from 4,001 to 6,000; 14 pounds from 6,001 to 8,000 |

Those are the green bean numbers and no others. Other foods have their
own times, and some have their own pressures, so read the table in your
recipe every time.

For Silverdale the column is the first one either way: the locale data
puts the area between sea level and about 178 metres, which is about 584
feet ([Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa), from USGS
elevation data), so a weighted gauge runs at 10 pounds and a dial gauge
at 11. Anyone canning outside that area looks up their own elevation
first; the USDA guide says your county extension agent can tell you.

### A jar that did not seal, and a batch that went wrong

A jar that failed to seal on canning day has the same three choices as in
a water bath: process it again within 24 hours with a new lid, for the
same time; refrigerate it and eat it within several days; or freeze it,
after adjusting the headspace to 1-1/2 inches (USDA; [Water-Bath
Canning](/library#water-bath-canning)).

A whole batch is different when you learn afterwards that something went
wrong: the pressure dropped and you did not restart the timer, you used
the wrong altitude column or the wrong jar size, the canner was not
vented, or it was cooled with water. That food was under-processed. The
USDA's guide says its pre-serving boil "is not intended to serve as a
recommendation for consuming foods known to be significantly
underprocessed", and the CDC's rule is: "Do not eat food if you do not
know whether safe canning guidelines were followed." Our reading of the
two together, the same as [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) reaches for low-acid food put through a
water bath: such a batch is thrown out, by the disposal procedure in
[Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew).

### Before you eat a jar

Every jar gets the checks in [Water-Bath Canning](/library#water-bath-canning),
and any jar that fails one is thrown out without tasting, by the disposal
procedure in [Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew).

Then one more step. The CDC advises boiling all low-acid home-canned
foods in a saucepan before eating them: 10 minutes below 1,000 feet, with
1 more minute for each additional 1,000 feet. The USDA guide is a little
less strict: it says low-acid foods canned by its approved
recommendations "may be eaten without boiling them when you are sure of
all the following", and lists eight conditions, among them that the food
was processed in a pressure canner whose gauge was accurate, by
up-to-date times and pressures for that jar size, pack, food and
altitude, and that the lid is firmly sealed and concave, nothing has
leaked, nothing spurts out when the jar is opened, and there is no off
smell. This Library follows the CDC and boils every time, because the
boil costs nothing and you can rarely be sure of all eight ([Why Canning
Has Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)). The boil is a habit for jars that
pass every check; it never rescues one that fails.

## Worked example: green beans in Silverdale

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on. The recipe is the USDA's snap and Italian beans (Guide 4) and
the canner procedure is the USDA's (Guide 1).

It is August, and the bean rows have produced about 14 pounds in two
days, which is a canner load: the USDA puts 7 quarts at an average of 14
pounds and 9 pints at 9 pounds. You have a pressure canner with a
weighted gauge and seven quart jars. The beans were picked this morning;
the USDA guide asks for vegetables to be canned within 6 to 12 hours of
harvest for best quality.

1. **The numbers.** Raw or hot pack, 1-inch headspace. At Silverdale
   (below 1,000 feet) a weighted gauge runs at 10 pounds. Quarts take 25
   minutes. Write the three numbers on a card by the stove.
2. **The canner.** The gasket is clean and soft, the vent port is clear.
   (With a dial gauge, you would also check the date of its last test,
   and the pressure would be 11 pounds.) Put 2 to 3 inches of hot water in
   the canner and heat it to 140 F, the temperature NCHFP gives for a raw
   pack.
3. **The beans.** Choose filled but tender, crisp pods and throw out any
   that are diseased or rusty. Wash them, trim the ends, and leave them
   whole or snap them into 1-inch pieces.
4. **Pack.** Fill each hot jar tightly with raw beans, leaving 1 inch of
   headspace. Add a teaspoon of canning salt per quart if you like it (it
   is seasoning, not safety). Pour in boiling water, leaving the same 1
   inch, free the air bubbles, wipe the rim, and put on the lid and band.
5. **Load and lock.** Seven quarts on the rack, lid fastened, weight off.
   Heat on high until steam flows freely from the vent port.
6. **Vent.** Set the timer for 10 minutes and let the steam pour out the
   whole time. Then put the 10-pound weight on.
7. **Process.** When the weight starts to jiggle the way the canner's
   manual describes, set the timer for 25 minutes. Adjust the heat to keep
   it jiggling steadily. Halfway through, the jiggling stops; the
   pressure has dropped. You turn the heat up until the weight jiggles
   again, and start the full 25 minutes over from zero.
8. **Cool.** Heat off. Leave the canner alone until it is fully
   depressurized and its vent lock has dropped. Tilt the weight to check
   that no steam comes out, then take it off, wait 10 minutes, unfasten
   the lid and lift it away from your face.
9. **Out.** Jars onto a towel, an inch apart, for 12 to 24 hours. Next
   day, test the seals, take off the bands, wash, label and date the jars,
   and store them somewhere cool and dark.
10. **In January.** You open a jar that passes every check, and boil the
    beans in a saucepan for 10 minutes before they go on the table.

## Meat, fish and soup

These follow the same procedure, with tested recipes of their own in the
USDA guide, and a few rules worth knowing before you start (USDA, Guides
1, 4 and 5):

- **Meat and poultry.** Chill fresh home-slaughtered meat and poultry and
  can it without delay, and never can meat from sickly or diseased
  animals.
- **Fish.** Ice fish after the catch, gut it at once and can it within 2
  days; for fatty fish such as salmon, the guide's caution is to bleed and
  gut it immediately after catching, never more than 2 hours after, and
  keep it on ice until you can it. Fish in quart jars
  has a canner procedure of its own, with more water and a longer heating
  and venting time than usual: follow it exactly. [Fishing](/library#fishing)
  covers the hours between the water and the kitchen.
- **Soup.** No noodles, pasta, rice, flour, cream, milk or other
  thickening, and dried beans rehydrated first; the guide's soup times
  are longer when the soup contains seafood.
- **Smoked fish** has its own tested canning process, with its own way
  of filling the canner ([Salting, Curing and
  Smoking](/library#salting-curing-and-smoking)).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Testing a dial gauge** is a job for your extension office or the
  canner maker (USDA). Around Silverdale, WSU Extension Kitsap County
  does it.
- **A damaged canner** gets the maker's replacement parts (the USDA guide
  says to order them with the canner's model number) or it is not used
  (general practice). Never repair a gauge, a gasket or a safety fuse by
  improvising.
- **A food with no tested process** is frozen or dried instead ([Keeping
  What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew), [Drying Food](/library#drying-food)).
- **Electric multicookers** are not canners, whatever the button says.
- **Selling home-canned food** is regulated. Ask your state's agriculture
  or health department before you sell any (general practice).

## Traps

- **"It's a pressure cooker; same thing."** A multicooker or a small
  pressure saucepan is not a canner.
- **"I'll skip the venting; it gets to pressure anyway."** Trapped air
  means a lower temperature at the same pressure.
- **"The pressure only dipped for a moment."** Bring it back up and start
  the whole time again.
- **"It'll cool faster under the tap."** Forced cooling ruins the
  process and the seals.
- **"The dial reads ten, so it's ten."** Only if the gauge was tested this
  year.
- **"The sea-level pressure is close enough."** For the green beans, not
  above 1,000 feet with a weighted gauge, or above 2,000 feet with a dial;
  read your recipe's table.
- **"A thick stew will can fine."** No thickening, ever.
- **"Mashed pumpkin is just pumpkin."** Cubes only.
- **"It sealed and smells fine."** That is not evidence that it was
  processed enough.

## How the game models it

The game makes canned food, but not by pressure canning.

- **Three recipes make Canned Food.** On the Crafting page, Can Food turns
  two Potatoes, two Carrots and one Salt into three Canned Food, and its
  in-game description reads "Pressure-seal preserved meals". Cook Soup
  turns two Potatoes, a Carrot, a Purified Water and a Salt into two
  Canned Food, and Cook Stew turns two Potatoes, an Onion, a Jerky and a
  Purified Water into three (`data/recipes.csv`). The page lists the parts
  by their item ids, so the vegetables appear there as Vegetable Potato,
  Vegetable Carrot and Vegetable Onion, Salt as Salt Food and Purified
  Water as Water Purified (`src/gui/pages/crafting.rs`). All three are
  made at the home's Stove, which stands in the default home's kitchen
  (`data/machines/home.ron`), and all three need a Utility Knife in your
  backpack, which the Crafting page lists as Knife Utility
  (`data/crafting/tools.ron`). Can Food and Cook Stew need Cooking level
  2; Cook Soup is a level-1 recipe, and the game's skill gate starts at
  level 2, so anyone can make it (`src/gui/pages/crafting.rs`,
  `src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`).
- **Under the default settings.** In the default Dev play mode (Settings >
  Gameplay > Play mode) a craft uses up none of its parts and wears no
  tool, but the Craft button still waits until the parts are in your
  backpack, or in your home's storage while you are at home, and the
  knife is in your backpack (`src/gui/pages/crafting.rs`,
  `src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`). The page's "Dev: stock all materials"
  button fills your backpack with every recipe's parts but no tools; a
  new character's starting kit holds the Utility Knife
  (`data/world/player.ron`). Cooking the level-1 cooking recipes trains
  the Cooking skill, and the Profile page's "Dev: max skills" button
  raises every skill at once.
- **What the recipes get right, and wrong.** Potatoes, carrots, onions and
  meat are all low-acid foods, so in real life every one of these would
  need a pressure canner and a tested process. The salt in Can Food is
  seasoning: the USDA guide says salt is not needed for the safety of
  canned vegetables and meat. Cook Stew's "thick meat stew" is the
  opposite of the USDA's way with soup, which is to fill each jar only
  halfway with solids, cover them with liquid and never thicken. And its
  Canned Food is a steel tin, a "Sealed tinned meal" (`data/items.csv`),
  where home canners use glass jars: the USDA guide says metal cans need
  special sealing equipment and can be used only once.

The food data also lists canning as a preservation method that makes food
keep a hundred times as long and needs Cooking level 4, but no code acts
on that row yet (`data/food_system.ron`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no pressure canner, gauge, vent, weight, headspace or processing time,
and no recipe changes with altitude, so nothing can be under-processed.
Botulism is not in the game either: a record for its toxin sits in
`data/chemistry/toxins.csv`, but no game code reads it.

## You own this when

- You can say why low-acid food needs a pressure canner, and why it is
  the temperature and not the pressure that does the work.
- You know what is not a canner: a multicooker, an electric pressure
  cooker, a pressure saucepan.
- You check the gasket, the fuse and the vent before every batch, and get
  a dial gauge tested every year.
- You vent for 10 minutes every time, start timing only at pressure, and
  start again from zero if the pressure drops.
- You let the canner cool by itself, check that the pressure is gone
  before taking off the weight, wait 10 minutes after taking it off, and
  lift the lid away from your face.
- You know your altitude, and the pressure and time it gives for your
  gauge and your recipe.
- You do not can purees, mashes, thickened soups, dairy or anything with
  no tested process.
- You throw out a batch you know went wrong, and boil every jar of
  low-acid food before eating it: 10 minutes below 1,000 feet, and 1
  minute more for each additional 1,000 feet.
