# Where Your Own Electrical Work Stops

Most of what goes wrong with electricity in a home can be noticed,
reset, unplugged or reported by anyone in the household. Much less of it
can safely be repaired by someone who has not been trained. And in the
United States, most work on a household's wiring is regulated, permitted
and inspected locally. The Consumer Product Safety Commission describes
the national model electrical code as the most widely adopted in the
country, taken into law by states, counties or cities for their
inspectors to enforce, and its advice is that every change or addition to
a home's installed wiring be done and inspected under those local rules.
Washington's version, with its permits and licences, is worked through
below. This guide draws the line plainly, in three parts:

- **what everyone in a household should do**, which never involves
  opening up the wiring;
- **a narrow middle**: like-for-like replacements, such as an outlet or a
  light switch, that the law in some places lets an owner do, and that
  are safe only for someone with a voltage tester, the training to use
  it, and a circuit proved dead;
- **where it stops**: everything else, which belongs to a licensed
  electrician, and some of it to the electricity utility alone.

The lines in this guide are safety lines, not legal ones. In Washington,
this guide's worked example, the law lets a homeowner do more than this
guide recommends. What the law allows tells you who may be blamed; it
does not tell you what will hurt you.

The physics behind every rule here (why a breaker does not protect a
person, why wet skin matters, why a loose connection starts a fire) is in
[Electricity and How It Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows). The
procedure for proving a circuit dead before you touch it is in [Working
Out Why Something Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke), and this
guide does not repeat it. Electric tools are in [Power
Tools](/library#power-tools).

Its sources are the Occupational Safety and Health Administration
(OSHA), the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health
(NIOSH), the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC) and the Federal
Emergency Management Agency's Ready.gov, all US federal and in the public
domain, and, for the worked example, Washington State law and the
Washington State Department of Labor and Industries, restated in our own
words. Where something is general practice or our own reading rather
than a published rule, the text says so.

## First: the rules for every job

These hold whatever the job, and they are not negotiable.

1. **Never work on anything live.** NIOSH's manual for electrical trades
   students: "No work should be done on energized electrical circuits."
   OSHA's rule for workplaces lets only qualified persons work on circuit
   parts that have not been switched off and locked out (29 CFR
   1910.333(c)(2)), and defines a qualified person as one who has
   received training in, and has demonstrated skills and knowledge of,
   the construction and operation of electric equipment and the hazards
   involved (29 CFR 1910.399). Nobody in a household is qualified by
   default, however handy (our reading).
2. **A switch is not isolation; prove it dead.** Turn off the breaker,
   lock or label it, and prove the circuit dead with a voltage tester at
   the point you will touch, checking the tester on a known live circuit
   before and after. If you have no tester, or have not been shown how to
   use one, stop: the job is not yours. The full procedure and its
   reasons are in [Working Out Why Something
   Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke). NIOSH's lockout checklist
   puts the first half plainly: "Simply turning a switch off is NOT
   enough." Its case reports show why: a furnace technician was killed by
   a toggle switch that had been wired so that power still reached the
   furnace with the switch in the "off" position, and NIOSH's
   conclusion was that "a simple circuit tester may have saved the
   victim's life."
3. **Find every source of power.** NIOSH's checklist starts by
   identifying all sources of electrical energy and disabling backup
   sources such as generators and batteries. A home with solar panels, a
   battery bank or a generator has more than one supply. Solar panels
   generate electricity on their own, which is why OSHA's guidance for
   solar workers says to cover the panels while installing or servicing
   them; see [Your First Solar Power](/library#first-solar-power) and [Batteries
   and Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage).
4. **Remember stored energy.** OSHA requires capacitors to be discharged
   before work on equipment that holds a dangerous charge (29 CFR
   1910.333(b)(2)(ii)(C)). At home, read that as a reason to stop: a
   microwave oven, an old tube television and power supplies can hold a
   charge after they are unplugged, as [Working Out Why Something
   Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke) explains.
5. **Dry hands, dry floor.** OSHA's rule: hands may not be wet when
   plugging and unplugging cords (29 CFR 1910.334(a)(5)(i)). NIOSH tells
   students not to work on circuits or use electrical equipment in damp
   or wet areas at all.
6. **Take off rings, watches and other metal.** OSHA bars conductive
   jewellery near exposed live parts (29 CFR 1910.333(c)(8)), and NIOSH
   explains that metal worn near high currents can cause burns.
7. **Never reach where you cannot see.** OSHA: "Employees may not reach
   blindly into areas which may contain energized parts." (29 CFR
   1910.333(c)(4)(ii))
8. **Never work alone.** NIOSH: work with a "buddy", both trained in
   CPR, both knowing what to do in an emergency, and know where the
   breakers for your work area are. Its case reports include a restaurant
   worker who was held for 3 to 8 minutes between a live plug and a
   grounded outlet box while the manager searched for the right breaker,
   and died.
9. **Know what to do if someone is shocked:** do not touch them; cut the
   power, or push them clear with something dry that does not conduct;
   call 911. The detail is in [Electricity and How It
   Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows) and [Treating
   Burns](/library#treating-burns).

NIOSH's manual asks two questions before any electrical task, and they
are the whole of this guide in one line: "What could go wrong? Do I have
the knowledge, tools, and experience to do this work safely?"

## What everyone in a household should do

None of this opens up the wiring, and all of it is yours, whoever you
are.

### Know your panel

- **Know where the panel and its main switch are,** and label every
  breaker with what it feeds. NIOSH: "Always label circuit breakers."
  Finding the right breaker at once is exactly what was missing in the
  restaurant case above.
- **Test the protection devices every month.** The CPSC says to test
  every ground-fault circuit interrupter (GFCI) at least once a month,
  after a power failure and after installation, because GFCIs "can fail
  without showing any sign of failure", and to test arc-fault circuit
  interrupters (AFCIs) monthly too, since they can also stop working
  without any sign. The lamp test is in [Working Out Why Something
  Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke). What each one protects
  against is in [Electricity and How It
  Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows).
- **Exercise the breakers once a year.** The CPSC's home electrical
  checklist says breakers must be worked periodically so they do not
  stick: turn off the freezer, the refrigerator and the air conditioner
  first, since motors with compressors can be damaged by repeated power
  interruptions, then switch each breaker off and on three times, at
  least once a year. That means opening the panel's door, never taking
  off its cover (general practice). Removing the cover is another matter
  entirely: NIOSH lists "removing panels and dead fronts" among the tasks
  that put a worker near live circuits.

### When the power goes off

- **A tripped breaker: reset it once.** If it trips again straight away,
  stop. OSHA's rule for workplaces forbids switching a circuit back on
  after a breaker or fuse has opened until it has been determined that
  the equipment and circuit can be safely energized, and it prohibits
  "The repetitive manual reclosing of circuit breakers or reenergizing
  circuits through replaced fuses" (29 CFR 1910.334(b)(2)). NIOSH:
  "When a circuit breaker trips or a fuse blows, the cause must be
  found." Unplug what is on the circuit, or call an electrician; the
  diagnosis is in [Working Out Why Something
  Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke).
- **A blown fuse: the same size only.** The CPSC says to install the
  correct size of fuse, and to have an electrician identify and label it
  if you do not know it. Never a bigger one and never a bypass: OSHA's
  workplace rule says overcurrent protection "may not be modified, even
  on a temporary basis," beyond what the installation rules allow (29
  CFR 1910.334(b)(3)), and its construction rule forbids changing a
  circuit's protection to carry more load than its wiring is rated for
  (29 CFR 1926.416(c)). Why a bigger fuse lets the wiring overheat is in
  [Electricity and How It Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows).
- **A tripping GFCI is a warning, not a nuisance.** NIOSH: "If a GFCI
  keeps tripping while you are using a power tool, there is a problem.
  Don't keep resetting the GFCI and continue to work."

### Cords, plugs and adapters

- **Look at every cord before you use it.** OSHA requires cords and
  corded equipment at work to be inspected visually before use on each
  shift, for loose parts, deformed or missing pins, damage to the jacket
  or insulation, and signs of internal damage such as a pinched or
  crushed jacket, and anything damaged to be taken out of use until it
  is repaired and tested (29 CFR 1910.334(a)(2)). The CPSC's checklist
  says to replace damaged cords, because they may expose live wires.
- **Never cut off or defeat a ground pin.** The CPSC: "NEVER defeat the
  adapter's grounding feature or break the ground pin from a 3-prong
  plug." OSHA does not allow adapters that interrupt the ground
  connection to be used at work (29 CFR 1910.334(a)(3)(iii)).
- **An extension cord is temporary.** The CPSC: "Extension cords are not
  as safe as permanent house wiring." NIOSH says flexible cords may not
  be used instead of a building's fixed wiring, nor run through holes in
  walls, ceilings or floors, through doorways or windows unless
  protected, or hidden in walls, ceilings, floors or conduit. The CPSC
  adds: not under rugs or furniture, not fastened with nails or staples,
  not wrapped tightly round anything, and if one is in permanent use,
  have an outlet installed where it is needed. Outdoors, use cords
  marked for outdoor use, and with a three-prong tool a three-prong cord.
- **Size the cord for the load.** The CPSC says to check the rating on
  the appliance and the cord, and that a cord, plug or outlet that feels
  warm may be overloaded. [Electricity and How It
  Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows) works an example.

### Water

- **Keep electricity away from water.** The CPSC's checklist says to keep
  countertop appliances away from the sink, or on GFCI-protected outlets
  if they cannot be moved, and to unplug small appliances such as hair
  dryers when not in use: "Even when turned off, plugged-in electrical
  appliances may cause a shock hazard if they fall into water." It says
  to avoid portable heaters in bathrooms, where the many grounded
  surfaces and the water make them an electrocution hazard.
- **Never reach for anything electrical that has fallen into water.**
  Cut the power first. The CPSC says to unplug equipment that is wet or
  immersed before trying to rescue it; that this means pulling the plug
  or switching off the breaker, never touching the appliance itself, is
  general practice.
- **After a flood.** Ready.gov: "Do not touch electrical equipment if it
  is wet or if you are standing in water. Turn off the electricity to
  prevent electric shock if it is safe to do so." It adds that
  underground or downed power lines can electrically charge floodwater.
  Anything that has been under water needs an electrician before it is
  switched back on (general practice; for a well pump the EPA says the
  same, in [Wells and Groundwater](/library#wells-and-groundwater)). The CPSC
  says a power tool that has been immersed should be tested at a
  qualified repair centre before it is used again.
- **Outdoors and with tools.** The CPSC says to consider a portable GFCI
  whenever you use electric garden equipment or electric tools, and that
  portable GFCIs are as effective as installed ones. Older homes may have
  none: the CPSC's GFCI fact sheet lists the years the electrical code
  began requiring them, from outdoor outlets in 1973 and bathrooms in
  1975 to kitchens in 1987 and laundry and utility sinks in 2005, and, as
  NIOSH notes, the code is not retroactive.

### Generators

A power cut is when a household with a generator is tempted to connect
it to the house wiring (our reading), and the CPSC's warning about that
is plain: "NEVER try to power the house wiring by plugging the generator into a
wall outlet, a practice known as 'backfeeding.' This is extremely
dangerous and presents an electrocution risk to utility workers and
neighbors served by the same utility transformer." Run appliances from
the generator itself, through heavy-duty outdoor extension cords whose
wattage rating exceeds the total load, with all three prongs on the plug
(CPSC). Keep it dry and outdoors, away from windows, doors and vents (the
CPSC's rules, and Ready.gov's distance of at least 20 feet). A generator
that is to run house circuits is connected through a transfer switch that
an electrician installs (general practice); [Wells and
Groundwater](/library#wells-and-groundwater) says the same for a well pump.

### Warning signs: stop and call

The CPSC's checklist and its aluminium wiring booklet, with NIOSH on
breakers and fuses, list the signs that something in the wiring is wrong.
When you see one, stop using that circuit and call a qualified
electrician:

- an outlet or switch that is warm to the touch (some dimmer switches
  are warm in normal use, the CPSC notes);
- plugs that fit loosely in an outlet;
- outlets or switches that do not work, or lights that flicker;
- a smell of burning plastic at an outlet or switch;
- a breaker that trips again, or a fuse that blows again;
- a shock, even a slight one, from an appliance. The CPSC: do not touch
  it again until an electrician has checked it, and turn its power off at
  the breaker, because "A shock indicates an extremely hazardous wiring
  condition."

The US Navy's beginners' course, written for sailors, gives a similar
list and the same instruction: "Do not operate faulty equipment."

## The middle: like-for-like work on your own home

Replacing a worn outlet, a broken light switch or a light fitting with the
same kind, on the same circuit, in the same place, is the work most
people mean by "doing it myself". Washington's law describes exactly that
kind of job: it calls the like-in-kind replacement of a receptacle
outlet, a snap switch, a dimmer, a residential light fitting, a lamp, a
thermostat and a short list of other parts "basic electrical work",
needing no permit unless defects call for an inspection, and it lets an
owner do electrical work at their own home without a licence (RCW
19.28.006, 19.28.101 and 19.28.261; the details are in the worked example
below). "Like-in-kind" there means similar characteristics, such as
voltage, current draw and function, in the same location (RCW 19.28.006).
Other states, counties and cities draw the line in other places, so ask
your city or county building department, or your state's electrical
program, before you start (our caution). The CPSC's aluminium wiring
booklet states the general rule: "All modifications and additions to
installed wiring should be performed and inspected in accordance with
local regulations."

Legal is not the same as safe for you. The CPSC's description of who
should install a GFCI outlet is the honest test for every job in this
middle: an outlet-type GFCI may be installed by a consumer with adequate
knowledge and skill to follow proper wiring practice and the device's
instructions, and when in doubt about the procedure, contact a qualified
electrician and do not attempt it yourself.

NIOSH's manual works through that very job, changing a wall GFCI, as a
job hazard analysis. The hazards it lists are electric shock from exposed
live wires, possible other live wires in the opening, the possibility of
connecting the wires wrongly, and a possibly defective new GFCI. Its
controls: switch off by opening the breaker or removing the fuse, test
the wires with an appropriate voltmeter to make sure all of them are
dead, check the wiring diagram, and test the GFCI afterwards.

So, before a like-for-like job is yours, all of these have to be true
(our list, built from the sources above):

- you own a voltage tester and have been shown how to use it, and you
  will check it on a known live circuit before and after;
- you can find the right breaker, switch it off and lock or label it;
- the law where you live lets you do it, and you know whether it needs a
  permit;
- the new part is the same kind as the old one, and is listed or
  certified for the purpose (Washington's rules require electrical
  materials and equipment to meet recognised product standards or be
  listed, WAC 296-46B-010).

And when you open the box, stop and close it up again if anything is not
what you expected (general practice): more wires than the old part had, a
wire from another circuit, a bundle joined together in the back,
scorched or brittle insulation, any sign of heat, or wire that is not
copper-coloured. That last one may be aluminium. The CPSC's booklet on
aluminium wiring says to look for "Al" or "Aluminum" marked every few
feet along the cable's jacket, to have a qualified electrician decide if
you cannot tell, and to hire a qualified electrician for the repair;
where there are signs of trouble, its words are "DO NOT TRY TO DO IT
YOURSELF."

**Not in the middle: breakers.** Washington's list of basic electrical
work includes replacing a circuit breaker. This guide puts it in the next
section anyway, because it means taking the cover off a panel, and NIOSH
counts that as working near live circuits. Switching off the main
breaker does not make the panel dead: the wires from the meter and the
terminals they connect to stay live, and only the utility can switch
those off (general practice).

## Where it stops

Each of these is a licensed electrician's job, or the utility's. That is
our recommendation for a household, built on the sources given with each
item; where your local rules are stricter, they win (our caution).

- **Anything inside the panel or the meter.** Breakers, the main switch,
  the service wires and the meter itself. Washington's electrical law
  exempts the utility-owned equipment between the meter base and the
  meter from its rules (RCW 19.28.010), and Washington's Department of
  Labor and Industries tells owners to contact their serving utility
  before any change that needs the power disconnected and reconnected.
- **Any new wiring.** A new circuit, an extended circuit, a new or moved
  outlet or light, anything run inside a wall. This is the work that
  permits and inspections exist for (below).
- **Aluminium branch wiring,** found in some houses built from the mid
  1960s to the mid 1970s: the CPSC highly recommends hiring a qualified
  electrician, and the connector repair it recommends may be installed
  only by electricians the connector's maker has trained.
- **Anything showing the warning signs above.**
- **Connecting a generator to the house.** A transfer switch, installed by
  an electrician (general practice; the CPSC's backfeed warning is above).
- **Solar panels, batteries and inverters** connected to a house:
  [Your First Solar Power](/library#first-solar-power) and [Batteries and
  Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage) both draw this line at a licensed
  electrician.
- **Well pumps, their pressure switches and controls,** above all after a
  flood: [Wells and Groundwater](/library#wells-and-groundwater).
- **Pools, hot tubs and spas.** NIOSH's case report of a swim club's
  assistant manager electrocuted by a mixing motor whose plug had lost
  its ground pin, on a circuit whose GFCI had not been installed
  properly, lists among its lessons: "All pool-area electrical circuits
  should be installed by qualified electricians."
- **Anything that has been flooded,** as above.
- **Overhead lines, the service drop to the house, and any line that is
  down.** These belong to the utility. OSHA's rule keeps a worker who is
  not a trained line worker, and the longest conductive object they might
  touch, 10 feet from an overhead line of up to 50,000 volts (29 CFR
  1910.333(c)(3)), and its hazard alert for snow removal says: "Always
  treat power lines, wires and other conductors as energized, even if
  they are down or appear to be insulated." If a tree falls on a line,
  OSHA's logging rule says to notify the power company immediately and
  stay clear until the company says there is no electrical hazard (29
  CFR 1910.266(d)(8)(ii)). Ladders and roofs near lines are in [Roofs and
  Keeping Water Out](/library#roofs-and-keeping-water-out).
- **Microwave ovens, old tube televisions and anything that makes high
  voltage.** The CPSC's staff report on electrocutions from consumer
  products found that fractal wood burning, which uses high voltage to
  burn patterns into wood, "often involved tampering with microwaves and
  other devices to produce the electricity the practice requires," and
  that it became the product most often involved in electrocutions in
  2018, 2019 and 2020. Never take apart a microwave oven, and never build
  a high-voltage device from its parts (our recommendation).
- **Anything at work.** On a job, OSHA's rules apply: only qualified
  persons may work on circuits that are not switched off and locked out,
  and only qualified persons may do testing work on electric circuits
  (29 CFR 1910.333(c)(2) and 1910.334(c)(1)).

Every one of these is also a reason to know how to find a good
electrician before you need one: see "Hiring" below.

## Why permits and inspections exist

**One code, adopted locally.** The CPSC describes the National Electrical
Code, published as NFPA 70, as the most widely adopted code for
electrical installations in the United States, which states, counties
or cities may adopt into law for their inspectors to enforce, and which
is revised every three years (CPSC GFCI fact sheet). Washington, for
example, enforces the 2023 edition, and its rules adopt the 2026 edition
from 31 December 2026 (WAC 296-46B-010). The code is copyrighted and is
not quoted here; your inspector or electrician works from it.

**Inspection happens before the wiring disappears.** Washington's law
says no wiring that needs inspection may be concealed until the inspector
has approved it, and that whoever makes an installation must have it
inspected and approved before asking the utility to connect it (RCW
19.28.101). Wiring sits inside a wall for decades; the inspection is the
one time a second, trained person looks at it (our reading).

**Old wiring is not brought up to date for you.** NIOSH's furnace case,
the switch that let power through when off, was found when the county's
electrical inspector came after the death; an electrician called it
"haphazard and confusing." NIOSH's lesson: "Although the NEC is not
retroactive, all homeowners should make sure their systems are safe." The
code in force when a house was wired is the code it was built to; a house
wired before kitchens needed GFCIs (1987, in the CPSC's list) does not
grow them by itself.

**An inspector checks; an inspector does not design.** Washington's rules
say inspectors will give information on interpreting the rules, but will
not lay out work or act as consultants for owners (WAC 296-46B-010).
Planning the work is the electrician's job, or yours.

## Worked example: a household near Silverdale, Washington

The project's canonical place is Silverdale, on the Kitsap Peninsula (see
[Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa)). Washington's
electrical law is chapter 19.28 of the Revised Code of Washington, its
rules are chapter 296-46B of the Washington Administrative Code, and the
Department of Labor and Industries (L&I) runs both. What follows is
restated from them, read on 4 October 2026.

**Who inspects.** L&I issues electrical permits and inspects everywhere
in the state except inside the cities that run their own electrical
inspection and the area Tacoma Power serves. L&I's list, read on 4
October 2026, names 25 such cities and Tacoma Power, and none of them is
in Kitsap County, so a house in Silverdale gets its permit and its
inspection from L&I (our reading of the list). Check the list for your own
address; it changes.

**Who may do the work.** Washington's law makes it unlawful to carry on
the business of installing or maintaining wiring without an electrical
contractor's licence, and requires electricians to hold a certificate of
competency (RCW 19.28.041 and 19.28.161). L&I's own summary: only
licensed electrical contractors employing certified electricians may
perform electrical work, and property owners may apply for an exemption.

**The owner's exemption.** RCW 19.28.261 lets a person do electrical work
at their own residence, farm, place of business or other property they
own without a licence or a certified electrician, except on a new
building intended for rent, sale or lease, or on property offered for
sale within 12 months of acquiring it (a new building of up to four
homes, one of which the owner will live in, has a narrow exception). A
friend, neighbour or relative may help, as long as nobody involved holds
themselves out as being in the electrical business. L&I's permit form
adds the practical conditions: the owner's name must be on the deed, and
relatives, property managers and estate agents cannot buy the permit for
the owner; a tenant may apply only with a lease that gives permission to
do electrical work.

**The permit and the inspection.** The owner buys a property owner's
electrical work permit from L&I and signs an affidavit claiming the
exemption, before starting. The permit is good for one year. L&I's
instructions: request the inspection before you bury or cover anything,
and no later than 3 business days after finishing the work or 1 business
day after any part of it is energized, whichever comes first; do not
cover any electrical work before it is inspected; and contact your
serving utility if the work needs the power disconnected and reconnected,
or connected for the first time. Breaking the chapter's installation,
inspection and licensing rules for contractors can bring a penalty of $50
to $10,000 (RCW 19.28.131).

**What needs no permit.** The like-in-kind replacements in the middle
section above, which the law calls class A basic electrical work (RCW
19.28.006 and 19.28.101).

**What L&I itself advises.** Its page for property owners says to buy a
permit only if you are doing the work yourself on your own property, and
that it strongly encourages hiring an electrical contractor for all
electrical work. If you hire one, the contractor buys the permit for the
work, and L&I's site has a tool for checking that the contractor is
licensed.

**The code edition.** The 2023 National Electrical Code, with
Washington's amendments, until the 2026 edition takes over on 31
December 2026 (WAC 296-46B-010).

## Hiring

- **Licensed, and the licence checked,** with your state's licensing
  agency (in Washington, L&I's lookup), and insured: [Trading
  Fairly](/library#trading-fairly) gives the Federal Trade Commission's
  checklist for hiring any contractor.
- **The contractor pulls the permit** for work that needs one (L&I says
  so for Washington), and the job is not finished until it has passed
  inspection.
- **Describe the symptom, not your guess.** What happened, when, and what
  you have already reset or unplugged; [Working Out Why Something
  Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke) shows how. (General
  practice.)

## Traps

- **"It's only like for like."** Until the box has an extra wire in it.
- **"The light switch is off."** A switch is a control, not proof that
  the box is dead.
- **"I'll reset it again."** A breaker that trips twice is reporting a
  fault.
- **"A bigger breaker will stop the tripping."** It will, by letting the
  wire overheat instead.
- **"It works, so it's right."** A wiring mistake can leave everything
  working while something unexpected is live. NIOSH reports an
  apprentice killed because a fixture he had cross-wired by mistake
  carried power from a live circuit into the one he was working on.
- **"It's quicker live."** NIOSH's answer, in a case where a worker
  grabbed two live wires while looking for a fault in two lamps: "No work
  should be done on energized electrical circuits."
- **"An extension cord will do for now."** "For now" becomes permanent,
  under a rug, with a heater on it.
- **"The utility's wire is just a wire."** The service drop and the meter
  are the utility's, and nobody else's to touch.

## How the game models it

The game lets anyone wire anything, and it cannot hurt you.

- **Wiring on the Construction page.** You connect machines with power
  runs and choose each run's cable, and the Buildability section's
  Conduits check tells you whether the cable is big enough for its load
  and length (how it decides is in [Electricity and How It
  Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows)). That check is the nearest the
  game comes to an inspection: a design check on a plan.
- **Who may edit what.** In the default Dev play mode you can edit the
  ship's own wiring as well as your home's; outside Dev, the ship's wiring
  is shown but locked, and only your home's is yours to change
  (`src/gui/pages/construction.rs`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there are
no permits, licences or inspections; no utility, meter or service drop;
no breakers, fuses or GFCIs; no shock, no burns and no electrical fires;
and nothing you wire in the game is ever live while you work on it.
Nothing in the game teaches you that you may do any of this in a real
house.

## You own this when

- You know where your panel is, every breaker is labelled, and you test
  the GFCIs and AFCIs every month.
- You reset a tripped breaker once, replace a fuse only with the same
  size, and call someone when either happens again.
- You check cords before use, never cut a ground pin, and never use an
  extension cord as permanent wiring.
- You never plug a generator into a wall outlet, and you know a transfer
  switch is an electrician's job.
- You know the warning signs (warmth, burning smells, flickering, a
  shock from an appliance) and you stop using the circuit and call an
  electrician.
- You never work on anything live, and without a tester and the training
  to use it you do not open up the wiring at all.
- You know what your own state, county or city allows a homeowner to do,
  and whether it needs a permit.
- You can say why the inside of a panel, new wiring, aluminium wiring,
  pools, generators, solar connections and overhead lines are not yours.
- You know who inspects electrical work where you live, and how to check
  an electrician's licence.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- That nobody in a household is qualified by default, that the
  inspection is the one time a second trained person sees the wiring,
  and that a house in Silverdale is inspected by L&I (our readings of
  OSHA's definition, Washington's law and L&I's city list).
- The list of conditions for a like-for-like job, and the things in a box
  that mean stop (our list, from the CPSC's and NIOSH's material).
- That switching off the main breaker leaves the incoming wires and their
  terminals live, that only the utility can switch those off, and that a
  generator for house circuits goes through an electrician's transfer
  switch.
- Cutting the power before reaching for anything that has fallen into
  water, and an electrician before switching on anything that has been
  flooded.
- Describing a symptom rather than a guess when calling an electrician.
- The lines this guide draws between the three kinds of work are our
  recommendation for a household, which in many places the law also
  draws.
