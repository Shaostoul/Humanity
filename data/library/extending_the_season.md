# Extending the Season

Your growing season is set by two dates, the last frost of spring and the
first frost of autumn, and by the daylight in between. You cannot move the
dates. You can change what the frost reaches. A sheet of fabric, a box with
a glass lid or a plastic tunnel keeps a few degrees of warmth around your
plants on a cold night, and a few degrees is often the difference between a
dead crop and a live one. Used well, that buys weeks at each end of the
year, and in mild places a garden that feeds you through the winter.

This guide covers the tools from the lightest to the heaviest: row covers,
cloches, cold frames and hotbeds, low tunnels, high tunnels and heated
greenhouses, then the crops that make the most of them, and growing indoors
under lights. It ends with what the covers cannot do, which is add
daylight.

Temperatures are given in degrees Fahrenheit with Celsius in brackets. A
temperature **difference** converts without the 32: a cover that keeps
plants 2 F warmer keeps them 1.1 C warmer, because each Fahrenheit degree
is five ninths of a Celsius one.

## Start from your frost dates

Every tool here is measured against your frost dates, so get those first.
[The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar) explains how to find yours
from NOAA's climate normals, and one point from it matters more than any
other: the average last frost date is a coin flip, finished in only half
of all years. Plan tender crops to the date that is finished in nine years
out of ten. For Silverdale, Washington, the worked example there, that is
April 24, nearly four weeks after the average of March 29.

The calendar also explains why the official number is optimistic. Frost
dates are measured at 32 F (0 C) in a shaded shelter at head height, but
on a clear, still night the ground and the leaves on it radiate their heat
to the open sky and get colder than the air above them, and cold air
drains downhill into hollows. Those clear, still nights are exactly the
ones covers are for.

## How a cover keeps a plant warm

By day the sun warms the soil. At night the soil gives that heat back,
and a cover holds some of it near the plants instead of letting it escape
to the sky. Iowa State University Extension describes a cold frame this
way: the sun heats the ground and the frame by day and they radiate the
heat at night, keeping the inside warmer. Purdue Extension says the same
of row covers inside a high tunnel, and adds a detail worth knowing: when
moisture condenses on the fabric, it insulates better.

Three things follow from that.

- **A cover works by holding heat the soil stored,** so it does the most
  good put on while the soil is still warm from the day, not after the
  night has already drawn the heat out.
- **It has to reach the ground,** or the warm air slips out at the sides.
  Anchor the edges with soil, boards, bricks, sandbags or pins (University
  of Maryland Extension).
- **What keeps the heat in at night also traps it by day.** On a sunny day
  every covered space heats up fast, and Iowa State warns that a cold frame
  left shut can get hot enough to kill the plants inside. Venting is half
  of the skill.

## Row covers

A row cover is a light, white, non-woven fabric of spun polyester or
polypropylene that lets air, light and water through (University of
Maryland Extension, the main source for this section). It can be laid
straight on the plants, which push it up as they grow ("floating"), or held
above them on hoops. It comes in weights, and the weight sets how much
frost it stops and how much light it takes away:

| Grade | Weight | Light let through | Frost protection |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lightweight | 0.45 oz per square yard (about 15 g per square metre) | 90 to 95 percent | 2 F (1.1 C) |
| Medium | 0.5 to 1.0 oz per square yard (about 17 to 34 g per square metre) | 70 to 85 percent | 4 to 6 F (2.2 to 3.3 C) |
| Heavy | 1.5 to 2.2 oz per square yard (about 51 to 75 g per square metre) | 30 to 50 percent | up to 8 F (4.4 C) |

The lightweight grade is sold as an insect barrier and can stay on
some crops (beets, snap beans, salad greens) from sowing to harvest. The
medium grade is for spring and autumn crops and for overwintering; the
heavy grade for overwintering. Maryland recommends hoops under the medium
and heavy grades. The University of Wisconsin's figures, for row cover in
general, are plants kept 5 to 10 F (2.8 to 5.6 C) warmer than uncovered
ones, and frost protection down to about 28 F (-2.2 C).

Before you rely on one, know its costs, all from Maryland:

- **Heat.** In summer the air under a row cover can be 5 to 15 F (2.8 to
  8.3 C) warmer than outside, and beans, tomatoes and peppers can drop
  their flowers and young fruit once the day passes 90 F (32 C). So a
  summer cover needs watching, or swapping for insect netting.
- **Pollination.** Bees cannot get in. Crops that need insects to carry
  their pollen must be uncovered when they flower; crops that do not, such
  as spring leafy greens and green beans, can stay covered to harvest.
  (Cucumbers and squash are the usual examples of the first kind: Utah State
  finds they set fruit poorly even in the middle of a high tunnel, where
  bees rarely go.)
- **Trapped pests.** A cover keeps new insects out but shuts in any already
  there, including those that spent the winter in the soil beneath, such as
  root maggot flies, flea beetles and Colorado potato beetles. Check the
  plants under it.
- **Weeds** grow faster under it too. **Watering**: rain and hose water pass
  through, but it is better to pull the cover back to hand-water, or to run
  drip underneath. **Storage**: mice nest in stored fabric, so keep it in a
  sealed container. **Life**: one to three years, depending on the grade and
  the weather, and as it weathers it sheds plastic fibres.

In winter, light is the price of warmth. Purdue measured it inside high
tunnels in northwest Indiana: a lightweight row cover left over the crop
all day cut the light reaching the plants from 72 percent of the outdoor
level to 48 percent, about a third less. So in cold weather take heavier
covers off during the day when you can, and put them back before evening.

## Cloches

A cloche is any clear cover over a single plant: the University of
Wisconsin's examples are a clear plastic jug with the bottom cut off and a
water wall, a ring of water-filled plastic tubes. Hot caps are the conical
kind. Wisconsin says cloches and hot caps
are used mostly for tomatoes and peppers and add three to four weeks to the
spring season, and that, like every other cover, they must be vented on
sunny days to let the hot air out. Lift it, or take the cap off the jug.

## Cold frames and hotbeds

A cold frame is a bottomless box with a clear, hinged lid, set on the soil
and heated only by the sun. Iowa State's guidance:

- **Build it** no more than 3 feet (90 cm) from front to back so you can
  reach the far side, about 12 inches (30 cm) high at the front and 18
  inches (45 cm) at the back, so the lid slopes to the sun. Old windows, a
  shower door or clear rigid plastic make the lid. Avoid wood treated with
  creosote or similar chemicals.
- **Place it** facing south, on well-drained ground that does not flood,
  with the north side against a house, garage or fence to cut heat loss,
  and near water.
- **Vent it.** Open the lid when the inside passes 85 F (29 C) and close it
  before sunset. Check it often, even on cloudy days, because the inside
  heats fast. Wax-filled automatic openers push the lid up as it warms and
  let it down as it cools. Banking straw bales or soil around the frame
  evens out the swings.
- **What it buys:** two to four weeks at each end of the season. Lettuce,
  spinach and radishes go in two to four weeks before their normal outdoor
  date in spring; in autumn, cool-season crops sown in late August or early
  September and shut in on frosty nights keep Iowa gardeners harvesting well
  into November.

A cold frame is also the easiest place to harden off transplants: close it
on cold nights, open it by day, and the plants get used to the outdoors
without being carried in and out.

Purdue Extension adds two useful points. Divide a frame into sections so you
can vent the cool-season seedlings while keeping warm-season seeds closed
up to germinate. And ventilating does more than cool: it helps prevent
damping-off, the disease that topples seedlings.

**A hotbed** is the same box with heat added from below, so it can raise
warm-season transplants such as tomatoes and peppers, which grow more
slowly in a cold frame. Purdue's modern version is an insulated electric
heating cable, about 2 feet of cable for each square foot of bed (about 6.5
metres per square metre), laid in loops 6 inches (15 cm) apart on a layer
of sand or cinders, covered with soil, and run by a thermostat. The traditional
hotbed was heated by a thick layer of fresh manure rotting under the soil.
The University of Wisconsin no longer recommends that, because of the
safety concerns with fresh manure, particularly for food crops.

## Low tunnels

A low tunnel is a row cover or clear plastic held on short hoops over a bed.
Maryland's easy version is 15 to 30 inches (40 to 75 cm) high with hoops 2
to 4 feet (60 to 120 cm) apart. Its simplest hoop is a 7-foot (2.1 m) length
of #9 gauge wire pushed about 8 inches (20 cm) into the soil on each side
of the bed, which makes an arch 3 feet (90 cm) wide and 2 feet (60 cm)
tall. Plastic pipe slid over short rebar stakes works too. Hoops let you use
the heavier row cover grades without crushing the crop, and in cold weather
they can carry clear plastic for more warmth; in summer, shade cloth or
insect netting.

The University of Wisconsin puts hoop houses, the larger version, at six to
eight extra weeks of season, and says they must be vented on warm days by
lifting the plastic at the ends or opening it along the sides or top. Small
tunnels swing hardest: Purdue photographed spinach in a low tunnel that
reached 80 F (27 C) the day after a night low near 20 F (-7 C), its leaves
bleached and badly cupped, which Purdue says could be heat injury.

## High tunnels

A high tunnel is a walk-in hoop house, usually unheated, covered in plastic
and vented by hand: Utah State University Extension describes them as 14 to
30 feet wide and 50 to 150 feet long, tall enough to stand in, and not
greenhouses, because most have no heater and rely on rolled-up sides and
open doors for cooling. What they give, and what they ask:

- **Earlier crops.** Utah State transplants cold-sensitive crops such as
  tomatoes and peppers 3 to 4 weeks before the earliest outdoor date, or 5
  to 6 weeks early with row covers or low tunnels inside the tunnel.
- **A thermometer.** Keep a maximum and minimum thermometer inside and
  track it (Utah State). On a sunny winter day a closed tunnel can go from
  below freezing to over 80 F (27 C) in a few hours; Purdue measured it, and
  warns that the swing is worst when the soil is frozen and the roots cannot
  replace the water the heat pulls out of the leaves.
- **Double covers in deep cold.** Purdue notes that row covers are often put
  over the crops inside the tunnel when the outside temperature drops below
  the mid-20s F (about -4 C).
- **Venting against damp.** With the tunnel shut, the air fills with water
  from the soil and leaves, which invites disease. Purdue: small openings
  for cross ventilation are often enough when cooling is not needed, placed
  high in the end walls so frigid air does not blow straight onto the crop.
  Utah State notes that a well-ventilated tunnel, which also keeps the rain
  off the leaves, has little disease, though powdery mildew, bacterial
  diseases and root rots can still take hold when it is shut up in cool
  weather.
- **Summer shade.** Utah State takes the plastic off when the weather warms
  and replaces it with 30 percent shade cloth.
- **Bees.** They do not like flying into tunnels, so cucumbers and squash
  set fruit well at the edges and poorly in the middle (Utah State).
- **Snow.** Remove heavy, wet snow from the roof; left there, it drips cold
  water down the side walls inside (Utah State). The University of Vermont's
  growers add: clear both sides evenly, a little at a time, so the hoops are
  not loaded on one side, and brace weak hoops with temporary posts.

## Greenhouses: heat, air and water

A greenhouse is a structure with its own heating and ventilation, so the
grower sets the climate instead of borrowing it. That brings three jobs:
keeping it warm, keeping it from cooking, and keeping it from getting damp.
The University of Georgia's greenhouse bulletin (B 792) and UMass Extension
are the sources here unless noted.

**How warm.** Georgia gives most plants days of 70 to 80 F (21 to 27 C) and
cooler nights. Cool-season crops such as lettuce, beet, carrot, parsley and
radish grow best with nights near 50 F (10 C); tomatoes want nights near 65
F (18 C). Since the heat a greenhouse loses grows with the difference
between inside and outside, a cool house for greens costs far less to heat
through a winter night than a warm one for tomatoes.

**Heat loss.** A greenhouse loses heat through its glazing, through air
leaking in and out, and by radiating to the night sky. Georgia's rule for
sizing a heater: design for the average daily minimum January temperature
less 15 F (8.3 C); a site on a windy hill needs about 20 percent more heat.
It also advises two smaller heaters rather than one large one, so a
failure does not freeze the crop, and an alarm that warns of a heating
failure or a power cut.

**Which heater.** Georgia accepts any heating system that holds an even
temperature without releasing anything harmful to the plants: natural gas,
propane, fuel oil, wood or electricity. The catch is in "without
releasing". A UMass fact sheet sets out the problems with **unvented**
heaters, which have no flue and send all their exhaust into the greenhouse.
The fuels are sold as clean-burning, and UMass says that holds only while
the burner is clean and well adjusted; a heater serviced in autumn will
probably not be burning at its best by spring. What comes out:

- The exhaust carries carbon monoxide and nitrogen dioxide, and sulfur
  dioxide, a major pollutant from kerosene that every fuel gives off in
  traces, which can injure some plants at half a part per million.
- It carries ethylene, which at a hundredth of a part per million can
  deform leaves and flowers, stunt growth and make buds drop.
- It carries water. For a 30 by 100 foot double-plastic house held at 60 F
  (16 C) through a 12-hour night at 0 F (-18 C), UMass works out about 22
  gallons (83 litres) of water added by unvented propane or kerosene heat,
  which, on top of what the plants and soil give off, could keep the
  humidity above 90 percent, where disease thrives.
- A tight house starves the flame of air, which makes it burn dirtier.
  UMass calls for a fresh air inlet of at least 1 square inch for every
  1,000 Btu an hour of heater input (about 6.5 square centimetres per 290
  watts).

UMass's advice, if you do install one, is plenty of makeup air, frequent
maintenance, and indicator plants (tomatoes and white petunias can show
sulfur dioxide injury in as little as an hour) or indicator tubes near the
heater. It also notes that a badly burning heater puts out pollutants that
can affect the people working in the greenhouse as well as the plants.

The CDC describes carbon monoxide as a gas with no colour and no smell
that can kill without warning; it lists headache, dizziness, weakness,
upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion as its common symptoms.
Its rules for homes include never burning anything in a stove or
fireplace that is not vented, and a battery-powered or battery-backed
carbon monoxide detector near every sleeping area. A closed greenhouse
traps the gas the same way a closed room does. The safe course that
follows from both (this guide's reading, not a rule either source states
for greenhouses): heat with a vented heater or with electricity; keep a
carbon monoxide alarm where people work; and if a tunnel ever has to be
saved on a frost night with a portable fuel heater (Utah State lists
clean-burning propane heaters among the ways to do it), do not work or
stay inside while it burns, and air the tunnel out before going back in.
If the alarm sounds, or someone feels those symptoms in a heated
greenhouse, get everyone out into fresh air first and call emergency
services from outside, as [Firewood](/library#firewood) advises for a home; that
guide has the full treatment of carbon monoxide.

**Cooling.** Ventilation removes heat, brings in carbon dioxide for the
plants, and carries out damp air. Georgia's figures for fan ventilation: one
complete change of air a minute keeps the inside about 8 F (4.4 C) above the
outside in summer, half that rate lets it rise about 15 F (8.3 C), and
two changes a minute hold it to about 5 F (2.8 C). For vents instead of
fans, the ridge vents should equal about a quarter of the floor area, with
side vents about the same. Shade screens cut the sunlight by about half,
at the cost of light the plants could have used.

**Humidity and disease.** One of the commonest greenhouse diseases is gray
mold, Botrytis, which covers infected flowers, fruit, leaves and dead plant
material with fuzzy gray spores. UMass gives the conditions it needs to
infect: a film of water on the plant for 8 to 12 hours, relative humidity
of 85 percent or more, and temperatures of 55 to 75 F (13 to 24 C). It is worst in spring and
autumn. The film of water forms in the evening, when warm, humid air cools
and its humidity climbs until dew forms. The remedy UMass gives is cheap:
in the evening, turn on the heat and open the vents for 5 to 10 minutes, so
the warm wet air goes out and cool outside air comes in and is warmed,
which drops its humidity; repeat it through the evening if needed, or put
it on a timer. Also keep air moving with fans, space plants so air flows
between them, water in the morning, and clear out dead leaves and plant
debris. [Pests and Disease](/library#pests-and-disease) covers the rest.

## Choosing crops that take the cold

Covers buy degrees; the crop decides how many degrees you need. South
Dakota State University Extension sorts vegetables by how much cold they
take near maturity:

| Tolerance | Crops |
|---|---|
| **No frost at all.** Protect from, or harvest before, even a light frost | Beans, sweet corn, potatoes, peppers, eggplant, cucumbers, summer squash, melons, sweet potatoes (ideally dug before the soil falls below 50 F, 10 C). Tomatoes, which can be picked at the mature green stage and ripened indoors |
| **Light freezes**, 28 to 32 F (-2.2 to 0 C) | Beets, carrots, cauliflower, lettuce, onions, garlic, chives, parsley, peas, radishes, rhubarb, spinach, Swiss chard |
| **Moderate freezes**, 24 to 28 F (-4.4 to -2.2 C) | The cabbage family: broccoli and cauliflower to about 26 F (-3.3 C); Chinese cabbage, cabbage, kale, kohlrabi, turnips |
| **Best after several moderate freezes** | Parsnips, salsify, Brussels sprouts |
| **Best after several severe freezes**, below 24 F (-4.4 C) | Horseradish, which can stay in the ground under mulch over winter |

South Dakota State adds that some crops tolerate brief chilling better if
warm days follow: pumpkins can take 35 F (1.7 C) nights if the days reach
70 F (21 C). And some improve with it: root crops grow sweeter when the soil
is below 40 F (4.4 C), and Brussels sprouts are often best after a light
freeze.

Two things decide whether a hardy crop survives a given night.

**Acclimation.** Purdue explains that plants harden to cold by growing
through cool weather above freezing, making their own antifreeze and
changing their cell chemistry. An autumn crop that has lived through
nights in the 40s and 30s F (the single digits C, falling to near
freezing) can survive a freeze that would kill the same crop moved
straight from a warm greenhouse. Unacclimated plants cannot survive
freezing at all.

**Leave frozen plants alone.** In the low 20s F (about -6 C), water freezes
between the cells and the leaves go stiff. The sharp ice damages the cells
if the leaves are handled, or whipped by wind. In a sheltered tunnel,
Purdue says, plants left undisturbed thaw as the day warms, recover, and
can be harvested undamaged. Wait until they thaw.

**Bolting.** A crop that flowers stops making the leaves or roots you want.
Purdue warns that kale, mustard, bok choy and others of the mustard family
planted too early in the year are likely to be pushed into flowering while
still small by the cold, and that spinach is triggered by long days and
lettuce by heat together with long days, so spring-sown greens run to seed
as summer comes.

**Choosing within a crop.** Days to maturity matter more at the ends of
the season than in the middle, because growth slows as it cools. The
autumn arithmetic, with its allowance for slower growth, is in
[The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar): for a late sowing, pick
the variety that finishes soonest.

## What no cover can add: light

A cover can keep a plant warm. It cannot make the day longer or the sun
stronger. Purdue measured the light in northern Indiana: from mid-November
to mid-February, the daily light was usually under 10 moles per square
metre, and that period lines up closely with the weeks of fewer than 10
hours of daylight, which the winter grower Eliot Coleman named the
Persephone period. In those weeks, Purdue says, light can limit growth
even when the crop is warm enough.

So winter under cover is mostly for holding crops, not growing them. Purdue
describes a winter tunnel as, for crops such as lettuce heads, napa cabbage,
radishes, carrots and turnips, cold storage for live plants. Sow the autumn
crops early enough that they are nearly full size as the days shorten past
ten hours, then harvest from them through the winter.
[The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar) gives Silverdale's dates
for that threshold.

## Growing indoors under lights

Indoors you supply the light yourself, and three separate ideas decide
whether it is enough. The University of New Hampshire Extension explains
them well:

- **How much light a day**, the daily light integral (DLI): the number of
  photons that land on a square metre over a whole day, counted in moles.
  This is what grows the plant.
- **How strong the light is at any moment**, the intensity, in micromoles
  of photons per square metre per second. A horticultural light meter
  measures it at the leaves.
- **How long the day is**, the photoperiod. The plant reads it as the
  season, and it mostly governs flowering, not growth.

They connect by simple arithmetic: daily light integral = intensity x hours
of light x 3,600 seconds, divided by a million. A lamp giving 200 micromoles
at the leaves for 16 hours delivers 200 x 16 x 3,600 / 1,000,000 = 11.5
moles a day.

How much is enough:

- **Seedlings.** New Hampshire works out its lamp run times for seedlings
  wanting about 12 moles a day, and gives a feel for the gap between
  sources: a tomato seedling can get its day's light from about
  five hours of full sun, but would need about 22 hours under a four-tube
  fluorescent shop light, even with the tubes kept within a foot of the
  tops. A high-end horticultural LED might do it in 10 hours; a cheap one
  could not do it in a day at all.
- **Lettuce.** Cornell's controlled-environment programme found 17 moles a
  day worked best for the Boston bibb cultivar it grew, and warns that at
  high light lettuce gets tipburn, brown leaf edges, unless air is blown
  down onto the plants; without that air movement Cornell had to hold the
  light down to about 12.
- **Darkness.** More hours are not always better. Michigan State's Erik
  Runkle notes that some crops, tomato especially, are stressed and
  yellow under continuous light, and suggests four to six hours of
  darkness each night.

**Distance matters more than people expect.** Light weakens quickly as the
lamp moves away. New Hampshire measured one LED light bar that gave
seedlings their day's light in 8 hours from 8 inches (20 cm) above, but
needed 16 hours from 20 inches (50 cm). Keep the lights close and raise
them as the plants grow; watch for hot spots, such as the ballast end of an
old fluorescent fixture, which can scorch plants under it.

**Let the plants tell you.** Seedlings short of light stretch: long, thin
stems with wide gaps between the leaves. Once a stem has stretched, more
light will not shorten it, though it can stop more stretching (New
Hampshire). The fixes are a better lamp, more lamps, a closer lamp, or
longer hours.

To reach a given daily light in a set number of hours, turn the arithmetic
around: 17 moles in an 18-hour day needs an average of 17,000,000 / (18 x
3,600) = about 260 micromoles per square metre per second at the leaves;
the same 17 moles in 12 hours needs about 390.

## A year of it, worked

- **Late winter:** start transplants indoors under lights, close to the
  lamps, with a few hours of darkness a night.
- **Early spring:** sow lettuce, spinach and radishes in the cold frame two
  to four weeks before their outdoor date. Move transplants into it to
  harden off, closing it on cold nights and venting it above 85 F (29 C).
- **Mid-spring:** tomatoes and peppers go into the high tunnel 3 to 4 weeks
  before the outdoor date, or outdoors under cloches or a low tunnel, with
  row cover ready for a late frost. Uncover squash and cucumbers when they
  flower.
- **Summer:** take covers off, or swap them for netting and shade cloth.
- **Late summer:** sow the autumn and winter greens and roots, early enough
  to be near full size before the days fall under ten hours.
- **Autumn:** have row cover out and reachable by the early frost date, not
  the average one. Pick tomatoes at the mature green stage before a frost.
- **Winter:** harvest the hardy crops under double cover, vent on sunny
  days, leave frozen plants alone until they thaw, and keep snow off the
  tunnel.

## How the game models it

The garden in this project models the pieces the sources above measure, and
its numbers come from them.

- **Row cover** (`data/garden/pests.ron`). A piece covers about 12 square
  metres of soil. It warms the crops under it by 5.6 C (10 F, the middle of
  Maryland's 5 to 15 F) in the sun and by 1.1 C (2 F, the lightweight
  grade's frost protection) at night and indoors, and both the crops' growth
  and the pests read that warmth (with the garden's pests setting switched
  on). It keeps about 90 percent of arriving aphids, mites, whiteflies,
  cabbage caterpillars and potato beetles off (thrips a little less), but
  shuts in whatever was already there. Bees cannot reach the flowers, so
  the game warns you when it goes over a crop that needs insects to set
  fruit. It wears out after a year in the weather, the low end of Maryland's
  one to three years, and taking it off returns it to your pack.
- **Outdoor fields and the weather** (`src/systems/farming/mod.rs`). A field
  crop grows at a quarter speed out of its season, and a degree outside its
  temperature range slows it by 5 percent, down to a fifth of full speed.
  The indoor grow rooms are climate controlled and skip the weather.
- **Greenhouse humidity** (`data/garden/humidity.ron`). Almost all the water
  the crops are given, they breathe into their room's air. A room exchanges
  half its air an hour through leaks, the low end of Georgia's figure for
  new double-plastic houses, and where a room has an exhaust fan it runs to
  hold the air at 80 percent, five points under UMass's line for gray mold.
  At 85 percent the game tells you, and the Ventilate control does UMass's
  heat-and-vent: one
  change of air, which clears about two thirds of the extra damp. Gray mold
  and the mildews follow damp rooms (`data/garden/pests.ron`).
- **Grow lights** (`data/garden/lighting.ron`). A light's output is its watts
  times 2.30 micromoles per joule, the minimum efficiency an LED grow light
  must reach to be listed by the DesignLights Consortium (its horticultural
  requirements, version 3.0, in effect since 31 March 2023). It covers as
  much bed as it can light to each crop's own daily light need, measured
  against Cornell's 17 moles
  for lettuce, and it runs on a timer from sunset that leaves six hours of
  darkness, after Runkle. The mushrooms need no light at all.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: frost
slows a crop but never kills it; there are no cloches, cold frames,
tunnels or greenhouse heaters to build, and the row cover comes in one
grade; the grow rooms sit at a fixed 21 C (70 F) inside the station and
keeping them warm costs nothing; and the sun rises at 6:00 and sets at
18:00 every day of the year, so there are no short winter days and no
Persephone period to plan around.
