# Keeping What You Grew

A garden does not hand you food evenly. It hands you nothing for months
and then, over about three weeks, more than you can eat. Preserving is
what turns that spike into a supply, and it is the difference between a
garden that decorates your summer and a garden that feeds your winter.

There are four methods worth a beginner's time, and they are not
interchangeable. Drying and freezing are easy and forgiving. Root
cellaring is nearly free and requires only the right corner of a
building. Canning is the one with real stakes.

**Read this before anything else.** Home canning done wrong can kill
people. The organism is *Clostridium botulinum*, and the poison it makes
cannot be seen, smelled or tasted. The National Center for Home Food
Preservation, which publishes the USDA's tested procedures, states it
without cushioning: "Even a taste of food containing this toxin can be
fatal."

That risk is entirely manageable, because it is entirely understood. It
is managed by following tested procedures exactly and by never improvising
one. This guide gives you the safety architecture, one complete tested
canning procedure, and clear boundaries around everything it does not
teach. Where a method is outside its scope, it says so and points you to
where the real instructions live, rather than teaching you half of it.

## The one rule that everything else hangs on

Some foods are acidic enough that *C. botulinum* cannot grow in them.
Everything else is not, and requires far more heat to make safe.

NCHFP draws the line at **pH 4.6**. Acid foods have a pH of 4.6 or lower;
low-acid foods are above it.

- **Acid foods**, in NCHFP's list: "Fruits, pickles, sauerkraut, jams,
  jellies, marmalades, and fruit butters."
- **Low-acid foods**, in NCHFP's list: "Red meats, seafood, poultry,
  milk, and all fresh vegetables except for most tomatoes."

Read that second list again. **All fresh vegetables.** Green beans, corn,
carrots, potatoes, peas, squash, beets, greens. Every one of them is
low-acid.

Now the heat. A boiling water canner reaches the temperature of boiling
water, 212 F at sea level, and no higher; that is a physical ceiling, not
a matter of boiling longer. A pressure canner reaches 240 to 250 F,
operating at 10 to 15 pounds of pressure. NCHFP: "Botulinum spores are
very hard to destroy at boiling-water temperatures; the higher the canner
temperature, the more easily they are destroyed."

Which produces the rule:

> **Low-acid foods CANNOT be safely canned in a boiling water bath. They
> require a pressure canner.** NCHFP: "Pressure canning is the only
> recommended method for canning meat, poultry, seafood, and vegetables,"
> and "using boiling water canners for these foods poses a real risk of
> botulism poisoning."

Green beans, corn, carrots and meat in a water-bath canner are not a
shortcut and not an old family tradition worth continuing. They are the
classic route to botulism. A sealed jar is exactly the low-oxygen
environment the organism wants: NCHFP lists its growth conditions as a
moist low-acid food, a temperature between 40 and 120 F, and less than 2
percent oxygen. A sealed jar of green beans on a shelf is all three.

**This guide does not teach pressure canning.** Pressure canning is safe,
well documented, and genuinely worth learning, but it needs specific
equipment, gauge testing, and a tested time and pressure for every single
food, and it is not something to learn from a general article. Learn it
from the USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning or from NCHFP directly, both
linked in Sources, or from a class at your local extension office. Note
also that NCHFP does not support using USDA canning processes in electric
multicookers, even models with a "canning" button, because the processes
were developed and tested for stovetop pressure canners.

## Method 1: Freezing (easiest, start here)

Freezing keeps quality better than any other home method, and unlike
canning it introduces no hazard of its own. What it does not do is kill
anything. Freezing pauses spoilage; it does not sterilize. Food that was
questionable going in is questionable coming out, and it starts spoiling
again the moment it thaws, which is why frozen food is thawed in the
refrigerator or under cold running water and not left out on the counter.

**Temperature.** Colorado State University Extension: 0 degrees F or
lower. A freezer compartment inside a refrigerator often runs warmer than
that, which is why food stored there declines faster.

**Blanching, and why it is not optional.** Vegetables carry enzymes that
kept them growing, and those enzymes keep working in the freezer. CSU:
after maturity, they "cause loss of quality, flavor, color, texture, and
nutrients." Blanching, a brief scald, "slows or stops the enzyme action."
Skip it and your green beans turn khaki and tasteless by January.

CSU's method:

1. **Boiling water blanch:** at least 1 gallon of rapidly boiling water in
   a covered kettle. "Start counting time as soon as the vegetables are in
   the boiling water."
2. **Or steam blanch:** 1 to 2 inches of boiling water, vegetables held at
   least 3 inches above the bottom of the kettle. "Start counting steaming
   time as soon as the lid is on."
3. **Cool immediately.** Plunge the basket straight into a large quantity
   of cold water at 60 F or below, using about 1 pound of ice for each
   pound of vegetables. Cooling matters as much as the scald; vegetables
   left to cool slowly keep cooking.
4. **Drain, pack, freeze.**

Times are per vegetable and per method, and they are short. CSU's
examples: green beans 4 minutes in water; broccoli 4 minutes in water or
6 in steam; diced carrots 3 minutes in water.

**Read that table's own footnote before you use those numbers, because it
is a good lesson in where a published figure comes from.** Colorado State
notes that its "blanching times given are for 5,000 feet or higher. At
altitudes below 5000 feet, subtract one minute from times given." Colorado
is a high-altitude state and wrote its table for its own readers. Most of
the world is below 5,000 feet, so for most readers those three examples
are 3 minutes, 3 minutes (5 steaming), and 2 minutes.

Get the full list from the CSU page in Sources, or from your own
extension service, and check whose altitude a table was written for.
Guessing a blanching time costs you quality rather than safety, but there
is no reason to guess.

**Packing.** CSU: leave 1/2 inch headspace at the top of rigid containers
so the food can expand, and fill freezer bags to within 3 inches of the
top. Press the air out of bags; air is what causes freezer burn.

**How long.** CSU: "Most vegetables maintain high quality for 12 to 18
months at 0 degrees F or lower." That is a quality window, not a safety
deadline. Food held continuously at 0 F stays safe indefinitely; it just
stops being nice to eat.

**The honest limitation.** Freezing depends on the power staying on. A
freezer full of the year's harvest is a single point of failure, which is
exactly why the other three methods in this guide exist.

## Method 2: Drying

Drying removes the water that spoilage organisms need. It stores at room
temperature, takes almost no space, and needs no power once the food is
dry.

**Temperature.** Penn State Extension: start at 145 F "when there is
surface moisture on the fruit or vegetable," then "reduce the temperature
to 135 to 140 F to finish drying." Starting hot while the surface is wet
is safe because evaporation keeps the food itself cool; keeping it hot
after that cooks the outside shut and traps moisture inside, a failure
called case hardening, and food that is still damp in the middle will
mold in the jar.

**Equipment.** A food dehydrator is the straightforward answer. An oven
works only if yours will genuinely hold a low temperature: Penn State
says you need to set it to 140 to 150 F and prop the door open 2 to 3
inches. Many ovens will not go that low.

**Sun drying is region-dependent, and this is a good example of why you
should look up local guidance rather than trusting any single article.**
Penn State says outright that "the high humidity in Pennsylvania makes
this method impractical here." In a hot dry climate it is a traditional
and effective method. The variable is your humidity, not your intent.

**Knowing when it is done.** Penn State: fruit is done when it is
"pliable and no beads of moisture form when pressed between your
fingers." Vegetables are done when "tough, brittle, or crunchy." Test a
piece after it has cooled, because warm food always feels softer.

**Conditioning.** Pieces never dry evenly. Penn State: pack the cooled
dried fruit loosely into an airtight glass or plastic container for
several days so the remaining moisture spreads out evenly. Check daily.
If you see condensation inside the jar, it is not dry enough; put it back
in the dehydrator.

**Insects.** Dried food from a home dryer can carry insect eggs. Penn
State's treatment: heat in the oven at 160 F for 30 minutes, or freeze at
0 F or below for 48 hours, then store.

**Storage.** Airtight, cool, dark. Cooler storage extends life
substantially.

## Method 3: Root cellaring (the one that costs nothing)

Some crops need no processing at all. They need the right corner. The
insight worth carrying away is that there is no single set of "storage
conditions"; there are three distinct climates, and putting a crop in the
wrong one is why home storage usually fails.

Iowa State University Extension's figures:

| Crop | Temperature | Relative humidity | Typical storage life |
|---|---|---|---|
| Potatoes | 40 F | 90 to 95 percent | 5 to 8 months |
| Carrots | 32 to 35 F | 98 to 100 percent | 4 to 6 months |
| Beets | 32 to 35 F | 95 percent | 1 to 3 months |
| Onions | 32 to 40 F | 65 to 70 percent | 5 to 8 months |
| Winter squash | 50 to 55 F | (dry, well ventilated) | acorn 5 to 8 weeks, butternut 2 to 3 months, Hubbard 5 to 6 months |

The three climates:

- **Cold and very damp** for root crops. Carrots want humidity so high it
  is nearly condensing. Packed in damp sand or sawdust in a box is the
  traditional way to get there.
- **Cold and dry** for onions and garlic. Same temperature as the roots,
  opposite humidity. They must not share a container with carrots.
- **Cool and dry** for winter squash and pumpkins, notably warmer than
  the others. A refrigerator ruins winter squash.

Two rules that apply throughout. Keep potatoes in the dark; light turns
them green and bitter. And keep everything away from apples and pears,
which give off a ripening gas that shortens storage life for potatoes and
squash alike.

Most homes have at least one of these climates somewhere: an unheated
attached garage, a basement corner away from the furnace, an insulated
crawl space, a spare room kept closed. Buy a cheap thermometer, put it
where you are considering, and watch it for a week before you commit a
harvest to it.

## Method 4: Water-bath canning, acid foods only

Now the one with rules. Everything below applies **only** to acid foods:
fruits, jams, jellies, pickles, sauerkraut, and tomatoes with acid added.
Nothing else. Do not adapt it.

### Tomatoes and the acid problem

Tomatoes sit right on the line, and they used to sit on the safe side of
it. They no longer reliably do. As extension services now explain, newer
varieties, over-mature fruit and tomatoes from frost-killed or dead vines
can have a pH above 4.6, so tomatoes are no longer treated as acid foods,
and the USDA requires acid to be added before processing.

NCHFP's amounts, added directly to the jars before filling:

| Jar size | Bottled lemon juice | OR citric acid | OR 5 percent vinegar |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pint | 1 tablespoon | 1/4 teaspoon | 2 tablespoons |
| Quart | 2 tablespoons | 1/2 teaspoon | 4 tablespoons |

NCHFP notes vinegar "may cause undesirable flavor changes."

**It must be bottled lemon juice, not fresh.** This is not fussiness.
Bottled lemon juice is standardized to a known acidity under FDA rules,
while the acidity of a fresh lemon varies with variety, ripeness and
growing conditions, and Iowa State Extension notes that fresh juice pH
varies enough that you cannot rely on it for a safety margin. A lemon off
your own tree is a lovely thing and it is not a measuring instrument.

Never reduce the acid because the result tastes sharp. Add sugar if you
must; the acid is not a seasoning. And note that acidification is
required for tomatoes regardless of which canner you use. NCHFP is
explicit that when a tomato procedure offers both a boiling water and a
pressure option, every preparation step still applies, acidification
included. Choosing the pressure canner does not excuse you from the
lemon juice.

**Use jars made for home canning.** NCHFP's procedures assume Mason-type
canning jars with two-piece lids. Repurposed commercial food jars,
mayonnaise jars and the like are not built for repeated heat cycling and
break more often, sometimes during processing.

### A complete tested procedure: crushed tomatoes

This is NCHFP's process, given in full so you can see what "tested
procedure" means in practice.

1. Wash the tomatoes and dip them in boiling water for 30 to 60 seconds,
   or until the skins split. Dip in cold water, slip off the skins, and
   remove the cores. Trim any damaged areas and quarter them.
2. Heat about one-sixth of the pieces rapidly while crushing them to
   release juice. Add the rest, continuing to heat and stir until the
   whole pot is boiling, then keep it at a gentle boil for 5 minutes.
3. Add the bottled lemon juice or citric acid to each jar, in the amounts
   in the table above. Optionally add 1 teaspoon of salt per quart.
4. Fill the jars immediately with the hot tomatoes, leaving 1/2 inch
   headspace. Wipe the rims, apply the lids.
5. Process in a boiling water canner for the time in this table:

| Altitude | Pints | Quarts |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 1,000 ft | 35 min | 45 min |
| 1,001 to 3,000 ft | 40 min | 50 min |
| 3,001 to 6,000 ft | 45 min | 55 min |
| Above 6,000 ft | 50 min | 60 min |

**Altitude is not optional.** Water boils cooler as you go up, so the
same minutes deliver less heat. Look up your elevation once and write it
inside your cupboard door.

### Running the canner

NCHFP's boiling-water canner procedure:

- Fill the canner about half full with clean warm water for a load of
  pints, and preheat the water to 140 F for raw-packed foods or 180 F for
  hot-packed foods.
- Load the jars upright, using a jar lifter gripped below the neck.
- Add boiling water until it stands at least 1 inch above the jar tops,
  or 2 inches above for any process longer than 30 minutes. Add more
  boiling water during processing if the level drops.
- Start the timer only after the water is boiling, and time the full
  number of minutes. If the water stops boiling at any point, bring it
  back to a boil and start the timing over from the beginning.
- When the time is up, turn off the heat and wait 5 minutes before
  removing jars, so the canner settles.
- Set jars on a towel or a cooling rack with at least an inch between
  them, away from drafts and cold surfaces.
- Leave them undisturbed for 12 to 24 hours. Do not tighten the ring
  bands and do not press the lid centers while they cool.
- Remove the ring bands from sealed jars, label them, and store in a
  cool, dry, dark place.

### What not to do, ever

- Do not water-bath any low-acid food. See the rule at the top.
- Do not scale, halve, double or otherwise adjust a tested recipe.
  Processing times are tied to the exact contents and jar size tested.
- Do not use a recipe without a tested source behind it. A recipe from
  social media, a blog, or a relative's notebook is not a tested process,
  however confident it sounds and however long it has been used without
  incident. Botulism is rare per jar, which is exactly why the anecdote
  "we always did it this way" is not evidence.
- Do not can pureed or mashed vegetables. Pumpkin and winter squash are a
  standard example: NCHFP has a tested process for them cubed and
  pressure canned, and none for puree or butter, because in a dense
  puree there is no way to know the center of the jar reached
  temperature. No tested process means do not do it, not do it carefully.
- Do not reuse the flat sealing lids. Rings are reusable; flats are not.

## When a jar goes wrong

**Do not taste it.** NCHFP: "Do not taste food from a jar with an
unsealed lid or food that shows signs of spoilage."

Signs, in NCHFP's words: swelled lids and broken seals; "streaks of dried
food originating at the top of the jar"; "rising air bubbles and unnatural
color"; "cotton-like mold growth (white, blue, black, or green)";
unnatural odors; spurting liquid on opening.

A jar can also be dangerous with no sign at all. That is the whole reason
the procedures are strict.

**Disposal, from NCHFP.** Wearing disposable rubber or heavy plastic
gloves, place the suspect containers on their sides in an 8-quart or
larger stock pot or boiling-water canner. Add water to cover them by at
least 1 inch. Heat to boiling and boil 30 minutes. Then cool, and discard
the containers, lids and food in the trash or at a landfill.

**Cleanup.** NCHFP: use 1 part unscented liquid household chlorine bleach
at 5 to 6 percent sodium hypochlorite to 5 parts clean water. Spray or
wet the contaminated surfaces, let stand 30 minutes, repeat, then rinse
thoroughly. Wash your hands afterward. Handle bleach in a ventilated
space, keep it away from children and pets, and never mix it with other
cleaners.

**One more safety margin.** NCHFP: "Boiling food 10 minutes at elevations
below 1,000 feet elevation should destroy this poison when it is
present," and "for elevations at and above 1,000 feet, add 1 additional
minute per 1,000 feet additional elevation." This is a sensible habit
before serving home-canned low-acid food that was properly pressure
canned. It is **not** a way to rescue food that was canned incorrectly,
and it is not a reason to relax any rule above. Anything showing signs of
spoilage gets the disposal procedure, not the stove.

## What can go wrong

- **Jars did not seal.** Common causes: a chipped jar rim, food or acid
  left on the rim before the lid went on, a reused flat lid, headspace
  wrong, or the ring tightened too hard. An unsealed jar is not a
  disaster; refrigerate it and eat it within a few days, or freeze it.
- **Liquid siphoned out during processing.** Usually the jars were cooled
  too fast or removed too soon. If the jar sealed, it is safe; it just
  looks poor. Wait the 5 minutes before removing jars next time.
- **Frozen vegetables lost color and taste.** Blanching was skipped or
  cut short, or the freezer runs above 0 F. Check with a thermometer.
- **Dried food molded in the jar.** It was not dry enough, or it was not
  conditioned. Redry the batch if you catch it early. Discard anything
  visibly moldy.
- **Root-cellared carrots went limp.** Not humid enough. Pack in damp
  sand next time, or in perforated bags.
- **Onions sprouted or rotted.** Too warm, or too humid because they were
  stored beside the roots. They need the cold dry corner.
- **Potatoes turned green.** Light got in. Store them in the dark and
  discard badly greened tubers.

## How you know it worked

- Every preserved item is labeled with what it is and the date.
- Nothing you canned is a low-acid food in a water-bath jar.
- Every canning recipe you used traces back to NCHFP, the USDA Complete
  Guide, or your extension service, without adjustments.
- Your frozen food is at 0 F, your dried food shows no condensation
  inside the jar, and your stored roots, onions and squash are each in
  the climate the table says they want.
- In February you open something you grew and it is good.

## The loop this closes

Growing Food You Can Live On covers producing a real harvest. This guide
is what stops that harvest turning into compost in September. Saving Your
Own Seeds means next year's crop does not start with a purchase. Together
they are a full year of food that runs on your labor and your ground
rather than on anyone's permission, which is the entire point of the
exercise.
