# Pressure in Water, Air and Steam

A household is full of things under pressure: the water in the pipes,
the air in the tyres, the steam in a pressure cooker, the gas in a
propane cylinder and an aerosol can, the water in the bottom of a deep
pool. Most of the time pressure is quiet and useful. When it gets loose
it is sudden: a lid that flies, a cylinder that bursts in a fire, a
stone that explodes in a campfire, an eardrum that tears underwater.

This guide explains what pressure is, how it behaves in water, air and
steam, and why a closed container that is heated can burst, with the
places a household meets it: the taps and the well, the tyres and the
compressor, cylinders and cans, pressure cookers and canners, and the
deep end. It says plainly which jobs belong to a professional.

Several guides cover parts of this, and it links to them rather than
repeating them. [Moving Water Without Power](/library#moving-water-without-power)
works through water pressure from height (head), siphons and suction
pumps. [Heating Water](/library#heating-water) covers the water heater's
temperature and pressure relief valve. [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules) explains why low-acid food needs a
pressure canner; this guide covers the canner as a pressure vessel.
[Reading the Weather](/library#reading-the-weather) covers the barometer,
[Floating and Boats](/library#floating-and-boats) buoyancy, and [Units and
Converting Them](/library#units-and-converting-them) the conversion factors.

Its sources are the Department of Energy's training handbook on
thermodynamics and fluid flow, NOAA, the National Institute of Standards
and Technology, the Occupational Safety and Health Administration
(OSHA), the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, the
Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the USDA's canning guide, the
Chemical Safety Board, the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
(CDC), the National Weather Service, and the Army's and the Navy's
manuals, all US federal and in the public domain; and, restated in our
own words, the International Residential Code and a HUD inspection guide.
OSHA's rules bind employers, not households; they are used here because
they set out plainly what keeps people safe around pressure (our
reading). Where something is general practice or our own reading, the
text says so.

## First: how pressure hurts people

- **Things burst.** A closed container that is heated can split, with a
  force that throws its pieces a long way: a cylinder in a fire, a can in
  a campfire, a stone with water in it, a water heater whose relief valve
  cannot open. Why, below.
- **Things fly.** A lid opened on a pressure cooker that still holds
  pressure, a truck tyre's split rim, a gas cylinder whose valve snaps
  off.
- **Steam and hot liquid scald.** Steam escaping from a cooker, a canner
  or a relief valve burns in an instant (see [Heating
  Water](/library#heating-water) and [Treating Burns](/library#treating-burns)).
- **Pipes hammer.** A valve shut suddenly sends a shock wave through the
  water that can burst pipes.
- **Ears and lungs.** Water pressure squeezes the ears as you go down,
  and air breathed from a tank swells in the lungs as you come up.

That list is our summary; the sources for each are in the sections
below.

## What pressure is

The Department of Energy's training handbook for plant operators defines
it: "Pressure is a measure of the force exerted per unit area on the
boundaries of a substance (or system). It is caused by the collisions of
the molecules of the substance with the boundaries of the system."

So pressure is push per area, and the push on any surface is the
pressure times the area. That is why a pressure that sounds small can do
a great deal. The same handbook works an example: a water tank 40 feet
high and 20 feet across has 17.2 pounds per square inch (psi) on its
floor, and that adds up to about 778,000 pounds of force on the whole
bottom.

### Worked example: the lid of a pressure canner

A canner held at 10.5 psi, with a lid opening about 10 inches across
(an illustrative size): the opening's area is about 3.14 x 5 x 5 = 78.5
square inches, so the steam pushes on the lid with 10.5 x 78.5, about 820
pounds of force (our arithmetic). Even 1 psi left inside is about 80
pounds trying to lift the lid. That is why a canner's or a cooker's lid
locks while there is pressure inside, and why you never force it.

### The units

In the United States pressure is mostly given in pounds per square inch.
Elsewhere, and on many gauges, it is kilopascals (kPa) or bar. The
National Institute of Standards and Technology's conversion tables
give one standard atmosphere as 101.325 kPa and one bar as 100 kPa, and
[Units and Converting Them](/library#units-and-converting-them) gives
NIST's factor for psi: 1 psi is 6,894.757 pascals, about 6.9 kPa. The
Department of Energy's handbook gives the atmosphere in psi: 14.7.

So, roughly (our arithmetic): 1 bar is about 14.5 psi, 1 atmosphere is
14.7 psi or about 1 bar, and 30 psi is about 207 kPa or 2.1 bar.

### Gauge pressure and absolute pressure

Almost every gauge you will use reads zero in open air, even though the
air around it is pressing at 14.7 psi. The handbook: "When pressure is
measured relative to a perfect vacuum, it is called absolute pressure
(psia); when measured relative to atmospheric pressure (14.7 psi), it is
called gauge pressure (psig). The latter pressure scale was developed
because almost all pressure gauges register zero when open to the
atmosphere."

So a tyre gauge reading 35 psi means the air inside is 35 psi more than
the air outside: 49.7 psi absolute at sea level (our arithmetic). The
difference matters in the gas law below, which works only in absolute
pressure.

NOAA explains why you do not feel the atmosphere: "At sea level, the air
that surrounds us presses down on our bodies at 14.7 pounds per square
inch. You don't feel it because the fluids in your body are pushing
outward with the same force." The air's pressure also changes with the
weather and falls as you climb, which is what a barometer reads; see
[Reading the Weather](/library#reading-the-weather).

## Pressure in water

### Depth makes pressure

In still water, pressure depends only on how deep you are.
[Moving Water Without Power](/library#moving-water-without-power) gives the
EPA's figure, 0.433 psi for every foot of fresh water, and works through
what that means for a tank on a stand. In the sea, NOAA: "For every 33
feet (10.06 meters) you go down, the pressure increases by one
atmosphere." The handbook's own example: at the bottom of a swimming pool
6 feet deep, the water adds 2.6 psi, for 17.3 psi absolute.

The width of the water does not change the pressure at a given depth,
only how much water there is ([Moving Water Without
Power](/library#moving-water-without-power) explains this). And the extra push
on the bottom of anything submerged, compared with its top, is the
buoyancy that [Floating and Boats](/library#floating-and-boats) is built on
(our explanation of the two together).

### Push it, and the push goes everywhere

The handbook states Pascal's law: "Pressure applied to a confined fluid is
transmitted undiminished throughout the confining vessel of the system."
That is how a hydraulic jack works: a small push on a small piston
becomes a large force on a large piston, because the pressure is the same
on both and the large piston has more area to push on (our explanation;
[Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](/library#force-levers-and-mechanical-advantage) has the rest of the
trade between force and distance). It is also why a raised load must
never be left on a jack's pressure alone: one leaking seal anywhere in
the system lets the whole load down (our reading; [Keeping Things
Working](/library#keeping-things-working) gives OSHA's rule for holding a
load).

### Water barely squeezes, and that is the danger

Air squashes; water hardly does. The handbook: raising the pressure of
water from 15 to 15,000 psi absolute increases its density by only about
6 percent. That one fact explains two household pressure problems (our
reading).

**Water hammer.** The handbook: "Water hammer is a liquid shock wave
resulting from the sudden starting or stopping of flow." Because water
cannot squeeze to absorb it, the moving water's energy turns into a
pressure spike. In the handbook's example, water at 120 psi flowing at 10
feet a second through a pipe, stopped by a check valve slamming shut,
briefly reaches about 763 psi. Its warning: "Water hammer in pipes has
been known to pull pipe supports from their mounts, rupture piping, and
cause pipe whip." And its cure is the household's too: "If the valve is
closed slowly, the loss of kinetic energy is gradual." Close taps and
valves smoothly (general practice).

**Water heated with nowhere to go.** The handbook: "If the fluid is an
incompressible liquid in a closed container, an increase in the
temperature will have a tremendously greater and potentially catastrophic
effect." Water tries to expand as it warms, the container stops it, and
the pressure climbs steeply. Its rule of thumb for a system completely
full of water, with no air anywhere in it, is about 100 psi more for every
degree Fahrenheit. That is the reason a storage water heater has a
temperature and pressure relief valve, and why the plumbing codes call
for an expansion tank or another pressure control on a supply with a
check valve or a pressure-reducing valve in it (our reading of the
handbook with the International Residential Code). [Heating
Water](/library#heating-water) covers the valve: never cap it, never block it,
and treat a drip from it as a warning.

## The water in a home

### From the mains

The International Residential Code, the model code many places adopt for
houses, sets a maximum of 80 psi for the static water pressure in a
house; where the main's pressure is higher, it calls for a
pressure-reducing valve where the service enters the house. It also calls
for water-hammer arrestors where quick-closing valves are fitted, and for
a way of controlling thermal expansion where a pressure-reducing valve,
check valve or backflow preventer is fitted with a storage water heater
(all restated). The code in force where you live is the one your city or
county adopted, which may differ, so ask the building department before
you change anything (our caution). A plumber can measure your pressure
with a gauge on an outside tap (general practice).

Know where your main shutoff valve is. Ready.gov says everyone in the
household should learn how to shut off the main water valve to the
house: find it, tag it, and make sure it closes completely, replacing it
if it is rusted open (restated). Shut it slowly (the handbook's
slow-valve rule above).

### From a well

A well pump does not run every time you open a tap. It fills a pressure
tank, and air squeezed in the top of the tank pushes the water out to the
taps until the pressure falls far enough for a pressure switch to start
the pump again. HUD's inspection guide describes it: the switch holds the
pressure inside a 20 psi band, usually 20 to 40, 30 to 50 or 40 to 60
psi; when a tank loses its air it becomes waterlogged and the pump starts
every time water is used; newer tanks keep the air in a bladder; and it
says to check that the tank has a pressure relief valve (restated). A
pump that starts that often wears out early (general practice). If the
pressure slowly falls when no water is being used, the guide says,
something is leaking.

Two rules follow (general practice, with [Wells and
Groundwater](/library#wells-and-groundwater) and [Working Out Why Something
Broke](/library#working-out-why-something-broke)):

- **The tank holds pressure with the power off.** Switch off the pump at
  its breaker, then open a tap until the water stops before you open any
  part of the plumbing.
- **The tank, the switch and the relief valve are a pump contractor's or
  electrician's work.** The switch is wired to the mains, and an air
  charge set wrong makes the pump cycle itself to death.

### Hot water heating

A boiler that heats a house with hot water has its own expansion tank,
pressure gauge and relief valve. HUD's guide says that when the
expansion tank loses its air it can no longer take up the expansion, and
water then discharges from the boiler's relief valve each time the system
heats; it says draining and repressurising the tank is a heating or
plumbing contractor's job (restated).

## Pressure in air and other gases

### The gas laws, in one paragraph

A gas squeezes, and its pressure follows its volume and its temperature.
The handbook states Boyle's law, that "the pressure of a gas expanding at
constant temperature varies inversely to the volume", and Charles's,
that "the pressure of a gas varies directly with temperature when the
volume is held constant". Combined, they give the ideal gas law, which
works only when, in the handbook's words, "the pressure and temperature
are absolute values": absolute pressure, and temperature counted from
absolute zero. Its plain-language version: "A 5% increase in absolute
temperature will result in a 5% increase in the absolute pressure." For
Fahrenheit, add 460 to get the absolute scale the handbook calls
Rankine.

### Tyres

**The number to use is on the car, not the tyre.** The National Highway
Traffic Safety Administration says the recommended pressure is on the
tyre and loading information label on the driver's door edge or post, or
in the owner's manual: "Remember, the correct pressure for your tire is
what the vehicle manufacturer has listed, NOT what is listed on the tire
itself." The number moulded into the tyre is a limit for the tyre, not
the setting for your car (our reading).

**Check them cold.** NHTSA's figure is the right one only "when a tire is
cold, meaning it has not been driven on for at least three hours." And:
"To get an accurate tire pressure reading, you must measure tire pressure
when the tires are cold or compensate for the extra pressure in warm
tires." It says to check every tyre, including the spare, and, if one is
too high, to let air out slowly by pressing the valve stem with the edge
of the gauge.

**Worked example** (our arithmetic, from the handbook's laws). A tyre set
to 35 psi on a 50 F morning holds 49.7 psi absolute at a temperature of
510 on the absolute scale. Driven until it is at 100 F, 560 on that
scale, its absolute pressure rises by the same 9.8 percent, to about 54.6
psi: 39.9 on the gauge, nearly 5 psi more, with no air added. That
assumes the tyre does not stretch, so treat it as a rough guide. Let air
out of a hot tyre to the cold figure and it will be under-inflated once it
cools, which is why NHTSA says to measure cold.

NHTSA counts 511 people who died on US roads in tyre-related crashes in
2024, and says poor maintenance, not having enough air in a tyre among it,
can lead to a flat, a blowout or the tread coming off.

**Truck tyres and split rims are a tyre shop's job.** OSHA's rule for
servicing the wheels of trucks, tractors, trailers, buses and off-road
machines (not ordinary cars or light-truck tyres) requires a cage or
other restraining device when a tyre on a multi-piece wheel is inflated,
a long enough hose with a clip-on chuck that the worker can stand out of
the path the parts would fly along, the tyre deflated by removing the
valve core before it is taken apart, and no hammering on rings while it
is under pressure. And: "No heat shall be applied to a multi-piece wheel
or wheel component." Those rules exist because those wheels come apart
with lethal force (our reading).

### Compressors and their tanks

A home air compressor stores air in a tank, which OSHA's rules call an
air receiver. For workplaces they require a drain valve at the lowest
point, opened often enough to keep oil and water from building up; a
pressure gauge you can see; and spring-loaded safety valves that keep the
pressure within 10 percent of what the tank is rated for. Two of those
rules, in OSHA's words: "No valve of any type shall be placed between the
air receiver and its safety valve or valves." And: "All safety valves
shall be tested frequently and at regular intervals to determine whether
they are in good operating condition."

At home (general practice, from those rules): drain the tank after use,
because the water that collects in it rusts it from the inside where you
cannot see; never block, cap or wire shut the safety valve; and stop
using a tank that is dented, rusted or leaking, rather than patching or
welding it. Blowing dust out of things with compressed air has its own
limit, less than 30 psi with guarding and eye protection at work, in
[Keeping Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working). Never point an air
hose at a person (general practice).

### Gas cylinders

A gas cylinder holds a great deal of energy in a small metal shell: a
welding cylinder, a camping or barbecue propane cylinder, a scuba tank.
OSHA's construction rule for the cylinders used in gas welding and
cutting gives the habits that keep them from becoming missiles or fires
(our household reading of a workplace rule):

- "Compressed gas cylinders shall be secured in an upright position at
  all times", except briefly while they are being moved, and kept from
  being knocked over while in use.
- Valve caps on and secured whenever they are moved or stored.
- Cylinders are not to be "intentionally dropped, struck, or permitted to
  strike each other violently."
- Fuel gas cylinders stand valve end up when in use, and "shall not be
  placed in a location where they would be subject to open flame, hot
  metal, or other sources of artificial heat."
- When opening a valve, "The person cracking the valve shall stand to one
  side of the outlet, not in front of it."
- "No damaged or defective cylinder shall be used", and only the owner or
  someone the owner authorises refills a cylinder.
- A cylinder frozen in place is freed with warm water, never boiling.

Where and how to store fuel cylinders is in *Fuels and Their Hazards*.

**A cylinder in a fire: get far away.** Propane is stored as a liquid
under pressure, and heat raises that pressure (our reading of the gas
laws above and the investigation below). The US Chemical Safety and
Hazard Investigation Board investigated a farm propane tank that burst
in a fire in Albert City, Iowa, in 1998. It calls this kind of explosion
a BLEVE, a boiling liquid expanding vapour explosion: "A BLEVE can occur
when a pressure vessel containing a flammable liquid, like a propane
tank, is exposed to fire." Its account: once the leaking propane
caught fire under the tank, "As the propane boiled, the pressure inside
the tank increased because of the expanding vapors"; about 10 minutes
after the fire started the relief valves opened, with a noise the
firefighters compared to a jet engine; and in the end the steel above the
liquid, with nothing inside to cool it, grew too hot to hold and tore.
The board's timeline puts the ignition at 11:05 pm, the firefighters'
arrival at 11:21 and the explosion at 11:28. Its finding about the
firefighters: "In this incident, flying tank fragments from the explosion
killed two fire fighters located approximately 100 feet from the side of
the tank." A relief valve venting does not mean the danger is over: the
tank burst seven minutes after the firefighters arrived to find flames
roaring from its valves.

For a household the lesson is short (our reading): if a propane cylinder
or tank is in a fire or roaring from its valve, get everyone well away,
farther than seems reasonable, call 911, and do not go back to turn it
off or cool it.

### Aerosol cans and lighters

The CPSC's regulation for the warning on a pressurised can includes:
"Do not puncture or incinerate container." It goes on to say not to
expose the can to heat or store it above 120 F. The National Weather
Service says a dark dashboard or seat in a parked car can reach 180 to
over 200 F. So a can or a lighter left on the dashboard in summer is past
its own label's limit (our reading of the two together). Never put a
can, full or "empty", on a fire (general practice; the label above).

## Steam: why a closed container that is heated can burst

Water turns to steam at 212 F at sea level, and at a higher temperature
under more pressure: [Heat and How It Moves](/library#heat-and-how-it-moves)
gives the handbook's rule, "The higher the pressure, the higher the
saturation temperature." And steam is enormous. The handbook's fluid
volume says "The density of steam is approximately 1600 times less than
that of water." A spoonful of water that turns to steam inside something
sealed needs about 1,600 spoonfuls of room (our arithmetic). Put that
together with the gas laws and the stiffness of water, and any sealed
container with water in it, heated, becomes a bomb (our reading).

Things that have burst that way, and the rule for each:

- **Stones from a stream bed.** The Army's survival manual: "Do not use
  wet or porous rocks as they may explode when heated." And of rocks for
  boiling water: "Do not use rocks with air pockets, such as limestone and
  sandstone. They may explode while heating in the fire." Use dry, dense
  stones, or none (general practice).
- **Bamboo.** The same manual: "A sealed section of bamboo will explode if
  heated because of trapped air and water in the section."
- **Cans and jars.** Never heat a sealed can or jar of food, or put one
  on a fire, without opening or venting it (general practice).
- **Water heaters and fire-heated coils.** [Heating
  Water](/library#heating-water) gives the CPSC's 1982 warning about
  fireplace-powered water heaters, which can turn their water to steam
  and explode if the flow stops and there is no proper relief valve, and
  HUD's warning about a water heater whose relief valve is blocked.

### Pressure cookers

A pressure cooker raises the boiling point so food cooks hotter. The
danger is opening it, or letting it vent, while it still holds pressure.
The CPSC recalled about 930,000 electric pressure cookers in 2023
because wrong fill lines inside their pots let people overfill them, and
hot food and liquid could then be ejected when the cooker was vented
quickly or opened under pressure; the seller had 31 reports of contents
expelled under pressure, 17 with burns. The CPSC's advice for every user:
"Consumers should always check that the inner pot is not filled beyond
two-thirds capacity when pressure cooking, that the lid is fully locked
before pressure cooking is started, and that the floating locking valve
has dropped before attempting to open the lid when pressure cooking."

### Pressure canners

A canner is a bigger pressure cooker with a gauge, used for food that
must be heated past boiling (the reason is in [Why Canning Has
Rules](/library#why-canning-has-rules)). The USDA's canning guide is clear about
what the pressure is for: "Pressure does not destroy microorganisms, but
high temperatures applied for an adequate period of time do kill
microorganisms." At sea level, it says, a canner at 10.5 psi on the gauge
reaches 240 F inside. Its rules for the vessel:

- **Vent it.** "Air trapped in a canner lowers the temperature obtained at
  5, 10, or 15 pounds of pressure and results in under processing." So:
  "To be safe, all types of pressure canners must be vented 10 minutes
  before they are pressurized."
- **Check the gauge.** "Check dial gauges for accuracy before use each
  year." Many county extension offices can test one, it says.
- **Look after the gasket and the safety fuse.** "Nicked or dried gaskets
  will allow steam leaks during pressurization of canners." And: "Lid
  safety fuses are thin metal inserts or rubber plugs designed to relieve
  excessive pressure from the canner. Do not pick at or scratch fuses
  while cleaning lids." It recommends canners approved by Underwriters
  Laboratories.
- **Let it cool by itself.** "Do not force-cool the canner." Newer canners
  are depressurised when their vent lock piston drops.
- **Open it away from you.** After it is depressurised, take the weight
  off or open the petcock, then: "Wait 10 minutes, unfasten the lid, and
  remove it carefully. Lift the lid away from you so that the steam does
  not burn your face."

The food side, the tested times and pressures for each food and
altitude, is not in this guide. Learn it from the USDA guide itself, as
[Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) says.

## Pressure on the body: swimming and diving

NOAA: "Dive down into the ocean even a few feet, though, and a noticeable
change occurs. You can feel an increase of pressure on your eardrums." At
33 feet of sea water the pressure on you is twice what it is at the
surface (NOAA's one atmosphere per 33 feet, our arithmetic).

**Ears.** The Navy's diving manual: "Middle ear squeeze is the most common
type of barotrauma." The water presses the eardrum inward, and unless air
reaches the space behind it through the tube from the back of the nose,
the eardrum stretches, bleeds and can tear. Its advice: clear early and
often on the way down; "Many divers can clear by yawning, swallowing, or
moving the jaw around"; "Never do a forceful Valsalva maneuver during
descent" (forcing air up by blowing hard against a pinched nose); and if it
hurts, "Simply stopping the descent and ascending a few feet usually
brings about immediate relief." It adds: "Divers who cannot clear their
ears on the surface should not dive." A head cold can block the tube,
and the manual says a partly blocked tube makes a squeeze more likely. A
torn eardrum needs a doctor: the manual sends suspected tears to medical
staff.

**Breath-holding.** The CDC: "Do not hyperventilate before swimming
underwater or try to hold your breath underwater for long periods of time.
This can cause you to pass out and drown." The Navy manual explains why:
deep fast breathing beforehand lowers the body's carbon dioxide, which is
the signal to breathe, without adding useful oxygen, so a swimmer can
black out from lack of oxygen before feeling the need to surface, and it
calls this "a common cause of breathholding accidents in swimming pools."
Swim with a buddy, as the CDC says, and never play breath-holding games.

**Breathing air underwater.** Air from a scuba tank is delivered at the
pressure of the water around you. Breathe it in at 33 feet and come up
holding your breath, and that air tries to double in volume (Boyle's law,
above; our arithmetic). The Navy manual: "Divers must never hold their
breath during ascent because of the danger of an air embolism", which it
explains is gas forced from over-stretched lungs into the blood, where it
can block the arteries of the brain and heart. Breathing from a tank
underwater is a skill to learn from a certified instructor, not from a
guide (general practice).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Pressure-reducing valves, expansion tanks, a dripping relief valve,
  water hammer that will not stop:** a plumber ([Heating
  Water](/library#heating-water); the code requirements above).
- **A well's pressure tank, pressure switch and relief valve:** a pump
  contractor or electrician ([Wells and
  Groundwater](/library#wells-and-groundwater)).
- **A boiler's expansion tank and relief valve:** a heating contractor
  (HUD's guide).
- **Truck, tractor and trailer tyres, and any split rim:** a tyre shop with
  a restraining cage (OSHA).
- **A dented, rusted or leaking compressor tank or gas cylinder:** stop
  using it; return a cylinder to its supplier. Never weld, heat or patch a
  pressure vessel (general practice).
- **A cylinder or tank in a fire, or venting with a roar:** get away and
  call 911.
- **A pressure canner's dial gauge:** tested every year, at an extension
  office or by the maker (USDA).
- **Breathing from a tank underwater:** a certified course first.

Yours to do: knowing gauge from absolute pressure, checking tyres cold
against the door label, closing valves slowly, never capping a relief
valve, draining a compressor tank, keeping cylinders upright and secured
and away from heat, never heating anything sealed, never opening a
cooker or canner under pressure, and clearing your ears early.

## Traps

- **"The tyre says 44 psi, so I'll fill it to 44."** That is the tyre's
  limit. Use the door label.
- **"I'll let air out now, it's hot."** Then it will be low when it
  cools.
- **"The lid's stuck, I'll lever it."** It is stuck because it is still
  under pressure.
- **"A little cold water will cool the canner faster."** The USDA says
  never force-cool it.
- **"The relief valve keeps dripping, so I capped it."** That is how water
  heaters burst; see [Heating Water](/library#heating-water).
- **"These river stones will make a good fire ring."** Wet stones can
  explode.
- **"The propane's venting, so it's safe now."** The Iowa tank burst while
  its valves were venting.
- **"I'll hold my breath and see how far I can swim underwater."** That
  is how swimmers black out and drown.
- **"Can't clear my ears, but I'll push on down."** Stop, go up a few
  feet, and try again gently.

## How the game models it

The game models air pressure where it matters to a body, and almost
nothing else in this guide.

- **Air pressure and height.** On a planet, the air's pressure falls as
  you climb, worked out from each world's sea-level pressure in
  `data/environment/climate.ron` (Earth's is 101.325 kPa), and the body
  heat model uses it (`src/systems/env_layer1.rs`,
  `src/engine/survival_env.rs`, `src/systems/body_heat.rs`).
- **The home's air.** The Homes page's Live air card shows the sealed
  home's oxygen, carbon dioxide, temperature and pressure, the pressure in
  atmospheres (`src/gui/pages/homes.rs`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: its water
has no pressure or head, so height and pipe length do not matter to it
(see [Moving Water Without Power](/library#moving-water-without-power)); below a
planet's sea surface you swim where you look and keep breathing, with no
breath to hold and nothing pressing on your ears however deep you go;
and there are no tyres to inflate, no cylinders, cookers, canners,
compressors or relief valves, and nothing that bursts from pressure or
heat.

## You own this when

- You can say what pressure is, and why a small pressure on a big lid is
  a big force.
- You know gauge pressure from absolute, and convert psi, kPa and bar
  roughly in your head.
- You know that water pressure depends on depth, that one atmosphere is
  about 33 feet of sea water, and that pressure in a closed liquid goes
  everywhere.
- You can explain water hammer, close valves slowly, and say why heated
  water in a full, closed system needs a relief valve and room to expand.
- You know how a well's pressure tank works and that it holds pressure
  with the power off.
- You set tyres cold to the door label and can work out how heat raises
  their pressure.
- You keep cylinders upright, secured, capped and away from heat, and you
  would get far away from one in a fire.
- You never heat anything sealed, from a can to a wet stone.
- You never open a cooker or canner under pressure, and you vent, gauge
  and cool a canner as the USDA says.
- You clear your ears early, never hyperventilate before swimming
  underwater, and know never to hold your breath coming up from a tank.
