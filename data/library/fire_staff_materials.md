# Building a Fire Staff: Every Material, and Why

A fire staff is a shaft with a fuel-soaked wick at each end. Four
subsystems decide whether it is a good one: the shaft, the wicks and the
hardware that holds them on, the ends and plugs that the hardware bites
into, and whatever keeps the heat out of your hands. This guide covers
all four, plus grips, LED and practice ends, and the safety kit that is
part of the build.

Three companion guides carry the detail this one only summarises:

- [Choosing a Staff Tube](/library#staff-tubes) covers length, diameter,
  wall thickness, every metal alloy side by side, and which metals,
  platings and coatings give off fumes when heated.
- [Fire Performance Fuels](/library#fire-performance-fuels) covers what
  is in the fuel can, flash points, burn behaviour and fuel handling.
- [Clothing for Fire Performance](/library#fire-performance-clothing)
  covers what to wear, and what the safety wears.

One hard boundary before anything else: **do not light a fire staff
without a trained spotter holding a wet cotton towel or a fire blanket,
and do not learn fire spinning from a document.** Learn the movement with
an unlit staff until it is boring, then learn fire safety in person from
experienced spinners. Burns, hair fires, and setting bystanders alight
are the real risks, and none of them are material-selection problems.
This guide makes your equipment sound. It cannot make you safe.

Where a material number matters, it comes from a government publication,
a standard, or a named manufacturer document that measured or specified
it, not from a shop selling the part. That distinction matters here more
than usual, because most fire-spinning technical writing is published by
retailers. Every number below traces to something in the Sources list at
the bottom. Where a figure comes only from staff makers, retailers or
practitioners, it is labelled **community figure** in the text: treat it
as what experienced spinners report, not as something anyone tested.
Prices and stock were checked on **September 24, 2026** and are a
snapshot that will age quickly.

One honest limit runs through everything about heat. **Nobody appears to
have published a measurement of how hot the metal under a burning wick
gets.** Like the staff tube guide, this guide compares published ratings
against an assumed range of roughly 200 to 400 C for the metal near a
wick, with direct flame contact possibly hotter. Where that assumption
carries a conclusion, the text says so.

## Five things you will be told that are not quite right

<!-- quote-ok: stating a common belief in order to correct it, not quoting a source -->
**"Titanium staffs are a myth."** An earlier version of this guide said
so, and it was wrong. Grade 9 titanium tube (Ti-3Al-2.5V, cold worked and
stress relieved) is widely stocked in 3/4 inch thin wall, and at least two
brands sell complete titanium fire staffs, mostly spin staffs rather than
contact staffs (**community figure**). It costs several times more than
aluminium, and the staff tube guide covers when it is worth it.

<!-- quote-ok: stating a common belief in order to correct it, not quoting a source -->
**"7075-T6 is the stiff aluminium."** It is the strong one, not the stiff
one. NASA's materials data handbooks put 7075-T6 at 83.0 ksi ultimate
tensile strength against 45.0 ksi for 6061-T6, a little over 1.8 times the
strength. But every aluminium alloy has nearly the same elastic modulus,
so the same tube flexes the same in either alloy. 7075 buys resistance to
permanent bends and dents, not less bounce. It also loses its heat
treatment at lower temperatures than 6061 (see the heat table below).

<!-- quote-ok: stating a common belief in order to correct it, not quoting a source -->
**"The wick is Kevlar."** It is usually a blend. Standard wick tape is
aramid, Kevlar with Nomex in some grades, over a fibreglass core. Thickness
matters more than brand.

<!-- quote-ok: stating a common belief in order to correct it, not quoting a source -->
**"Silicone tape is fuel-proof."** It has no adhesive for fuel to
dissolve, which is a real advantage, but silicone rubber itself swells and
softens when petroleum fuel soaks into it. More on that in the grip
section.

<!-- quote-ok: stating a common belief in order to correct it, not quoting a source -->
**"The parts are the shaft, the grip and the wick."** Three parts are
missing: a **heat shield or thermal break** between wick and grip, the
**fastening hardware** that holds the wick on, and the **end plug** that
hardware bites into. All three are safety parts. The hardware and the plug
are the only places where failure means a lit wick leaves the staff.

## The one fact that explains every other choice

Nothing you would willingly build a staff from is safe from a wick flame
indefinitely, and most materials give up their useful strength far below
their melting point. So the whole craft is distance, shielding and
sacrifice.

The column that matters is the second one. Melting is where a material is
finished; the working limit is where it starts losing what you bought it
for, and for heat-treated metals that loss is permanent and adds up over
many burns.

| Material | Working limit (where it starts losing strength or protection) | Melts or decomposes | Where the numbers come from |
|---|---|---|---|
| Epoxy (carbon fibre and fibreglass binder) | Resin specific. One named maker rates its standard epoxy to 60 C (140 F) | Softens, then chars | DragonPlate; get your own tube's figure from its maker |
| EPR "self-fusing" rubber tape | 90 C operating, 130 C emergency | n/a | 3M Scotch 23 data sheet |
| EPDM rubber (foam grips) | About 121 C | n/a | Parker O-Ring Handbook service range |
| Aramid, long-term service | 149 to 177 C for Kevlar, 204 C for Nomex | Kevlar decomposes at 427 to 482 C in air and never melts; Nomex visibly damaged at 350 C | DuPont Kevlar and Nomex technical guides |
| Silicone self-fusing tape | 180 to 200 C continuous, 260 C intermittent | Burns once ignited | Rogers ARLON and Permatex data; Shin-Etsu |
| 7075-T6 aluminium | Its T6 heat treatment is a bake at 110 to 127 C, and time above that slowly and permanently softens it | Starts to melt at 477 C | NASA materials data handbook, alloy 7075 |
| 6061-T6 aluminium | Baked at 171 to 182 C. Read by eye from the MMPDS handbook, it starts losing room-temperature strength after about 1,000 hours at 150 C, and long exposure above about 230 to 260 C leaves around a fifth of its T6 yield strength, roughly the annealed O temper (8 ksi against 40 ksi in the NASA handbook). Ultimate strength falls less, toward the O temper's 18 ksi against 45 | Starts to melt at 582 C | NASA handbook, alloy 6061; MMPDS-04 |
| Stainless 304, annealed | No temper to lose; typical yield falls from about 241 MPa at room temperature to 159 MPa at 204 C and 131 MPa at 427 C | Melting range about 1399 to 1421 C | Rolled Alloys 304 data sheet |
| Titanium grade 9 CWSR | About 72 percent of room-temperature strength at 316 C; usable to about 427 C. Long soaks above about 370 C may relax its cold work (an inference, untested on staffs) | About 1627 C | Alleima grade 9 data sheet and safety information sheet, as read in the staff tube guide |
| Titanium grade 2 | Falls to roughly 35 to 45 percent of room-temperature strength at 315 C | About 1660 C | Zapp grades 1 to 4 data sheet |

A flame does not have one temperature, and the honest range is wide.
NIST measured a 30 cm methanol pool fire and found the largest mean
temperatures, about 1300 K or roughly 1030 C, along the centreline near
the burner. That is a time-averaged mean in a turbulent fire, so the flame
is hotter and cooler than that from moment to moment. Published work on
sooty candle flames assumed 2000 K, about 1730 C, as a typical diffusion
flame temperature for its soot analysis; it is a stated assumption, not a
measurement. The two figures are not like-for-like, but a fire staff wick
is a diffusion flame of the same family, so our inference is that its hot
regions sit somewhere between them.

Every material in the table melts or decomposes below the top of that
band, titanium included: NIST's pulse-heating measurement puts the melting
point of Ti-6Al-4V at 1943 K, about 1670 C, which is inside it. So no
material here is immune. What saves the metal under a wick is that it is
never actually at flame temperature. Evaporating fuel keeps the wick
cooler than an open flame, and the shaft conducts heat away. That is why
aluminium works at all. It is also why **a staff left burning on the
ground, or a wick allowed to burn dry, is the thing that actually
destroys equipment.**

### Fuel choice does not change this table

You will see confident numbers quoted for the flame temperature of each
fuel, usually adiabatic flame temperatures around 2000 C. Two problems
with reasoning from those. First, the adiabatic flame temperature is a
theoretical ceiling: it assumes perfect mixing and no heat lost to the
surroundings, and an open wick fire in moving air never comes close to
it. Second, the tables of those values that circulate online are not
reliable. One dataset cited by an earlier version of this guide lists
naphtha at 4591 C, which is not physically possible for a hydrocarbon
burning in air, so its other figures cannot be trusted either.

So do not pick a fuel to protect your shaft. What genuinely differs
between fuels is **radiant heat, soot, burn time and wick wear**.
Candle-flame research is explicit that a flame's visible brightness is
thermal radiation emitted by incandescent soot particles. Kerosene soots
heavily, so it radiates more toward your skin and your prop. Camp fuel
soots less than the oily fuels. Alcohol soots least of all, which is
exactly why its flame is so hard to see.

Burn times are **community figures**, and they match the fuels guide.
One maker's fuel article gives about 3 to 4 minutes per dip for camp fuel,
5 minutes or more for paraffin lamp oil, and about 5 to 7 minutes for
kerosene; the same maker quotes 5 to 6 minutes for its own 4 inch staff
wicks on camp fuel, so treat the per-fuel figures as rough. Wick size,
wind and how hard you spin off change them a lot. One Kevlar wick maker
reports that kerosene-based fuel at least doubles wick life compared with
white gas (**community figure**). That is not the fuel attacking the fibre (see the
wick section); it is how fast each fuel burns off and leaves the fibre
exposed.

## The shaft

Detailed alloy tables, wall thicknesses and prices live in the staff tube
guide. Here is the summary, with the non-metal shafts that guide leaves
to this one.

Thermal conductivity is in watts per metre-kelvin at room temperature; it
tells you how fast heat travels from the wick toward your hands.

| Material | Cond. | Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|---|---|
| **6061-T6 aluminium** | 167 | Cheap, stocked everywhere, bends instead of cracking and can often be bent back | About half the bending strength of 7075 in the same tube; conducts heat well |
| **7075-T6 aluminium** | 130 | Roughly twice the bending strength of 6061 in the same tube, so the usual premium staff alloy | Same stiffness as 6061; loses its temper at lower temperatures; a bent 7075 staff may crack if straightened |
| **Titanium grade 9 CWSR** | About 7.6 | Strong, and keeps its strength far hotter than aluminium. A 0.040 inch grade 9 tube carries roughly 30 to 40 times less heat up the tube than a 0.065 inch 6061 staff: about 22 times from the metal, the rest from the thinner wall (our arithmetic) | Several times the price of aluminium; the metal right next to the wick holds its heat longer |
| **Commercially pure titanium (grades 1 to 4)** | About 22 | Corrosion proof | Softer; grade 1 is too soft for a fire staff. It conducts more heat than stainless, not less |
| **Stainless steel 304 or 316** | About 15 to 16 | No temper to lose, no coating to burn, excellent for hardware | About three times the density of aluminium, and annealed tube is no stronger than 6061; a full-length stainless staff is heavy |
| **Carbon steel (DOM, 4130)** | Roughly 43 to 52 | Strong and cheap as aircraft tube | Rusts, so it needs a coating, and the coating burns at the wick. LED and practice staffs only |
| **Hardwood dowel** | 0.1 to 0.2 | Roughly a thousand times less conductive than 6061 aluminium, cheap, extra mass slows rotation, which helps a beginner | It is fuel. Chars even under sheet metal near a wick, and screws lose their hold in charred wood (our inference), so check them |
| **Bamboo** | Low; not covered by the Wood Handbook | Light, and often free to cut | It is fuel, like wood. Unlit practice only |
| **Carbon fibre** | Low across the laminate | Light; makers describe it as stiff (**community figure**) | The epoxy softens far below any metal, so it *requires* metal heat shields; one retailer says it usually cracks or shatters rather than bending (**community figure**); heated or burning epoxy gives off fumes; corrodes aluminium it touches in the wet (below) |
| **Fibreglass** | Low | Does not conduct electricity, which is why a glass-fibre layer is the standard insulator between carbon fibre and metal (FAA) | Same resin problem; rarely sold for staffs |

The aluminium figures are from the NASA handbooks for those alloys. The
titanium and stainless figures are from the manufacturer data sheets used
in the staff tube guide (Fine Tubes for grade 9, Zapp for the pure grades,
the ASTM tube row data for stainless), and the carbon steel range covers
the 4130 and 1020 rows there. The wood figures come from the Forest
Products Laboratory Wood Handbook, 2010 edition, which gives 0.10 to 0.14
W/(m K) for structural softwood at 12 percent moisture and publishes the
equation that puts dense hardwoods such as ash and hickory at roughly 0.16
to 0.19 (our arithmetic from that equation). The 1999 edition prints the
softwood range as "0.1 to 1.4", a misprint the 2010 edition corrects. Set
against 6061 at 167, dense hardwood is about 880 to 1,040 times less
conductive; against 7075 at 130, about 680 to 810 times.

**Titanium is not the only answer to hot hands, and not every titanium
helps.** Grades 5 and 9 conduct heat roughly 20 to 25 times less than
6061, but pure titanium conducts more than stainless, and a wooden shaft
beats every metal. A deliberate thermal break (below) and a grip placed well back from
the wick do more for your hands than the shaft metal does.

**Wood under a wick, and where it stops.** The Wood Handbook explains
that heated wood breaks down into char and flammable gases. Below about
300 C inside the wood, the main product is char; above about 300 C the
flammable gases become far more pronounced, and it is those gases that
feed flaming. Wood surfaces have been measured at somewhere between 300
and 400 C just before piloted ignition. A wooden shaft is nowhere near
that at the grip. It is very close to it at the wick. NAFAA's performer
safety guidelines, which describe themselves as voluntary, say a staff
should either be made of fireproof material or have a protective covering
extending **at least 4 inches beyond the flame contact zone, in both
directions from each wick.** Treat those wooden ends as wear parts and
check the screws and the char before every burn.

**Carbon fibre and fibreglass need metal heat shields at the ends.** The
maker convention is an aluminium sleeve about **twice the length of the
wick** it shields (**community figure**). It can end up heavier than a
plain aluminium staff. NAFAA's guidelines list carbon fibre among the
fireproof materials, but the epoxy holding the fibres together is rated
far lower than any metal (see the heat table), which is why makers shield
it anyway.

**Keep the aluminium sleeve from touching bare carbon.** Carbon fibre is
far more noble than aluminium: NASA's galvanic ranking puts graphite at
35 against aluminium at 4 to 9. The FAA's corrosion-control circular calls
graphite/epoxy a very noble material that sets up galvanic corrosion with
metals, and says the most effective fix is a non-conductive layer, such
as fibreglass/epoxy or a sealant, between the composite and the metal. A
short aluminium sleeve on a long carbon shaft is the bad arrangement, a
small anode against a large cathode, and sweat supplies the electrolyte.
So keep an insulating layer, such as a glass-fibre wrap or a
non-conductive sleeve, between them, and rinse off sweat. See
[Metals and Alloys](/library#metals-and-alloys) on galvanic corrosion and
the area rule.

**The one to avoid outright at a fire end: galvanised steel, including
steel electrical conduit (EMT).** It is the classic cheap DIY shaft and it
is strong enough for practice. The problem is the zinc. NASA's fastener
manual gives zinc plate a useful service limit of only 121 C (250 F), and
the galvanizers' association limits long-term service of a hot-dip
coating to 200 C; both are limits on the coating's protection, not fume
thresholds. Zinc reaches a small but measurable vapour pressure at about
337 C and melts at 419 C, so direct
flame at the wick edge could get there, and zinc oxide fume causes metal
fume fever: NIOSH lists chills, muscle ache, nausea, fever, dry throat,
metallic taste, headache and difficulty breathing, and sets the exposure
limit at 5 mg per cubic metre. Most documented cases come from welding
and cutting, not spinning, but there is no reason to put zinc at a wick.
For a fire staff, use one of the tubes the staff tube guide recommends.

**If you want one recommendation:** spin an unlit hardwood dowel first,
cut down a little at a time until the length feels right, and learn
whether you like long or short, heavy or light. Then build the fire staff
on 3/4 inch 6061-T6 or 7075-T6 aluminium at about 0.055 to 0.065 inch
wall, which is what most makers use. The dowel costs less than the
shipping on the aluminium and answers questions no table can.

## The wick

"Kevlar" is a brand that became a category name. What you buy is a
blend, and the choices that matter are grade, thickness, and
construction, in that order.

Portland Fire & Rescue's fire performance policy recommends wick material
of aramid, such as Kevlar or Nomex, combined with fibreglass. The grade
descriptions below are one supplier's (**community figure**).

| Grade | What the supplier says it is | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **K1 tape** | A blend of aramids (Kevlar and Nomex) with fibreglass | The common staff wick. Sold 1/16, 1/8 and 1/4 inch thick |
| **K2 tape** | An aramid blend, cheaper per volume | Frays more with handling; slightly more tolerant of heat from metal hardware; sold as 1/8 inch but closer to 3/16 |
| **Aramid rope** | Rope for knotted wicks | See construction below |
| **Cotton, rags, towels, denim** | Natural fibre | **Never.** Cotton burns away, and in a Kevlar-cotton blend it leaves a loose weave that sheds |
| **Polyester, nylon, acrylic, polypropylene, fleece** | Meltable synthetics | **Never.** They melt and drip burning plastic |

**What the fibre maker actually says.** DuPont's Kevlar technical guide
gives a decomposition temperature in air of 427 to 482 C and is clear
that the fibre never melts. But the same guide recommends a maximum of
only 149 to 177 C for long-term use in air, and says rising temperature
reduces tensile strength, modulus and break elongation. The Nomex guide
is similar: no melting point, a recommended maximum continuous operating
temperature of 204 C, roughly half breaking strength at 254 C, and
scorching or charring in as little as 30 seconds at 350 C in air. Your
wick spends every burn far above what either maker recommends for
sustained use. It is a consumable, by design.

Kevlar is flame resistant but **can be ignited**, with a limiting oxygen
index of 29. Burning usually stops once the ignition source is removed,
and, unlike nylon or polyester, it does not drip. The same guide lists
what burning Kevlar releases. Carbon dioxide, water and nitrogen oxides
make up most of it, and carbon monoxide and traces of hydrogen cyanide can
appear too, in amounts that vary with how it burns. That is one more
reason to put a wick out before it burns dry and to keep your face out of
the smoke.

**Fuel does not chemically attack the fibre.** The Kevlar guide's
chemical resistance table lists gasoline for 1000 hours at 21 C, and
kerosene for 500 hours at 60 C, both with no effect on breaking strength.
What wears a wick out is heat and abrasion. Fuel choice still changes wick
life, because a fuel that burns off quickly leaves the fibre exposed to
the flame sooner. Makers report wear starting about 4 minutes into a
white gas burn, and longer wick life on kerosene (**community figures**).
Keep additives out of the fuel: one troupe reports that flame-colouring
salts tend to destroy wicks (**community figure**; the fuels guide has
more).

**Put the flame out while it still covers the whole wick.** While there
is liquid fuel in the wick, the flame is burning fuel vapour and the
fibres are shielded. Once the fuel runs out, the fibres themselves burn
and smoulder. Makers agree on this, and it follows from DuPont's figures.

**How a wick dies:** in a blend, the yellow aramid on the outside chars
away first, exposing the white fibreglass core. Once that happens the
wick has lost its abrasion armour and degrades much faster from ground
strikes. **White showing through yellow is your signal to inspect more
often and plan a replacement.** Retire a wick when burns get noticeably
shorter or it frays or starts breaking apart; when in doubt, retire it
early. Trim stray strands and stitch loose spots with aramid thread
(**community figures**, but the charring mechanism behind them is exactly
what DuPont describes). Practising over grass rather than pavement extends
wick life.

**Thickness and width.** 4 inch (100 mm) wide wicks are standard on
commercial fire contact staffs, with 2 to 6 inch wicks on sale, and 1/8
inch is the most common thickness (**community figures**). One supplier
says 1/4 inch material lasts longer and suggests it for staffs that get
dropped a lot. Note the catalogue catch: on the snapshot date that
supplier stocked 1/4 inch tape only up to 2 inches wide, so a 4 inch wick
in 1/4 inch material was not available from it at all. Wider means more
fuel, a bigger flame, a longer burn, and more end weight.

**Keep each head under about 2 inches thick.** One wick maker warns that
a thicker head can burn on the inside, where it is very hard to put out
even after the fuel is gone (**community figure**).

**Fuel roughly doubles a wick's weight.** The same maker weighed 2 inch by
1/8 inch Kevlar at about 20 g per foot dry and 41 to 46 g per foot fully
soaked, and suggests about 36 inches of 1/8 inch wick for a staff head
(**community figures**). Scaled to a 4 inch wide wick, that is roughly
120 g dry and 245 to 275 g soaked per head, before screws (our arithmetic
from those figures). Spin-off removes some, but a fuelled staff swings
noticeably heavier than it does dry.

## Attaching the wick

This is the part that decides whether a lit wick stays on the staff.

**Fasten mechanically, through the wick and the staff.** NAFAA's
performer safety guidelines, which are voluntary, say wicks should be
attached by a hard limiting method: wire, screws or bolts run through the
wick and the device. Glue or friction should not be the primary hold. The
same guidelines ask for handles to be attached with the same care, and
say any connector that could be exposed to heat should be tempered metal,
not plastic, drop-forged or spring metal. That covers the joint of a
swappable fire head.

**What makers do.** Recessed rivets (one maker uses four per wick, and
says they leave less exposed metal than screws and never need
retightening), or screws through the wick into a solid plug inside the
tube (**community figures**). A thin tube wall cannot hold screws on its
own; see the next section.

**Hide the metal.** Dark Monk warns that bare metal next to the wick, not
the wick itself, causes the worst burns from equipment, and covers the
tube end with two layers of wick (**community figure**). Another wick
maker says metal parts on a burning prop run much hotter than the wick
surface (**community figure**). Recess fastener heads, lap the wick over
the sharp tube end, and stitch an aramid flap over any heads that stick
out.

**Tug test, every time.** NAFAA's check before each use is to take hold
of each wick and pull it away from where it normally sits, confirm that
grips and wicks are firmly attached, and count any wear as a failure.
Check that every screw, rivet and pin is tight before fuelling.

## Heat shielding and thermal breaks

The subsystem beginner advice omits. It decides whether the staff
survives its tenth burn and whether you get burned setting it down.

Temperature ratings below are the named manufacturers' own figures. They
are usually continuous-service ratings that say nothing about brief flame
contact. Treat them as upper bounds you should stay well under.

| Option | Rating | For |
|---|---|---|
| **Aluminium sleeve** | Same temper limits as the aluminium shaft (see the heat table) | Needed on carbon fibre and fibreglass shafts, about twice the wick length (**community figure**), with an insulating layer between the sleeve and bare carbon (see the shaft section) |
| **Silicone self-fusing tape** | Permatex: 200 C continuous, 260 C intermittent. ARLON: 180 C continuous | The standard covering for bare metal between wick and grip. One maker recommends about 6 inches of silicone between wick and a foam grip (**community figure**). A flame-retardant grade exists (Rogers ARLON sells one) and is the better pick for the end of the wrap nearest the wick, still started a few inches back from it |
| **Fibreglass fire sleeve** | Davlyn rates its silicone-coated sleeve 260 C continuous, and up to 1,200 C for brief direct flame or molten splash | Silicone-coated knitted or braided glass, sold by the foot as industrial hose protection. A poor hand grip, a good hidden layer |
| **Aramid sleeve** | Vendors quote figures near the decomposition temperature; the honest figure is DuPont's service limit of 149 to 177 C | Woven tube slipped over the shaft |
| **Metal covering on wood** | To the metal's limits | At least 4 inches beyond the flame zone on each side of each wick (NAFAA) |
| **Air gap or standoff** | Free | Untested reasoning, not a published method: mounting the wick on a short stainless collar or plug that stands off the tube should keep the shaft out of the hottest zone. But every contact-staff impact then lands on the plug-to-tube joint, so the standoff must be pinned through the tube with the same positive mechanical fastening as a wick (NAFAA: no glue or friction as the main hold) and tug-tested the same way |

Shielding buys margin, not immunity. Wick covers between burns, and
putting wicks out before they burn dry, do more for equipment life than
any material upgrade.

## The grip

Grippiness is the obvious axis. For a fire staff three others matter as
much: behaviour when wet, what fuel does to it, and heat. The feel and
wear descriptions are makers' and practitioners' experience (**community
figures**); the heat and fuel columns come from the named data in the
next subsection.

| Material | Feel and wear | Heat | Fuel | Where it goes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Silicone self-fusing tape or tubing** | Tacky its whole life and grippiest when clean and dry; slippery with sweat or fuel; pulls arm hair; tears on gravel and sharp edges | 180 to 200 C continuous (tape) | Swells and softens in petroleum fuels, then mostly recovers once dry | Heat buffer between wick and grip, starting a few inches back from the wick, or the grip itself |
| **EPDM foam** | The most common padded staff grip; gets tackier with age; leaves black marks on skin | About 121 C | Unsatisfactory in gasoline and kerosene | Behind the silicone buffer, out of the fuel zone |
| **Silicone foam** | Flowtoys sells an adhesive-backed silicone foam grip for contact and dragon staffs and calls it very fire resistant, while still advising silicone tape at the ends | No published rating for the foam | Silicone, so expect the same swelling (our inference) | Behind the buffer, on clean metal |
| **Racquet overgrip** (usually polyurethane) | The most common commercial staff grip; cheap, thin, adhesive backed, loses tack quickly | Polyurethane is rated to only about 82 C | No published rating for the tape or its adhesive | Middle of the staff only |
| **Leather** | Feels good; chars rather than melts | n/a | Porous: soaks up sweat and fuel and turns slippery | Middle of the staff only |
| **PVC electrical tape, vinyl wrap** | Goes gooey when hot | Heated PVC gives off hydrogen chloride readily from about 225 C (NIST), mainly at 250 to 350 C | n/a | Decoration in the middle only |
| **Bare metal** | Nothing to wear out | Conducts heat to your hands | Slippery when fuelled | Acceptable, unpadded |

**Is there fireproof grip tape?** No, and you do not need one. Nothing you
would want to hold survives direct flame. The answer is geometric: the
grip belongs well back from the wick, with silicone between grip and wick
as the sacrificial barrier.

### Self-fusing silicone tape, the honest verdict

**First, check it really is silicone.** Many tapes sold as "self-fusing"
or "self-amalgamating" are a different rubber. 3M's Scotch 23 rubber
splicing tape, for example, is ethylene propylene rubber (EPR), rated to
90 C operating and 130 C in an emergency overload, and the Parker O-Ring
Handbook rates ethylene propylene and butyl rubber unsatisfactory in
gasoline, kerosene and similar solvents. An EPR or butyl tape near a wick
or in the fuel zone is worse on both counts. The label or data sheet
should say silicone.

**The good.** Genuine silicone self-fusing tape bonds only to itself, not
to the tube, so there is no adhesive for fuel to dissolve and it comes
off without residue. Makers rate it 180 to 200 C continuous and 260 C
intermittent, and Rogers says it fuses permanently within 24 hours.

**The bad.**

- **Fuel swells it.** Shin-Etsu, a silicone rubber manufacturer, says
  silicone swells in non-polar solvents such as benzene, toluene and
  gasoline, softening and losing strength, but unlike most rubbers does
  not decompose or dissolve, and returns to its former state when the
  solvent is removed. The Parker handbook rates silicone unsatisfactory in
  gasoline, kerosene, Stoddard solvent (mineral spirits) and hexane. Its
  "fair" ratings are for mineral oils and a generic paraffins entry;
  whether paraffin lamp oil on a staff behaves like those rows has not
  been tested. Permatex's data sheet claims its tape stands up to fuel
  exposure, which fits a splash, not a soak. The swelling is temporary and the soak is
  the problem.
- **Heat resistant is not fireproof.** Shin-Etsu says silicone rubber is
  hard to ignite but will continue burning once ignited, unless a flame
  retardant has been added. (Permatex lists its tape as non-flammable, so
  the sources disagree; the flame-retardant grade settles it.) And a named
  silicone rubber's safety data sheet says it can give off formaldehyde
  vapour above 150 C in air. Keep silicone a few inches back from the
  wick, off the hottest metal, and replace any that has browned or
  scorched.

**So:**

- Wrap it onto a clean, dry, fuel-free tube. Permatex's directions say to
  remove oil and dirt first; fuel or oil between layers stops the tape
  fusing.
- Let it fuse fully before fuel comes near it. Makers give 24 hours for a
  full cure; waiting that long before the first burn is our precaution,
  not a published rule.
- Keep fuel off it. Spin off one end before you dip the other, so fuel
  does not run down the shaft to your grip. Let it dry out between burns.
- Clean the soot off and check it for cuts before each burn.
- **Do not put adhesive grip tape on top of it.** Silicone is notoriously
  hard to bond: Permabond, an adhesives maker, says the only adhesives that
  hold well are silicone-based ones, or a cyanoacrylate over a special
  primer. Ordinary tape adhesive will not hold. Put the main grip on clean
  metal in the middle of the staff instead of stacking it over silicone.

### What fuel does to grip materials

From the Parker O-Ring Handbook's compatibility tables, which are
long-soak tests on seal rubbers (a splash that evaporates is gentler, and
a grip is not a seal):

| Rubber | Gasoline | Kerosene | Service range |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silicone | Unsatisfactory | Unsatisfactory | -54 to 232 C |
| Fluorosilicone | Satisfactory | Satisfactory | -73 to 177 C |
| Fluorocarbon (Viton, FKM) | Satisfactory | Satisfactory | -26 to 205 C |
| Nitrile | Satisfactory | Satisfactory | -34 to 121 C |
| Polyurethane | Fair | Satisfactory | -40 to 82 C |
| Neoprene | Unsatisfactory | Fair | -37 to 107 C |
| Ethylene propylene (EPDM, EPR) | Unsatisfactory | Unsatisfactory | -57 to 121 C |
| Butyl, natural rubber | Unsatisfactory | Unsatisfactory | About -59 to 120 C (butyl) |

Two warnings on the good rows. No self-fusing fluorosilicone tape and no
fluorocarbon staff grip was found for sale. And fluorocarbon rubber is
fuel-proof on paper but a bad idea near fire: the Viton safety data sheet
lists hydrogen fluoride among its decomposition products, and warns that
above 315 C the surface may carry hydrogen fluoride condensate that can
cause severe burns. If a fluorinated rubber part ever burns, do not touch
the residue with bare hands.

No rating chart for tape adhesives in fire fuels was found. Keeping every
adhesive out of the fuel zone is the cautious call.

## Ends, plugs and swappable heads

**Plug or cap every end.** An open tube under a wick acts like a chimney
and heats very quickly, and a bare tube edge is sharp (**community
figure**). A plug also keeps fuel out of the inside of the shaft, where it
cannot be spun off.

**A thin wall cannot hold threads.** A screw tapped straight through a
0.040 inch wall catches only about 1.3 threads of a No. 8-32 or 10-32
screw (the wall thickness times 32 threads per inch). Nord-Lock, a maker of
bolt-securing systems, gives about one bolt diameter of thread engagement
in steel and two in aluminium as the basic rule for a joint where the bolt
breaks before the threads strip. For a No. 10 screw that is about 6
threads in steel and 12 in aluminium, against the 1.3 a thin wall
offers.

Cutting a thread into the tube itself is worse. A standard 60-degree
external thread is about 0.61 of its pitch deep. On a 0.040 inch wall
that removes about half the wall for a fine 32 threads-per-inch thread and
nearly all of it for a standard 3/4-16 thread, right at the end where
drops land, and a coarse 3/4-10 thread is deeper than the whole wall
(our arithmetic from the standard thread form).

**The fix: a solid plug with the threads in it.** Fit a close-fitting
solid plug in each end, held by two or more small radial screws, pins or
recessed rivets through the wall into the plug. NASA's fastener design
manual gives the common design practice for edge distance as about 2
fastener diameters from the hole centre to the edge, and never less than
1.5, so keep those holes at least 2 diameters from the tube end. Wick
screws and any swappable-head thread then bite into the plug, not the
wall. (Applying that general fastener guidance to staff plugs is our
adaptation, not a flow-arts standard.) Check what fits first: the staff
tube guide shows how the wall sets the inside diameter, and why a 5/8 inch
dowel is loose in a 0.040 inch wall.

**Plug materials.**

- **Fire ends: metal, or the classic hardwood plug.** Most metal fire
  staffs use wood plugs (**community figure**). Drill pilot holes; wood
  chars over time and its screws loosen, so check them.
- **Match expansion in titanium.** Stainless expands with heat about twice
  as much as grade 9 titanium (roughly 16.6 against 7.9 millionths per
  degree C on the Rolled Alloys and Fine Tubes sheets), so a tight
  stainless plug in titanium tube gets tighter every burn and loads the
  thin wall and its holes. A titanium plug matches the tube. If you use
  stainless, do not rely on a press fit; let the radial pins or screws do
  the holding. (Our reasoning from the published figures.)
- **Plastic plugs (Delrin, nylon) are for LED and practice ends only.**
  Distributor data sheets put acetal's maximum operating temperature at
  about 82 C and nylon's at about 93 to 110 C (**community figure**), and
  NAFAA advises against plastic connectors wherever heat can reach.

**Swappable heads.** Friction, compression and cam-lock joints can slip
when fuel or oil gets into them; one maker says so of its own system
(**community figure**), and NAFAA says friction should not be the main
thing holding a wick on. So back up any friction, compression or cam-lock
fire head with a **positive mechanical lock**: a cross pin or a set screw
into the plug. NAFAA says any connector that could be exposed to heat
should be tempered metal, not plastic, drop-forged or spring metal. A
swappable fire head's joint is such a connector, so avoid spring
push-button releases at a fire end. Clean and dry
every joint before swapping heads. Off-the-shelf heads are made for
particular tubes, so check the inside diameter before buying.

## Fasteners, galling and locking

Small parts, disproportionate consequences.

**Fastener metal in the flame zone: plain, unplated stainless.**
Stainless keeps useful strength hot and needs no coating. Keep brass back
from direct flame: it is about a third zinc, no source gives a temperature
at which zinc starts to leave it, and exposed screw heads in the flame run
hotter than the rest of the end (the staff tube guide covers this). Do not use
aluminium fasteners in the wick zone, and never zinc-plated, yellow or
cadmium-plated hardware (see the platings section below).

**Same-metal threads gall.** Galling is threads cold-welding together and
tearing as they turn. TIMET's titanium handbook names titanium's tendency
to gall, and NASA's fastener manual flags galling on 300-series
stainless fasteners, reduced by oxidising or passivating the surface.
Titanium on titanium is the worst common case and stainless on stainless
is close behind. For a swappable-head thread that is screwed in and out
constantly:

- Use **two different metals**, for example a passivated stainless stud
  into a titanium plug (see the galvanic note below for why passivated).
  Skip brass or bronze inserts in this joint: NASA's galvanic ranking
  puts yellow brass at 21 and silicon bronze at 26, below both titanium
  and passive stainless, so a small insert between them would be the
  part that corrodes.
- **Use anti-seize every time**, tighten slowly by hand, and never drive
  the thread dry or with a power driver.
- **On titanium, use a nickel-based anti-seize, never a silver-based
  one.** The current military surface-treatment specification,
  MIL-DTL-5002F (2022), bars silver and silver plating on or in contact
  with titanium parts and fasteners, and bars silver-plated hardware,
  clamps, tools, fixtures and jigs from titanium fabrication or assembly;
  its coating temperature table adds that silver must not touch titanium
  above 177 C (350 F) in service. (The earlier E edition limited the ban
  to parts used above 177 C (350 F); silver can embrittle titanium.)
  NASA's fastener manual describes one common high-temperature thread
  compound as 20 to 30 percent silver, so read the label. Henkel gives its
  LOCTITE LB 771 nickel anti-seize an operating range up to 1,315 C and
  says its nickel stays intact to that temperature. The mineral oil it is
  mixed in will not last that long: NASA's manual describes the same
  pattern in another nickel-bearing anti-seize, whose oil boils off and
  leaves non-galling metal oxides between the threads. Henkel markets LB
  771 for preventing galling, especially of stainless.
- **Keep cadmium off titanium entirely.** The same specification forbids
  cadmium plating on titanium parts or on anything that touches them, and
  says cadmium can cause solid or liquid metal embrittlement of the
  titanium. Old yellow or gold plated hardware may be cadmium.
- Anti-seize carries a cancer warning on many labels. Use a little and
  keep it off your hands.

**Aluminium touching titanium corrodes.** NASA's galvanic ranking puts
titanium at position 30 and the common aluminium alloys at 4 to 9, far
apart, and the manual's rule is that the anode (here, the aluminium)
should be the larger part. Ordinary aluminium pop rivets in a titanium
tube break that rule: with sweat or rain the small aluminium parts corrode
fastest. Use stainless or Monel rivets on titanium (Monel sits at 31,
next to titanium).

**Stainless in titanium is close in the ranking only while the stainless
stays passive.** Passive 304 sits at 32, beside titanium at 30, but active
304 drops to 14, and the manual warns that stainless fasteners cause
galvanic corrosion in a joint unless they are passivated or preoxidised.
A sweaty thread or the gap under a screw head is exactly the kind of
crevice where the protective film can fail, and that would make the small
stainless part the anode. So buy passivated fasteners, use the nickel
anti-seize, and rinse and dry the ends after sessions. The metals guide
explains the ranking, the area rule and why crevices defeat stainless
([Metals and Alloys](/library#metals-and-alloys)).

**Locking near the wick.**

- **Nylon-insert locknuts:** NASA's fastener manual puts the nylon's
  maximum operating temperature at about 121 C (250 F). Near wicks, use
  all-metal locknuts or a mechanical lock such as a cross pin.
- **Blue thread locker:** Henkel rates LOCTITE Threadlocker Blue 242 to
  149 C (300 F), and its release method is heating the joint to about
  250 C (482 F), which is roughly the metal temperature assumed at a wick.
  At a fire end, treat it as a backup to a mechanical lock, never as the
  lock. Henkel also says not to use it on plastic parts, where it can
  stress-crack them, which rules it out on LED-head threads.
- **Never Teflon (PTFE) tape on fire-end threads.** The fluoropolymer
  industry's handling guide puts hazardous fumes from about 330 C (the
  staff tube guide has the detail).

**Safety wire** through the wick and staff is cheap redundancy on the one
failure mode that matters, and wire is one of NAFAA's named methods.

**Working titanium.** A chlorine-free cutting oil or tapping paste is the
simple choice. TIMET says titanium should be cleaned with non-chlorinated
solvents before it is heated, and that parts machined with chlorinated
fluids need carefully controlled cleaning afterwards. Clean off all
residue away from any flame and let the cleaner dry completely, because
most non-chlorinated cleaners are highly flammable. Never use chlorinated
brake cleaner on anything that will be lit: OSHA requires chlorinated
degreasing vapour to be kept away from welding, and lists phosgene among
the toxic gases welding can produce. Flush
filings out of the tube and plugs before the first burn, because fine
titanium can self-ignite at about 250 C; smother a titanium chip fire
with dry sand, dry salt or a Class D extinguisher, never water or carbon
dioxide (the staff tube guide has the sources).

## LED heads

A typical LED end is a polycarbonate capsule around a rechargeable
lithium-ion cell. Standard 21 mm capsules do not fit inside 3/4 or 7/8
inch metal tube, so LED staffs carry them in clear 1 inch polycarbonate end
tubes (**community figure**; the staff tube guide shows the arithmetic).

- **Cool ends only.** A lithium-ion cell safety data sheet from
  Panasonic's Sanyo cells says to keep cells away from heat and fire, and
  that exposure above 100 C (212 F) can cause heat generation and
  ignition. Never put an LED head on an end that is still hot from a burn,
  and never store LED heads with fuel-soaked wicks.
- **Keep fuel off polycarbonate.** Covestro, which makes Makrolon
  polycarbonate, says it is not resistant to standard petrol and diesel
  fuels, and that the aromatic hydrocarbons in petrol cause cracking in
  parts under stress. Screw threads and fixings are exactly where the
  stress is. Wipe fuel off the tube and threads before an LED head goes
  on.
- **No thread locker on their plastic threads** (Henkel's warning above).

## Practice ends and weight matching

Common practice ends are silicone balls (about 70 mm), padded ends, soft
plastic ends with weights inside, steel rods, or rubber weights in place
of the wicks. One teacher suggests about 100 to 300 g (4 to 10 oz) per
end, with soft padded ends around 250 g for beginners (**community
figures**).

**Match your ends.** Swapping ends changes the feel of a staff far more
than the tube metal does. A 4 inch wick head works out to roughly 120 g
dry and about 245 to 275 g soaked (our arithmetic from one wick maker's
figures, in the wick section), while LED heads often weigh 200 to 350 g
(**community figure**). So a dry wick is much lighter than an LED head
and a fuelled one is about level with it. Weigh each head, allowing for
fuel in a wick, and add weight to the lighter ones. One maker sells
felt-lined wick covers made to mimic the weight of fuelled wicks
(**community figure**). Plug length and material are the easiest place to
tune a fire end.

**What to weight with.** Steel, tungsten or lead-free brass. Never lead in
a fire end: it melts at about 327 C, too close to the assumed wick-zone
temperatures. Some commercial practice ends use lead; in a family
workshop, skip lead and leaded brass altogether (ordinary free-machining
brass, alloy C36000, carries about 2.5 to 3.0 percent lead, per the Copper
Development Association), and wash your hands after handling either.

## Platings and coatings in the wick zone

The short version, from the staff tube guide's heat and fumes section:
**the bare metal is not the problem; what is on the metal is.** Bare
aluminium, stainless and titanium give off no meaningful fume at wick
temperatures. These do, or fail, in the wick zone:

| Surface | Why not near a wick |
|---|---|
| **Cadmium plating** (often yellow or gold, on old, surplus or aerospace hardware) | MIL-DTL-5002F bars cadmium on parts that see more than 232 C (450 F), and on or against titanium. Cadmium reaches a measurable vapour pressure at about 257 C. You cannot reliably tell it from yellow zinc by eye |
| **Zinc plating and galvanising** | The published limits spread widely. NASA's fastener manual gives zinc plate a useful limit of 121 C (250 F); the galvanizers' association limits hot-dip coating to 200 C long-term; MIL-DTL-5002F lists 260 C (500 F). All three are limits on the coating's protection, not fume thresholds. Zinc melts at about 419 C |
| **Yellow, gold or olive chromate** | Traditionally hexavalent chromium; no reason to have it at the fire end |
| **PTFE** (thread tape, coated or PTFE-sealed parts) | Hazardous fumes from about 330 C |
| **PVC and vinyl** (electrical tape, wraps) | Benzene from about 220 C; hydrogen chloride readily from about 225 C (NIST), mainly at 250 to 350 C |
| **Paint and powder coat** | Chars and smokes at the wick |
| **Old or unknown paint, salvaged tube** | Possibly lead. Use new tube, and do not strip suspected lead paint at home |
| **Silicone** | Formaldehyde above 150 C; keep it back from the hottest metal |
| **Epoxy** (carbon fibre, fibreglass) | Burning composite gives off fumes; always shield it |

Ordinary clear or dyed anodising will craze and fade at the wick, but is
very unlikely to matter as a fume source.

## Fuel

The fuels guide covers fuel in full. What the staff builder needs:

- **Almost every fuel used on props is a flammable liquid** under OSHA's
  definition (flash point at or below 93 C, 199.4 F). The highest-flash
  paraffin lamp oils are the exception: Lamplight's Ultra-Pure sheets give
  97 to 121 C, above that line, yet they still burn readily once held in a
  wick or spun off as a fine spray (the fuels guide has the detail). The
  split that matters for handling is whether a fuel gives off ignitable
  vapour at ordinary temperatures.
- **Camp fuel (white gas, Coleman fuel)** flashes below -18 C (0 F) on
  Coleman's sheet: its vapour is always there. Paint-aisle "naphtha" (VM&P)
  is a different, heavier product and not a spinning fuel.
- **Retail denatured alcohol** flashes at about 7 C (45 F) on Klean-Strip's
  sheet, may be up to 60 percent methanol, and burns nearly invisibly in
  daylight.
- **Kerosene** meeting ASTM D3699, the US kerosene specification that
  states such as Maryland make mandatory, must flash at 38 C (100 F) or
  higher.
- **"Lamp oil" is two products.** Paraffin lamp oils flash well above
  kerosene (83.5 C for Firefly, 97 to 121 C on Lamplight's Ultra-Pure
  sheets), while some clear lamp oils are kerosene-type (63 C for
  Lamplight's Medallion). Read the sheet for your bottle.
- **Gasoline: never.** Portland's fire performance policy prohibits it
  outright.

For the builder specifically:

- **Never re-dip a hot end, whatever the fuel.** A wick that looks out can
  still hold embers, and the metal under it can stay hot. The fuels guide
  gives self-ignition temperatures, with no flame needed, of roughly 200
  to 230 C for kerosene and lamp oil, and 260 to 280 C for the lighter
  camp-fuel type on the two sheets that state one; nobody has measured a
  staff end, but both overlap the assumed wick-zone range. Heavy ends,
  such as solid titanium or stainless plugs, stay hot longer. Wait until the whole head
  is cool to the touch.
- **Do not store wicks in fuel.** Fuel damages hardware, plugs and grips
  (**community figure**). Let wicks air out somewhere ventilated before
  covering or bagging them, then store them dry.
- **Keep carbon steel and chromoly parts coated or oiled.** Solvent fuels
  strip protective oil, and sweat speeds rust.

**One toxicity note that is not about fire at all.** The serious
poisoning risk from kerosene and naphtha is aspiration: swallowing them
and then breathing them in. NIOSH lists chemical pneumonitis from
aspirated liquid for both kerosene and VM&P naphtha, and ATSDR's fuel oils
profile describes kerosene aspiration, including fatal cases. This is why
fuel never goes in a drink bottle and why making someone vomit is the
wrong response. Methanol is a separate problem: it blinds, and it is
absorbed through the skin.

## The safety kit is part of the build

Budget for this at the same time as the staff. A first fire staff
without these is not finished.

- **A trained, sober safety spotter.** The single most important item,
  and not a material. They watch you, the audience and the fuel, and keep
  the crowd back. Never light up alone.
- **Something to smother flames.** Portland's fire performance policy
  requires each spotter to have at least a wet cotton towel of at least 15
  by 24 inches, or a wool or Nomex blanket or duvetyne. Only the towel is
  wetted: duvetyne's flame-retardant treatment washes out, so keep it dry
  and never wash it. The clothing guide
  covers what the spotter wears.
- **A fire extinguisher within reach of each spotter, and one covering the
  fuel depot.** NAFAA says every spotter and guard should ideally have
  one, rated at least class B, ABC preferred; Portland requires at least
  one 2A-10BC extinguisher close to each spotter. CAMEO's response
  guidance for petroleum naphtha is foam, carbon dioxide or dry chemical,
  and warns that water may be ineffective; for
  kerosene it warns that water spray may be inefficient. A towel handles a
  wick or a person; it does not handle a spilled and ignited depot.
- **Clothing that will not melt onto you.** In the US Forest Service's
  undergarment tests, each garment was worn under a flame-resistant aramid
  shirt and pants, then engulfed in flame for 4 seconds or exposed to
  radiant heat for 10. The polypropylene and polyester-elastane shirts
  melted, and melted material can stick to skin, while the 100 percent
  cotton and wool undergarments, shielded by that aramid layer, did not
  ignite, melt or char. That is a finding about melting against skin, not
  about cotton resisting fire: exposed cotton will still catch fire. Wear
  100 percent natural fibre or genuine aramid, snug, so nothing can melt
  onto you. The clothing guide covers what actually resists flame.
- **Hair tied back and covered, nothing sprayed or oiled in it.**
- **A fuel depot away from the performance area**, with a sealed metal can
  in a drip pan, a spin-off can, and one person responsible for it. The
  fuels guide has the distances.
- **Burn first aid, and know which kind of burn you are dealing with.**
  - *Minor burns* (small, skin unbroken): cool running water, not ice
    water. MedlinePlus says to keep a minor burn under cool water for at
    least 5 to 30 minutes, and burn-care guidelines summarised by CADTH
    call for at least 20 minutes of cool running water and no ice or ice
    water. Then cover it with a clean, dry dressing. Portland's policy
    requires each spotter to have an open 5 gallon bucket of water, at
    least three-quarters full, for cooling burns.
  - *Serious or large burns, and any clothing or hair fire:* put the
    flames out first (stop, drop and roll, and smother with the towel or
    blanket), then call emergency services at once. Cool with water, but
    do not put a large, severe burn into cold water; MedlinePlus warns
    this can cause shock. Do not pull off clothing that is stuck to the
    skin. Cover the burn with a clean, dry dressing or cloth.
  - Put no butter, oil, ice, cream or household remedy on a serious burn.
    Know where the nearest hospital is before you light up.
- **A wick cover** for between burns.
- **Damp ground, not pavement and not dry grass.** Damp grass, dirt or
  gravel. Never dry grass, leaf litter, scrub or forest floor: a dripping
  staff plus dry ground is how a wildfire starts. Check local fire
  restrictions and that you have permission to be there at all.

## Build order

This is genuinely sequential. Out of order means re-buying parts.

1. **Decide length and style before material.** Contact and rolling work
   wants longer and end-weighted; fast spinning wants shorter and lighter.
   Test the length on a dowel first.
2. **Pick the tube.** Order it cut to length. If it is composite, order
   the metal heat sleeves in the same purchase; the staff is unusable
   without them.
3. **Make and fit the end plugs.** Turn or sand them to a close fit, then
   drill and fit the radial pins or screws at least 2 fastener diameters
   from the end. Clean out every filing.
4. **Fit the thermal break and sleeves.** Retrofitting a sleeve past a
   mounted wick means taking the wick off again.
5. **Mount the wicks.** Through the wick into the plug with stainless
   screws or rivets, heads recessed, the wick lapped over the tube end.
   Tight matters: a loose barrel unrolls as the tape relaxes with heat.
6. **Silicone the transition zone** on clean, dry metal, from a few inches
   back from the wick toward the grip, and let it fuse.
7. **Grip last, and mark the centre** in a contrasting colour. Every
   production staff marks it, and it matters more than it sounds.
8. **Weigh and match the ends**, allowing for fuel in the wicks.
9. **Tug-test, then burn it in** with a spotter and a towel or blanket.
   Time the first burns and put the wick out as soon as the flame starts
   to shrink or go patchy. Watch for the wick loosening and for how hot
   the shaft gets at your hands. Retighten afterwards; almost every new
   barrel wick needs it.

## Worked example: the ends of a thin-wall titanium staff

A reader planning a 60 inch staff in 3/4 inch titanium at a 0.040 inch
wall, with swappable fire, LED and practice ends, is the case that
exercises every section above. The tube choice itself (grade 9 CWSR, and
whether 0.040 inch is thick enough) is worked through in the staff tube
guide. Here is what the rest of the research says:

- **The wall cannot hold head threads.** At 0.040 inch, a No. 10-32 screw
  gets about 1.3 threads, and cutting a 3/4-16 thread into the tube would
  remove nearly the whole wall. So every end needs a solid plug with the
  thread in it, held by two or more radial pins or screws at least 2
  diameters from the end.
- **Plug material: titanium for fire ends.** It matches the tube's
  expansion; a stainless plug expands about twice as much. The 0.670 inch
  inside diameter means turned plugs, since a 5/8 inch dowel would be
  loose. Delrin or nylon plugs are fine for the LED and practice ends
  only.
- **The head thread: two different metals.** A passivated stainless stud
  into a titanium plug, never titanium on titanium or stainless on
  stainless, with nickel anti-seize every time, never silver-based, and no
  silver or cadmium plated parts anywhere on the staff. Rinse and dry the
  thread after sweaty sessions, because a wet crevice is where stainless
  loses the passive film that keeps it close to titanium.
- **Rivets: stainless or Monel, never aluminium pop rivets**, which would
  corrode against the titanium.
- **Locking:** a cross pin or set screw backing up any quick-release fire
  head; no nylon-insert nuts, no thread locker as the only lock, and no
  Teflon tape on any fire-end thread.
- **Grip:** flame-retardant silicone tape (checked to be silicone, not EPR
  or butyl) in the zones between grip and wicks, with the main grip on
  clean metal in the middle rather than grip tape stacked over silicone.
  Leather is possible in the middle, well away from fuel.
- **Weight:** match every head on a scale. Solid plugs add roughly 25 g
  per inch in titanium or 45 g in stainless at this size (our arithmetic
  from density and the inside diameter), which is the easiest way to
  bring a light fire end up toward an LED head.
- **After burns:** the metal right by the wick holds heat longer than on
  aluminium, so let ends cool before handling, before an LED head goes on,
  and before re-dipping.
- **In the workshop:** chlorine-free oil and paste, residue cleaned off
  and dried before the first burn, filings swept up and flushed out, and
  no grinding near fuel.

to something that is exactly what you want, once you know what that is.
Either way, budget for fuel, a proper extinguisher and practice.

## Never on a fire staff

Each of these is explained above or in the staff tube guide; the two
that are covered only there get a one-line reason here.

- Gasoline as fuel, or paint-aisle VM&P naphtha in place of camp fuel.
- Re-dipping a hot end, with any fuel.
- Cotton wicks or meltable synthetic wicks.
- Glue or friction as the main wick hold, including friction-only
  quick-release heads.
- Letting wicks burn dry, storing them in fuel, or bagging them wet.
- Bamboo, PVC, polycarbonate or acrylic near fire; a bare wood shaft under
  a wick; carbon fibre or fibreglass without metal heat shields.
- Magnesium anywhere near fire: magnesium alloys ignite when heated in
  air near their melting point, and water on burning magnesium makes
  hydrogen and can explode (the staff tube guide has the source).
- Galvanised or zinc-coated tube (including steel EMT) at a fire end.
- Zinc-plated, yellow-chromated or cadmium-plated hardware near wicks;
  PTFE tape or PTFE-coated parts near wicks; paint, powder coat or vinyl
  wrap in the wick zone; old, painted or salvaged tube with an unknown
  finish.
- Soft tube for a fire contact staff (grade 1 titanium, 6063-T5, thin
  hardware-store aluminium under about 1.6 mm wall). That 1.6 mm minimum
  is one maker's rule for soft, usually 6063, hardware-store tube (a
  **community figure**). It is not a limit on the 6061-T6 or 7075-T6 tube
  this guide recommends at 0.055 to 0.065 inch (1.4 to 1.6 mm); strong
  7075 is commonly sold at 1.4 mm, and the staff tube guide shows the
  strength comparison.
- Open tube ends, plastic plugs or spring push-button releases at a fire
  end.
- Threads tapped straight into a thin wall; same-metal threads on
  swappable heads; head threads driven dry or with a power tool.
- Silver-based anti-seize, silver or cadmium plated parts, or aluminium
  parts (including pop rivets) on titanium; unpassivated stainless in
  titanium; an aluminium sleeve touching bare carbon fibre.
- Lead weights in fire ends; lead or leaded brass in children's props.
- Nylon-insert locknuts near the wick, or thread locker as the only lock.
- Chlorinated cutting oils, pastes or degreasers left on anything that
  will be heated; grinding titanium or aluminium near fuel; filings left
  in the tube.
- Water or carbon dioxide on burning titanium or magnesium chips.
- Non-silicone "self-fusing" tape (EPR or butyl) near fuel or wicks.
- Fluorinated rubber (Viton, FKM) anywhere it could burn.
- Adhesive grip tape over silicone tape.
- Grips that soak up fuel (absorbent foam, cloth, leather) where fuel can
  reach.
- PVC electrical tape near wicks.
- LED heads on a hot or fuel-wet end, or thread locker on their plastic
  threads.

## You own this when

- You can say what your shaft is made of, and at what temperature it
  starts losing strength, not just where it melts.
- Your wicks are fastened through the wick and the staff into a solid
  plug, with no glue or friction doing the holding, and you tug-test them
  before every burn.
- You know why a thin wall cannot hold threads, and your swappable heads
  use two different metals, anti-seize suited to the tube, and a positive
  mechanical lock.
- There is a deliberate thermal break between wick and grip, you checked
  that your self-fusing tape is really silicone, and you keep fuel off it.
- Your ends are matched by weight, with no lead in a fire end, and your
  LED heads only go on cool, dry ends.
- You picked your fuel for burn time, soot and handling, not because
  someone told you it burns colder, and you never dip a hot end.
- You have a spotter, a wet towel or blanket, an extinguisher, clothing
  that will not melt, and damp ground underfoot, every single time, and
  you know which burns you cool with water and which ones mean calling
  emergency services straight away.

When all seven are true you own a sound staff and you understand why each
part is what it is. That understanding is the part that transfers:
matching a material to a temperature, keeping heat and fuel away from the
parts that cannot take them, and knowing which failure mode actually hurts
someone are the same skills behind every piece of equipment you will ever
build or repair.

### Removed from this guide

These were in earlier versions and are no longer used. They are listed so
nobody re-adds them.

- Every retailer and hobbyist blog previously cited for material
  properties, including dark-monk.com, bonoboflow.com, firemecca.com,
  homeofpoi.com, fireandflow.co.nz, flowartsinstitute.com,
  drexfactor.com, kloecknermetals.com, sendcutsend.com and hontitan.com.
  Marketing copy is not an authority on melting points. Several of these
  reappear above, but only for practical details labelled community
  figure, never for a material property.
- hypertextbook.com, a student-compiled physics figure collection, which
  was the basis for the flame temperature claims.
- zenodo.org/records/7236629, a self-published dataset of adiabatic flame
  temperatures. It lists naphtha at 4591 C, which is not physically
  possible, so nothing in it is safe to quote.
- **The "starts to fail" table built on melting points.** It put 7075-T6
  at 477 C, 6061-T6 at 582 C and Ti-6Al-4V at 1670 C as where failure
  starts. Heat-treated aluminium loses strength permanently at a fraction
  of those temperatures, so the table now leads with working limits. The
  related claim that 6061 has "more than a hundred degrees of margin" over
  7075 went with it.
- **"Titanium is largely a myth", "effectively absent from the market",
  "the lowest conductivity of the metals here" and "the only material
  that actually solves heat reaching your hands".** Grade 9 tube is widely
  stocked and titanium staffs are sold; commercially pure titanium
  conducts more heat than stainless; and wood conducts less than any
  metal.
- **The claim that every material except titanium falls inside the flame
  band.** Titanium's own melting point, as quoted, is inside it.
- **"Pick 7075 for stiffness"** and **"carbon fibre is the stiffest option,
  no flex".** Aluminium alloys share one modulus, and no sourced figure
  supported the carbon fibre claim.
- **"6061 gives the heaviest metal build".** The steels in the same table
  weigh far more.
- **The Forest Products Laboratory figure of 216 W/(m K) for pure
  aluminium**, which sat below a supplier's figure for 99 percent
  aluminium and contradicted the point it was making.
- **"Mild steel: cheapest metal, about 45" and "buy plain or black steel
  tube instead, it costs barely more".** On the snapshot date carbon steel
  DOM tube cost more than the same size in 304 stainless, and bare carbon
  steel needs a coating that burns at a wick.
- **"Silicone and industrial rubber shed fuel instead of absorbing it"**
  and **"fuel dissolves the adhesive" in sports tape.** Rubber charts and
  a silicone maker contradict the first; no rating chart supports the
  second, so the text now says only that no chart exists.
- **The sizing table's burn time of 7 to 9 minutes**, which contradicted
  the per-fuel figures of 3 to 4 and 5 to 7 minutes. It described one
  monkey-fist staff, and now appears only as that.
- **The old Fuel table.** It grouped camp fuel with VM&P paint thinner,
  put lamp oil inside the kerosene flash point range when paraffin lamp
  oils flash well above it, used CAMEO's 35 C as kerosene's lower bound
  instead of the 38 C specification minimum, gave denatured alcohol as 11
  to 13 C (pure methanol and ethanol) instead of the retail product's 7 C,
  called kerosene the longest burn, and said camp fuel burns "bluer". The
  fuel material now lives in the fuels guide, and the short summary here
  matches it.
- **Brass screws recommended in the wick zone.** Brass is about a third
  zinc; plain stainless is the better flame-zone fastener.
- **Stainless hose clamps as a wick fastening, and aramid stitching as a
  primary attachment.** Neither passes through the wick and the staff as
  NAFAA's guidelines ask. Stitching remains as a repair for fraying.
- **The wick grade rows for "K2 for eating torches", "pure aramid" and
  "Technora, poor fuel absorption".** They had no surviving source; the
  grade table now uses the supplier's own descriptions.
- **"Buy 1/4 inch" as the thickness recommendation.** 1/8 inch is the
  common thickness, and 1/4 inch was not stocked in 4 inch widths.
- **"Traditional fitting is chin to nose height".** The common rule is
  floor to chin; the staff tube guide has the detail.
- **The old cost table** ($55 for a 6061 shaft, $90 to $200 for 7075, and
  estimated totals for heat shielding, grip, hardware and safety kit).
  The tube prices were several times what stockists charged on the
  snapshot date, and the other rows had no source. It is replaced by the
  dated snapshot of individual listed prices.
- **"Duvetyne is the community standard; a wet towel the substitute"**,
  replaced by Portland's actual list: a wet cotton towel, or a wool or
  Nomex blanket, or duvetyne.
- **"Charring becomes the dominant process above about 300 C
  internally".** The Wood Handbook says the reverse: char dominates below
  about 300 C, flammable volatiles above it.
- **The 6061-T6 onset of "half an hour at 190 C"**, left out of this
  guide and the staff tube guide, not disproved. It is a by-eye reading
  of an MMPDS chart; a review could not re-check the chart and doubted a
  measurable loss so soon after the 8 hour bake at 171 to 182 C that
  makes T6, so both guides use only the longer-exposure points, and say
  a fifth of the T6 yield strength rather than of "its strength",
  because the ultimate strength falls less.
- **"Over a thousand times less conductive than aluminium"** for
  hardwood, and **"35 to 40 times less heat"** for titanium with no wall
  stated. Dense hardwood is about 680 to 1,040 times less conductive
  depending on the alloy, and most of the titanium ratio came from
  comparing a thinner wall.
- **"Methanol is the cool case, because it makes almost no soot"**, which
  no source here supports and which gets the physics backwards: soot
  radiates heat away.
- **"Dry 4 inch wicks weigh about 75 to 100 g per end"**, which had no
  source; replaced by arithmetic from a wick maker's per-foot weights.
- **The claim that the construction table's burn times sit inside the
  per-fuel ranges.** They run longer.
- **"A complete production staff costs less than the parts for one staff
  bought retail"**, which the guide's own price snapshot contradicted.
- A staff that **"must"** be fireproof, and NAFAA's connector rule
  stated as a requirement. NAFAA's guidelines are voluntary and say
  "should". A later version wrongly narrowed the heated-connector item to
  chains and called the push-button advice our own reasoning; the item
  covers any connector that could be exposed to heat, so the advice rests
  on NAFAA directly.
- **"Fibreglass: tougher than carbon fibre"**, **"bamboo splits at nodes
  and with moisture change"** and **"fuel warps hardwood"**, none of which
  had a source.
- **The blanket burn first aid of "cool running water first"** for every
  burn. MedlinePlus gives that for minor burns only, and says a severe
  burn should not go into cold water.
- **"Every fuel used on props is a flammable liquid"**, which the guide's
  own Ultra-Pure lamp oil figure contradicted.
