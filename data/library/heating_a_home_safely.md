# Heating a Home Safely

Heating a home goes wrong in two ways, and both are common.

The first is fire. The US Fire Administration says heating fires remained
"the second leading cause of home fires in 2021": an estimated 32,200
home heating fires that year, with 190 deaths, 625 injuries and $442
million in property loss.

The second is poison. Everything that burns fuel makes carbon monoxide
(CO), a gas you cannot see, smell or taste, and the Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention (CDC) puts the deaths from unintentional carbon
monoxide poisoning not linked to fires at more than 400 Americans a
year. In western Washington, eight of them followed a single windstorm,
from barbecues and generators used indoors or improperly while the power
was out (the worked example below).

There is a third way, and it is the reason people take the risks of the
first two: cold itself. [Cold and Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia)
explains why hypothermia so often kills people indoors, in a house that
has lost its heat, rather than in the wilderness. Staying warm is not
optional. This guide is about staying warm without burning the house down
or poisoning the people in it.

It covers the rules that hold for every heater, what each common kind of
heater needs from you, what never to heat with, what to do when the
power or the heat goes out, heating a workshop, a tent or a car, getting
help with the cost, and where your own work stops and a professional's
starts. The worked example is a December windstorm near Silverdale,
Washington, where the simulation is set.

Several guides carry parts of this subject, and this one does not repeat
them. Wood stoves, chimneys, creosote, ashes and burn bans are in
[Firewood](/library#firewood). Carbon monoxide alarms, backdrafting and why a
tightly sealed house changes how a flue behaves are in [Ventilation, Damp
and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould). Keeping the heat in is
[Insulation and Heat Loss](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss). Fuels, their
storage and gas leaks are in [Fuels and Their Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards),
and starting and putting out a fire is in [Making and Controlling
Fire](/library#making-and-controlling-fire). A space heater's load on a circuit
is worked out in [Electricity and How It Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows),
and generators are in [Where Your Own Electrical Work
Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops). Water heaters are in
[Heating Water](/library#heating-water).

Its sources are the US Fire Administration (USFA), the Consumer Product
Safety Commission (CPSC), the CDC, the Environmental Protection Agency
(EPA) and its ENERGY STAR programme, the Department of Energy and its
Building America Solution Center, the National Weather Service, NOAA's
storm records, the Federal Emergency Management Agency's Ready.gov, the
Department of Health and Human Services and the Army's survival manual,
all US federal and in the public domain. For the worked example it also
uses Washington State law and Washington agencies, restated in our own
words. Where something is general practice or our own reading rather
than a published rule, the text says so.

## First: the rules for every heater

These hold whatever heats your home, and they are not negotiable.

1. **Burn nothing indoors unless it was built to vent outside.** The CDC
   says not to cook or burn anything on a stove or in a fireplace that is
   not vented. A charcoal grill, a camp stove, a patio heater and a
   generator are all built for outdoors.
2. **Never heat a home with the oven or the stove top.** The USFA: "Never
   use an oven to heat your home." The CPSC's carbon monoxide leaflet
   widens it: "Never use a gas range, oven, or dryer for heating."
   Ready.gov adds that an oven or stove top used this way also risks fire
   and burns and can damage the appliance.
3. **Never bring a generator, grill, camp stove or charcoal indoors.**
   Ready.gov's power outage page: "Generators, camp stoves or charcoal
   grills should always be used outdoors and at least 20 feet away from
   windows." The CPSC: "Never burn charcoal in homes, tents, vehicles, or
   garages." A federal rule makes this a warning on the bag itself: the
   labelling rule for charcoal (16 CFR 1500.14(b)(6)) exists "Because
   inhalation of the carbon monoxide produced by burning charcoal indoors
   or in confined areas can cause serious injury or death".
4. **Three feet of clear space.** The USFA: "Keep anything that can burn
   at least 3 feet from all heat sources including fireplaces, wood
   stoves, radiators, space heaters or candles." Bedding, clothing drying
   on a rack, curtains, paper and furniture all count.
5. **Heaters off when you sleep or leave.** The USFA: "Turn space heaters
   off when leaving the room or going to bed." The CPSC says the same
   about electric heaters: never leave one running unattended or while you
   are sleeping.
6. **Alarms, both kinds, on every level.** Smoke alarms inside and
   outside each bedroom and sleeping area and on every level, including
   the basement, tested monthly and replaced 10 years from the date they
   were made (USFA). Carbon monoxide alarms in a central location outside
   each separate sleeping area and on every level (USFA), replaced
   following the maker's instructions or every 5 years, with the batteries
   checked when the clocks change each spring and autumn (CDC). They are
   two different devices. [Ventilation, Damp and
   Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould) has the detail.
7. **A professional looks at it every year.** The USFA: have heating
   equipment and chimneys cleaned and inspected each year by a
   professional. The CDC says to have the heating system, the water heater
   and any other gas, oil or coal burning appliance serviced by a
   qualified technician every year, and ENERGY STAR suggests doing the
   heating system's check-up in the autumn, before the contractors get
   busy.
8. **If the CO alarm sounds, everyone goes outside first.** Then call
   911 or the fire department from outside or from a neighbour's home.
   The CPSC's leaflet says to get fresh air immediately, call for help
   from a neighbour's home, and let the fire department decide when it is
   safe to go back in. Do not go back in to find the cause. [Ventilation,
   Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould) says the same.

## Know what heats your home

Most homes have one main heater and, sometimes, a second one people turn
on when the first is not enough or the power is out. Each kind fails in
its own way.

### A furnace or boiler that burns gas, oil or propane

The burner sits in a basement, a garage, a closet or a crawlspace, and a
vent pipe or chimney carries its exhaust outside. The CPSC says that
heating appliances kept in good working order produce little carbon
monoxide, and a whole, clear vent sends what they make outdoors (our
reading). Your part:

- **Keep its space clear.** The USFA's basement advice: keep anything
  that can burn at least 3 feet from the furnace, the oil burner, wood
  stoves, water heaters and other heat-generating equipment, and keep
  the burner access doors on gas furnaces and gas water heaters closed
  "to prevent flames from escaping and starting a fire."
- **Keep its vents clear.** The USFA: make sure the vents for the dryer,
  furnace, stove and fireplace are clear of snow and other debris. The
  National Weather Service adds that after a snowstorm you should check
  that a gas furnace's vent is not blocked by a drift as soon as it is
  safe to go out.
- **Change the filter.** ENERGY STAR: inspect, clean or change the air
  filter in a furnace or heat pump once a month. A clogged filter wastes
  energy and wears out the equipment.
- **Put a CO alarm near it.** The USFA: if you have oil-, gas- or
  wood-burning equipment in the basement, you should also have a carbon
  monoxide alarm in the basement.
- **Look for the clues.** The CPSC's leaflet *Carbon Monoxide (CO): The
  Invisible Killer* lists signs you can see: rust or water streaking on a
  vent or chimney, a loose or missing furnace panel, soot, debris or soot
  falling from a chimney or appliance, a loose or disconnected vent pipe,
  loose masonry on a chimney, and moisture on the inside of windows. It
  adds problems you will notice in use: less hot water than usual, a
  furnace that cannot heat the house or runs all the time, and an
  unfamiliar or burning smell. The EPA adds one more: "A persistent
  yellow-tipped flame is generally an indication of maladjustment and
  increased pollutant emissions." Writing about gas ranges, it says to ask
  the gas company to adjust the burner so that the flame tip is blue.
  The CPSC's leaflet says what to do about any of these: "Only a trained
  service technician can detect hidden problems and correct these
  conditions!"

The annual check-up is not only about efficiency. ENERGY STAR lists what
a contractor checks on a heating system: the gas or oil connections, the
gas pressure, the burner's combustion and the heat exchanger. Its reason
is blunt: faulty gas or oil connections are a fire hazard, and a dirty
burner or a cracked heat exchanger makes the equipment run less safely.

### A heat pump, electric baseboards or electric radiant heat

These burn nothing in the house, so they make no carbon monoxide there
(general knowledge). They still get hot enough to matter: the USFA's
3 foot rule names radiators, and a baseboard heater behind a sofa or a
curtain is a heat source with something that burns against it (our
reading). Keep furniture and curtains back from them.

### Portable electric space heaters

A common second heater, and the one behind a large share of the worst
fires. The USFA reports that home portable heater fires were only
3 percent of home heating fires in 2017 to 2019, "but they accounted
for 41% of fatal heating fires in homes." The CPSC estimated about
1,200 fires a year involving portable electric heaters from 2008 to
2010, and explains the problem: they are high-wattage appliances that can
ignite nearby curtains, beds, sofas, paper, clothing and flammable
liquids.

The rules, from the USFA's *Heater Fire Safety* card and the CPSC's
*Reducing Fire Hazards for Portable Electric Heaters*:

- **Buy one that has been tested.** The USFA: "Make sure your heater has
  been tested by a recognized testing laboratory." The CPSC says a
  certified heater carries a safety certification mark, and to check
  whether your model has been recalled at cpsc.gov or SaferProducts.gov.
- **Tip-over shut-off.** The USFA: make sure it "has an automatic
  shut-off, so if it tips over, it shuts off."
- **A solid, flat surface,** and somewhere it will not be knocked over
  (USFA, CPSC).
- **Three feet from anything that can burn,** in front, at the sides and
  behind (CPSC).
- **Straight into a wall outlet.** The USFA: "Plug portable heaters
  directly into outlets. Never use an extension cord or power strip." It
  adds: do not plug anything else into the same circuit as the heater,
  because that can cause overheating. [Electricity and How It
  Flows](/library#electricity-and-how-it-flows) works through the arithmetic: a
  1,200 watt heater draws 10 amps, most of the 12 amps a 15 amp circuit
  should be asked to carry for hours at a time.
- **A loose or hot plug means stop.** The CPSC: the plug must fit tightly
  in the outlet; during use, check whether the plug, cord, outlet or
  faceplate is hot, and if it is, stop using the heater and have an
  electrician check the outlet or a repair person check the heater.
- **Cords in the open.** The CPSC: never run the cord under a rug or
  carpet, which can damage it and set it and things nearby alight.
- **Away from water.** The CPSC says to keep electric heaters away from
  water and never to touch one if you are wet. The CPSC's home electrical
  checklist, quoted in [Where Your Own Electrical Work
  Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops), says to avoid portable
  heaters in bathrooms altogether.
- **Never left on unattended or while you sleep** (CPSC), and off when
  you leave the room or go to bed (USFA).
- **Never with a damaged cord or plug.** The USFA flyer: check your space
  heater for cracked or damaged cords and plugs. The CPSC: never operate
  a heater you suspect is damaged.

### Kerosene heaters

A portable kerosene heater has no chimney: everything it burns stays in
the room. The CPSC's safety alert *CPSC Stresses Kerosene Heater Safety*
says that although these heaters burn efficiently, they produce low
levels of pollutants such as carbon monoxide and nitrogen dioxide, which
may be harmful, especially to people with chronic respiratory or
circulatory problems. Its rules:

- **Only 1-K kerosene, and never gasoline.** The CPSC: "Use only
  water-clear 1-K grade kerosene. Never use gasoline." Even a small amount
  of gasoline or another volatile fuel mixed into kerosene, it says, can
  substantially increase the risk of fire or explosion. Ready.gov calls
  the same fuel "crystal clear K-1 kerosene". Buy it from a dealer who can
  certify that it is 1-K, because the CPSC warns that different grades can
  look the same. Why gasoline is so different is in [Fuels and Their
  Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards).
- **Its own container.** Kerosene goes in a container meant for kerosene,
  never in a gasoline can or one that ever held gasoline. Kerosene cans
  are usually blue and gasoline cans usually red (CPSC).
- **Refuel outside, cold.** The CPSC: "Never refuel the heater inside the
  home." Fill it outdoors, away from anything that burns, only after it
  has been turned off and has cooled, and never above the "full" mark,
  because the space above it lets the fuel expand without leaking.
- **Air.** The CPSC says to run the heater in a room with a door open to
  the rest of the house, and to open an outside window about an inch if
  the door must be closed, or if the house is new and tight or has been
  weatherized. The EPA gives the same advice for any unvented kerosene or
  gas space heater. Set the wick as the maker says and keep it clean
  (CPSC).
- **Off at bedtime,** like every space heater (USFA).
- **If it flares up, do not carry it out.** The CPSC: do not try to move
  or carry the heater, because that can make the fire worse; use its
  manual shut-off switch if it has one, and if that does not put the fire
  out, leave the house and call the fire department. Its warning label
  adds: do not try to smother the fire or put water on it.
- **Local rules.** The label the CPSC reproduces ends: "Follow All
  Applicable Code Requirements When Using Heater." Ask your fire
  department whether your city or county limits kerosene heaters indoors
  (our caution).

### Unvented gas and propane heaters

Some gas and propane heaters are sold to be used without a vent, and
small portable propane heaters are common. Treat every one as a carbon
monoxide source in the room you are breathing in. The CPSC: "Never
operate an unvented gas-burning appliance in a closed room or in a room
in which you are sleeping." [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould) gives Building America's advice not
to install unvented combustion appliances in the living space at all,
notes that the National Fuel Gas Code forbids vent-free heaters in
bathrooms and bedrooms, and explains the gallon of water such a heater
puts into the room for every 100,000 Btu it burns. The CDC also says not
to use portable flameless chemical heaters indoors: no flame does not
mean no fuel being burned (our reading of its advice).

A propane cylinder belongs outside the house. The CPSC's grill fact sheet
says never to use or store an LP gas container indoors. [Fuels and Their
Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards) explains why propane that leaks
sinks and gathers low.

The best answer, if you are choosing a heater, is one that cannot put
exhaust into the room at all: a direct-vent, sealed-combustion heater
that takes its air from outside and sends its exhaust outside, or one
that burns nothing. That is Building America's best practice, set out in
[Ventilation, Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould).

### Wood stoves, pellet stoves and fireplaces

[Firewood](/library#firewood) covers these in full: dry wood, burning hot rather
than damped down, creosote and chimney fires, ashes in a lidded metal can
10 feet from the house, carbon monoxide, and burn bans. A few points from
other sources belong with them:

- **Inspect and clean every year, and look every month.** Ready.gov:
  "Inspect and clean woodstove pipes and chimneys annually and check
  monthly for damage or obstructions."
- **A screen across a fireplace.** Ready.gov: use a screen "heavy enough
  to stop rolling logs and big enough to cover the entire opening of the
  fireplace to catch flying sparks."
- **Out before you leave or sleep.** Ready.gov: "Make sure the fire is
  completely out before leaving the house or going to bed." The USFA says
  the same about fireplaces and wood stoves, as [Firewood](/library#firewood)
  quotes.
- **A gas fireplace needs its flue open.** The EPA: "Always make certain
  the flue in your gas fireplace is open when the fireplace is in use."
- **Old stove door gaskets may contain asbestos.** The EPA says some old
  wood stove door gaskets do, and points to the booklet *Asbestos in the
  Home* from the CPSC, the American Lung Association and the EPA before
  you replace one; new gaskets are fibreglass.
- **A certified stove, properly installed.** In Washington, the
  Department of Ecology says never to install a stove that is not
  certified, and that it does not regulate installation itself: your local
  building permit department does (restated). The worked example below
  has more.

### The kitchen stove and oven

Never as a heater, as rule 2 says. Two smaller points from the CPSC's
leaflet: never put foil on the bottom of a gas oven, because it interferes
with combustion, and a malfunctioning range is on its list of carbon
monoxide sources.

## When the power or the heat goes out

This is when people get hurt, so plan it before the storm.

**Keep the heat you have.** The National Weather Service's advice if your
heat goes out:

- close off rooms you do not need, so you heat less space;
- stuff towels or rags in the cracks under doors;
- close blinds or curtains to keep some heat in;
- eat and drink, because food is the fuel your body makes heat from, and
  drink plenty of water and other non-caffeinated, non-alcoholic drinks,
  because cold air is very dry;
- wear layers of loose-fitting, lightweight, warm clothing, and take
  layers off before you sweat.

Choose one room, preferably on the side of the house away from the wind
and with few windows, and gather everyone in it (general practice,
following the Weather Service's advice to close off the rest). One
caution about sealing that room: if anything in it burns fuel, it still
needs air. The CPSC says a kerosene heater needs a door open to the rest
of the house, or an outside window open about an inch, and
[Firewood](/library#firewood) explains how a wood stove takes its air from the
room, and how a house made too tight can have the smoke pulled back down
the chimney. Close off the rooms you are not using, not the one the fire
is in, and keep a carbon monoxide alarm in the room where everyone sleeps
(our reading of the two together).

**Know when to leave.** Ready.gov: "Go to a community location with power
if heat or cold is extreme," and check with local officials about heating
and cooling locations open near you. Infants, older adults and people who
are ill are at greater risk from cold, as [Cold and
Hypothermia](/library#cold-and-hypothermia) explains; for them, going somewhere
warm early is the safe choice (our reading).

**Light without flame.** The USFA: "Never use candles for emergency
lighting. Use flashlights and stock up on extra batteries." Its candle
page adds: do not use lit candles in bedrooms, bathrooms or sleeping
areas.

**Heat without carbon monoxide.** Every rule in the first section applies
twice as hard in an outage, because the temptation is greatest then. If
you have a generator, it runs outdoors only, at least 20 feet from
windows, doors and attached garages (Ready.gov), never plugged into a wall
outlet, and is refuelled only once it has cooled; [Where Your Own
Electrical Work Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops) has the rest.
An electric space heater run from a generator still follows every space
heater rule above (our reading).

**Have fuel in hand before the storm.** The National Weather Service:
refuel before you are empty, because fuel carriers may not reach you for
days after a winter storm. Store it as [Fuels and Their
Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards) describes.

**After it is over.** If you shut off the gas during the outage or an
earthquake, Ready.gov says only a qualified professional can turn it back
on.

## Heating other spaces

**A garage, workshop or shed.** The same rules, and one more: fuel
vapour. The CPSC reported in 2003 that gas water heaters lighting
flammable vapours were involved in nearly 800 home fires a year, and
said gasoline should never be stored or used indoors, in a basement or a
garage, where its vapours can ignite. A heater with a flame in a room
with a petrol can, paint thinner or a mower's fuel tank is the same
mechanism (our reading). Keep fuels out of any space you heat with a
flame; [Fuels and Their Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards) says where
they go instead.

**A car.** The CPSC: "Never leave a car running in a garage even with the
garage door open." If you are stuck in a car in snow, the National Weather
Service says to stay in it, run the engine about 10 minutes each hour for
heat with a window open a little, and clear snow from the exhaust pipe
so the fumes do not come in.

**A tent, cabin or shelter.** The CPSC's charcoal warning names tents. The
Army's survival manual, written for soldiers in cold-weather shelters:
"Never fall asleep without turning out your stove or lamp." It adds that
any open flame may generate carbon monoxide, and that even in a
ventilated shelter incomplete combustion can poison you without warning.

**A greenhouse or hoop house.** Heaters there, and why an unvented one
harms both the plants and the people working in it, are in [Extending the
Season](/library#extending-the-season).

## Paying for heat without taking risks

Many of the risky choices in this guide start with a heating bill nobody
can pay (our reading). Three things help.

**Keep the heat in first.** [Insulation and Heat
Loss](/library#insulation-and-heat-loss) shows where a house loses its heat and
which fixes pay back fastest. Air sealing a house that has a fuel-burning
appliance changes how that appliance vents, so have it checked afterwards
([Ventilation, Damp and Mould](/library#ventilation-damp-and-mould)).

**Turn the thermostat down at night and when you are out.** Building
America, quoting the Department of Energy, says households can save as
much as 10 percent a year on heating and cooling by turning the thermostat
back 7 to 10 degrees F for eight hours a day from its normal setting, and
that the saving is larger in mild climates than in severe ones. With a
heat pump, it recommends a thermostat made for heat pumps, which brings
the temperature back up without leaning on the expensive backup heat.

**Ask for help before the shutoff notice.** The federal Low Income Home
Energy Assistance Program (LIHEAP), run by the Department of Health and
Human Services, helps households with heating bills, with preventing a
shutoff or reconnecting one, with making a home more efficient, and with
"repairing or replacing heating equipment." It is applied for through
your state or local agency; the department's page points to
EnergyHelp.us and the National Energy Assistance Referral line,
1-866-674-6327. It also warns that LIHEAP does not give direct grants to
individuals and does not charge a fee: a message offering you a LIHEAP
grant or asking for a fee is not from LIHEAP, and the page says to report
it to the department's fraud hotline, 1-800-447-8477. The Department of Energy's Weatherization
Assistance Program improves the energy efficiency of low-income
households' homes "while ensuring their health and safety", and says a
national evaluation found those households save on average $372 or more
a year.

## Worked example: a December windstorm near Silverdale

The project's canonical place is Silverdale, on the Kitsap Peninsula (see
[Silverdale, Washington](/library#silverdale-wa)). Its winters are
mild and wet rather than hard: at the Bremerton weather station, the
nearest with a full record, the average December low is 1.7 C and the
record low is -13.9 C (`data/locales/silverdale_wa/climate.json`, from
NOAA normals). The danger here is not usually the cold. It is the outage,
and what people do about it.

**What happened in December 2006.** NOAA's storm record describes a
Pacific storm that struck Washington on the night of 14 December 2006,
the strongest windstorm since 1993, which blew down thousands of trees
and cut power to close to 1.5 million customers in western Washington.
For the forecast zone it calls the Seattle/Bremerton area, the record
lists eight indirect deaths from carbon monoxide poisoning after the
storm, from barbecues and generators used improperly or indoors, and 275
people treated for carbon monoxide poisoning at area hospitals. The same
record notes that a man died when his home burned in a fire apparently
started by a candle used for light. [The Hazards Your Place Actually
Has](/library#the-hazards-where-you-live) draws the lesson: the wind did not
kill those people, the response to the outage did.

**What Washington did about it.** In 2009 the state legislature passed a
carbon monoxide alarm law, and its statement of intent names that storm,
saying that more than a thousand people in the state were seen at
emergency rooms with symptoms of carbon monoxide poisoning and eight
died. The law (RCW 19.27.530) had the state building code council
require carbon monoxide alarms in residential buildings: in new ones by 1
January 2011, and in the rest by 1 January 2013. Owner-occupied
single-family houses already lived in before 26 July 2009 were left out,
but when such a house is sold, the seller must put in alarms that meet
the building code before the buyer moves in. In a rented home, the law
makes keeping the alarm working, batteries included, the tenant's job
(all restated). Whatever the law requires of your house, the alarm is
what keeps you alive; put one on every level (rule 6).

**The household.** This part is an illustration with made-up details.
It is a December evening in a two-storey house near Silverdale with a gas
furnace, an electric water heater and a certified wood stove in the
living room. The wind rises, the lights go out at 8 pm, and the utility's
outage map says the next day at the earliest.

1. **The furnace is out too,** because its fan and controls need
   electricity even though it burns gas (general knowledge). Nobody turns
   on the oven.
2. **One warm room.** They close the upstairs doors and stuff towels
   along the bottom of the doors to the rooms they are not using, close
   the curtains, and bring the sleeping bags down to the living room. The
   living room, where the stove is, stays open to the hall so the stove
   gets its air. Everyone eats and drinks something warm.
3. **The wood stove.** It was swept in September, the wood is dry, and
   it burns hot with the screen closed, nothing within 3 feet. If the
   Puget Sound Clean Air Agency had called an air quality burn ban, its
   rules and the exemption for a home whose only adequate heat is wood
   would decide whether it could be lit at all; [Firewood](/library#firewood)
   explains both.
4. **Light.** Flashlights and a battery lantern, no candles.
5. **The neighbour's generator.** It runs in the open, more than 20 feet
   from any window or door, with its exhaust pointing away from both
   houses.
6. **Alarms.** The carbon monoxide alarm outside the bedrooms has a
   battery, so it works without mains power. If it sounds, everyone goes
   outside and calls 911 from there.
7. **If it gets too cold,** they check with Kitsap County's emergency
   information for a warming centre and go, taking the elderly neighbour
   with them (general practice; Ready.gov says to go to a community
   location with power when the cold is extreme).

**Help with the bill.** In Washington, LIHEAP is run through a network of
community action agencies and local partners; you apply by appointment
with the provider for your area, once in each program year, which runs
from 1 October to 30 September (Washington State Department of Commerce,
restated). Its map of providers is the place to start.

**A new stove.** The Department of Ecology says it is illegal in
Washington to sell an uncertified wood stove, that a stove you install
must be certified, and that installation rules come from your local
building permit department (restated). Ask your city's or county's
building department what it needs before you buy (our caution).

## Where your own work stops

Each of these belongs to a trained professional, the utility or the fire
department. That is our recommendation for a household, built on the
sources given with each.

- **Installing, moving, connecting or repairing anything that burns
  fuel,** and anything to do with gas or oil lines. The CPSC: "Most
  appliances should be installed by professionals," and never install or
  service combustion appliances without the proper knowledge, skills and
  tools. That includes burners, heat exchangers and vent pipes (ENERGY
  STAR's contractor checklist).
- **Chimneys and stoves:** the yearly inspection and cleaning by a
  professional (USFA), and installation under a building permit
  (Washington's Department of Ecology, restated).
- **Any clue on the CPSC's list above,** or a yellow, lazy flame on a gas
  burner: stop using the appliance and call a qualified technician or the
  gas company (our reading of the CPSC's and the EPA's advice).
- **A smell of gas:** leave, and call from outside; [Fuels and Their
  Hazards](/library#fuels-and-their-hazards) has the steps. Once the gas has
  been shut off, only a qualified professional turns it back on
  (Ready.gov).
- **A carbon monoxide alarm sounding:** out, 911, and back in only when
  the fire department says so (CPSC).
- **A new circuit or outlet for a heater:** a licensed electrician, as
  [Where Your Own Electrical Work Stops](/library#where-your-electrical-work-stops)
  explains.
- **A heater fire you cannot put out with its own shut-off, or a chimney
  that roars or throws flames or sparks from the top:** get everyone out
  and call 911 (general practice, built on the CPSC's kerosene heater
  advice and the USFA's rule to leave whenever you are unsure, in
  [Making and Controlling Fire](/library#making-and-controlling-fire)).

## Traps

- **"Just an hour with the oven door open."** An oven used as a heater is
  a carbon monoxide and fire risk for every minute it is on (CDC,
  Ready.gov). Never.
- **"The garage door is open, so the generator is outside."** The CPSC's
  generator label says never inside a home or garage, even if doors and
  windows are open.
- **"It's a vent-free heater, so it's made for indoors."** It puts all its
  exhaust and a gallon of water per 100,000 Btu into the room you are in,
  and the CPSC says never in a closed room or a room where you sleep.
- **"The heater has a tip-over switch, so it can run all night."** A
  tip-over switch does nothing about the duvet that slid against it. Off
  at bedtime (USFA).
- **"A power strip is fine, it has a switch."** The USFA: never an
  extension cord or a power strip for a space heater.
- **"The alarm keeps beeping, it must be faulty."** A sounding alarm means
  out first and questions later. A chirp is usually a low battery or an
  old alarm; replace it the same day (general practice).
- **"I'll keep the kerosene in the old gas can."** The CPSC says even a
  small amount of gasoline mixed into kerosene can substantially increase
  the risk of a fire or an explosion.
- **"A candle is safer than nothing."** In the 2006 storm a man died in a
  house fire apparently started by a candle he was using for light.
  Flashlights.
- **"The rack of wet clothes can dry in front of the heater."** Three feet.

## How the game models it

The game models being cold. It does not model heating, and it does not
model what heating does to people.

- **Inside the home, nothing heats anything.** Sessions start in the home
  aboard the station in orbit, and the air there stays at about 20 C with
  nothing burning or running to hold it (`src/systems/atmosphere.rs`,
  `src/engine/survival_env.rs`). The code has a room heating system
  (`src/systems/hvac.rs`) that nothing registers, so it never runs.
- **A heater you can make, that heats nothing.** The Crafting page has a
  Build Heater recipe for an "Electric space heater" (two steel ingots,
  three wire and a copper ingot at a workbench, Engineering level 2), and
  heaters are parts in some ship and station recipes, but no game system
  gives a heater any effect (`data/recipes.csv`, `data/items.csv`).
- **Shelter keeps the weather off but adds no warmth.** On a planet, your
  body heat is modelled from the weather, your clothes and any shelter you
  have built: a roof keeps the rain off and, under it, walls on the
  windward side keep the wind off, but the air under it is as cold as the
  air outside (`src/systems/body_heat.rs`, `src/engine/survival_env.rs`;
  a test there notes that on a 5 C day a shelter with no fire is still 5 C
  inside).
  While fly mode is on, the weather does not reach your body at all, and
  the Dev page's Land and Travel buttons leave fly mode on even though the
  line under the compass reads WALK. Under the default Body heat setting,
  Forgiving, cold does much less harm than under Realistic, and
  Hypothermia halves your movement (`data/status_effects.csv`).
- **No carbon monoxide anywhere.** The air system can call air toxic when
  carbon monoxide passes 50 parts per million, but nothing in the game
  makes any (`src/systems/atmosphere.rs`). The home's backup generator
  sits inside, in the plant room, with its own 200 litre fuel drum
  (`data/machines/home.ron`); in real life a generator used indoors is
  one of the two things NOAA's record names in the carbon monoxide deaths
  after the 2006 storm.
- **No fires, no alarms that do anything.** Nothing in the game burns, so
  no heater, stove or candle can start a fire. A Smoke Detector and a Fire
  Extinguisher can be crafted, but neither does anything once made.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is the default during
  development, only your character carries between launches.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: fuel
bills, space heaters that start fires, ovens that poison, generators that
must run outdoors, alarms that wake you, and a house that gets colder
every hour the power is out.

## You own this when

- Your home has working smoke alarms and carbon monoxide alarms on every
  level and outside each sleeping area, and you know the year each one
  has to be replaced.
- Nothing that burns fuel runs indoors unless it was built to vent
  outside, and you would never heat with the oven, a grill, a camp stove
  or a generator.
- Everything that can burn is 3 feet from every heater, and every space
  heater is off when you sleep or leave the room.
- Your space heater is tested, has a tip-over shut-off and plugs straight
  into a wall outlet with nothing else on that circuit.
- If you use kerosene, it is 1-K, in its own blue can, and you refuel
  outside with the heater off and cold.
- Your furnace, boiler, stove and chimney are checked every year by a
  professional, and you change the furnace filter yourself.
- You know the visible clues of a carbon monoxide problem, and what to do
  if the alarm sounds.
- You have a plan for a long outage: one warm room, flashlights not
  candles, the generator outdoors, and where the nearest warming centre
  is.
- You know whom to call for help with a heating bill before the shutoff
  notice comes.
- You can say which parts of your heating system are yours to touch and
  which belong to a technician, the utility or the fire department.
