# How Plants Work

Go outside on an overcast day and stand there a minute. Then come back
in and stand in the middle of the room with the lights on. Both places
look like daylight. Your eye adjusts so completely between them that
you will not feel any difference worth mentioning.

NC State Extension publishes what a light meter says about places like
those. Outdoors in full sun is about 10,000 foot-candles. Outdoors
under overcast is about 1,000. An office is about 50, and a library
about 20.

So the dull grey day you were just standing in is about a tenth of a
bright one, and the lit interior of a building is about a twentieth of
the grey day, which is about a two-hundredth of full sun. Your eye
smoothed over a factor of two hundred and reported all three as
"daylight".

A plant cannot smooth over it, because a plant is not looking at the
light, it is eating it.

The window itself is the interesting case. NC State classes areas
within 4 feet of large windows facing south, east or west as high
light, 4 to 8 feet from south and west windows as medium, and more
than 8 feet from a window as low. Which means that in the room you are
standing in, the difference between a good place for a plant and a
slow death for one is about six feet of floor and which wall the
window is in, and you cannot see either.

That is the whole shape of this guide. Almost every piece of growing
advice you have ever been given is the visible end of a mechanism, and
once you can see the mechanism the advice stops being a list of rules
to memorise and starts being something you can work out for yourself.

## What this guide does and does not do

It explains how a plant makes food, moves water, feeds, grows, changes
shape, flowers, and starts from seed, and after each mechanism it says
what that mechanism predicts. The prediction is the point. If you
finish this and can answer "why would that be true?" about a growing
instruction you meet somewhere else, it worked.

It does not tell you when to plant. Your dates depend on where you
live, and [The Growing Calendar Where You Live](/library#the-growing-calendar)
does that properly with real frost data and soil temperatures.

It does not tell you what your ground is made of. That is
[What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is), and for your specific address,
[Your Soil, Specifically](/library#your-soil-specifically). This guide leans
on both and deliberately does not repeat their numbers.

It does not qualify you to eat anything. Knowing how a plant works
tells you nothing about whether a particular plant will kill you.
[Identifying Wild Plants](/library#identifying-wild-plants) opens by
refusing to be a field guide, and it is right to.

And the honest limit: understanding does not substitute for a season.
You will still kill things. The difference is that you will know which
of the four or five possible reasons you are looking at, which is
worth a great deal when you only get one attempt a year.

## Part 1: The budget

### Light in, sugar out

A plant is a chemical factory with one product and one power source.
NC State Extension gives the reaction as carbon dioxide plus water
plus light making sugar and oxygen and water, and summarises it as
plants using "energy from sunlight, carbon dioxide from air, and water
from the soil to produce simple carbohydrates."

Notice what is not on that ingredient list. There is no fertiliser in
it. The carbon, hydrogen and oxygen that the sugar is built from come
out of the air and out of water. Everything you buy in a bag is for a
different job entirely, and we will get to it.

The sugar can be used at once, moved elsewhere in the plant, or stored
as starch. Every potato, every carrot, every grain of wheat and every
drop of maple sap is that sugar, parked.

Leaves are where most of it happens. Any green tissue can photosynthesise,
but NC State notes that "leaves are generally the site of most food
production", because their internal tissue, the mesophyll, is packed
with the chloroplasts that hold the green pigment, chlorophyll, that
actually catches the light.

### The bill that never stops

Here is the half that gets left out of school diagrams.

The plant spends that sugar continuously, in a process called
respiration, which NC State describes as converting the carbohydrates
produced by photosynthesis back into usable energy. The equation runs
the other way: sugar plus oxygen gives carbon dioxide plus water plus
energy.

Two things about respiration matter more than anything else in this
guide.

**It happens in every cell, and it does not stop at night.**
NC State: "Respiration occurs in all life forms and in all cells."
Photosynthesis needs light. Respiration does not. A plant in the dark
is still burning sugar, all night, every night, the way you burn
calories while asleep.

**It does not stop at harvest either.** NC State: "Respiration
continues in flowers, fruits, and vegetables even after they are
harvested", which is exactly why refrigeration extends the shelf life
of produce. The lettuce in your fridge is still alive and still
spending. You are not preserving it, you are slowing its metabolism
down. That is the mechanism under everything in
[Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew).

So growth is not photosynthesis. Growth is photosynthesis minus
respiration. A plant that makes exactly as much sugar as it burns is
not growing slowly. It is not growing at all.

### The break-even point, named

That break-even has a name and a definition, and the definition is
worth having exactly.

A US Forest Service synthesis of forest physiology defines the
photosynthetic light compensation point as "the light level at which
gross photosynthesis equals leaf respiration and net photosynthesis is
zero."

Below that light level the plant runs a deficit. It is not idling, it
is drawing down reserves, and eventually it dies of a slow financial
problem rather than any disease you can name.

The same source shows what evolution does with this. Light under a
closed northern hardwood canopy is "typically less than 1 or 2% of full
sunlight". Shade-tolerant trees survive there by pushing their
compensation point below 5 percent of full sunlight, achieved "by
minimizing leaf respiration per unit of light-absorbing leaf area,
which can be accomplished by having thin leaves with only a single
layer of chlorophyll-containing mesophyll cells."

And they pay for it. Those same thin leaves cannot use bright light
when it arrives: they saturate early, at something like 15 percent of
full sun, and their maximum photosynthetic rate is low. The Forest
Service calls this an "inevitable trade-off", and notes the
consequence that when a gap opens overhead, the leaves of the trees
that were waiting in the shade are not well adapted to the light they
have been waiting for.

The same source sorts the trees of that forest into tolerance classes
by the light each one needs, and the spread is the thing to take away:

| Tolerance class | Typical light availability |
|---|---|
| Very tolerant | 1 to 3 percent of full sunlight |
| Mid-tolerant | 5 to 25 percent |
| Intolerant | 25 to 50 percent |
| Very intolerant | more than 50 percent |

Say what that means. Two trees standing in the same forest can differ
by a factor of fifty in the light they need to make a living.

Say what it does not mean. These are the tree species of the Hubbard
Brook Ecosystem Study, a northern hardwood forest, and the numbers are
about them. **Do not carry them to a vegetable.** Carry the shape:
species differ enormously in where they break even, and a species that
breaks even very low pays for it by being unable to use bright light
when it arrives.

### What the budget predicts

**Why a bright-looking windowsill can still be starving a plant.**
Look again at the light table. NC State's guidance for indoor plants
splits each category in two, and the split is the compensation point
wearing gardening clothes: a low-light plant needs 25 to 74
foot-candles "to maintain growth" and 75 to 300 "to promote growth".
A medium-light plant needs 75 to 149 to maintain and 150 to 1,500 to
promote. There is a band where a plant persists and does not grow.
That band is where most unhappy houseplants live, and no amount of
water or fertiliser will move them out of it, because the missing
input is light.

And it is why the geometry in the opening matters so much. Three feet
from a south window against nine feet from it is the difference
between NC State's high-light class and its low-light class. Moving a
plant across a room is a real intervention, not fussing.

**Why a February seedling on a sunny sill comes out pale and
stretched.** It is short of light twice over: the sill is a fraction
of outdoor light, and February's day is short. Silverdale, the
canonical locale for this project at about 47.65 degrees north, gets
8.4 hours of daylight in midwinter against 16.0 at midsummer;
[The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar) has the month-by-month
table and the sourcing for it. University of Minnesota Extension
recommends 16 to 18 hours a day of light for seedlings under lights,
which tells you what growers think seedlings actually need. Starting
too early is the classic beginner's mistake and
[Starting Seeds](/library#starting-seeds) says so; the budget is why.

**Why a warm dark room is worse than a cold dark one.** Respiration is
metabolism, and refrigeration slows it, which is the reason NC State
gives for refrigerating produce. Anything with no light income and a
warm room has a higher bill against no earnings. That is also the
whole principle of a root cellar: a stored potato is still alive and
still spending, and cold is how you make it spend slowly.
[Keeping What You Grew](/library#keeping-what-you-grew) covers the practice.

**Why "more sun is always better" is wrong as stated.** The Forest
Service defines the light saturation point as "the light level above
which light no longer limits photosynthesis". Past that point, extra
light adds no extra sugar, and a shade-adapted species reaches it
early. Meanwhile the hot bright afternoon that delivered the surplus
light is exactly the condition USGS names as driving transpiration up,
so a plant past saturation is paying more water for no extra sugar.
That is Part 2's problem, and it is why full sun is a requirement for
some species and a stress for others.

## Part 2: Water, and the door it leaves by

### Ninety percent

NC State Extension gives the number plainly: "Approximately 90% of the
water taken up by a plant is lost through transpiration. The other 10%
of the water is used in chemical reactions and in plant tissues."

Say what that means. Nearly all the water you put on a garden does not
end up in the plant. It passes through it and evaporates.

Say what it does not mean. It is not waste. NC State lists what the
flow does on its way through: it carries minerals up from the soil, it
cools the tissue, it moves sugars and hormones around, and it holds
the plant up. A plant without turgor pressure is a limp plant, which
is what wilting is.

And it is not optional, which is the real point.

### One door, two directions

Transpiration is, as NC State puts it, "the process by which a plant
loses water, primarily through its stomata, pulling more water up the
xylem." Stomata are the pores on the leaf. Each one, NC State says, is
bordered by two guard cells, and "the bending of the two guard cells
regulates the passage of water, oxygen, and carbon dioxide."

Read that list of three gases again. The pore that lets water vapour
out is the same pore that lets carbon dioxide in.

There is no version of this where the plant gets the carbon without
paying the water. The leaf is waterproofed everywhere else: NC State
describes the cuticle as a thin waxy layer over the epidermis, which
is exactly a raincoat. The stomata are the deliberate holes in the
raincoat, and they exist because the factory needs an air intake.

So the plant has one lever, and it is a bad lever. Open the stomata
and photosynthesise and lose water. Close them and keep the water and
stop photosynthesising. NC State: "The stomata generally close during
drought or high temperature stress, thus reducing further water loss."

Everything in this part follows from that single trade-off.

### How much water, really

USGS puts numbers on transpiration at field scale. An acre of corn
gives off about 3,000 to 4,000 gallons of water each day, which USGS
also gives as 11,400 to 15,100 litres. A large oak tree can transpire
40,000 gallons in a year. And over a growing season, a single leaf
will transpire many times its own weight in water.

The corn figure is worth converting, because it lands somewhere
familiar. An acre is 43,560 square feet. An inch of water over an acre
is 43,560 divided by 12, which is 3,630 cubic feet, and at 7.48052
gallons to the cubic foot that is 27,154 gallons. So:

- 3,000 gallons a day is 21,000 gallons a week, and 21,000 / 27,154 =
  0.77 inch a week.
- 4,000 gallons a day is 28,000 gallons a week, and 28,000 / 27,154 =
  1.03 inches a week.

Which is to say a full corn canopy in the growing season transpires
roughly three quarters of an inch to an inch of water per week, and
that is where the familiar "an inch of water a week" gardening rule
lives. Treat the agreement as a sanity check, not a derivation: the
USGS figure is a closed corn canopy at peak, and your lettuce bed in
May is not that. If you want the unit arithmetic worked slowly,
[Units and Converting Them](/library#units-and-converting-them) has it.

### What drives the loss

USGS names the drivers, and each one is a lever you either have or do
not have:

- **Temperature.** "Transpiration rates go up as the temperature goes
  up, especially during the growing season."
- **Humidity.** "As the relative humidity of the air surrounding the
  plant rises the transpiration rate falls."
- **Wind and air movement.** "Increased movement of the air around a
  plant will result in a higher transpiration rate", and USGS gives
  the mechanism in the next sentence: "Wind will move the air around,
  with the result that the more saturated air close to the leaf is
  replaced by drier air."
- **Plant type.** Desert plants such as cacti and succulents transpire
  less, which is a whole different budget with a whole different set
  of compromises.
- **Soil type and saturation.** When water is short, plants can begin
  to senesce and transpire less, which is the plant giving up leaf
  area rather than an efficiency gain.

Put the wind and humidity bullets side by side, because together they
are one idea. USGS's reason for the humidity effect is that "It is
easier for water to evaporate into dryer air than into more saturated
air", and its reason for the wind effect is that wind replaces the
saturated air at the leaf with drier air. So wind does not dry a plant
by force. It dries a plant by continuously taking away the small
pocket of humid air the leaf had just made for itself.

### What the water budget predicts

**Why a wilted transplant recovers overnight but not at noon.** This
is the flagship example, and it is entirely mechanical. Wilting means
loss is running ahead of uptake. Midday is when two of the drivers
USGS names are both at their worst: temperature is at its highest and
relative humidity at its lowest. At night both reverse, and the plant
keeps absorbing through roots that never stopped. The deficit closes
and by morning the plant is rigid again.

So the plant standing up at 7am has not been cured. It has been
refilled, and it will be back in deficit by 1pm, every day, until its
roots can supply as fast as its leaves spend.

**Why a wilted plant at noon is not necessarily a thirsty plant.**
Because the stomata close under heat stress anyway, a plant can wilt
at midday with damp soil underneath it. Before you water a
midday-wilted plant, put a finger in the soil. If the soil is wet and
the plant is wilting, adding water addresses nothing and starts the
problem in Part 3. Check again in the evening: a plant that is still
flat at dusk has a real supply problem, and one that has recovered had
a delivery-rate problem.

**Why cuttings root better with the leaves cut in half.** A cutting is
a plant with a full set of transpiring leaves and no roots at all.
Demand is normal, supply is zero, and the cutting has only its own
stored water. So you attack the demand side twice. NC State's
propagation guidance does both: "On large-leafed plants, the remaining
leaves may be cut in half perpendicular to the midvein to reduce
moisture loss", and "Maintain high humidity by covering the cuttings
with a bottomless milk jug or by placing the container into a clear
plastic bag."

Halving the leaf halves the evaporating area. The bag raises humidity,
which USGS tells us directly lowers the transpiration rate. You keep
enough leaf to pay for root construction, and no more. That is the
entire logic of a propagation setup, and once you see it you can
improvise one out of anything.

**Why wind matters more than gardeners expect.** A windbreak is a
water conservation device. A plant on an exposed corner is on a
different water budget from one six feet away in shelter, at the same
air temperature, in the same soil.

**Why humidity appears as a column in the crop data.** We will come
back to this in Part 9, but `humidity_min` and `humidity_max` in
`data/plants.csv` are describing where on this trade-off a species is
comfortable, not how much it likes to be damp.

## Part 3: Roots, and the half that suffocates

### Where absorption actually happens

Dig up a seedling gently and you will see a root system. What you will
not see is the part that does the work.

NC State describes the root tip as a sequence: the zone of cell
division including the root cap, "which protects the interior cells as
the root pushes through the soil", then elongation, then a zone of
maturation where "root hairs begin to emerge... developing as
outgrowths of the epidermal cells." And then the sentence that matters:
"The majority of the water and nutrients absorbed by the plant enter
through the root hairs."

Root hairs are single-cell projections, effectively invisible, and
they are not permanent. NC State: "Most plants produce root hairs that
live only a few days or a few weeks. New root hairs form as roots
continue to grow through the soil."

So the absorbing surface of a plant is a thin moving band just behind
each growing root tip. It is not the thick roots you can see. Those
are plumbing and anchorage.

### What that predicts about where you put things

NC State draws the practical conclusion itself: "Watering and
fertilizing close to the trunk of a plant are of little use to the
plant... It is much more effective to apply water and fertilizer at
the dripline of the plant where root hairs are most prolific."

The dripline is the circle on the ground under the outer edge of the
canopy. Water poured at the trunk of an established tree lands on
plumbing. This is one of the highest-value sentences in this whole
guide, because almost everyone does it wrong and it costs nothing to
fix.

### And what it predicts about transplanting

Penn State Extension states the problem in one line: "Most plants
experience a certain amount of shock from transplanting, when roots
are disturbed and unable to absorb enough water."

Combine that with the budget. Lift a plant out of the ground and you
keep the leaves, which is the entire demand, and you lose an unknown
fraction of the fine absorbing surface, which is the entire supply.
That is why a transplant wilts even in wet soil, and why it wilts
worst in the middle of the day.

The specific claim you will hear, that lifting a plant strips off its
root hairs, is the standard explanation, and it is consistent with
everything above. It is not stated in those words on any page cited
here. What is sourced is the outcome: roots disturbed, water uptake
insufficient.

Every piece of transplanting advice now becomes obvious:

- **Transplant in the evening or under cloud.** Penn State: "While
  cloudy conditions are best for transplanting, it is also acceptable
  to plant in late afternoon or early evening, when temperatures are
  cooler and the sun is less intense." You are handing the plant a
  whole night of low demand to rebuild with.
- **Water before you lift, not just after.** Penn State recommends
  watering within an hour before transplanting, to keep root balls
  moist and intact during handling. A dry root ball crumbles, and
  crumbling is the damage.
- **Support the root ball, do not pull the stem.** Penn State says to
  support the top of the root ball with your fingers, and to "gently
  tease" crowded seedlings apart rather than forcing them.
- **Container-grown plants barely notice.** Nothing was disturbed.

The one deliberate exception is a pot-bound plant. Utah State
Extension: "If roots have formed a thick circular mat around the root
ball, it is often beneficial to disrupt the roots by making several
shallow, vertical cuts in the root ball with a knife or gently
breaking up the roots with your hands." You accept a short-term supply
hit to stop roots from circling permanently.

### The partnership most plants are in

Roots are not the whole absorbing system. The US Forest Service
describes mycorrhizae as "a mutual partnership between certain soil
fungi and fine root tips" that "contribute to tree growth and vigor by
increasing both water and nutrient uptake, especially nitrogen (N) and
phosphorus (P)."

The deal is explicit: "The fungal hyphae increase root surface contact
with the soil, while the fungi are supplied with a reliable source of
carbon." The plant pays in sugar, the fungus pays in reach.

There are two main kinds. Endomycorrhizae, also called vesicular
arbuscular mycorrhizae, colonise the majority of plants including many
trees. Ectomycorrhizae, which are mostly the mushroom-forming fungi,
are "almost exclusively limited to woody species". Some plants host
both.

Three USDA Natural Resources Conservation Service documents were tried
for the rest of it, the hyphal length figures and the list of plant
families that opt out of the partnership, and none of them would load.
So the partnership is sourced here and the arithmetic of it is not,
and the widely repeated claim about which vegetable families are
non-mycorrhizal is left out rather than repeated on trust. The Sources
section says which documents failed.

The useful implication that does follow: a large part of your plant's
nutrient supply is being run by an organism that lives on your plant's
sugar, in your soil, and that organism is part of what
[What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is) means when it says soil is alive.

### Roots need air, and this is what kills most houseplants

[What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is) already establishes that about half of
good soil by volume is pore space, and that water and air share it in
direct competition. Go and read that section; the numbers are there
and there is no point repeating them here.

What it means for the plant is this. Roots respire. They burn sugar
and they need oxygen to do it, all day and all night, exactly like the
leaves. University of Minnesota Extension states the consequence
directly: "Water covering the soil reduces the supply of oxygen to
tree roots. Roots must have oxygen to survive and grow."

Waterlogging does not drown a plant in the sense of filling it with
water. It suffocates it, by filling the air space its roots were
breathing from. And the soil then turns actively hostile: UMN notes
that "the high concentrations of ethanol and hydrogen sulfide that are
produced in waterlogged soil can damage tree roots", and that the main
end products of organic matter decomposition in flooded soil are
carbon dioxide, methane and humic materials. That is anaerobic
chemistry, and the smell of it is the smell of a pot that has been
overwatered for a month.

The timescales are worth knowing because they are shorter than people
expect:

- **Young corn.** UMN reports that for corn younger than the six-leaf
  stage, "Only 3 to 4 days of being submerged in floodwater can be
  fatal to these young plants."
- **Soybean.** UMN reports photosynthesis reduced by a third with 48
  hours of flooding. Not dead, just running a third of an income.
- **Trees, dormant.** "Most tree species can withstand one to four
  months of flooding during the dormant season."
- **Trees, growing.** "One to two weeks of flooding can cause major,
  long-term damage to sensitive trees and shrubs, even death with some
  species", while tolerant species "can survive as long as three to
  five months in flooded situations."

Those last two figures are not about the same set of species, so do
not read them as a clean ratio. The shape is still unmistakable: a
tree stands in water for months in winter and dies in it in weeks in
summer. That is the same mechanism as everything else in this guide. A
dormant tree has a low metabolic rate and therefore a low oxygen
demand. A tree in full leaf in July is running hot.

### What this predicts

**Why overwatering kills.** It is not that the plant gets too much
water. It is that the water evicted the air. This is why a pot with no
drainage hole kills reliably, why a saucer left full of water kills
slowly, and why the failure looks like underwatering: roots that have
died of suffocation cannot absorb, so the plant wilts, so a
well-meaning person waters it again.

**Why a bed that floods in January is a different problem from one
that floods in July.** Same water, two different plants.

**Why garden soil fails in a pot.** NC State is blunt: field soils
"often retain too much water, have too little pore space (for oxygen),
are too heavy, and potentially harbor harmful diseases, insects, and
weeds." [Your First Tomato](/library#your-first-tomato) covers the practical
side of this.

**Why gravel in the bottom of a pot makes drainage worse, not
better.** NC State: "Never put a layer of gravel or rocks in the
bottom beneath potting mix... water will collect in potting mix just
above the gravel." The water does not fall into the gravel; it hangs
in the fine material above it. You have not added drainage, you have
raised the waterlogged zone into the root ball.

**Why compacted ground produces symptoms that look like everything
else.** Same cause, less dramatic. [What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is)
covers bulk density and what compaction does to structure.

## Part 4: Nutrients, and the single most useful diagnostic in gardening

### Seventeen elements, and where they come from

NC State Extension says plants need "17 essential nutrients, each in
varying amounts." Three of them, carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, come
from air and water. The other fourteen come from the soil.

The fourteen are split into macronutrients and micronutrients, and NC
State is careful about why: micronutrients are so called because "they
are needed in much smaller amounts than the macronutrients."

Say what that does not mean. It does not mean micronutrients are
optional or less important. A plant short of boron is as stopped as a
plant short of nitrogen. The word describes the quantity on the
delivery note, nothing else.

### Presence is not availability

This is the sentence to take away from the whole of soil fertility, and
NC State states it in one line: "For a plant to absorb an element, it
must be in a chemical form used by the plant and dissolved in the soil
water."

Two conditions, both of which can fail while the element is sitting
right there in the soil. It must be in a usable chemical form, and it
must be in solution.

This is why pH matters so much, and why it matters as a control on
availability rather than on supply. [What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is)
carries the pH scale, the class names, and NC State's warning that
below about 5.5 manganese, zinc, copper, iron and aluminium can reach
levels that damage plants. The other end of the scale does the
opposite: University of Georgia Extension states that "High pH in the
soil (over 7.0) is the most common cause of iron deficiency", and that
manganese deficiency "typically occurs in soils that have a high pH
(alkaline soils)."

So a soil test that says you have plenty of iron and a plant that is
clearly short of iron are not in conflict. They are describing the two
different conditions in that sentence.

Missouri Extension's diagnostic guide makes the same point about the
physical side, listing water stress, anaerobic soil conditions, and
"issues with compaction, crusting, drainage, or waterlogging" among
the things to ask about before concluding that a nutrient is absent.

### Mobility: where the symptom appears tells you more than what colour it is

Here is the diagnostic. It is simple, it is free, and most gardeners
have never been told it.

Some nutrients can be moved around inside the plant after they have
been installed. Some cannot.

NC State: nitrogen is "mobile in plant, moving to new growth", so
"symptoms appear first on older growth". Phosphorus behaves the same
way. Calcium has "limited mobility in plant", so its symptoms show on
younger growth.

University of Georgia Extension gives the same split with more
detail. Of nitrogen: it "is mobile in the plant and when deficient,
will quickly be moved to new growth, depleting the amount in mature
foliage." Deficiency symptoms of nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium
"typically occur first on the older, lower leaves." Magnesium shows
"interveinal yellowing in the older, lower foliage first." Iron and
sulfur show on the newer leaves near the top.

So the rule:

**Symptoms starting on the OLD leaves, at the bottom: a mobile
nutrient.** The plant is rationing. It is dismantling its old leaves
to build new ones, which is a sensible thing for a plant to do and a
clear signal that supply is short. Nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium,
magnesium.

**Symptoms starting on the NEW leaves, at the top: an immobile
nutrient.** The plant cannot rob the old leaves even though the
element is sitting in them. So the new growth gets whatever is
arriving right now, and if nothing is arriving, the new growth fails
while the old leaves stay perfectly green. Calcium, iron, sulfur, and
boron, whose symptom is at the growing tip itself.

That second case is the informative one, because an immobile-nutrient
symptom very often means a delivery problem rather than an absence.
The element may be in the soil in quantity and simply not reaching the
plant, because of pH, or because of water, or because the root system
is too small or too cold.

The specific symptoms, from University of Georgia Extension except
where the note says otherwise:

| Nutrient | Where it shows | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | Older, lower leaves | Yellowing of mature foliage on the lower limbs |
| Phosphorus | Older leaves | Puckering and purple colour through the leaf |
| Potassium | Older leaves | Brown outer margin on the leaf; fruit underdeveloped with yellow areas |
| Magnesium | Older, lower leaves | Yellowing between the veins |
| Calcium | New growth (location from NC State, not UGA) | Overall yellowing and cupping; blossom end rot on fruit |
| Sulfur | New growth | Pale green, yellowish leaves |
| Iron | New growth | Yellowing between the veins, veins staying green |
| Boron | Growing tip | Distorted growing tip, which can lead to tip death; brittle foliage |

That one parenthesis is not pedantry. University of Georgia describes
the calcium symptoms without saying where on the plant they appear.
NC State is the source for calcium having limited mobility in the
plant and for its symptoms showing on younger growth. The conclusion
is the same either way, and splitting it across two sources changes
nothing about what you should do. It is simply worth being able to say
which page each half of a claim came from, because a reader who checks
one source and cannot find the other half has no way to tell a
misattribution from a mistake.

Note that magnesium and iron produce the same picture, yellow between
green veins, and the only thing separating them is which end of the
plant it started at. That one observation is the difference between
buying the right thing and the wrong thing.

### Blossom end rot, worked

The black leathery patch on the bottom of a tomato is the worked
example, because it is a calcium symptom, and calcium is the nutrient
NC State singles out as having limited mobility in the plant.

University of Georgia Extension says "Either a lack of calcium in the
soil or poor irrigation practices can lead to deficiency", describes
blossom end rot as an indicator of calcium deficiency appearing as "a
black, leathery lesion", and advises monitoring irrigation "to keep
soil evenly moist."

Note that both causes are on the table, and note which one the advice
addresses. The commonly given mechanism, that calcium travels in the
transpiration stream and is therefore delivered unevenly when watering
is uneven, is the conventional account and is not stated on the page
read here. What is stated is the pair of causes and the instruction to
keep soil evenly moist, and those are enough to act on: if your
watering has been erratic, fix that before you buy calcium for a soil
that may already have plenty.

### Why the same fertiliser does nothing in cold soil

Three separate mechanisms stack up, and every one of them is physical
rather than chemical.

Iowa State University Extension: "With cold soil, root growth is
slowed, the capacity to absorb nutrients is reduced, and the diffusion
of nutrients through soil towards the root surface is slowed."

Penn State Extension: "Cold, wet soils cause reduced root growth rate,
nutrient mobility, and nutrient mineralization." And: "Even though a
soil may have high fertility, a seedling's root system may not be able
to obtain the necessary nutrients due to lack of size and density
within the soil."

So:

1. **The root system is small and growing slowly**, so there is less
   absorbing surface in contact with less soil.
2. **The nutrients are not moving to the root**, because diffusion
   through soil water is slower when it is cold.
3. **Less nutrient is being released at all**, because mineralisation,
   the biological breakdown of organic matter into plant-usable forms,
   is done by soil organisms that are also cold.
   [What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is) covers what organic matter releases
   in a year; that whole process runs on biology, and biology slows
   down in the cold.

Phosphorus takes this worst, because it barely moves in soil to begin
with. Penn State: "Phosphorus compounds are bound and do not readily
move within the soil." Iowa State adds that phosphorus is "less mobile
in soil than K, and much less than mineral N forms", and that "P is
very critical for plants during very early growth, and an early P
deficiency seldom can be fully corrected after crop emergence."

### What this predicts

**Why your March fertiliser application seemed to do nothing and your
May one worked.** Nothing was wrong with the fertiliser.

**Why a purple cast on seedlings in a cold spring is ambiguous.**
Purple is the phosphorus symptom in University of Georgia's list. But
everything above says a cold soil can produce a phosphorus symptom
without a phosphorus shortage, so the same purple has two possible
causes and they call for opposite responses. If the cause is
temperature, the mechanism says the symptom should ease as the soil
warms and the root system grows, and that follows from the sources
rather than being stated by them. If the shortage is real, Iowa State's
warning applies: early phosphorus deficiency "seldom can be fully
corrected after crop emergence". Which is an argument for knowing your
soil's phosphorus level before the season rather than diagnosing from
a leaf colour during it.

**Why "feed the soil, not the plant" is not just a slogan.** Two of
those three mechanisms are about the soil's biology and physical
structure rather than about how much nutrient you added.

**Why a soil test is worth more than a fertiliser purchase.**
Everything above says the same thing: you cannot see availability, and
guessing costs more than measuring. [Your Soil,
Specifically](/library#your-soil-specifically) covers where to get it done.

## Part 5: Form, or why a plant is the shape it is

### The tip suppresses the rest

Every shoot has a terminal bud at its end and lateral buds along its
sides; NC State names them exactly that way. What you cannot see is
that they are in a chemical argument, and the tip is winning.

University of Georgia Extension: "Auxin moves downward in the shoot
(toward the Earth's center) from the shoot apex and inhibits the
growth and development of lateral buds."

That is apical dominance. The tip is not simply growing faster than
the buds below it, it is actively holding them back, and that is why a
shrub left entirely alone tends to run upward and get bare at the
bottom.

Which means every cut you make is a message.

### What pinching actually does

Remove the tip and you remove the source of the inhibition. The buds
below it are released, and they grow.

University of Georgia distinguishes the two cuts:

- A **heading cut** "removes the terminal portion of shoots or limbs"
  and is highly stimulating, producing dense regrowth near the cut.
  "Vigorous shoot growth will usually occur within 6 to 8 in. of the
  pruning cut."
- A **thinning cut** "removes an entire shoot or limb to its point of
  origin", which maintains apical dominance on the tips that remain
  and gives less vigorous, more natural growth.

So the two cuts do opposite things, and the difference is not about
how much you remove. Pinching the growing tip out of a basil plant is
a heading cut: you have removed the source of the inhibition, the buds
below are released, and you get branching where you had a single stem.
That is why pinching makes a bushy plant.

Shearing the outside of a shrub is a very large number of heading cuts
at once. Each one produces dense regrowth within a few inches of
itself, so what you build over several years is a solid green shell.
And once that shell is dense, Part 1 finishes the job: the interior is
below its compensation point, and it goes bare. Neither half of that
is stated on the pruning page, but both halves are, and the shrub is
the evidence.

### Pruning does not make plants smaller

This one is counter-intuitive and it is worth stating flatly.
University of Georgia describes pruning as reducing "the size of the
above-ground portion of the plant in relation to the root system",
which stimulates compensatory regrowth. It is invigorating, not
dwarfing.

Cut a vigorous shrub back hard and it will come back harder, because
you have not touched the root system that supplies it, and you have
removed the buds that were suppressing the rest. The plant that was
too big for its spot in year one is more too big in year three. The
answer to a plant that is too big is a different plant, not an annual
fight.

### Branch angle is a dial

University of Georgia: apical dominance is strongest in vertical
shoots, because auxin moves downward. Horizontal branches lose it
entirely, which produces the vigorous vertical shoots called water
sprouts. Limbs held at 45 to 60 degrees from vertical reduce vigour
near the apex and increase lateral growth further down the limb, and
their regrowth after a cut appears further from the cut.

So the angle you train a limb to is a dial on how vigorously it grows
and where. Straight up is maximum dominance and maximum growth at the
tip. Flat is no dominance and a row of water sprouts. The 45 to 60
degree band that University of Georgia names is the setting that
spreads growth along the limb instead of concentrating it, which is
what you want on anything you intend to pick fruit from.

### Stretching toward the light, and what it costs

NC State: "Too little light causes a long internode and a spindly
stem, an occurrence known as stretching or etiolation."

University of Minnesota describes the same plant from outside: stems
become "long and thin and appear to be reaching toward the source of
light", leaves go "pale green to yellow to white" for lack of
chlorophyll, and plants may drop leaves, especially older ones.

And Utah State Extension states the structural consequence: "Poor
light intensity is often the cause of tall, skinny plants that have
difficulty staying upright."

Go back to Part 1 and this is a budget decision. The plant has a
limited sugar income and it is spending it on length instead of
girth. In a forest that is a rational gamble: grow tall fast, reach
the light, then thicken. On a windowsill in February it is a losing
bet, because the light is not going to arrive, and the plant has spent
its capital on a stem that cannot hold itself up.

The fix has two parts, and the second one surprises people.

**More light.** Which means closer, brighter, and longer, and
"closer" is closer than most people think. The published numbers do
not fully agree, and it is worth seeing the spread rather than one
figure presented as settled:

| Source | Distance above the plants | Hours per day |
|---|---|---|
| Utah State, seedling culture | 2 to 3 inches | 12 to 14 or more |
| Minnesota, starting seeds indoors | no more than 4 inches, 2 ideal | 12 to 16 |
| Minnesota, lighting for indoor plants | 4 to 6 inches for seedlings | 16 to 18 for seedlings |

Two of those rows are the same extension service on two different
pages, which is a useful reminder that "extension says" is not a
single voice. What they agree on is the shape: a few inches, not a few
feet, and most of the waking day, not a couple of hours. Nobody in
that group is describing a plant on a windowsill.
[Starting Seeds](/library#starting-seeds) works from the seed-starting page
of that table and gives the practical routine.

**Air movement.** Utah State: "Very light, intermittent, direct
airflow on your seedlings can be beneficial", applied "one to several
times a day for a short duration (5 to 10 minutes or more)", and a
small fan at plant level "can help lower humidity and increase stem
strength."

Read that last phrase again, because it is doing real work. A fan is
not there to dry the surface or discourage mould, though it does both.
It is there because a stem that is pushed around builds itself
stronger, and a seedling raised in perfectly still air has never been
given the signal to do so. Then you put it outdoors.

Which is the other half of hardening off. Penn State describes
gradually acclimating seedlings over 10 to 14 days before
transplanting to prevent "damage from sudden changes in light
intensity and temperature." Utah State says the process should
"minimally begin about two weeks before transplanting", starting with
a few hours, starting in shade, increasing daily.

The important thing about those numbers is that they are measured in
days rather than minutes. The plant is not being psychologically
toughened. It is making physical changes to itself, and physical
changes take physical time, which is why hardening off cannot be
compressed into an afternoon no matter how the week has gone.
[Starting Seeds](/library#starting-seeds) covers the routine.

## Part 6: Making more of itself

### The parts, and why you care

NC State names the structures:

- The **stamen** is the male organ. It has an **anther**, containing
  the pollen-producing chambers, on a stalk called the **filament**.
- The **pistil** is the female structure in the centre, made of a
  **stigma** at the top which is "the site of pollination", a
  **style**, and an **ovary** at the base.

A flower with sepals, petals, stamens and pistils is a **complete**
flower; one missing any of those is **incomplete**. A flower with both
male and female parts is **perfect**; one with only one sex is
**imperfect**.

And the arrangement across the plant has two names worth knowing.
NC State: plants with separate male and female flowers on the same
plant are **monoecious**; species where the sexes are on separate
plants are **dioecious**.

### What that predicts

**Why squash fruit shrivels when it is the size of your thumb.** The
hand-pollination method in [Saving Your Own
Seeds](/library#saving-your-own-seeds) works by moving pollen from a male
flower to a female flower with a paintbrush. That instruction only
makes sense because of a structural fact: squash carries its two sexes
in separate flowers on the same plant, which is the monoecious
arrangement, so every single fruit depends on something physically
carrying pollen between two flowers.

Which means a squash plant covered in flowers and setting nothing is
not hungry. It is unvisited. Adding fertiliser to it does nothing at
all, and that is a genuinely common and expensive mistake.

**Why buying one plant is sometimes buying nothing.** If a species is
dioecious, the sexes are on separate plants, so one plant cannot fruit
on its own no matter how well you grow it. Find out which arrangement
a species uses before you buy a single specimen of it.

**Why "fruit" and "vegetable" do not mean what you think.** In botany
a fruit is the matured ovary of a flower, which is a definition rather
than a claim about any particular plant, and it puts tomatoes,
cucumbers, peppers and squash in the same category as apples. There is
no corresponding botanical definition of "vegetable" at all; it is a
kitchen word. We will meet the consequences of that in the data file
in Part 9.

**Why whether saved seed comes true depends on the flower, not on the
plant.** Whether pollen reaches a stigma from the same flower or from
a different plant decides whether the seed is a near-copy or a cross.
[Saving Your Own Seeds](/library#saving-your-own-seeds) has the sourced
crop-by-crop lists, the traps, and the isolation distances, and they
are not repeated here. The mechanism is the only thing this guide
adds: a plant that fertilises itself before anything
else can reach it is genetically conservative, and a plant that relies
on a bee has handed the choice of the other parent to the bee.

## Part 7: The switch, and why things bolt

### Three life strategies

NC State:

- **Annuals** "complete their entire life cycle, from seed germination
  to seed production, and die in one growing season."
- **Biennials** "complete their life cycles in two growing seasons."
- **Perennials** "live for more than two growth seasons, up to several
  years, decades, or even centuries."

The strategy is the plant's answer to a scheduling problem. An annual
bets everything on one season and must flower. A biennial spends year
one building a store, usually the root or the bulb or the head we
happen to eat, and year two spending that store on flowers and seed. A
perennial amortises the cost of a body over many seasons.

### The switch from growing to flowering

Somewhere in every plant's life there is a switch from vegetative
growth to reproductive growth. After it, the plant is building
flowers. The leaves stop getting bigger and the sugar goes somewhere
else.

For a gardener this switch is the single most consequential event in
the season, because for many crops we eat the plant before the switch.
After the switch a lettuce is bitter and a broccoli has bolted and a
basil has gone woody.

Two environmental signals control it in most temperate crops: day
length and cold.

### Day length, which the plant measures as night length

NC State defines photoperiod as "the amount of time that a plant is
exposed to sunlight", and then gives the two classes in a way that
repays close reading:

- "Short-day plants produce flowers only when the period of darkness
  exceeds a critical minimum."
- "Long-day plants form flowers only when the period of darkness is
  less than the critical night length."

Both definitions are stated in terms of DARKNESS. The plant is
measuring the night, not the day. The names are historical and
slightly misleading.

The onion is the cleanest example a gardener meets. University of
Illinois Extension: "Onions start bulb formation when the day length is
of the proper duration and different varieties of onions require
different day lengths to initiate bulbing." Most varieties are
long-day, for northern latitudes, or short-day, for southern. And the
consequence, in Illinois's words: "onion varieties that are grown in
the South are not adaptable to the North and vice versa."

That is a very strong statement for an extension service to make.
Not "performs less well". Not adaptable.

The reason usually given is that a variety which bulbs at a shorter
day receives its signal earlier in the season, before the plant has
built enough leaf to fill a bulb with, which is the mechanism you
would predict from the definitions above. The Illinois page does not
spell that out, so treat the conclusion as sourced and the reasoning
as inference. What matters either way is that the failure is in the
seed catalogue, not in the garden, and no amount of good growing
recovers from it.

No page could be opened for this guide that gives the specific hour
thresholds for the short-day, intermediate-day and long-day classes,
so no numbers are stated for them here. Buy by the latitude class
printed on the packet, and if the packet does not say, buy elsewhere.

One further implication of the definitions, which was likewise not
found stated on any page opened for this guide: because the plant is
measuring the dark period rather than the light one, a night
interrupted by light is not a long night. A security light or a
streetlamp over a bed is, to a short-day plant, a shorter night than
the calendar says.

### Cold, which is the trigger people blame on heat

The second switch is vernalization: a requirement for a period of cold
before the plant will flower. The obvious function, though this is an
interpretation rather than something quoted from a source, is that it
works as a calendar. A biennial that has been through a winter can
tell spring from autumn, which a thermometer alone cannot.

University of Minnesota Extension, on cool-season vegetables:
"Bolting refers to premature flowering. It occurs at any point after a
plant has shifted into its reproductive stage." And then the crucial
sentence: "Since bolting is partially triggered by the accumulation of
cold, these strategies can reduce exposure to cold temperatures for
young plants."

And on what happens after: "After this shift, high heat and longer
days will speed up flowering."

**Read those two in order, because between them they overturn the most
common garden diagnosis there is.** The June heat wave did not make
your spinach bolt. The cold snap in April made the decision. The heat
wave carried out a decision that had already been taken.

University of Minnesota gives the related failure in broccoli and
cauliflower, called buttoning: "Leafy biomass is what allows a plant
to photosynthesize and create energy for itself, and so it's important
for plants to put on plenty of leaves before transitioning to head
formation. But when spring weather is quite cool, plants can experience
vernalization early, and the result is buttoning up." A tiny head on a
small plant, because the switch was thrown before the factory was
built.

University of Illinois notes the same effect in onions from sets:
large sets "may 'bolt' and form flower stalks", and "extremely cold
weather during early season growth also may condition onions from sets
to flower." Plants that throw a flower stalk "do not produce good
bulbs for dry storage."

And the quality consequence, from Minnesota: "Once a plant flowers,
the heads are no longer marketable, and the taste of the foliage often
becomes bitter. This is especially true for spinach and lettuce, which
both become extremely bitter after flowering."

### What this predicts

**Why sowing a biennial too early in spring backfires.** You are
handing it the cold it needs to decide to flower, at a stage when it
has no reserves to flower well from. Later sowings into warmer soil
often overtake early sowings and do not bolt.

**Why the crop you eat determines when bolting matters.** For lettuce,
spinach, broccoli and cabbage, bolting ends the harvest. For a crop
where you want the seed, the switch is the goal. And for a biennial
whose seed you want to save, you must deliberately get it through a
winter, which is why [Saving Your Own
Seeds](/library#saving-your-own-seeds) puts carrots, beets, onions,
parsnips and cabbage in the second-year category and tells you to store
the roots over winter.

**Why cilantro seems to bolt out of spite.** It is a fast annual; its
whole strategy is to flower. The data file even encodes this: cilantro
is the one row in `data/plants.csv` whose growth stage list contains
an explicit `bolt` stage.

**Why "grow it in a cooler spot" and "grow it in a shadier spot" are
different advice.** Shade changes the light budget. Cool changes the
switch and the transpiration rate. They are not interchangeable.

## Part 8: Starting from zero

### Why a seed does not just grow

A seed that germinated the moment it was wet would germinate in the
first warm rain of autumn and die in the first frost. So many of them
do not.

The US Forest Service's Woody Plant Seed Manual gives the definition,
and it is a good one: dormancy is "a physiological state in which a
seed disposed to germinate does not, even in the presence of favorable
environmental conditions."

That is a seed refusing to grow in conditions that would work. It is a
timer, not a failure. The manual puts the purpose plainly: "survival
of the species requires that they germinate at a time and place
favorable for growth and survival of the seedlings", and in many
species "germination at unfavorable times is prevented by a mechanism
that is commonly described as dormancy."

Note the source before you carry any of this to a carrot. The Woody
Plant Seed Manual is about trees and shrubs, and deep dormancy is
normal there in a way it is not in a packet of radish seed. Penn State
covers the same mechanisms for garden seed and treats them as
something occasional to be broken rather than the default condition.
So read this part as the full range of what a seed can do, not as a
description of your vegetable packets.

### Two places a seed can lock itself

The Forest Service divides dormancy by where the lock is.

**Seedcoat, or external, dormancy.** In the most common mode "the
seedcoats (or other covering structures) are impermeable to the entry
of moisture or gases." The legume family is the classic case, and
people who handle seed call it hardseededness. A second mode is purely
mechanical: the coat physically resists the swelling of the embryo. A
third possibility is germination inhibitors sitting in the coat
itself.

**Embryo, or internal, dormancy.** This "arises from a condition
within the embryo itself", most likely from germination inhibitors in
the embryo or in its food storage tissue, with abscisic acid the most
common one identified. For germination to happen "these inhibitors
must be metabolically inactivated, or their effect must be overcome by
germination-promoting substances."

Penn State Extension gives the same two in garden language: physical
dormancy is a "hard or thick seed coat" broken "by soaking or
scarifying (scratching the surface) the seed", and chemical dormancy
is governed by "plant hormones, notably, abscisic acid, which inhibits
germination, and gibberellin, which ends seed dormancy", with cold
stratification helping to break it.

### Picking the lock: scarification

Scarification is any treatment that breaches the seed coat. The Forest
Service lists the methods in order of increasing severity, and gives
the operating principle, which applies to everything in this guide:
"there are risks to seed viability inherent in all of them. In
selecting a scarification treatment, the most gentle method should be
tested first; then increasingly severe treatments until the desired
effect is obtained."

- **Cold water soak.** Where the coat is not completely impermeable,
  "soaking such seeds in water at room temperature for 24 to 48 hours
  may be sufficient."
- **Hot water soak.** Seeds are put into very hot or boiling water and
  left there as the water cools. "The hot water softens the seedcoats
  or causes them to crack, and imbibition occurs as the water cools."
  The manual says numerous leguminous species can be treated this way,
  naming acacia, albizia and prosopis, all of them woody.
- **Hot wire.** A heated needle or an electric woodburning tool burns
  small holes through the coats.
- **Acid treatment.** Concentrated sulfuric acid, 15 to 60 minutes
  depending on species and seedlot, then washed thoroughly in running
  water.
- **Mechanical.** For small samples, "seedcoats can be scarified by
  hand with knives, files, clippers, sandpaper, etc."

**Do not do the acid method at home.** Concentrated sulfuric acid is a
professional reagent handled with dedicated protective equipment and
a plan for the waste and for a spill on a person. It is on that list
because the manual is written for production nurseries. For a
gardener, sandpaper or a nail file does the same job to the same seed,
and the only thing you risk is the seed. The hot water method deserves
respect too: boiling water into a container is an ordinary scald
hazard, and it is worth doing at the sink rather than over your lap.

### Picking the other lock: stratification

Internal dormancy is usually broken by imitating winter. The Forest
Service: "For temperate species, this is usually a moist, chilling
period, commonly called stratification, because it was formerly done
by alternating layers of seeds and sand or peat in a pit in the
ground."

The procedure: "refrigerate fully imbibed seeds at 1 to 5 degrees C
for 1 to 6 months." That is about 34 to 41 degrees F, which is
refrigerator territory, and the seed must be moist, not dry, because
the chemistry is happening.

What happens during it, again from the manual: enzyme systems are
activated, stored foods are changed to soluble forms, and the balance
between inhibitors and promoters shifts. The seed is not waiting. It
is working.

Two practical notes the manual supplies. The optimum length "varies
greatly among species and among different seedlots of the same
species", so treat published durations as starting points. And there
is a bonus: stratification gives nurseries "an increased uniformity of
emergence", because chilling holds back the seeds that would otherwise
have raced ahead.

### What germination actually requires

Penn State gives four conditions for garden seed: "Temperature,
moisture, air, and light conditions must be correct for seeds to
germinate." Taking them in turn with the Forest Service's detail:

**Water.** Penn State describes the first step, imbibition: "The seed
rapidly takes up water and the seed coat swells and softens." The
Forest Service describes three phases, a rapid initial uptake, a lag,
and then a second rapid uptake just before germination. The first
phase "is a physical process of moisture moving from a substance with
high water potential (soil) to one with a low water potential (dry
seed)", and it happens in dead seeds as well as live ones. A seed
swelling proves nothing about whether it is alive.

**Oxygen.** Penn State: "Seeds respire just like any other living
organism. They need oxygen and produce carbon dioxide." The Forest
Service says insufficient oxygen is not usually a barrier "except when
seeds are buried too deeply in the soil or are submerged in water",
and adds a detail that explains a lot of failed seed trays: "Moisture
will actually form a film around seeds and inhibit the entry of
oxygen."

**Temperature.** Seeds of temperate woody plants germinate from a
minimum of 2 or 3 degrees C to a maximum of about 45 degrees C, and
"the major effect of temperature on germination, however, is on rate
rather than total germination." Natural seedbeds swing between night
and day, and many temperate woody species germinate most rapidly at
about 20 degrees C at night and 30 in the day, that is about 68 and 86
degrees F; work with thermogradient plates suggests "an amplitude of
change between day and night of 10 to 12 degrees C may be more
important than the cardinal points."

For vegetable crops, use the sourced soil-temperature table in
[The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar) rather than any of
those woody-plant numbers.

**Light.** Penn State: "Not all seeds have the same light
requirements. Most seeds germinate best under dark conditions", while
some require light. The Forest Service explains the machinery: a
pigment called phytochrome exists in two forms, one absorbing red
light at 660 nm and one absorbing far-red at 730 nm, and "red light
converts Pr to Pfr in imbibed seeds, which is associated with
overcoming dormancy", while far-red light drives it back "accompanied
by a partial return of dormancy."

So a seed can measure not just how much light there is, but what
colour it is. That is a depth sensor and a canopy sensor in one, and
it explains why seed on the surface behaves differently from seed an
inch down.

### What this predicts

**Why sowing depth matters twice over.** A seed has a finite food
reserve. Penn State: after the shoot emerges "the seedling grows
slowly while the storage tissue of the seed diminishes", until true
leaves appear, and those leaves, "unlike cotyledons, photosynthesize
light into energy." So too deep means the reserve runs out before the
first leaf reaches the light. And too deep also means less oxygen. Two
independent reasons for one rule.
[Starting Seeds](/library#starting-seeds) has the practical depth rule.

**Why a few kinds of seed must be surface-sown.** If a species needs
light to germinate, burying it is a permanent instruction to stay
dormant. The packet will say so.

**Why a waterlogged seed tray rots instead of sprouting.** A film of
water excludes oxygen, and a seed that has imbibed and cannot respire
is a nutrient parcel sitting in warm damp soil.

**Why weeds appear every time you dig.** The Forest Service notes that
dry dormant seeds normally do not germinate in the dark. Buried seed
therefore sits in the one condition that keeps it waiting, and turning
the soil hands some of it the signal it has been waiting years for.
That last step is an implication of the sourced mechanism rather than
something stated on a page opened here, but the pattern in the garden
is not in any doubt.

**Why a packet of tree seed behaves nothing like a packet of radish.**
Because it has a lock on it, and the lock has a key, and the key is
somewhere between one and six months of damp cold.

## Part 9: Reading data/plants.csv

The simulation behind this project reads its crops from a single file,
`data/plants.csv`, which as of this writing holds 189 plant
definitions in 26 columns. It is worth learning to read, partly
because it drives what happens when you plant something in the game,
and mostly because a row is a compact statement of everything in this
guide.

### What the columns are, in groups

**Identity.** `id`, `name`, `description`, `type`, and `harvest_item`,
which names the item in the inventory that a harvest turns into.

**The clock.** `growth_days` is total days to first harvest.
`growth_stages` is the ordered list of named stages the plant passes
through, colon-separated. `seasons` is the seasons it is grown in.

**The inputs.** `water_liters_per_day` is the water demand per plant
per day, which is Part 2 in one number. `nutrient_n`, `nutrient_p` and
`nutrient_k` are relative demands for the three macronutrients most
often limiting, which is Part 4.

**The tolerance windows.** `ph_min` and `ph_max` are the preferred
soil or reservoir pH band, which is Part 4's availability point and
not a statement about how much nutrient is present. `temp_min_c` and
`temp_max_c` are the temperature window. `humidity_min` and
`humidity_max` are the relative humidity window, from 0 to 1, which is
Part 2's transpiration trade-off.

**The output.** `yield_min` and `yield_max`, in units of produce per
plant. These are stored as fractional numbers on purpose: some crops
genuinely yield less than one unit per plant, saffron being the
example the file's own code comments give.

**The game layer.** `seed_value`, `harvest_value`, `skill_required`,
`seed_source`.

**The ecology layer.** `companion_plants` and `adverse_plants`, as
colon-separated lists of other ids.

### Reading one row

Take the tomato row:

    tomato,Tomato,...,fruit,70,1.5,0.15,0.05,0.20,6.0,6.8,18,30,
    0.50,0.80,2,8,seed:sprout:vegetative:flower:fruit:ripe,
    spring:summer,5,15,1,store:trade,basil:carrot:parsley,fennel,...

Now read it as biology rather than as numbers.

`70` days is a trap unless you read the file's header, which says so
explicitly: eight rows count their days FROM TRANSPLANT rather than
from seed, "because that is the basis every extension service
publishes for them", and tomato is one of them. The others are pepper,
eggplant, broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, celery and leek. The header
notes that direct-sowing those adds roughly 30 to 45 days.
[The Growing Calendar](/library#the-growing-calendar) covers this same trap
from the seed packet's side, with the sourced numbers.

`1.5` litres a day is a transpiration budget. Compare it to carrot at
0.3 and radish at 0.15. A tomato is a large plant carrying large fruit
in the hottest part of the year with its stomata open; a radish is a
small plant in cool weather that is finished in 25 days.

`0.15, 0.05, 0.20` is nitrogen, phosphorus, potassium. Note that
potassium is the largest of the three here, which is consistent with a
fruiting crop: University of Georgia's deficiency descriptions are the
only place in this guide's sources where a nutrient shortage is
described as showing up in the fruit itself, and that nutrient is
potassium.

`6.0` to `6.8` pH is the availability window from Part 4. Two rows in
the whole file have a window that closes in the acid range, blueberry
at 4.5 to 5.5 and cranberry at 4.0 to 5.5, and those are the two
famous acid-soil crops. Meanwhile 134 of the 189 rows keep their
entire window inside 5.5 to 7.5. That distribution is the real shape
of crop pH preference, and [What Soil Is](/library#what-soil-is) covers why
it sits there.

`18` to `30` degrees C is the temperature window, and it is worth
reading as a growth window rather than a survival window, because a
tomato does not die at 17 degrees. Twelve of the real-world rows carry
a `temp_min_c` below zero, which is where frost tolerance is actually
encoded: mache at minus 15 is the hardiest in the file, then kale,
collard and chicory at minus 8, then spinach, brussels sprout and bok
choy at minus 7.

`seed:sprout:vegetative:flower:fruit:ripe` is the life cycle from Part
7, made explicit. Compare beet, whose stages run
`seed:sprout:leaf_growth:root_swell:mature`, with no flowering stage
at all, because we harvest a biennial before the switch. Compare
cilantro, whose stage list contains `bolt` outright.

### What the perennials tell you

Look at `growth_days` across the file and one thing jumps out.
Asparagus is 730. Cranberry is 1095. Apple is 1460. Redwood is 7300.

The header explains the convention: unless noted, the number counts
from the propagule the plant is actually started from, clove for
garlic, slip for sweet potato, crown for asparagus, cutting for the
berry shrubs, through to the FIRST harvest. "Perennials therefore
carry the establishment years... not the length of one fruiting
season."

That is an honest and unusual modelling choice, and it teaches the
real lesson about perennials: the cost is paid up front. Two years of
asparagus bed before a single spear is cut is the actual deal, and the
file says so rather than hiding it.

### What the file cannot say, which is the more interesting half

A data schema is a theory about what matters. Reading what is missing
tells you where the simulation is simpler than the biology.

**There is no light column.** Nothing in a row expresses light
requirement, shade tolerance, or a compensation point. Part 1 of this
guide, the budget that governs everything else, has no field at all.
A row cannot distinguish a lettuce that will grow in partial shade
from a tomato that will not.

**There is no photoperiod field and no vernalization field.** So
Part 7, the switch, cannot be expressed. The `seasons` column is doing
the work of both, and it is a label rather than a mechanism: it says
when a crop is grown, not what makes it flower. Bolting cannot emerge
from this data, it can only be scripted.

**There is no pollination field.** Nothing says whether a species is
self-pollinating, insect-pollinated, wind-pollinated, monoecious or
dioecious, which is exactly the information that
[Saving Your Own Seeds](/library#saving-your-own-seeds) turns on.

**There is no rooting depth and no root type.** So the interaction
between a crop and a shallow soil over a restrictive layer, which is
the single most important fact about a great deal of ground in the
Puget lowland, cannot be modelled.
[Your Soil, Specifically](/library#your-soil-specifically) covers depth to a
restrictive layer, and it matters enormously.

**There is no soil-oxygen or drainage field.** `humidity_min` and
`humidity_max` are about air, not about soil pores. Part 3's
suffocation failure has no column.

**There is no mycorrhizal status.**

None of that is a complaint. It is what a schema is: a decision about
which of the infinite properties of a real thing the simulation is
going to carry. Knowing which ones were left out is knowing where your
own judgement has to fill in.

### Two things to notice in the file itself

**The documented category list is shorter than the file.** The comment
block at the top defines `type` as "Category: fruit, vegetable, grain,
legume, herb, fiber", which is six values. The file actually contains
ten: those six plus `tree`, `medicinal`, `decorative` and `alien`. If
you are reading the header to understand the data, that will mislead
you. The same comment block documents every column from `id` through
`adverse_plants` but does not mention `harvest_item`, which is a real
column in the data.

**Some of the rows are not plants.** Three real-world rows,
`oyster_mushroom`, `shiitake` and `button_mushroom`, are fungi. Their
stage lists say so plainly: `spore:mycelium:pinning:fruiting:mature`.
All three carry `type` = `vegetable`. Two more, `shadow_fungi` and
`phase_mushroom`, are the science-fiction equivalents and carry
`type` = `alien`.

Fungi are not plants. They have no chloroplasts, no leaves and no
stomata, they do not photosynthesise at all, and they get their carbon
by digesting material that something else built. So the entire budget
of Part 1 simply does not apply to them, which means the columns in
those rows do not mean what they mean elsewhere. A
`water_liters_per_day` for a mushroom is not a transpiration demand,
because there is nothing transpiring. A `ph_min` is about a growing
substrate rather than a soil solution feeding a root. Nothing is
broken, but you are reading a different organism through a schema
designed for a plant.

It is worth noticing rather than fixing, because the same confusion
runs through ordinary language. A mushroom sits next to the vegetables
in a shop for culinary reasons, not biological ones. If you are
foraging, that is more than a technicality:
[Identifying Wild Plants](/library#identifying-wild-plants) treats fungi as
a separate discipline with a separate risk profile, and it is right
to.

## The habit this leaves you with

A plant runs four budgets at once, and everything above is one of
them:

1. **Sugar**, earned from light, spent every hour of the day and night.
2. **Water**, which can only be earned through the same pore that
   spends it.
3. **Oxygen at the roots**, which shares its space with water and
   loses.
4. **Fourteen elements from the soil**, only useful when they are in a
   usable form, dissolved, and physically reaching a root.

When something looks wrong, the useful move is not to identify the
symptom. It is to work out which budget is short, and then which of
the two or three possible reasons is causing the shortfall. A yellow
leaf is not a diagnosis. A yellow OLD leaf with green new growth,
three weeks after a cold wet spell, on a plant that has not been
repotted, is nearly one.

That habit also tells you when to stop guessing. Three of the four
budgets are invisible: you cannot see foot-candles, you cannot see
soil air, and you cannot see availability. That is what a light meter,
a spade and a soil test are for, and it is why they are worth more
than anything you can buy in a bag.
