# Saving Your Own Seeds

A seed you saved yourself cannot be discontinued, cannot be recalled, and
cannot be priced out of your reach. That is the whole point of this
skill. Everything else in a garden can be bought; the seed is the one
part that can also be made, by you, out of a plant you already grew. Do
it once and you stop being only a customer of the food system and become
a small node inside it.

This guide is the companion to Starting Seeds. That guide is about sowing
a seed someone else produced. This one is about producing the seed. Save
your own, start them with that guide next spring, and the loop closes.

One honest thing first: seed saving has two ways to fail that you will
not discover for a full year. Save from the wrong kind of plant, or let
the wrong plants breed together, and the seed looks perfect, sprouts
perfectly, and grows into something you did not want. Both failures are
avoidable, and both are explained below, but neither shows up until the
following season. Read the two rules before you save anything.

## Rule 1: open-pollinated, never hybrid

Look at the seed packet or the plant label. If it says **hybrid** or
**F1**, do not save seed from it.

A hybrid is a deliberate cross between two different parent lines. The
plant itself is often excellent. Its seed is not. The University of Maine
puts it plainly: F1 hybrids are not true-breeding, and their seed "will
produce a population of plants displaying traits covering the full
genetic range." Minnesota Extension gives the everyday version, using
'Big Boy' and 'Early Girl' tomatoes as the example. The seeds are alive
and will grow, but "plants grown from that seed are not identical to the
hybrid parents."

What you want instead is **open-pollinated**, which includes everything
sold as **heirloom**. Minnesota's wording: open-pollinated varieties
"will come true from seed," producing nearly identical fruit. If the
packet does not say either way, assume hybrid. Open-pollinated is a
selling point, and seed companies print it.

This rule costs you nothing to obey. Next spring, buy open-pollinated
packets of the crops you intend to save, and buy hybrids only for crops
you are content to re-buy forever.

## Rule 2: start with plants that pollinate themselves

Some plants fertilize their own flowers before anything else can reach
them. Their seed comes out like the parent even when another variety is
growing a few feet away. Others are pollinated by insects or by wind,
which means a bee or a gust chose the other parent, and you find out next
August.

**Start here (self-pollinating, easy):** beans, peas, tomatoes, lettuce.
Peppers and eggplant are also on the University of Maine's
self-pollinated list, and Minnesota names tomatoes, peppers, beans and
peas as good choices, with "seeds that require little or no special
treatment before storage."

**Insect-pollinated (harder):** squash, pumpkins, melons, cucumbers,
broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower, carrots, radish.

**Wind-pollinated (hardest):** corn, beets, Swiss chard.

Four crops is not a limitation, it is a curriculum. Beans, peas, lettuce
and tomatoes give you a protein crop, a cool-season crop, a salad crop,
and the fruit almost everybody starts with. Learn on those.

## The traps, named

**Hybrids.** Covered above. The most common disappointment there is.

**Biennials.** Some crops we eat in their first year do not flower and
make seed until their second. Carrots, beets, onions, parsnips, cabbage
and their relatives are biennial, so saving their seed takes two growing
seasons. In a cold climate it also means digging the roots in autumn and,
as Maine Extension describes, storing them at or near freezing until
spring so you can replant them. Minnesota simply notes that these "are
harder to save since the plants need two growing seasons to set seed."
None of this is impossible. It is a different and longer project, and it
does not belong in your first year.

**Squash and its relatives.** This is the trap that produces the famous
inedible mystery squash. South Dakota State University Extension groups
them by which will cross with which:

- Group one, all of which cross with each other: zucchini, yellow
  crookneck, acorn, spaghetti, patty pan, delicata, pumpkins and gourds.
- Group two, which cross with each other: butternut, buttercup, banana,
  Hubbard and turban squashes.
- Group three, which cross with each other but not with squashes,
  pumpkins or cucumbers: muskmelon, cantaloupe, Charentais, honeydew,
  casaba, Armenian cucumber, snake melon.
- Cucumbers, watermelon and loofah gourds cross only within their own
  kind, so they matter only if you grow two varieties of the same one.

To keep a variety pure, SDSU says these need to be separated by "one-half
to one mile," which no home garden has. Buildings, a thick stand of trees
or a hill in between will shorten that, but the reliable home answers are
simpler: grow only one variety from each group in a season, or
hand-pollinate. To hand-pollinate, in early morning move pollen from a
male flower to a female flower with a small paintbrush, do it within four
hours of the female flower opening, and bag or tape the flower closed
again afterward.

**Corn.** Wind carries corn pollen a long way. Minnesota's example is the
one to remember: "popcorn can pollinate a stand of sweet corn from a
nearby garden on a windy day," which ruins both. Corn is not a beginner's
seed-saving crop in a neighborhood.

## Choosing which plants to save from

You are not only collecting seed, you are choosing the parents of next
year's garden. So choose on purpose:

- Save from plants that stayed healthy all season. Never save from a
  diseased plant; some diseases travel on the seed.
- Save from plants that looked and tasted the way the variety is supposed
  to. If one bean plant matured three weeks before all the others, that
  is either a plant worth keeping or a stray from a different variety,
  and you have to decide which.
- Save from several plants, not one. For the easy self-pollinating four,
  a handful is plenty. For anything cross-pollinated, SDSU suggests
  saving from 5 to 10 plants to keep a variety healthy, and 25 plants for
  a rare variety you are trying to preserve.
- Let the seed crop stay on the plant far longer than the eating crop.
  Those plants are finished as food. They are seed factories now.

## Dry seeds: beans, peas, lettuce

These are the simplest. The seed dries on the plant and you collect it.

1. Stop harvesting from a few chosen plants and let their pods stay on.
   Minnesota's finish line for beans and peas: leave the pods until "they
   are dry and start to turn brown, with the seeds rattling inside,"
   which "may take as long as a month after you would normally harvest."
2. If a frost or a week of rain is coming, pull the whole plant and hang
   it somewhere dry and airy to finish.
3. Bring the pods indoors and dry them further before you open them.
   Minnesota says at least two weeks before shelling.
4. Shell the seed out. Maine describes the traditional way to do a large
   batch: put the dry pods in a sack and thresh them by crushing or
   treading, then winnow, which means pouring the mix from one bowl to
   another in front of a fan so the light chaff blows sideways and the
   heavy seed falls straight down.

Lettuce works the same way but looks different. Let the plant bolt, which
means send up a flower stalk, let the flowers turn into small
dandelion-style puffs, and collect the puffs as they dry. Lettuce ripens
over weeks rather than all at once, so plan on several passes.

## Wet seeds: tomatoes

Tomato seeds arrive wrapped in a jelly that stops them sprouting inside
the fruit, so they get a short deliberate ferment that removes it. It
smells bad for a few days. That is normal.

1. Pick fully ripe, healthy fruit from a plant that stayed healthy.
2. Scoop the seeds and jelly into a jar and add water.
3. Leave it at room temperature and, in Minnesota's instruction, "stir or
   swirl the mixture twice a day. The mixture will ferment and the seeds
   should sink to the bottom within five days."
4. Pour off the floating pulp and the floating seeds. Maine's rule is
   that the "strong, viable seeds will sink to the bottom," so the
   floaters are the ones to discard. Rinse with clean water repeatedly
   until only clean seed is left.
5. Spread the seed in a single layer to dry. Maine notes that tomato
   seeds stick badly to paper towels and that coffee filters work better,
   and that you should shake them loose several times a day.

Peppers need none of this. Minnesota: leave some fruit on the plant
"until they become fully ripe and start to wrinkle," then remove the
seeds and spread them out to dry. Wear gloves for hot peppers and keep
your hands away from your eyes.

## Drying: the step people rush

Wet seed rots or molds in the jar. This is the most common way a
carefully saved harvest is lost after all the real work is finished.

Colorado State University Extension is clearest on the target: "the drier
the seeds, the longer they will store," and seed should be dried "to less
than 8 percent moisture." Their method for finishing a batch is to dry
seed at 100 degrees F for six hours. Do not use a microwave, and if you
use an oven, keep it at its lowest setting with the door propped open so
it never exceeds 100 F.

Maine adds the ceiling that matters if you are drying in a warm room or a
sunny window: the temperature must not exceed 96 F, and keep seed out of
direct sun.

For most home savers, a week or two spread on a plate or a screen in a
dry, airy, shaded indoor spot does the job. A bean seed is dry enough
when it shatters rather than dents under a hammer tap, and when you
cannot mark it with a fingernail.

## Storing: cool, dry, dark, labeled

Two things decide how long seed lives: how dry it is, and how cold it is.

- **Container.** Colorado State says use "sealed cans or jars, rather
  than plastic bags," because those are genuinely moisture-proof.
  Minnesota says tightly sealed glass containers. A canning jar with a
  fresh lid is ideal and is probably already in the house.
- **Temperature.** Minnesota says store between 32 and 41 F, which in
  practice means a refrigerator. Colorado says keep the temperature below
  40 F, and a freezer also works for thoroughly dried seed.
- **Humidity.** Colorado gives the numbers. At 15 percent relative
  humidity, seed settles at about 6 percent moisture and "will store
  safely in this condition for several years." At 90 percent humidity it
  reaches 19 percent moisture and "germination will be poor after one
  year." A rule of thumb used widely in the seed trade: the storage
  temperature in degrees Fahrenheit plus the relative humidity in percent
  should add up to less than 100.
- **A drying agent, if your air is damp.** Minnesota suggests one to two
  tablespoons of silica gel, or powdered milk from a freshly opened
  package, wrapped in paper at the bottom of the jar. Powdered milk keeps
  absorbing moisture for about six months, then needs replacing.
- **Label everything** with the crop, the variety, and the date you
  collected it. You will not remember. Nobody remembers.

## How long saved seed lasts

Two extension services publish longevity tables, and they do not fully
agree. That disagreement is itself worth knowing about.

University of Maine, expected storage life in years:

| Years | Crops |
|---|---|
| 1 to 2 | onion, parsnip, sweet corn |
| 3 | bean, carrot, leek, pea |
| 4 | beet, pepper, pumpkin, tomato |
| 5 | Brussels sprouts, cabbage, cauliflower, cucumber, lettuce, melon, radish, squash, watermelon |

Colorado State's table gives lettuce 1 year and onion 1 year, with beans
3, peas 3, carrot 3, tomato 4, squash 4, and sweet corn 2.

So Maine says lettuce keeps five years and Colorado says one. Both are
real extension guidance. The gap is mostly about how well the seed was
dried and how cold it was kept, which is exactly what you control. The
honest way to use these tables: plan on the shorter number, and settle
the question with a germination test rather than an argument. Minnesota
takes the most conservative line of all and simply advises using saved
seed within one year, because "the older the seed the lower the
germination and vigor."

## The germination test: ten minutes, all doubt removed

Do this in late winter, before you commit a season to seed that may be
dead. Maine's method:

1. Count out 25 seeds of a large-seeded crop, or up to 100 of a
   small-seeded one.
2. Space them on a dampened paper towel, roll it up, and seal the roll in
   a plastic bag.
3. Leave it somewhere warm for 7 days.
4. Unroll it and count how many sprouted.

Twenty out of 25 is 80 percent, which is fine; sow normally. Ten out of
25 is 40 percent, which is still usable if you sow twice as thickly. Two
out of 25 means buy fresh seed, and be glad you found out in February
instead of June.

## A note on seed-borne disease

Some plant diseases ride on the outside of a seed into next year's
garden. If you have had disease problems, Minnesota publishes two
treatments:

- A bleach soak: one part household bleach at 5.25 percent sodium
  hypochlorite to four parts water, with a drop of soap. Soak the seed
  for one minute, then rinse for 5 minutes.
- A hot water treatment, for tomato seed only: warm at 100 F for 10
  minutes, then hold at 122 F for 25 minutes, then rinse for 5 minutes.

Both are precise for a reason. The hot water treatment needs a real
thermometer, because a few degrees hotter kills the seed and a few
degrees cooler accomplishes nothing. The bleach solution is for seed,
never for skin and never for drinking. Mix it with ventilation, keep it
away from children and pets, and never combine bleach with other
cleaners.

## What can go wrong

- **Everything came out wrong the following year.** Almost always the
  hybrid rule. Check the packet you saved from. If it says F1, that is
  the answer, and nothing you did in the harvesting or drying was at
  fault.
- **The squash is a stranger.** Two varieties from the same crossing
  group flowered near each other. The fruit is usually still safe to eat
  and is occasionally good, but the variety is gone. Next year grow one
  variety per group, or hand-pollinate.
- **Mold in the jar.** The seed was not dry enough, or the jar went into
  a warm humid cupboard. Discard moldy seed. Next time dry it longer,
  add a drying agent, and store it cold.
- **Nothing germinated in the test.** Either the seed was harvested
  before it was mature, since green, soft, pale seed is not finished
  seed, or it was dried too hot. Both are common first-attempt mistakes.
- **The lettuce fluff blew away.** It does. Cut the whole seed stalk into
  a paper bag and let it finish drying in there.
- **You saved from the first plant to bolt.** For lettuce and the cabbage
  family, early bolting is the single trait you least want to breed for.
  Save from the last plants to bolt, not the first.

## How you know it worked

- Your jar holds clean, hard, fully dry seed, labeled with crop, variety
  and date, sitting somewhere cold and dark.
- A germination test in late winter comes back above roughly 70 percent.
- Next season, the plants that come up look like the plants you saved
  them from.

At that point something quiet and permanent has happened. You no longer
depend on anyone's catalog to grow that crop again. The garden that fed
you also reproduced itself, and it will keep doing that for as long as
you keep choosing good parents.
