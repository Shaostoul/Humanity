# What Not to Compost, Burn or Pour Away

Most of what a household throws out is harmless. Food scraps rot into
compost, and most of the rest can go out with the ordinary rubbish
collection. A small part of every household's rubbish is different. It
can start a fire in a bin lorry, poison the soil a garden grows in, foul
drinking water or a septic tank, or hurt the person who collects it. This guide is about that small part: what must stay out of the
compost, what must never be burned, what must never go down a drain, and
where each of those things goes instead.

The rules have a single idea behind them. Compost, smoke, drains and
landfills all send what you put into them somewhere else: into the soil
you grow food in, the air you and your neighbours breathe, the water
someone drinks. Some things break down on the way. Some do not, and some
become worse.

**Where you live changes the details.** What your town collects, where
its drop-off sites are, whether it allows burning at all and which
batteries it takes are set by your state, county or city. This guide
gives the federal baseline from United States agencies and tells you who
to ask for the local answer. The Environmental Protection Agency's
general advice is to contact your local environmental, health or solid
waste agency to learn about household hazardous waste collections near
you.

The advice here comes from the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the
Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Consumer Product Safety
Commission (CPSC), the US Fire Administration and the Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention (CDC). All are United States government
publications. Where something is general practice or our own reasoning
rather than a published rule, the text says so.

## First: the rubbish that hurts people

- **Lithium batteries in the bin.** The EPA says lithium-ion batteries
  must never go in household garbage or recycling bins: crushed in a
  truck or a sorting plant, they can start a fire. "To prevent fires from
  lithium-ion batteries, tape battery terminals and/or place batteries in
  separate plastic bags". It adds that batteries can hold enough energy
  to injure or start fires even when used and when they seem flat.
- **A swallowed button battery.** The CPSC says a button cell can burn
  through a child's throat in as little as two hours, and "If a button
  cell or coin battery is swallowed, immediately seek medical
  attention." Call the National Battery Ingestion Hotline (800-498-8666)
  or the Poison Help Line (800-222-1222), the numbers the CPSC gives.
  Keep new and used button batteries out of children's reach.
- **Hot ashes.** The US Fire Administration: "Put ashes in a metal
  container with a lid. Place the container outside at least 10 feet
  from your home." Never into a plastic bin, a paper sack or a compost
  heap (our reading of the same rule).
- **Mixing chemicals.** The EPA says never to mix leftover household
  hazardous products: "Incompatible products might react, ignite, or
  explode". The CDC's warning about bleach: "Never mix bleach with
  ammonia or other cleaners. This will produce a poisonous gas."
- **Burning rubbish.** The EPA's wood-burning programme: "Never burn
  garbage, plastic, or pressure treated wood, which can produce harmful
  chemicals when burned."
- **Loose needles.** The FDA: never throw loose needles and other sharps
  into the trash, never flush them, and never put them in a recycling
  bin.
- **Old medicines.** Unused medicine can be found and taken by a child or
  a pet. The FDA's first choice is a drug take-back location or a
  prepaid mail-back envelope.
- **A broken mercury thermometer or fluorescent bulb.** The EPA says
  never to vacuum or sweep up mercury from a broken thermometer, and not
  to vacuum a broken fluorescent bulb unless glass remains after every
  other step. Clear people and pets out of the room first, and open it
  to the outdoors with a window or door. The steps are below.
- **Old building materials.** The EPA: if you suspect a material
  contains asbestos, "don't touch it", and if in doubt treat it as if it
  does and leave it alone. It also warns that work which disturbs painted
  surfaces in a home built before 1978 can create large amounts of lead
  dust.
- **Oily rags.** Rags with linseed oil, oil-based stain or varnish on
  them can heat up and catch fire by themselves. How to store and get
  rid of them is in [Keeping Things Working](/library#keeping-things-working).

## Why some things must not go into soil, air or water

Each way of getting rid of something sends it somewhere.

- **Compost goes into the soil you grow food in.** A home heap breaks
  down food and garden waste, but the EPA says backyard piles generally
  do not get hot enough to fully break down some materials, and it lists
  others that do not belong in a heap at all. Whatever survives the heap
  ends up around your vegetables.
- **Smoke goes into the air, and ash into the ground.** The EPA's
  archived pages on backyard burning say burning trash in the open
  produces pollutants including dioxins, particle pollution, carbon
  monoxide and ash, and that the ash can contain toxic metals such as
  mercury, lead, chromium and arsenic. Some people, it says, scatter that
  ash in their gardens, where vegetables can take up the metals.
- **Drains go to water.** The EPA says household hazardous products
  poured down drains or toilets can contaminate septic tanks or
  wastewater treatment systems, and that many treatment systems are not
  equipped to remove all pesticides. On used motor oil it is blunt: "used
  oil from one oil change can contaminate one million gallons of fresh
  water".
- **The bin goes through other people's hands.** The EPA says some
  household hazardous wastes can injure sanitation workers, and a
  needle or a battery in an ordinary bag reaches someone who did not
  know it was there.

## What stays out of the compost

The EPA's guide to composting at home lists what to keep out of a
backyard pile. [Your First Compost](/library#your-first-compost) covers most
of these too; this is the EPA's own list, with the reasons it gives.

| Keep out | The EPA's reason | Where it can go instead |
|---|---|---|
| Meat, fish and bones | Can attract animals; backyard piles generally do not get hot enough to fully break them down | A commercial composting programme that takes them, or the trash |
| Cheese and dairy products | The same two reasons | The same |
| Fats, oils and grease | The same two reasons | The same |
| Cooked food (small amounts are fine) | The same two reasons | The same |
| Compostable foodservice ware and compostable bags | Backyard piles generally do not get hot enough to break them down | A commercial composting facility, if yours accepts them |
| Herbicide-treated plants and grass | Listed without a reason | Ask your local waste service |
| Aggressive weeds, and weeds with seeds | Listed | Ask your local waste service |
| Diseased and pest-infested plants | Listed | Ask your local waste service |
| Treated or painted wood | Listed | Usually the trash, after checking local rules (EPA, for CCA-treated wood); never burned (below) |
| Pet waste and cat litter | Listed | The trash, bagged |
| Dryer lint, glossy paper and produce stickers | Listed | The trash |

The EPA notes that the materials it marks as too hard for a backyard
pile "can be composted at commercial composting facilities", and says to
check first that your facility accepts them. The rest of the third
column is our suggestion, not the EPA's (general practice). For the
items it lists without a reason, the rule is still the EPA's; follow it
even where the reason is not stated. [Your First Compost](/library#your-first-compost) explains why
diseased plants and seeding weeds survive a cool heap.

The EPA's advice for keeping animals out of a compost bin is to give it
a lid, and to leave no holes or gaps larger than a quarter of an inch.
The same idea works for a rubbish bin (our application): a lidded,
sound container for kitchen rubbish, emptied often, keeps rats and flies
from treating it as food (general practice).

## What never gets burned

- **Garbage, plastic and pressure-treated wood.** The EPA's Burn Wise
  advice, quoted above, covers all three. About wood treated with
  chromated copper arsenate (CCA), which the EPA says was used in
  residential decks and play sets before 2004, it says: "Do not burn CCA or other
  preservative-treated wood in a residential setting to avoid possible
  inhalation of toxic chemicals in the smoke and ash." It also says such
  wood should not be reused as mulch, can usually go out with ordinary
  trash, and that you should check with your state or local waste
  programme first. When sawing it, wear goggles and a dust mask and wash
  your hands afterwards.
- **Paper in a fireplace or wood stove.** The US Fire Administration's
  fireplace and wood stove card says plainly: "Do not burn paper in your
  fireplace or wood stove."
- **Household rubbish in a barrel or a pit.** The EPA's archived pages
  explain that household burn barrels get little oxygen and so burn at
  fairly low temperatures, producing a great deal of smoke and the
  pollutants listed above, released at ground level where they are easily
  breathed in. They add that many state, local and tribal governments
  prohibit or restrict burning household waste. Check your local rules,
  and do not burn rubbish even where it is allowed (our recommendation,
  from the EPA's findings).
- **Ash from burned rubbish never goes on the garden** (our reading of
  the EPA's warning about metals in that ash).

What a wood fire should burn, and how dry it should be, is in
[Firewood](/library#firewood).

## What never goes down a drain, toilet or storm drain

- **Household hazardous products.** The EPA counts pouring them down the
  drain, on the ground or into storm sewers as improper disposal.
- **Pesticides.** The EPA: "Never pour pesticides down the sink, toilet,
  sewer, or street drain." Its advice for leftovers comes first: mix up
  only enough for the job, use up small amounts by applying them as the
  label directs, or offer them to a neighbour with the same pest
  problem. Then follow the disposal instructions on the label, and ask
  your local waste agency about collection. "Never reuse empty pesticide
  containers."
- **Mercury.** The EPA says never to pour mercury down a drain: it can
  lodge in the plumbing and cause trouble for whoever repairs it later,
  and pollute a septic tank or a sewage treatment plant.
- **Used motor oil.** Take it to a collection point (below).
- **Needles and other sharps.** Never flushed (FDA).
- **Most medicines.** Only the medicines on the FDA's flush list should
  go down a toilet, and only when a take-back option is not available.
  Everything else goes to take-back or into the trash the FDA's way
  (below).

## The shelf of hazardous leftovers

The EPA calls it household hazardous waste: leftover household products
that can catch fire, react or explode under certain circumstances, or
that are corrosive or toxic. Its examples include paints, cleaners, oils,
batteries and pesticides. Its rules for keeping them until they go:

- Follow the label's instructions for use, storage and disposal.
- "Never store hazardous products in food containers; keep them in their
  original containers and never remove labels." The EPA's household
  brochure on labels explains why: children think something in a
  familiar bottle is safe to drink or play with, and a container without
  a tight lid spills easily.
- Never mix leftovers together.
- A container that is corroding needs special handling: call your local
  hazardous materials official or fire department for instructions.
- Even empty containers can be hazardous, because of the chemical left
  inside.

To those, add one of our own (general practice): gas cylinders from
camping stoves, blowtorches and barbecues never go in the household bin
or near a fire, even when they seem empty. Ask your waste service or the
seller where they go.

Then take them to a household hazardous waste collection. The EPA says
many communities run permanent sites or collection days, that some local
garages may accept used motor oil, and that your local environmental,
health or solid waste agency can tell you what exists near you.

## Batteries, one kind at a time

The EPA's page on household batteries sorts them by what they are made
of, which is printed on the battery, not shown by its shape or colour.
Its overall rule: "Certain batteries should NOT go in household garbage
or recycling bins." Any battery can go to a recycler or a household
hazardous waste collection.

| Battery | Common in | The EPA's advice |
|---|---|---|
| Alkaline and zinc-carbon (AA, AAA, C, D, 9 volt) | Clocks, torches, remotes, toys | In most communities they can safely go in household trash; the EPA recommends a battery recycler, or asking your local solid waste authority |
| Button or coin cells | Watches, hearing aids, car keys, calculators | Not in the trash or recycling; tape them or bag each one; keep them away from young children |
| Lithium single-use | Cameras, smoke alarms, remotes | Look for the word "lithium" on it; not in the trash or recycling |
| Rechargeable: nickel-cadmium, nickel-metal hydride, lithium-ion and small sealed lead-acid | Power tools, phones, laptops, toys, backup power supplies | Not in the trash or recycling; to a recycler, a retailer take-back or a hazardous waste collection; tape the terminals or bag each one |
| Car and other lead-acid batteries | Cars, boats, motorcycles, sump pump backups | Up to 18 pounds of lead and about a gallon of corrosive acid; return to the retailer or a hazardous waste collection, never the trash or recycling bin |
| Batteries built into a device | Phones, laptops, many toys | Take the whole device to an electronics recycler or a take-back programme |

**Worked example: a drawer of dead batteries.** Sort them by what is
printed on them. Put a strip of electrical tape over the ends of each
button cell, lithium cell and rechargeable, or drop each into its own
small plastic bag, so no two terminals can touch. Keep them in a closed
box, out of children's reach, until a trip to the recycler. The EPA says
a damaged lithium-ion battery calls for the manufacturer's own handling
instructions; a battery that is swollen, hot, leaking or smoking is not
one to tape and keep in a drawer (general practice). [Batteries and
Storage](/library#batteries-and-storage) covers the large batteries of a home
power system.

## Medicines and needles

### Medicines

The FDA's order of preference:

1. **A take-back option**, at once: a drug take-back location, often at
   a pharmacy or police station, or a prepaid drug mail-back envelope.
2. **If the medicine is on the FDA's flush list** and take-back is not
   readily available, flush it.
3. **Otherwise, the trash, done the FDA's way:** take the medicine out of
   its original container; mix it, without crushing tablets or capsules,
   with something unappealing such as dirt, cat litter or used coffee
   grounds; seal the mixture in a container such as a plastic bag; put it
   in the household trash; and scratch out all personal information on
   the empty bottle's label before you throw it away or recycle it.

Some medicines, such as sprays, come with their own disposal
instructions; read them, or ask a pharmacist.

### Needles and other sharps

The FDA's two steps: put each used needle or lancet straight into a
sharps disposal container, and dispose of the full container the way
your community says. Its details:

- Use an FDA-cleared sharps container if you can. If not, the FDA notes
  that some organisations and community guidelines recommend a
  heavy-duty plastic household container instead.
- Stop filling it at about three-quarters full: overfilling raises the
  risk of a needle stick.
- Keep containers out of reach of children and pets, and never reuse
  one.
- Never throw loose sharps in the trash, flush them, or put them in a
  recycling bin.
- Never try to remove, bend, break or recap a needle someone else has
  used.
- Ask your local trash or health department which disposal routes exist
  where you live: drop boxes, hazardous waste sites, mail-back or
  special pickup.

## Mercury: fluorescent bulbs and old thermometers

Compact fluorescent bulbs and fluorescent tubes contain mercury, and so
do many old fever thermometers. The EPA's steps for a broken fluorescent
bulb:

1. **Before cleanup:** have people and pets leave the room; air it out
   for 5 to 10 minutes by opening a window or door to the outdoors; shut
   off the central heating or air conditioning.
2. **During cleanup:** do not vacuum, unless glass remains after every
   other step, because vacuuming can spread mercury powder or vapour.
   Scoop up glass and powder with stiff paper or cardboard, pick up the
   rest with sticky tape, and on a hard floor wipe the area clean with
   damp paper towels or disposable wet wipes. Put it all, tape and
   towels included, in a glass jar with a metal lid or a sealable
   plastic bag. A carpet or rug has its own steps on the EPA's page.
3. **After cleanup:** put the debris and cleanup materials outdoors in a
   trash container or protected area at once, and check with your local
   government whether fluorescent bulbs, broken or not, must go to a
   recycling centre. If practical, keep airing the room, with the heating
   or air conditioning off, for several hours.

For a broken mercury thermometer the EPA adds four things never to do:
never use a vacuum cleaner (it puts mercury into the air), never use a
broom (it breaks it into smaller droplets and spreads them), never pour
it down a drain, and never walk around in shoes that might have mercury
on them. Then, in order:

1. **Before cleanup:** have everyone else leave, without walking through
   the mercury on the way out, and keep pets out. "Open all windows and
   doors to the outside; shut all doors to other parts of the house." Do
   not let children help.
2. **During cleanup:** wear gloves, gather the beads with cardboard or a
   squeegee, and bag, label and dispose of everything as your local
   health department, waste authority or fire department directs.
   Mercury cleans up easily from wood, linoleum, tile and other smooth
   surfaces. If it lands on a carpet, curtains, upholstery or anything
   else absorbent, the EPA says those items should be thrown away, and
   for a carpet: "Only cut and remove the affected portion of the
   contaminated carpet for disposal."
3. **After cleanup:** "keep the area well ventilated to the outside
   (i.e., windows open and fans in exterior windows running) for at
   least 24 hours after your successful cleanup", and keep pets and
   children out of the area. If a thermometer's paper
scale says "mercury free", it is not mercury; if it does not, the EPA
says to assume it is. A spill bigger than a thermometer's is a different
job, and the EPA has separate guidance for it. With questions about
exposure, the EPA says to call your doctor or the poison control centre
at 1-800-222-1222.

## Used motor oil and oil filters

If you change your own oil, the EPA's advice is simple: do not spill any
when you collect it, put it in a leak-proof can or container, and ask
local car maintenance shops, waste collectors and government waste
officials where to drop it off. Drain the old oil filter and recycle it
too; the same collection centres usually take filters. Keep used oil
separate from everything else: the EPA's never-mix rule warns that
contaminated leftovers "might become unrecyclable."

## Old building materials: asbestos and lead paint

Rubbish from working on an old house can hide two hazards you cannot see.

**Asbestos.** The EPA says you generally cannot tell whether a material
contains asbestos by looking at it, unless it is labelled. Material in
good condition that will not be disturbed should be left alone. Its
don'ts: don't dust, sweep or vacuum debris that may contain asbestos;
don't saw, sand, scrape or drill it; don't track it through the house.
Removal and major repair are for people trained and qualified to handle
asbestos, and the EPA does not recommend taking samples yourself.

**Lead paint.** The EPA says homes built before 1978 are more likely to
have lead-based paint, and that renovation or repair that disturbs
painted surfaces in older homes may create large amounts of lead dust.
For a renovation in a pre-1978 home, it points you to a lead-safe
certified contractor. For small flakes found while cleaning, it says to
remove the chips and dust carefully with a damp paper towel and put them
in the trash, then wipe the surface with a clean wet paper towel. Where
paint is visibly chipping onto a carpet, it says not to dry sweep, and to
use only a vacuum with a HEPA filter.

## Worked example: clearing out a garage

A garage that has not been cleared in years holds most of this guide.
Sort it into piles before anything leaves (the sorting is our
arrangement; each rule comes from the sections above).

1. **Leave alone and ask:** any crumbling old insulation, tiles or board
   that might contain asbestos; anything you cannot identify.
2. **Hazardous waste collection:** half-used paint, solvents, pesticides
   and weedkillers, pool chemicals, in their original labelled
   containers, lids tight, nothing mixed, nothing poured together to
   save space. Read each label for its disposal directions first: the
   EPA says to do so "to reduce the risk of products exploding,
   igniting, leaking, mixing with other chemicals, or posing other
   hazards on the way to a disposal facility." Keep pool chlorine apart
   from fuels, solvents, oil and other chemicals in its own sealed bag
   or tub, never in the same tray, so that nothing can reach it if a
   container leaks (general practice).
3. **Battery recycler or retailer:** the old car battery, handled as the
   warnings on it say (the EPA's precaution for lead-acid batteries);
   power-tool packs and loose cells with their terminals taped.
4. **Oil collection:** used motor oil in a sealed, labelled container,
   and the drained filter.
5. **Bulbs:** fluorescent tubes and compact bulbs, unbroken, for the
   recycling centre if your area requires it.
6. **Ordinary trash, after a check:** painted or treated offcuts (never
   to the fire pit), dried-out empty containers once the label allows.
7. **Compost:** only the plant matter the EPA's list allows.

Carry the hazardous piles in a box that cannot tip, with nothing that
could leak loose in the car, the pool chemicals kept apart as above, and
drive them straight there (general practice). The label check in step 2
is the EPA's own precaution for exactly this trip.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Asbestos removal or major repair:** trained and accredited asbestos
  professionals (EPA).
- **Renovating painted surfaces in a pre-1978 home:** a lead-safe
  certified contractor (EPA).
- **A corroding container of chemicals:** your local hazardous materials
  official or fire department (EPA).
- **A mercury spill bigger than a thermometer's:** the EPA's separate
  guidance, and your health department.
- **A damaged, swollen or hot lithium battery:** the manufacturer's
  handling instructions (EPA); if it is smoking or burning, get away from
  it and call the fire service (general practice).
- **A swallowed button battery, or a child into medicines or
  chemicals:** emergency help at once, and the hotlines above.
- **Burning anything other than clean, dry wood:** don't.

## How the game models it

The game has one kind of household waste, and it all goes on the
compost.

- **The Waste meter.** On the Inventory page your vitals include a Waste
  meter, out of 100. It rises slowly while you live, filling in about
  three days of game time, and each solid meal adds 4 (a drink adds
  nothing). Above 75 you get the Unsanitary effect, which slows your
  walking a little: the effect's data makes you 5 percent slower.
- **One button empties it.** "Compost and draw off urine" turns every 25
  points of waste into a 2 kg bag of Fertilizer, which the game's
  nutrient data treats as finished compost, and empties the meter. The
  same button draws off the urine collected in your home's tank. The
  Waste meter and the tank are saved with your game, but while "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which it is by default,
  each launch starts with a new body and the default home, so both start
  empty.
- **Composting at the Composter.** Recipes at a Composter make Fertilizer
  from sawdust with seed press cake or wheat bran, or from olive or apple
  pomace. Under the default settings, the Dev play mode (Settings >
  Gameplay > Play mode) does not use up the materials when you craft by
  hand, though you still need them in your inventory to start, and while "Start every session from the default home" is on, which it
  is by default, only your character carries between launches.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: in the
game there is nothing that cannot be composted. Nothing ever asks you to
get rid of a battery, a medicine, a needle, a chemical, used oil, ashes
or old paint; no bin catches fire; nothing is burned; and nothing is
poured away. The game's data
includes a file of waste categories (`data/waste_management.ron`, with
chemical, electronic and medical waste among them), but nothing in the
game uses it yet. And in the game, compost is instant. The EPA says a
well-kept backyard pile takes about three to five months, and an
untended one may take a year.

## You own this when

- You know what the EPA keeps out of a backyard compost pile, and where
  each of those things goes instead.
- You tape or bag lithium, button and rechargeable batteries, and none of
  them goes in your household bin or recycling.
- You know what to do at once if a child may have swallowed a button
  battery.
- You put ashes in a lidded metal can, outside, at least 10 feet from the
  house.
- You never burn rubbish, plastic, painted or treated wood, and never
  spread the ash of burned rubbish on a garden.
- You keep chemicals in their original labelled containers, never in a
  food or drink container, and never mix them, above all bleach with
  ammonia or any other cleaner.
- Nothing hazardous goes down your drains, toilet or storm drains.
- You know where your nearest household hazardous waste collection is
  and when it runs.
- You get rid of medicines through a take-back, and sharps in a proper
  container, the FDA's way.
- You can clean up a broken fluorescent bulb or a mercury thermometer
  without a vacuum or a broom, with the room aired to the outdoors
  before and after.
- You recycle used oil and the filter.
- You leave suspected asbestos alone, and you treat old paint in a
  pre-1978 house as lead until shown otherwise.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- That hot ashes must never go in a plastic bin, paper sack or compost
  heap, as a reading of the US Fire Administration's metal-can rule.

  lidded, sound container for kitchen rubbish, emptied often, to keep
  rats and flies away.

  ash of burned rubbish on a garden: our recommendations from the EPA's

  apart from fuels, solvents and other chemicals in its own sealed bag or
  tub.
