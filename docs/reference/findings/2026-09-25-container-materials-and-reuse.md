# Storage containers: what can hold what, and what can go back to food or water

**Research date: 2026-09-25.** A reading of public sources for game design. **Not professional food-safety advice and not legal advice**; nobody who wrote it is a food scientist or a lawyer.

**The question** (operator, 2026-09-25): a single liquid container should not hold fuel, milk and water interchangeably; a container that held oil should not hold milk without a strict cleaning procedure, and the interior coating probably matters. What do reliable sources say about which containers can hold what, and which can go back to food or drinking water?

Prepared for the HumanityOS homestead simulation. Every claim below points at a source in the list at the end (S1, S2, ...). Where a source does not settle a question, the text says "not settled by the sources read".

**How quotations are handled.** Exact quotations (in quotation marks, under 15 words) are used for U.S. federal government works and regulatory text (FDA, USDA, CDC, FEMA/Ready.gov, EPA web pages, OSHA, the Code of Federal Regulations, the Pasteurized Milk Ordinance, state administrative code), which are public domain or edicts of government. Sources that are copyrighted (university extension pages, Codex/FAO, industry associations, manufacturers, NSF, ASTM, BfR, AWRI) are paraphrased closely with the section named, so the wording can be checked at the URL. One short Codex quotation is used because its wording matters.

## 0. The owner's example, answered first

The owner's example (his framing, not a source): a tank that held oil should not later hold milk unless it can be properly cleaned, and the interior material or lining probably matters.

What the sources say:

1. **If the oil was a petroleum or non-food oil (motor oil, fuel oil, lubricant): no, in any regulated food setting.** The FDA Food Code's definition of poisonous or toxic materials explicitly includes "nonfood grade lubricants" and products "such as petroleum products and paints" (S1, Chapter 1 definitions), and section 7-203.11 says: "A container previously used to store POISONOUS OR TOXIC MATERIALS may not be used" to store, transport or dispense food (S1). The Food Code's own reason: there is "lack of confidence that all of the material could or would be removed" by washing and sanitizing (S1, Annex 3). The dairy rule (Pasteurized Milk Ordinance) says milk tank trucks and bins shall "not be used to transport or contain any substances that may be toxic" (S2, Item 21p). This rule is written about the container's history, not its material: it applies to steel and plastic alike.
2. **If the oil was an edible oil (vegetable oil, lard): it is a food container,** and the question becomes food-to-food switching (section 2.5). Cleaning to remove fat is exactly what dairy cleaning is designed for (section 2.6). The sources read do not give a specific rule for edible oil followed by milk.
3. **The owner's hunch that the interior material matters is supported.** In the international bulk edible-oil trade (Codex code of practice, S3), a tank's past cargoes are tracked, some past cargoes are allowed after cleaning and inspection and others are banned. For two banned substances the ban lasts longer in organically coated (for example epoxy-lined) tanks than in stainless steel: banned as either of the last two cargoes in coated tanks, but only as the last cargo in stainless steel and inorganically coated tanks; "leaded products" are banned as any of the last three cargoes (S3, Appendix 3 footnote and list). So a lining "remembers" its previous contents longer than bare stainless steel. For plastics, the FDA says non-food containers "can sorb relatively large amounts of chemicals from their contents" (S20), and the EPA-published review of plastic water pipes found that once plastic has absorbed fuel-type chemicals, flushing does not fix it and the pipe must be replaced (S21). Glass and metal behave differently: "Post-consumer use contamination is not a major concern with glass and metals" (S20).

## 1. Materials: what each is good for, and its limits

### 1.1 Stainless steel (304 and 316)

- **What it is used for.** Stainless steel of the AISI 300 series is the baseline material for everything that touches milk on a regulated U.S. dairy: the PMO lists "Stainless steel of the American Iron and Steel Institute (AISI) 300 series" first among allowed materials for milk containers, utensils and equipment (S2, Item 9r). USDA's dairy equipment guidelines (which reproduce the 3-A Sanitary Standard requirements) require AIST 300-series stainless, or a non-toxic, non-absorbent stainless equal in corrosion resistance to the 300 series (the comparison excludes types 301 and 302) (S4, section 9.1.B). In the bulk edible-oil trade, stainless steel is the most preferred tank metal and is recommended for fully refined oils (S3, section 3.1.4 b).
- **304 versus 316.** The European stainless steel development association's guide (S5, section on grades) explains that 304 (18% chromium, at least 8% nickel) is used from brewing vessels and kitchen sinks to milk tankers, and that 316 is 304 with about 2% molybdenum added, which makes it resist high chloride and sulphur dioxide levels; the guide names white wine storage, salty foods and pectin for jam as uses for 316. For very corrosive products (vinegar and mustard making, cheese dairies, fish canning, soy sauce at about 17% salt) it points to duplex or super-austenitic grades above 316 (S5). USDA's dairy guidelines make this concrete for one case: for cheese brine systems, "Stainless steel tanks should be made from 316 series stainless steel." (S4, section 15).
- **Limits.**
  - Salt (chloride) is the main enemy of 304; crevices are where chloride attack starts, because oxygen cannot reach to repair the surface (S5, design section).
  - Particles of ordinary carbon steel embedded in stainless steel will rust and can start pitting (S5, fabrication section). In game terms: scrubbing stainless with a carbon-steel wire brush or steel wool damages it (this application to cleaning tools is my reading of S5, which discusses fabrication).
  - Surfaces must be smooth and crevice-free to clean well; welds are ground smooth (S5; S4).
  - Stainless does not taint milk; low-grade "white metal" (stainless with substantial copper) and copper or rust in contact with milk cause metallic and oxidized off-flavors. NDSU Extension advises using only high-quality stainless steel, glass, plastic or rubber for milk contact surfaces (S6).
- **NSF/ANSI 51 (food equipment materials)** is the U.S. standard that lists acceptable stainless grades and other materials for food equipment. It is a paid standard and was **not read**; secondary summaries claim limits such as a 16% chromium minimum, but those were not verified and should not be used as data.

### 1.2 Food-grade plastics (HDPE, PP, PET and the resin codes)

- **The resin code only says which plastic it is.** ASTM D7611 (the resin identification code standard) defines the codes as resin identification and states they are not "recycle codes" (S7, paraphrase of the Significance and Use section). None of the sources read treats the code number as proof a container is food-grade. A USU Extension article lists what each code is commonly used for (S8); an Iowa State AnswerLine post (S9) describes codes 2, 4 and 5 as safe and 1, 3 and 6 as single-use only, and calls every code 7 polycarbonate. USU instead describes code 7 as any other resin or a combination (S8). **The sources disagree on what the codes imply; the safest reading is that the code alone does not settle food suitability.**
- **HDPE (#2).** USU Extension (S8): used for milk bottles, some juice containers and laundry products; the plastic in milk jugs is food grade, but used milk jugs are hard to sanitize and break down over time, so they are poor long-term food storage; bleach bottles hold up better, but water stored in them should be used for laundry or dishes, not drinking. Food-grade plastic barrels used for bulk liquids in restaurants and grocery stores are named as suitable rain-barrel stock for garden watering (S10, Texas Manual on Rainwater Harvesting, rain barrel section).
- **PET (#1).** Used for soft drinks, juices, bottled water, peanut butter, pickles, salad dressing; containers that held strongly scented foods will always keep some of the scent (S8). Ready.gov recommends "two-liter plastic soft drink bottles" for home water storage (S11). UGA's food-preservation specialist discourages repeated reuse of pre-filled plastic water bottles because they are not built to last structurally (S12, paraphrase).
- **PP (#5).** Yogurt containers, margarine tubs and medicine bottles are food-grade polypropylene (S8).
- **LDPE (#4).** Bags and squeeze bottles; probably not the best for long-term food storage; think about what was in it before and whether it can be sanitized (S8).
- **Heat limits.** U.S. approval of a plastic for food contact is tied to specific food types and temperatures. FDA's reference table defines conditions of use from high-temperature sterilizing (A) through "Room temperature filled and stored" (E), refrigerated (F) and frozen (G) (S13). In the federal rule for polyethylene and polypropylene (21 CFR 177.1520), several grades are allowed for fatty food types (types III, IV-A, V, which include foods with free oil or fat, butter-type emulsions and fats and oils) only at lower temperatures: they "shall be used only under conditions of use D, E, F, and G" (S14, item 3.1c), meaning not above hot-fill below 150 F. Clemson Extension (S15) advises against microwaving in margarine tubs, yogurt containers and whipped-topping bowls not labeled microwave-safe, because they may melt, warp or let chemicals migrate into food. **So: a plastic container is approved for a range of foods and temperatures; fatty foods and hot foods are the cases with tighter limits. Which specific household container is approved for what is not settled by the sources read (the approval lives with the manufacturer).**
- **Fermenting.** Food-grade plastic and glass "are excellent substitutes for stone crocks" (S16, USDA text via NCHFP). Non-food-grade 1 to 3 gallon plastic containers may be used if lined with a clean food-grade plastic bag; never garbage bags or trash liners (S16). Penn State and NMSU say the same about trash bags (S17, S18).
- **Dairy.** Plastics and rubber in milk contact must be "non-toxic, fat resistant, relatively nonabsorbent, relatively insoluble" (S2, Item 9r); rubber milking parts wear out, and pores and cracks shelter soil and microbes, so liners are replaced on a schedule (S19, Virginia Tech, paraphrase).

### 1.3 Glass

- **Suitable for** water, acidic foods, fermenting, canning, milk. Glass is impermeable to vapors and gases (S22, USU Extension water fact sheet). The PMO allows "Heat-resistant glass" in milk contact (S2, Item 9r). FDA: glass and metals are "generally impervious to contaminants" (S20, background section).
- **Limits.** Breakage: CDC says emergency water containers should be of "durable, unbreakable materials (not glass)" (S23); Ready.gov: "do not use glass containers, because they can break and are heavy" (S11). The Codex oil code says to avoid glass where breakage could contaminate the product (S3, 3.1.4). Canning: Mason jars "may be reused many times, requiring only new lids each time"; commercial mayonnaise and salad-dressing jars can be used for acid foods in a boiling-water canner with new two-piece lids but expect more breakage and seal failures, are "not recommended" for pressure canning, and "Seemingly insignificant scratches in glass may cause cracking and breakage" (S24).

### 1.4 Aluminium

- **Suitable for** short contact such as heating pickling liquid: UNL Extension lists aluminum pots as acceptable for heating pickling liquids (S17b), and NMSU names aluminum among the best utensils for heating pickling liquid (S18).
- **Limits.** Germany's Federal Institute for Risk Assessment (BfR) advises not to wrap acidic or salty foods (examples it gives: cut apples, tomatoes, rhubarb, salted herring, marinated fish, cheese) in aluminium foil, because acids and salts dissolve aluminium into food, and to use coated trays for keeping acidic or salty dishes warm or stored (S25, paraphrase). USU Extension: metals other than stainless steel are not optimal water containers unless coated and made to hold food or water (S22).
- **Long-term storage of milk or water in bare aluminium: not settled by the sources read.**

### 1.5 Copper and brass

- **Rule.** Copper and copper alloys such as brass "may not be used in contact with a FOOD that has a pH below 6" (vinegar, fruit juice, wine), nor in water lines between a backflow preventer and a carbonator (S1, 4-101.14). The Annex explains that acid (and carbonated water, which is acidic) leaches copper, that "Brass is an alloy of copper and zinc and contains lead", and that brass pumps, fittings and goblets have caused food poisoning (S1, Annex 3).
- **Exception.** Copper may contact acidic beer ingredients in the pre-fermentation and fermentation steps of brewing (S1, 4-101.14 B). The Annex adds that brewers now typically "use only stainless steel or stainless steel-lined copper" after the hot brewing steps (S1, Annex 3).
- **Edible oils.** Codex says copper and its alloys (brass, bronze, gun metal) should not be used in any tank, pipe, valve, heating coil, pump or sampling gear touching edible oils, because even trace copper strongly speeds rancidity (S3, sections 2.1.1 and 3.1.4 c; exact wording in the S3 entry below).
- **Milk.** USDA dairy guidelines: "Alloys containing lead, leachable copper or other toxic metals are not to be used." (S4, 9.1.D). NDSU lists copper tubing and copper in wash water among causes of oxidized off-flavor in milk (S6).
- **Pickling and fermenting.** Do not use copper or brass utensils or containers (S17, S17b, S18).
- **Drinking water.** Copper in drinking water mostly comes from corrosion of copper plumbing, and acidic water (pH below 7) is corrosive (S26, Connecticut Department of Public Health, paraphrase). A copper water vessel is therefore safest with neutral water; exact limits for a storage vessel are not settled by the sources read.

### 1.6 Galvanized (zinc-coated) steel

- **Rule.** "Galvanized metal may not be used for UTENSILS or FOOD-CONTACT SURFACES" used with acidic food (S1, 4-101.15). Annex: zinc leaches into watery foods, more with acidity, and most reported zinc poisonings involved "contaminated food that resulted from storage in a galvanized metal container" (S1, Annex 3).
- **What it is fit for.** The American Galvanizers Association (industry) says contact with meat and non-acidic fruits and vegetables is acceptable, acidic foods are the exception (S27, paraphrase). Traditional galvanized stock and rain tanks: the Texas rainwater manual says galvanized tanks must be lined for potable use (food-grade polyethylene or PVC liner, or an FDA- and NSF-approved epoxy paint inside) (S10, tank section and table).
- **Pickling and fermenting:** do not use galvanized containers (S17).
- In edible-oil tanks, zinc silicate coatings on mild steel are acceptable but crude oils with high acid values deteriorate in them (S3, 3.1.4 b).

### 1.7 Plain carbon steel and cast iron

- **Cast iron.** Not allowed as a food-contact surface in food establishments, except "Cast iron may be used as a surface for cooking." and for serving straight from cooking (S1, 4-101.12). Reason: cast iron is somewhat porous and hard to clean; heat during cooking handles the risk (S1, Annex 3).
- **Carbon steel and iron with acidic or salty foods:** Penn State, UNL and NMSU all say no iron containers or utensils for pickling and fermenting (S17, S17b, S18); the reason given is that iron, copper, brass and galvanized metal react with acids or salts, causing off colors and flavors or toxic compounds (S17b, paraphrase).
- **Oils.** Iron speeds oil rancidity, less than copper (S3, 2.1.1). Mild (carbon) steel is acceptable for crude and semi-refined oils and pipelines, stainless preferred and required for fully refined oils; mild steel tanks should preferably be coated with a food-approved inert lining (S3, 3.1.2, 3.1.4, 3.2.1).
- **Drums.** A drum maker (Skolnik) says unlined steel drums get only a rust inhibitor against flash rust and are not suitable for long-term rust resistance or chemical storage (S28); a Skolnik staff reply notes motor oil is commonly shipped in unlined carbon-steel drums (S29) (paraphrase). The Texas rainwater manual flags steel drums as prone to rust and says to check what they held before (S10, table).

### 1.8 Epoxy- or phenolic-lined steel drums and tanks

- **What linings do.** A lining separates contents from steel for products that would react with or be contaminated by bare steel (S29, Skolnik). Manufacturers assign each lining a pH range:
  - Skolnik: epoxy/phenolic lining for foods, detergents, latex paints, products above pH 7; higher-phenolic linings for organic solvents, insecticides and liquids below pH 7, and less acid-resistant than epoxy/phenolic (S29, paraphrase).
  - Pipeline Packaging (distributor): epoxy phenolic for pH 7 to 9 products including foods; gold, green and red phenolic linings for pH 3 to 7 with high acid and solvent resistance, used for acids, agricultural chemicals, insecticides, solvents, food products, essential oils and petroleum products (S30, paraphrase).
  - **The two manufacturers disagree on whether phenolic or epoxy-phenolic is more acid resistant.** Not settled by the sources read.
  - **The same lining types are sold for foods and for pesticides and petroleum.** A lining makes a drum capable of holding food; it does not make a used drum's history safe.
- **Limits.** Linings have a heat ceiling. Skolnik's figures differ between its own pages: one gives about 550 F as the upper tolerance of its epoxy phenolic lining (S28), a staff reply on another gives a peak metal temperature of 350 to 425 F (S29); both warn that heating a lined drum, for example with a band heater or flame underneath, can crack or peel the lining (S28, S29, paraphrase of staff replies). Codex adds that coatings can be damaged by abrasion and unsuitable cleaning, that tanks must be inspected and coatings repaired before loading, and that temperature limits may rule out live steam for cleaning (S3, 3.1.2 and 3.1.4 b). The EPA-published water review notes new bituminous tank coatings released alkyl benzenes and PAHs into stored water, so coatings must be properly applied and cured (S21, paraphrase).
- **Memory of past contents:** see section 0, point 3 (S3).

### 1.9 Wood (barrels, tanks, crates)

- **Rule.** In food establishments, "wood and wood wicker may not be used as a FOOD-CONTACT SURFACE", except hard maple (or equivalent) cutting boards, rolling pins, bowls and similar, and whole uncut produce or nuts in the shell kept in wood shipping crates (S1, 4-101.17). Reason: moist foods make wood deteriorate and hard to clean, and wood preservatives can migrate into food (S1, Annex 3).
- **Barrels in practice.** The Australian Wine Research Institute says microorganisms can migrate about 8 mm into barrel wood as wine soaks in, which makes a barrel harder to sanitize fully than a stainless tank; none of the tested treatments (hot water, steam, ozone, sulfur dioxide) guaranteed killing yeast in grooves or at 8 mm depth (S31, paraphrase). A 2017 study cited spoilage yeasts reaching more than 1 cm into staves after 10 weeks of contact (S32, paraphrase). The Food Code Annex notes some brewers use pitch-coated oak vats (S1, Annex 3).
- **Water tanks.** Wood tanks for drinking water need a food-grade liner (S10).
- **Reuse of a wood barrel after non-food contents:** no source read gives a cleaning route. Given the soak-in depths above, treat it as no (this is an inference, not a stated rule).

### 1.10 Ceramic, stoneware and glazes

- **Rule.** Ceramic, china and crystal in food contact must be lead-free or within FDA leachable-lead limits (0.5 mg/L for mugs, cups and pitchers up to 3.0 mg/L for plates) (S1, 4-101.13). Pewter over 0.05% lead and solder over 0.2% lead are not allowed on food-contact surfaces (S1).
- **Why.** Lead glazes are the most durable, and commercial makers control them tightly, but "Small potters often can't control the firing of lead glazes as well" (S1, Annex 3). Decorative pieces may be stamped "Not for Food Use", and FDA says "no amount of washing, boiling, or other process can remove lead from pottery" (S33).
- **Fermenting.** Do not use lead-glazed crocks (S17).
- Crude handmade, antique, worn or damaged pottery, and bright orange, red or yellow glazes, are FDA's "problem types" (S33, paraphrase of the list).

## 2. Reuse after holding something else

### 2.1 The governing rule: history, not material

- FDA Food Code 7-203.11: "A container previously used to store POISONOUS OR TOXIC MATERIALS may not be used" for food, and since 2022 also not for equipment, utensils, linens or single-use articles (S1). "Poisonous or toxic materials" includes cleaners and sanitizers (so bleach and detergent jugs), pesticides, "nonfood grade lubricants", and "petroleum products and paints" (S1, definitions).
- CDC: "Do not use containers that were previously used to hold liquid or solid toxic chemicals", naming bleach and pesticides (S23).
- Texas rainwater manual: tanks for drinking water must never have held toxic materials (S10, tank basics, paraphrase).
- These sources give **no cleaning procedure that restores a toxic-history container to food use**. The Food Code states its reason as lack of confidence that washing removes all of it (S1, Annex 3).

### 2.2 Plastics that held fuel, oil, solvents or pesticides

- **Plastics absorb.** FDA's recycled-plastics guidance treats consumer "storage of pesticides or automotive chemicals" in plastic containers as the contamination case recyclers must prove they can remove (S20, section V), and says non-food containers "can sorb relatively large amounts of chemicals from their contents" over long shelf lives (S20, section VI). FDA also notes non-food plastics may contain additives never approved for food, "incorporated into the polymer matrix during manufacture", which washing cannot remove (S20, section VI). The process FDA evaluates is industrial recycling (grinding, washing, high-temperature treatment, with test data), not home washing.
- **Fuel-type chemicals are the worst.** The EPA-published review of drinking-water pipes (S21, paraphrase) found plastic pipes were involved in 98% of reported permeation incidents and metal pipe in none; diesel and gasoline-range products caused 89%; oily, non-polar chemicals permeate plastic most, while strongly polar pesticides (paraquat, malathion, atrazine) and long-chain heavy hydrocarbons did not permeate pipe walls from outside. Solvents swell plastic; after contamination, flushing removes the surface but the pipe stays swollen and highly permeable, and permeated plastic pipe must be replaced. A 2015 study measured benzene breaking through 1-inch HDPE pipe in 5 days when exposed to free gasoline (search-result summary of S34; abstract not directly read).
- **Vapors travel through plastic even from outside:** USU Extension says water stored in plastic should be kept away from gasoline, kerosene and pesticides because plastic is permeable to certain vapors (S22).
- **Pesticide containers specifically:** federal pesticide labels must carry reuse statements such as "Do not reuse or refill this container." or, for refillable containers, "Do not reuse this container for any other purpose." (S35, 40 CFR 156.140). The UNEP/WHO/FAO pesticide guidelines say even apparently empty containers hold residues that cannot be completely removed and must never be used for anything but the original pesticide (S36, paraphrase).
- **Answer: a plastic container that held fuel, oil, solvent, pesticide or cleaners cannot go back to food or drinking water.** It can take non-food duty. The only sourced workaround for a non-food-grade (never contaminated) plastic vessel is lining it with a clean food-grade bag, as for fermenting (S16). USU allows former bleach bottles to hold water for washing, not drinking (S8).

### 2.3 Steel drums and tanks that held chemicals

- **Metal does not soak contents in the way plastic does.** FDA: glass and metals "are generally impervious to contaminants" and are cleaned at recycling temperatures (S20). The EPA-published review found no permeation through metal pipe (S21).
- **But the history rule still applies.** Food Code 7-203.11 makes no exception for metal (S1). EPA's drum page lists the former contents of reconditioned drums as industrial chemicals, acids, oils, solvents, paints and more, saying "These containers may have previously contained hazardous or other materials" (S37); the page does not say reconditioned drums are fit for food. Texas: check steel drums for prior toxic use (S10).
- **The one sourced "clean back" pathway is industrial:** in bulk edible-oil shipping, stainless or coated steel tanks may carry oil after cargoes on the Codex acceptable list (which includes, for example, acetic acid, ethanol, glycerine, potable water, molasses and some grades of mineral oil), but only with good tank design, cleaning routines with verified effectiveness, inspection and records (S3, Appendix 2 notes). Cargoes on the banned list (benzene, toluene, xylene, styrene, lube oil additives, leaded products and others) disqualify the tank as the immediately previous cargo, or for some, the previous two or three (S3, Appendix 3). Where tanks held non-edible materials, all residues must be totally removed and verified by cleaning and inspection (S3, section 4.2, paraphrase). Edible oils are also often refined after transport (S3 mentions subsequent refining as a factor), so this pathway does not transfer directly to a farm milk can.
- **Lined steel:** the lining, not the steel, is what touches the contents, and it is a plastic-type (organic) coating; Codex bans some previous cargoes for longer in organically coated tanks than in stainless steel (S3, Appendix 3 footnote). Whether a home user can clean a lined drum back to food use after chemicals: not settled by the sources read; the Food Code rule says no for toxic materials.

### 2.4 Food-grade containers switching from one food to another

- **General rule of thumb:** USU Extension says a container that originally held food can generally be used for food again (S8, paraphrase), but some plastic containers are hard to disinfect, may let contents evaporate, or collapse over time (S8).
- **Odors stay in plastic.** Containers that held strong-smelling foods (the example given is PET for pickles, peanut butter, dressing) will always keep some scent (S8).
- **Milk and juice residues stay in thin plastic.** Ready.gov: use soda bottles, "not plastic jugs or cardboard containers that have had milk or fruit juice in them", because "Milk protein and fruit sugars cannot be adequately removed" and they feed bacteria in stored water (S11). USU's water fact sheet is looser, calling beverage bottles and jugs excellent water containers after cleaning with extra attention to former food or beverage containers (S22); USU's plastics article sides with Ready.gov on milk jugs (S8). **Sources disagree mildly; the federal source says no milk or juice jugs for water.**
- **Commercial one-trip containers.** In food establishments, "jars, plastic tubs or buckets, bread wrappers, pickle barrels, ketchup bottles" that do not meet multi-use standards count as single-use, and "SINGLE-SERVICE and SINGLE-USE ARTICLES may not be reused." (S1, definitions and 4-502.13). The PMO likewise: "Articles intended for single-service use are not reused." (S2). Home users do reuse such containers (S10 names restaurant and grocery food-grade barrels as rain-barrel stock); the difference is that a single-use container was not built to survive repeated washing (S1, Annex 3).
- **Bulk oils: order matters.** Codex loading and discharge order: fully refined before partly refined, partly refined before crude, edible before technical grades, fatty acids last; tanks must be clean and dry because water promotes fat breakdown (S3, sections 2.1.2 and 4.1.4, paraphrase).
- **Dairy direction matters.** Texas rules: milk tank trucks carrying pasteurized milk that will not be re-pasteurized "shall not be used to transport raw milk, raw egg products" or other products the department finds to be a contamination source (S38). The PMO requires tanker trucks dedicated to pasteurized product for one special whey case (S2).
- **Bulk food transport generally:** a shipper of bulk food must have written procedures "adequate to ensure that a previous cargo does not make the food unsafe", and a carrier must on request tell the shipper the previous cargo and the most recent cleaning (S39, 21 CFR 1.908).
- **Milk versus pickles versus oil in the same container:** no source read sets a specific switching rule between these foods. What is settled: residues must be removed (fat by alkaline detergent, minerals by acid, see 2.5), strong odors persist in plastic, and milk residues are hard to remove from thin plastic jugs.

### 2.5 Dairy cleaning, as the sources describe it

Virginia Tech Extension publication 404-400 (S19, paraphrase), backed by the PMO (S2):

1. **Rinse immediately after milking** with lukewarm water, about 100 to 110 F. Too hot cooks proteins into a film; too cold sets fat into a greasy film. (Bulk tank rinse: 90 to 120 F, S19.)
2. **Hot alkaline wash** (often chlorinated alkaline detergent): start about 170 F and keep it above 120 F through the cycle, 6 to 10 minutes; in pipelines, flow of at least 5 ft/s with air slugs for scrubbing. Alkaline cleaners dissolve milk fat, protein and sugar; the chlorine helps remove protein films. Detergents are not sanitizers. Detergent strength depends on water hardness.
3. **Acid rinse** at about pH 3 to 4, 2 to 3 minutes, after every milking: it removes detergent traces and prevents mineral deposits; minerals plus organic soil form "milkstone".
4. **Drain and air dry;** store equipment so it drains.
5. **Sanitize just before the next milking** (Virginia Tech: within 30 minutes of milking), then let sanitizer drain. The PMO says milk-contact surfaces "shall be sanitized before each usage", either by hot water at "at least 77°C (170°F) for at least five (5) minutes" or by approved chemical sanitizers used per label (S2, Item 11r). Multi-use milk containers must be cleaned after each milking or every 24 hours for continuous operation (S2, Item 10r).
6. **Rubber parts** are replaced on schedule (the example given is about 1,200 milkings) or when soft, cracked or rough (S19).
7. **Safety:** never mix chlorine products with acids or other detergents (chlorine gas) (S19).

Materials that survive this regime: 300-series stainless, heat-resistant glass, and approved plastics and rubbers (S2, Item 9r). Aluminium, copper, galvanized and plain steel are not on the PMO's list except as "Equally corrosion-resistant, non-toxic metal", which the PMO does not define by example (S2).

## 3. Gases and fuels

### 3.1 Liquid fuel containers

- **Approved safety cans (workplaces).** OSHA defines a safety can as "an approved container, of not more than 5 gallons capacity, having a spring-closing lid" and spout cover, designed to relieve internal pressure in a fire (S40, 1910.106(a)). OSHA's container table limits glass and plastic containers of the most flammable liquids to small sizes (1 pint for Category 1), with safety cans up to 5 gallons and DOT metal drums up to 60 gallons (S40, Table H-12, paraphrase).
- **Consumer portable fuel containers (gas cans).** Air regulators control fuel escaping by permeation through the walls of plastic cans, as well as spills and venting; California's combined limit of 0.3 grams of hydrocarbons per gallon per day took effect January 1, 2009, and EPA had proposed the same limit nationally when the summary was written (S41, Ozone Transport Commission summary, 2006, paraphrase). This confirms that fuel moves into and through plastic can walls.
- **Reuse:** a fuel can holds a petroleum product, which is a "poisonous or toxic material" under the Food Code, so a former fuel can may not hold food (S1). No source read discusses the reverse (putting fuel in food containers) beyond OSHA's requirement for approved containers.

### 3.2 Propane and other compressed-gas cylinders (pressure vessels)

- **Who may fill.** A cylinder may not be shipped filled with a hazardous material unless "filled by the owner of the cylinder or with the owner's consent" (S42, 49 CFR 173.301(e)).
- **Damage.** A cylinder with a crack, leak, bulge, bad valve or relief device, or "fire or heat damage, or detrimental rusting or corrosion, may not be filled" (S42, 173.301(a)(2)).
- **Periodic requalification depends on what the cylinder has held.** Cylinders of DOT types 4B, 4BA, 4BW, 4E "used exclusively for non-corrosive gas" may be requalified every 12 years instead of every 5 (S43, 49 CFR 180.209(e)); cylinders used exclusively for LP gas meeting ASTM specifications may be requalified by external visual inspection, then every 5 years (S43, 180.209(g)). And "A cylinder that previously contained a Class 8 material must be requalified" (Class 8 = corrosives) (S42, 173.301(a)(4)). **So a cylinder's service history changes its inspection rules, the same "history" idea as food containers.**
- **Disposable cylinders.** DOT-39 cylinders (for example 1 lb camping propane) are non-refillable and must be marked "Federal law forbids transportation if refilled" (S44, 49 CFR 178.65).
- **Repurposing a gas cylinder for food or water storage, or turning it into a non-pressure object:** not settled by the sources read. None of the regulations read addresses it. (Propane is a petroleum product, so under a strict reading of the Food Code definition, a former propane tank would fall under the toxic-materials container rule; this is an inference.)

## 4. What the sources do NOT settle

- Which specific household plastic item is approved for which food type and temperature (the approval is with the maker; the resin code does not say).
- A home cleaning method that returns any container with toxic history to food use: none given; regulated settings forbid it outright.
- Whether a steel or lined drum that held a non-toxic, non-food product (for example a mineral oil or glycerine) may hold food at home: the Codex bulk-oil lists allow some such previous cargoes in industrial tanks with verified cleaning and inspection, but no home-scale rule was found.
- Edible oil to milk (or other food-to-food switches) in the same stainless or plastic container: no specific rule beyond normal cleaning and sanitizing.
- Bare aluminium for long-term milk or water storage.
- Acid resistance ranking of phenolic versus epoxy-phenolic drum linings (manufacturers disagree).
- Repurposing propane or other gas cylinders.
- Anything in NSF/ANSI 51, 3-A Sanitary Standards (read only as reproduced by USDA, S4) or ASTM D7611 beyond its store-page summary; these are paid standards and were not read in full.
- Colour coding of fuel cans (commonly cited as red gasoline, yellow diesel, blue kerosene under ASTM F852): not verified from a primary source in this pass.

## 5. Design reading (my synthesis for the game, not a source statement)

The sources suggest three behaviours a container model needs:

1. **Material reactivity (what it can hold now).** Inert: stainless (304 general, 316 for salt), glass, lead-free glazed ceramic in good condition. Reactive with acids and salts: copper and brass (pH below 6), galvanized (acidic foods), aluminium (acidic or salty), carbon steel and cast iron (pickles, brines; rust). Copper and iron also spoil oils and milk flavour.
2. **Absorbency (how long it remembers).** Plastics, rubber, wood, organic linings and cracked or unglazed ceramic absorb contents; glass and metal do not. Codex gives numbers you can use directly: stainless or inorganic coating remembers certain banned cargoes 1 fill, organic coating 2 fills, leaded products 3 fills (S3).
3. **Content history (what it may hold next).** A "toxic" flag (petroleum, lubricants, fuel, solvents, pesticides, cleaners, paints) permanently bars food and drinking-water use for that container (S1, S23, S10, S35, S36), with non-food reuse still allowed. Food-to-food switches need a clean (and for dairy, the rinse, alkaline wash, acid rinse, sanitize sequence) and may leave odour in plastic. Single-use food packaging (buckets, jars, milk jugs) degrades with reuse. Linings add a pH range, a maximum temperature and a damage state.
