# Orchestral music in the game: which recordings are free, and what a live orchestra stream would need

**Research date: 20 November 2026** (Pacific time; already 21 November in UTC). Every statute,
treaty, licence field and archive page below was read that day. Each source carries its own date
beside it where it shows one, which is often years older. If you are reading this later, compare
those dates with whatever has changed since.

**I am not a lawyer. This is a reading of public sources by an AI working on the project, not legal
advice.** Nobody qualified has reviewed it. Statutes, treaties and terms of service are texts whose
reading a court could disagree with, and several of the questions below turn on facts (who made a
recording, where it was first published, what an orchestra's contracts say) that only the people
involved can supply.

This finding builds on two earlier ones and does not redo them:
[`2026-11-06-music-sources.md`](2026-11-06-music-sources.md) (sample libraries, AI generators, the
Copyright Office on machine-made music) and
[`2026-11-20-public-domain-scores.md`](2026-11-20-public-domain-scores.md) (the compositions of
Grieg and Beethoven, the US 1931 cutoff for editions, the EU term for authors, critical editions,
transcription as no authorship). Where they already settled a point, it is cited, not re-read.

## The question

What would it take to play real orchestral music inside the game, in in-world concert halls and
stadiums: which existing recordings of orchestral music are free to use, and what would a live
broadcast or stream of a real orchestra into the game need? Added later the same day (section F):
where free MIDI files come from, and what rights a sequenced MIDI file can carry when the composition
itself is public domain.

## Short answer

- **A free composition does not make a free recording.** A recording of Beethoven carries up to three
  layers of rights: the composition (long free for Beethoven and Grieg, per the scores finding), the
  performers' rights, and the rights of whoever made the recording (the phonogram producer). Wikimedia
  Commons states the rule plainly: for a music recording "the license for _all_ aspects has to be
  determined and mentioned explicitly". Each layer has its own term, set by LAW.
- **The cleanest orchestral audio is still our own.** US law limits a sound recording's reproduction
  right so that it does "not extend to the making or duplication of another sound recording" that
  "consists entirely of an independent fixation of other sounds" (17 U.S.C. 114(b)). Rendering a
  public-domain score through our own CC0 instruments, which the score player already does, needs no
  one's permission, however closely it imitates a famous recording.
- **Free recordings that exist, each with its own licence to check per file:**
  - **Musopen's uploads to the Internet Archive.** "The Complete Chopin Collection" carries CC0
    (`licenseurl` "http://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/") and "The Musopen DVD"
    (orchestral works, piano sonatas, string quartets) the Public Domain Mark
    ("http://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/mark/1.0/"). Not every Musopen recording is public
    domain: IMSLP lists Musopen's 2012 recordings of the Peer Gynt suite under "Creative Commons
    Attribution 3.0" (the scores finding, finding 14), which needs a credit. Musopen's own site
    refused automated reading.
  - **Wikimedia Commons**: only files that are free in every layer, under its licensing policy, but
    each file's own licence still has to be read.
  - **Open Music Archive**: "Public Domain or (cc) by-sa 4.0 unless otherwise stated"; its listed
    recordings are 1920s commercial recordings, not orchestral, as far as the front page shows.
- **Recordings that are NOT free to reuse:** the Internet Archive's Great 78 Project ("made available
  for use in research, teaching, and private study only"). The Library of Congress's National Jukebox
  page refused automated reading, so its terms are unknown here; do not assume it is free. The
  "European Archive" no longer exists at its address (europarchive.org is now an unrelated French
  media site).
- **US recordings made before 15 February 1972** come under the Music Modernization Act's Title II
  (17 U.S.C. 1401, LAW). Recordings first published before 1923 became free after 31 December 2021;
  those published 1923 to 1946 become free 101 years after publication (so 1925 and earlier are free
  now, and 1926 recordings on 1 January 2027); 1947 to 1956, 111 years after; everything else made
  before 1972, after 15 February 2067. Commons says the same: "Recordings that were first published
  prior to 1926 entered the public domain on January 1, 2026."
- **In the EU and the UK**, performers' and producers' rights in a published recording last 70 years
  from publication (Directive 2011/77/EU; UK CDPA s.13A), but the longer term applied only to
  recordings still protected on 1 November 2013. By that arithmetic (my reading, not a source's
  words), a recording first published in 1962 or earlier is free of those rights in the EU, and one
  published in 1963 or later is protected for 70 years from publication.
- **A live stream of a real orchestra into the game would need, in law:**
  1. **The performers' consent**, everywhere. The US makes it a violation to transmit "the sounds or
     sounds and images of a live musical performance" without "the consent of the performer or
     performers involved" (17 U.S.C. 1101); the UK, the EU, the WIPO Performances and Phonograms
     Treaty and the Rome Convention all give performers the same control over live broadcasts. LAW,
     satisfied by written consent.
  2. **The composer's (publisher's) licence for a composition still in copyright**: the public
     performance right (17 U.S.C. 106(4)) and the communication to the public right (Directive
     2001/29/EC, Article 3(1)), usually licensed through collecting societies (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and
     GMR in the US; PRS in the UK; GEMA in Germany). **None for a public-domain composition**, though a
     modern edition or arrangement used for the performance can carry its own rights (scores finding).
     LAW.
  3. **The broadcaster's permission, if the stream is someone else's broadcast** (a radio or TV
     station's concert relayed into the game): broadcasting organisations control rebroadcasting and
     fixation of their broadcasts (Rome Convention, Article 13; Directive 2006/115/EC, Article 8(3)),
     for 50 years from first transmission in the EU. The United States is not a party to the Rome
     Convention, but a US broadcast recorded as it is sent is itself a copyrighted work (17 U.S.C. 101,
     "fixed"). LAW.
  4. **The digital performance right in sound recordings, if recordings rather than live sound are
     streamed** (17 U.S.C. 106(6), with the statutory licences in section 114). None for public-domain
     or CC0 recordings. LAW.
- **Platform rules (not law, and they can change):** YouTube's developer policies forbid an app to
  "separate, isolate, or modify the audio or video components" of a YouTube video, to play it "from a
  background player", or to "download, import, backup, cache, or store copies". So a YouTube concert
  may appear only in YouTube's own player on an in-world screen, never piped into a concert hall's
  speakers as its sound. YouTube's own Terms of Service could not be read (its robots.txt disallows
  that path) and Twitch's Terms did not render for the reader.
- **Free MIDI files: only where the file's own licence says so (section F).** Mutopia's MIDI files are
  "Computer-generated audio previews" that LilyPond makes from each piece's source, listed under that
  piece's one licence line (Für Elise, piece 931: "Copyright: Public Domain"). A sequence someone made
  can carry its maker's own rights even for Beethoven: the US Copyright Office will not register a
  standard MIDI file as a sound recording but "may accept the claim as a musical work", and the UK
  Act's "sound recording" covers a recording of a musical work "from which sounds reproducing the work
  or part may be produced" (LAW). Wikimedia Commons MIDI files each carry their own licence; Classical
  Archives forbids redistribution ("You agree not to redistribute"); IMSLP takes no MIDI of standard
  repertoire.
- **None of these limits is a patent.** Music is governed by copyright and related rights; codec
  patents are a separate matter, settled in [`2026-09-19-video-codec-licensing.md`](2026-09-19-video-codec-licensing.md).

## How this was read

Statutes and treaties were read from their official or standard hosts (law.cornell.edu for the US
Code, copyright.gov, eur-lex.europa.eu, wipo.int, legislation.gov.uk) and archive terms from the
archives' own pages or their own metadata (the Internet Archive's metadata service, which returns
each item's licence field). Every page was read through an automated web reader that extracts the
passages asked for and caps each quotation at 125 characters, so long passages arrive in pieces:
where a quotation below is given as several quoted fragments, the fragments are exact and the gap
between them is marked. Each site's robots.txt was read first, on its own; sites that refused were
not worked around and are listed under "Sources not reached". No audio file, archive item or
document other than the pages quoted was downloaded. Section F, added later the same day, read one
PDF, the Copyright Office's Compendium chapter 800, which the reader saved and which was searched
locally with `pdftotext`; its quotations are therefore whole sentences, not capped fragments. No MIDI
file was downloaded.

## The findings

### A. A recording is a separate work with its own rights

**1. US law treats sound recordings as their own works.** 17 U.S.C. 101, definitions
(https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/101; no page date shown): sound recordings are "works
that result from the fixation of a series of musical, spoken, or other sounds."

**2. Wikimedia Commons names the three layers.** *Commons:Licensing*
(https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Commons:Licensing, permanent link to revision 1281550762; the
reader saw no last-edited date): "For a music recording, the following aspects must be taken into
account": "The score of the music (rights by the composer)", "The performance (rights by the
performers)", "The recording (rights by the technical personnel / recording company)". And: "Note
that the license for _all_ aspects has to be determined and mentioned explicitly." Commons accepts
only media "that are explicitly freely licensed, or" "that are in the public domain in at least the
United States _and_ in the source country of the work", and "Media licensed exclusively under
_non-commercial only_ licenses (like CC BY-NC-SA) are not accepted either." On US recordings: "In the
US, the copyright situation for sound recordings (including those published before 1931) is a
special case." and "Recordings that were first published prior to 1926 entered the public domain on
January 1, 2026." (A Commons policy page is Commons' own rule and a careful secondary summary of US
law; the statute itself is finding 6.)

**3. A recording's rights do not reach an independent recording of the same music.** 17 U.S.C. 114
(https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/114; no page date shown), subsection (b): the sound
recording owner's reproduction rights "do not extend to the making or duplication of another sound
recording" that "consists entirely of an independent fixation of other sounds". Subsection (a): the
owner's exclusive rights "are limited to the rights specified by clauses (1), (2), (3) and (6) of
section 106" and "do not include any right of performance under section 106(4)". (The rest of (b),
about imitation, was not returned by the reader; the words quoted carry the rule.)

### B. Free recordings: where they are and what licence each carries

**4. Musopen on the Internet Archive: CC0 for one collection, the Public Domain Mark for another.**
Read through the Internet Archive's metadata service, which returns the licence field each item
carries:
- *Musopen - The Complete Chopin Collection* (https://archive.org/metadata/musopen-chopin/metadata;
  "publicdate" "2015-01-29 21:57:07"): "creator" "Aaron Dunn", "licenseurl"
  "http://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/zero/1.0/" (CC0 1.0).
- *The Musopen DVD* (https://archive.org/metadata/musopen-dvd/metadata; "date" "2012-08",
  "publicdate" "2012-08-10 20:07:52"): "licenseurl" "http://creativecommons.org/publicdomain/mark/1.0/"
  (the Public Domain Mark, a label saying the work is free of known copyright, not a waiver).
  Description: "This project raised money to purchase and release music to the public domain." and
  "Musopen (www.musopen.org) is a non-profit dedicated to providing copyright free music content:"
  (the sentence continues; the reader returned only these words). The reader summarised the contents
  as orchestral works, Schubert's piano sonatas, the Goldberg Variations and string quartets; the
  track list was not read.
- Not every Musopen recording is public domain: IMSLP's Peer Gynt Suite No. 1 page lists Musopen
  recordings of the suite (2012) under "Creative Commons Attribution 3.0" (read on 20 November 2026
  for [`2026-11-20-public-domain-scores.md`](2026-11-20-public-domain-scores.md), finding 14).
- Musopen's own site (musopen.org) refused automated reading (HTTP 403), so its own licence wording
  was not read.

**5. The Internet Archive's Great 78 Project is NOT free to reuse.** *78rpm Records Digitized by
George Blood, L.P.* (https://archive.org/metadata/georgeblood/metadata; "publicdate" "2016-10-06
22:05:34"; no `licenseurl` field): "This collection has been made available for use in research,
teaching, and private study only." "Copyrights that may exist in these materials have not been
transferred to the Internet Archive." "The Internet Archive does not advise as to the copyright
status of items in our collections." "It is the user's responsibility to determine whether
permission may be required for a given use". The same holds for any Archive item without a licence
field: the Archive grants nothing itself. (Its general Terms of Use page, https://archive.org/about/terms,
is drawn by script and returned only a title to the reader.)

**6. Open Music Archive: public domain or CC BY-SA 4.0, 1920s recordings.** Front page
(https://www.openmusicarchive.org/; footer "Open Music Archive 2005-2026"): "a collaborative project,
initiated by artists Eileen Simpson and Ben White, to source, digitise and distribute out-of-copyright
sound recordings." Footer: content is under "Public Domain" or "(cc) by-sa 4.0" "unless otherwise
stated." The browse tags shown were 1926 to 1929 commercial recordings (Okeh, Vocalion, Edison), not
orchestral works. The front page also names a project, "Open Music Archive in the Hallé Archive (2026
- 2028)" (the Hallé is a Manchester orchestra); its about page
(https://www.openmusicarchive.org/about.php) says nothing about it. **The two sources disagree on UK
law**: the about page says a sound recording's term is "limited to 50 years from the date of
recording", which was the old rule; the current UK Act (finding 11) gives 70 years from publication.
The Act is the authority; the archive's sentence predates the change or simplifies it.

**7. The Library of Congress National Jukebox: not read.** Its rights page refused automated reading
(HTTP 403). Nothing here shows its recordings are free to reuse; treat them as not free until a
person reads the terms.

**8. The European Archive: gone.** europarchive.org (robots.txt read, it allows readers) is now
"le média européen", a French general-interest site that says its name is a contraction of
"EuropeanArchive"; no recordings and no licence statements. A web search showed Musopen listing
"European Archive" as a performer, which suggests its recordings live on at Musopen, but Musopen could
not be read to confirm it.

### C. How long recording rights last

**9. United States, recordings made before 15 February 1972: 17 U.S.C. 1401 (the Classics Protection
and Access Act, Title II of the Music Modernization Act).** https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1401
(no page date shown; added by Pub. L. 115-264, approved 11 October 2018):
- Liability, (a)(1): "Anyone who, on or before the last day of the applicable transition period under
  paragraph (2)," [...] "and without the consent of the rights owner, engages in covered activity"
  [...] "shall be subject to the remedies provided in sections 502 through 505 and 1203."
- The period, (a)(2)(A)(i)(I): "through December 31 of the year that is 95 years after the year of
  first publication," then (II) "for a further transition period as prescribed under subparagraph (B)
  of this paragraph." And (a)(2)(A)(ii): it does not apply to any recording after "February 15, 2067."
- The further periods, (a)(2)(B): for recordings first published before 1923, the period "shall end
  on December 31 of the year that is 3 years after the date of enactment of this section" (so 31
  December 2021); for 1923 to 1946, it "shall end on the date that is 5 years after the last day of the
  period described in subparagraph (A)(i)(I)."; for 1947 to 1956, "15 years after" that day; for every
  other recording fixed before 15 February 1972, it "shall end on February 15, 2067."
- The Copyright Office's page (https://www.copyright.gov/music-modernization/pre1972-soundrecordings/;
  no last-updated date shown) says the law "extends remedies for copyright infringement to owners of
  sound recordings fixed before February 15, 1972", that they "shall be available for 95 years after
  the year of first publication of the recording", and for the oldest group that the period "ends on
  December 31, 2021."
- Worked out (my arithmetic from the words above): a recording first published in year Y between 1923
  and 1946 is free from 1 January of Y + 101 (1925 recordings since 1 January 2026, 1926 recordings
  from 1 January 2027, 1946 recordings from 2047); between 1947 and 1956, from 1 January of Y + 111;
  every other pre-1972 recording, after 15 February 2067. Commons (finding 2) reaches the same date
  for pre-1926 recordings. A recording never published seems to fall in the last group (my reading
  of clause (B)(iv), which the reader paraphrased rather than quoted).
- **A narrow door for noncommercial use**, (c): use of a pre-1972 recording "that is not being
  commercially exploited by or under the authority of the rights owner" is allowed if the user makes a
  search and files a notice; the Office's page: "a user must file a notice of noncommercial use after
  conducting a good faith, reasonable search." and "the rights owner of the sound recording must not
  object to the use within 90 days."

**10. EU: performers and phonogram producers, 50 years, or 70 from publication of a phonogram.**
Directive 2006/116/EC as consolidated on 31 October 2011, Article 3
(https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:02006L0116-20111031):
- 3(1): "The rights of performers shall expire 50 years after the date of the performance." "However,
  if a fixation of the performance is lawfully published or lawfully communicated to the public within
  this period," "the rights shall expire 50 years from the date of the first such publication or the
  first such communication to the public," (the 2011 amendment replaced this for phonograms; next
  bullet).
- Directive 2011/77/EU (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32011L0077;
  OJ L 265, 11.10.2011, p. 1; "Done at Strasbourg, 27 September 2011."), the new sentence for a
  performance fixed in a phonogram: "the rights shall expire 70 years from the date of the first such
  publication or the first such communication to the public".
- 3(2): "The rights of producers of phonograms shall expire 50 years after the fixation is made."
  "However, if the phonogram has been lawfully published within this period, the said rights shall
  expire" "70 years from the date of the first lawful publication."
- 3(4): "The rights of broadcasting organisations shall expire 50 years after the first transmission of
  a broadcast," "whether this broadcast is transmitted by wire or over the air, including by cable or
  satellite."
- The transition, Directive 2011/77/EU, Article 10(5) as reported by the reader: the new terms apply
  to fixations of performances and phonograms "in regard to which the performer and the phonogram
  producer are still protected" on 1 November 2013, and to later ones. Terms run from 1 January of
  the following year (Article 8, quoted in the scores finding, finding 4).
- Worked out (my arithmetic): under the old 50-year rule a phonogram published in 1962 lost its
  protection at the end of 2012, before 1 November 2013, and was not revived; one published in 1963
  was still protected on that day and now runs to the end of 2033.

**11. UK: 70 years from publication.** Copyright, Designs and Patents Act 1988, s.13A(2)
(https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/13A; latest change in the page's timeline
31/12/2020): copyright in a sound recording expires (a) "at the end of the period of 50 years from the
end of the calendar year in which the recording is made", or, if published in that period, (b) "70
years from the end of the calendar year in which it is first published", or, if not published but
made available, (c) "70 years from the end of the calendar year in which it is first so made
available".

**12. The treaty floor: 50 years for performers.** WIPO Performances and Phonograms Treaty (WPPT),
WIPO Lex (https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/295578; "adopted in Geneva on December 20, 1996"; no
page date), Article 17(1): "The term of protection to be granted to performers under this Treaty shall
last, at least," (the reader summarised the rest as 50 years from the end of the year of fixation).

### D. A live stream of a real orchestra

**13. US: no transmission of a live musical performance without the performers' consent.** 17 U.S.C.
1101 (https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/1101; no page date), subsection (a): "Anyone who,
without the consent of the performer or performers involved—" (1) "fixes the sounds or sounds and
images of a live musical performance in a copy or phonorecord," [...] (2) "transmits or otherwise
communicates to the public the sounds or sounds and images of a live musical performance, or" (3)
"distributes or offers to distribute, sells or offers to sell, rents or offers to rent, or traffics
in" [...] "shall be subject to the remedies provided in sections 502 through 505," "to the same
extent as an infringer of copyright." Subsection (d) keeps state law: it does not "annul or limit any
rights or remedies under the common law" or "statutes of any State."

**14. UK: the same, by statute.** CDPA 1988 s.182(1)
(https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/182; "Latest available (Revised)", latest dated
change 01/02/2006): "A performer’s rights are infringed by a person who, without his consent—" (a)
"makes a recording of the whole or any substantial part of a qualifying performance directly from the
live performance," (b) "broadcasts live, . . . the whole or any substantial part of a qualifying
performance," (the dots are the Act's own mark for words repealed in 2003).

**15. EU: performers decide on live broadcasts and fixation.** Directive 2006/115/EC
(https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32006L0115; OJ L 376, 27.12.2006,
p. 28; dated 12 December 2006):
- Article 7(1): "Member States shall provide for performers the exclusive right to authorise or
  prohibit the fixation of their performances."
- Article 8(1): "Member States shall provide for performers the exclusive right to authorise or
  prohibit the broadcasting by wireless means" [...] "the communication to the public of their
  performances, except where the performance is itself already a broadcast performance or is made from
  a fixation."
- Article 8(2), for recordings used in broadcasts: "a single equitable remuneration is paid by the
  user, if a phonogram published for commercial purposes, or a reproduction of such phonogram, is used"
  "for broadcasting by wireless means or for any communication to the public".
- And on demand, Directive 2001/29/EC (https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32001L0029;
  OJ L 167, 22.6.2001, p. 10; of 22 May 2001), Article 3(2): the exclusive right to authorise making
  available "for performers, of fixations of their performances;" and "for phonogram producers, of
  their phonograms;".

**16. The treaties.** WPPT (finding 12), Article 6: "Performers shall enjoy the exclusive right of
authorizing, as regards their performances:" (the reader summarised the items as (i) the broadcasting
and communication to the public of their unfixed performances and (ii) their fixation). Article 15(1):
"Performers and producers of phonograms shall enjoy the right to a single equitable remuneration" for
commercial phonograms used in broadcasts. Rome Convention, WIPO Lex
(https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/text/289757; done at Rome on 26 October 1961), Article 7(1)(a):
performers may prevent "the broadcasting and the communication to the public, without their consent,
of their performance,". Article 12: "a single equitable remuneration shall be paid by the user."
Article 14: protection "shall last at least until the end of a period of twenty years,".

**17. The composer's rights: public performance and communication to the public.** 17 U.S.C. 106
(https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/17/106; no page date): "Subject to sections 107 through 122,
the owner of copyright under this title has the exclusive rights" "to do and to authorize any of the
following:" (4) "in the case of literary, musical, dramatic, and choreographic works, pantomimes, and
motion pictures" "and other audiovisual works, to perform the copyrighted work publicly;". Streaming
into a game is performing "publicly": 17 U.S.C. 101 defines it to include "to transmit or otherwise
communicate a performance or display of the work" to the public, "whether the members of the public
capable of receiving the performance or display receive it in the same place" or "in separate places
and at the same time or at different times." In the EU, Directive 2001/29/EC, Article 3(1): authors
have "the exclusive right to authorise or prohibit any communication to the public of their works."
For a public-domain composition there is no such right left to license (the scores finding, findings
1 to 4); for a modern edition or arrangement used to perform it, see that finding's findings 7 to 10.

**18. Someone else's broadcast carries the broadcaster's own rights.** Rome Convention (finding 16),
Article 13: broadcasting organisations may authorise or prohibit (a) "the rebroadcasting of their
broadcasts", (b) "the fixation of their broadcasts", (d) "the communication to the public of their
television broadcasts," in places open to the public against an entrance fee. Directive 2006/115/EC,
Article 8(3): "Member States shall provide for broadcasting organisations the exclusive right to
authorise or prohibit" "the rebroadcasting of their broadcasts by wireless means," "as well as the
communication to the public of their broadcasts if such communication is made in places accessible to
the public against payment of an entrance fee." Article 7(2) gives them the fixation right; Directive
2001/29/EC, Article 3(2)(d), the making available "for broadcasting organisations, of fixations of
their broadcasts,"; and the term is 50 years from first transmission (finding 10, Article 3(4)).
**The United States is not a party to the Rome Convention**: WIPO's list of contracting parties
(https://www.wipo.int/wipolex/en/treaties/ShowResults?search_what=C&treaty_id=17; header "Total
Contracting Parties: 100"; no date shown; the reader counted 103 table rows, a mismatch it could not
explain) does not list it, while the United Kingdom (ratified 30 October 1963, in force 18 May 1964)
and Germany (in force 21 October 1966) are listed. But a live US broadcast is still a protected work
if recorded as it goes out: 17 U.S.C. 101, "fixed": "A work consisting of sounds, images, or both,
that are being transmitted, is “fixed” for purposes of this title" "if a fixation of the work is being
made simultaneously with its transmission."

**19. Streaming recordings: the US digital performance right.** 17 U.S.C. 106(6): "in the case of
sound recordings, to perform the copyrighted work publicly by means of a digital audio transmission."
101: a "digital transmission" is "a transmission in whole or in part in a digital or other non-analog
format." Section 114(d) limits that right: (d)(1)(A) exempts "a nonsubscription broadcast
transmission", and (d)(2) opens "The performance of a sound recording publicly by means of a
subscription digital audio transmission" and sets out when such transmissions are subject to
statutory licensing (only the opening words were read; the conditions were not). None of this touches
a public-domain or CC0 recording, or audio the game renders itself.

**20. Collecting societies: the route, not the law.** The societies that license the rights in
finding 17 for works still in copyright (ASCAP, BMI, SESAC and GMR in the US; PRS for Music in the UK;
GEMA in Germany) could not be read: ASCAP refused even its robots.txt (HTTP 403); BMI's robots.txt
disallows ClaudeBot and anthropic-ai, so it was not read; PRS's licence page is drawn by script and
returned only its title; SESAC, GMR and GEMA were not attempted. A web search showed PRS pages titled
"Online Live Concert Licence" and "You need our licence for Classical Online Live Concerts", which
suggests PRS sells a per-event licence for streamed concerts, but no page body was read, so nothing
here relies on it.

### E. Platform rules

**21. YouTube: only its own player, unmodified, never the sound alone.** *YouTube API Services -
Developer Policies* (https://developers.google.com/youtube/terms/developer-policies; no "Last
updated" date on the page, which links a revision history not read). An API client must not:
III.I.7 "separate, isolate, or modify the audio or video components of any YouTube audiovisual
content"; III.I.8 "promote separately the audio or video components of any YouTube audiovisual
content"; III.I.9 "create, include, or promote features that play content, including audio or video
components, from a background player"; III.I.6 "modify, build upon, or block any portion or
functionality of a YouTube player;"; III.E.1.a "download, import, backup, cache, or store copies of
YouTube audiovisual content without YouTube's prior written approval,"; III.E.1.b "make content

### F. Free MIDI files: where they come from and what rights a sequenced file can carry

A MIDI file holds no sound: it lists notes (key, velocity, timing, channel, instrument number) for a
synthesizer to play. Two kinds matter here: a file a program generates from a written score, and a
sequence a person enters or plays in, choosing velocities, timing and instrumentation. Which open
Rust crates read such files is a separate, technical note:
[`../research/2026-11-20-midi-input/README.md`](../research/2026-11-20-midi-input/README.md),
section "Reading MIDI files (Standard MIDI Files)".

licence page and its Beethoven licence counts were read for
[`2026-11-20-public-domain-scores.md`](2026-11-20-public-domain-scores.md) (finding 20) and are not
redone. Read again on 20 November 2026 (robots.txt first: "User-agent: *" with an empty "Disallow:"):

  2015/Aug/18."): "Für Elise", "Copyright: Public Domain", "Source: Breitkopf & Härtel, 1888",
  "Typeset using: LilyPond, version 2.18.2", "Maintainer: Stelios Samelis"; the downloads are "LilyPond
  file", "MIDI file" (`fur_Elise_WoO59.mid`), "A4 PDF files" and "Letter PDF files". The page has one
  licence line for the piece and no separate statement for the MIDI file.
- The front page (https://www.mutopiaproject.org/; no date): "Computer-generated audio previews of the

  Mutopia, or by generating the MIDI from the public-domain `.ly` ourselves.

**24. United States: a standard MIDI file is not a sound recording; a claim in one can only be a
musical work.** *Compendium of U.S. Copyright Office Practices*, Third Edition, chapter 800, "Works of
the Performing Arts" (https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/chap800/ch800-performing-arts.pdf; page footer
"01/28/2021"; the Compendium's index page, https://www.copyright.gov/comp3/, says "This update is
effective as of January 28, 2021"; copyright.gov has no robots.txt, HTTP 404). The Compendium is the
Office's manual for its own examiners, not a statute or a court's ruling.
- Section 803.4(C), "Insufficiently Fixed Formats": "Certain formats do not sufficiently fix a
  specific series of sounds. In such cases, the Office will not register a claim in sound recording.
  For example, standard midi files capture the underlying musical score, but they do not capture a
  specific series of sounds. While they contain instructions for producing sounds, any instrumentation
  may be applied, resulting in a file that contains different sounds each time it is played. For this
  reason, the Office does not consider standard midi files to be phonorecords and will not register a
  copyright claim in a sound recording contained in a standard midi file (although it may accept the
  claim as a musical work)."
- What a musical-work claim needs. Section 802.8(B): "Transcribing or fixing a musical work in and of
  itself does not constitute authorship." Section 802.9(E)(6), on the word "transcription" meaning
  writing down someone else's music: "In this situation, the transcriber's contribution is not
  registrable." Section 802.5(C): "music generated entirely by a mechanical or an automated process is
  not copyrightable. For example, the automated transposition of a musical work from one key to
  another is not registrable."
- What can count. Section 802.6(I), "Editorial Authorship": "Musical editing generally consists of
  adding markings for the performance of a musical composition, such as additional or altered
  fingering, accents, dynamics, and the like." and "A work consisting of editorial revisions,
  annotations, elaborations, or other modifications which as a whole represent an original work of
  authorship, is a derivative work." Section 802.9(E)(6): arranging a work "for a different group of
  instruments" is described as "musical arrangement" or "orchestration".
- My reading (no court decision was read): in the US a note-for-note MIDI entry of a public-domain
  score is transcription and carries nothing; a MIDI file generated by a program from a score carries
  nothing; but a sequence whose maker chose velocities (dynamics), accents, timing and
  instrumentation may carry a thin copyright as a derivative musical work, owned by its maker. So a
  third party's MIDI file of Beethoven is not free to copy merely because Beethoven is.

**25. United Kingdom: a MIDI file may itself be a "sound recording".** Copyright, Designs and Patents
Act 1988, s.5A (https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/1988/48/section/5A; "Latest available
(Revised)", the only change in its timeline dated 01/01/1996; robots.txt read first, it disallows only
three paths, none of them `/ukpga`). (1): "In this Part "sound recording" means—" (a) "a recording of
sounds, from which the sounds may be reproduced, or" (b) "a recording of the whole or any part of a
literary, dramatic or musical work," "from which sounds reproducing the work or part may be
produced," "regardless of the medium on which the recording is made or the method by which the sounds
are reproduced or produced." (2): "Copyright does not subsist in a sound recording which is, or to the
extent that it is," (the reader paraphrased the rest: a copy taken from a previous sound recording).
My reading: a MIDI file is "a recording of" a "musical work" from which sounds reproducing it "may be
produced", whatever "the method by which the sounds are" produced, and s.5A asks for no originality,
only that the recording not be copied from an earlier one. If that reading holds, a person's MIDI
sequence of Beethoven is a sound recording in the UK, owned by its producer for 50 years from making
or 70 from publication (s.13A, finding 11), even where the US would see nothing to protect. No UK
court decision on MIDI files was read.

**26. The treaty definition leaves room: "a representation of sounds".** WPPT (finding 12), Article
2(b): a "phonogram" is "the fixation of the sounds of a performance or of other sounds, or of a
representation of sounds," "other than in the form of a fixation incorporated in a cinematographic or
other audiovisual work;". Article 2(c): "fixation" is "the embodiment of sounds, or of the
representations thereof," "from which they can be perceived, reproduced or communicated through a
device;". Article 2(d): the producer is the person "who or which takes the initiative" "and has the
responsibility for the first fixation of the sounds of a performance or other sounds," "or the
representations of sounds;". Whether a MIDI sequence is "a representation of sounds" is not settled by
anything read here; the US Office (finding 24) and the UK Act (finding 25) point different ways. The
EU's own position was not researched (no directive defining a phonogram, and no EU court decision on
MIDI, was read).

## What is still unknown, and what would settle it

1. **Musopen's own licence wording, recording by recording.** Settled by a person reading
   musopen.org in a browser, or by reading each Internet Archive item's `licenseurl` before a file is
   chosen (one metadata request per item, no download). Cost: minutes per recording.
2. **The Musopen DVD's track list and who performed each piece.** The Public Domain Mark says the
   uploader believes it is free; a performer who did not release their rights could still object.
   Settled by reading the item's file list and Musopen's project pages; for a recording we put in the
   game, by keeping that page's date and wording beside the file.
3. **The National Jukebox's terms.** Settled by a person reading
   https://www.loc.gov/collections/national-jukebox/about-this-collection/rights-and-access/ in a
   browser. Not needed while other sources serve.
4. **What a real orchestra's own contracts allow.** Orchestra players may work under union and
   broadcast agreements, and hired performance materials may come with a publisher's terms. None of
   this was researched (no union's or publisher's page was read). These are contracts, not laws, patents or platform rules: negotiable, but binding on
   the orchestra. Settled by asking the orchestra we would partner with, in writing. Cost: one
   conversation, then a signed consent.
5. **What a collecting society would charge, if a concert ever included a work still in copyright.**
   Settled by asking PRS, ASCAP or BMI for a quote for that event. Not needed for public-domain
   programmes.
6. **Whether a free, donation-funded game counts as "noncommercial" under 17 U.S.C. 1401(c).** A
   lawyer's question; moot while only public-domain, CC0 or our own recordings are used.
7. **Twitch's and YouTube's general terms** on embedding and on showing streams inside another
   program. Settled by a person reading them in a browser, the date noted.
8. **The Beijing Treaty on Audiovisual Performances (2012)**, which gives performers rights in
   filmed performances (relevant if an orchestra's video, not just its sound, is streamed in), was not
   read. Settled by reading it on WIPO Lex. The consent this finding already requires would cover it
   in practice.
9. **Countries outside the US, the UK and the EU.** Each sets its own related-rights terms (the
   treaties set floors: 20 years under Rome, 50 under the WPPT). Settled per country only if the
   project ever relies on a recording being free there and not in the US or the EU.
10. **How far a MIDI sequencer's rights really reach (section F).** No court decision was read in any
    country; the US reading rests on the Copyright Office's manual and the UK reading on the bare
    words of s.5A; the EU position was not researched at all. Settled by a lawyer, or by a dated
    search of case law (US, UK, and the Court of Justice of the EU). Moot for MIDI the project
    generates itself, for Mutopia files under their piece's licence, and for Commons files whose
    own licence is CC0; it matters only for a third party's sequence without a licence.
11. **Whether Mutopia's licence line is meant to cover each piece's MIDI file.** The pages read never
    say so in words (finding 23). Settled by asking Mutopia, or made moot by generating the MIDI (or,
    as the project already does, the MEI) from the public-domain LilyPond source ourselves.

## What it means for this project

### Certain (from the quotations above)

- A public-domain composition does not make a recording of it free: the performers' and the
  producer's rights are separate and have their own terms (findings 1 to 3, 9 to 12).
- Audio the game renders itself from a public-domain score, with CC0 instruments, needs no one's
  permission, even when it imitates a famous recording (17 U.S.C. 114(b), finding 3).
- Musopen's Chopin collection on the Internet Archive carries CC0 and the Musopen DVD the Public Domain
  Mark; some Musopen recordings are CC BY 3.0 instead (finding 4). Each file's own licence decides.
- The Great 78 Project's recordings may not be reused (finding 5); the Internet Archive grants nothing
  of its own.
- Open Music Archive material is public domain or CC BY-SA 4.0 unless an item says otherwise
  (finding 6).
- In the US, recordings first published before 1926 are free now; 1926 recordings become free on
  1 January 2027; most other pre-1972 recordings come free between 2027 and 15 February 2067
  (finding 9).
- Streaming a live orchestra into the game needs the performers' consent in the US, the UK and the EU
  (findings 13 to 16), whatever the music.
- Streaming a composition still in copyright needs its public performance licence; a public-domain
  composition needs none (finding 17).
- Relaying a broadcaster's stream brings the broadcaster's own rights in (finding 18).
- YouTube's developer policies forbid separating a video's audio, background play and caching
  (finding 21).
- Mutopia's MIDI files are generated by LilyPond from each piece's source and listed under that
  piece's single licence line, "Public Domain" for Für Elise (finding 23). The US Copyright Office
  does not treat a standard MIDI file as a phonorecord and will not register a sound recording in one,
  though "it may accept the claim as a musical work"; transcription and automated output carry no
  authorship (finding 24). Classical Archives' terms forbid redistribution, IMSLP accepts no MIDI of
  standard repertoire, and Commons MIDI files each carry their own licence (findings 27 to 29).

### Judgement calls (an AI's reading; reject freely)

- **Keep the concert halls' default music our own renderings** of public-domain scores (the score
  player and CC0 instruments). It is the only route with no third-party rights at all, it follows the
  tuning setting and the hall's acoustics, and nothing has to be streamed.
- **Add real recordings only file by file**, from items whose own licence field says CC0 or the
  Public Domain Mark, with the item's page, date and licence recorded beside the file. CC BY
  recordings (some Musopen ones) can ship with a credit row, since the operator allowed
  credit-required materials in the main pack on 2026-11-20 (scores finding). CC BY-SA recordings (Open
  Music Archive) can ship unchanged under their own licence beside the file, never relabelled CC0
  (the reasoning of [`2026-11-20-openmoji.md`](2026-11-20-openmoji.md): a collection, not an
  adaptation); a remix of one would be share-alike.
- **Prefer Musopen's CC0 and Public Domain Mark items over pre-1972 commercial recordings.** The 1401
  schedule frees old US recordings, but EU and UK terms differ, and the oldest orchestral recordings
  are, as far as general knowledge goes (not researched here), short 78 rpm sides of poor fidelity.
- **A live concert is a partnership, not a feed.** Invite an orchestra to stream a public-domain
  programme into the game with written consent from the orchestra and its players (the consent
  covering fixation, live transmission and any replay), carried on our own stream, never relaying a
  radio or TV broadcast. Then the only rights in play are the ones the consent grants.
- **Never stream commercial recordings** into a hall. That brings the US digital performance right and
  the EU's equitable remuneration in, which means licences and payments the project has no way to
  administer.
- **YouTube on an in-world screen stays a screen**: YouTube's own player, with its own sound, as the
  embedded browser already plans. It cannot become the concert hall's sound system.
- **MIDI files: treat every sequence someone else made as theirs unless its licence says otherwise.**
  Ship only MIDI the project generates itself (better still, the MEI it already converts from
  Mutopia's public-domain LilyPond, which makes a MIDI file unnecessary for the notes), Mutopia files
  under their piece's licence, and Commons files whose own page says CC0 or public domain (CC BY with a
  credit row), with the page, date and licence recorded beside the file. Never Classical Archives'
  files. A MIDI file a person imports from their own computer stays theirs: the game plays it locally
  and never uploads or redistributes it, and the import screen should say so in plain words.
- **Say it on the screen** where a person meets it, per the project rule: "Real orchestras can play
  here only with their own permission; recordings play only when their licence lets anyone use them."
  Name the law, so no one thinks it is an upsell.

**Again: I am not a lawyer, and this is not legal advice.** It is a dated reading of public sources,
written so a later reader can check the same words and see what changed.
