# Is 500 people the upper limit for one community, and how should a 500-person sector be organised inside?

**Research date: 2026-11-07.** Every source below was read on 2026-11-07. This is a reading of
public research papers and official pages, done so the ship's sectors can be sized and laid out
from evidence rather than from a number remembered secondhand. **It is not sociological or
anthropological advice**, and nobody who wrote it is a social scientist. Anyone planning a real
settlement should go to the primary sources and to people who study communities for a living.

## The question

Does the research support about 500 people as "the upper limit for one massive group to work
together", what population should a ship's sector therefore use, and how should a 500-person sector
be divided inside so that the group sizes the research does support are built into its spaces?

Asked by the operator on 2026-11-07, verbatim: "Let's start with a sector for 500 people. I've heard
that communities of 500 people is about the upper limit for one massive group to work together. Can
you research that number?" A sector is a self-contained community block aboard the colony ship.

Related design documents: [mothership-superstructure.md](../../design/mothership-superstructure.md)
("Residential zone sizing: Dunbar's number", written 2026-07-01 with no source),
[mothership-master-plan.md](../../design/mothership-master-plan.md) (a neighbourhood node of "a few
hundred people, the 150 to 500 band"), and
[ship-homes-and-logistics.md](../../design/ship-homes-and-logistics.md) (which noted that the Dunbar
layers were unsourced and disputed, and sized neighbourhoods by walking time instead). This finding
is the source those documents lacked.

## Short answer

- **500 is a real number in this research, but it is the outer ring, not the working group.** In
  Robin Dunbar's layered model of human social networks, about 500 is the layer of acquaintances:
  "people we would not consider as personal friends or family, but know well enough to have a
  conversation with" (F5). The group held together by personal relationships is about 150 (F1, F4,
  F6). Real communities that run themselves without police or written law split between about 150
  and 200: Hutterite colonies split at a mean of 166 (F12, F13, F14), Alpine villages governing
  shared pasture and forest held a mean of 176 and a median of 140 for six centuries (F15).
- **500 does mark a second threshold, and it is the one the operator heard of.** Above about 500,
  communities in the record need a formal structure (officials, specialist roles, written rules) to
  hold together: Naroll's 1956 analysis of 30 societies, as Dunbar reports it (F2, F12); Israeli
  kibbutzim settle at a geometric mean of 468 and leak members beyond it (F12); German residential
  campsites cluster near 50, 150 and 500 residents (F16); hunter-gatherer "mega-bands" average about
  567 (F3, F12). So the operator's number holds when it is read as **the upper limit of one community
  in which everyone can still recognise and talk with everyone, with a light formal structure**, not
  as the size of one group that works together as a team.
- **Every one of these numbers is an average with a wide spread, and the brain-size prediction
  behind 150 is disputed.** A 2021 reanalysis found 95% confidence intervals of 4 to 520 and 2 to
  336 and called any single number "futile" (F8); Dunbar replied that its statistics were wrong and
  that his method gives 158 (F9); its authors replied that his method gives 289.8 (F10). The
  evidence from real communities (F12 to F17) does not depend on that regression, and it clusters
  near 150 for self-governing groups.
- **What the design should use (a judgement, argued at the end):** keep 500 as the sector's cap,
  framed as the acquaintance community, and build the smaller layers into the space at a ratio of
  about three: homes of about 5 people, clusters of 3 homes (about 15), blocks of about 50,
  neighbourhoods of about 150 (three per sector, each one split rather than grown past about 200),
  civic places sized for all 500, and about 1,500 (three sectors) sharing a district's larger places.

## How the sources were read

- Papers were read in full from PubMed Central (pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) where they are open access,
  with abstracts and dates from Europe PMC's public search service; the campsite paper from the
  published PDF in Maynooth University's repository; NSF, NASA and the Cohousing Alliance from their
  own pages. Requests identified themselves as research reading. Pages that refused automated
  reading are listed under "Sources that could not be reached" and were not worked around.
- **Dunbar 1993's published full text is behind Cambridge's paywall.** Its abstract was read on
  Cambridge Core; its body was read from the author's unedited preprint of the same article, hosted
  at the University of Debrecen, whose first line says it is "the unedited preprint (not a quotable
  final draft)". Quotations from it (F1, F2) are therefore the preprint's words and may differ from
  the printed article.
- **Two sources are essays, not papers**: Dunbar's reply to his critics (F9) and their rejoinder
  (F10), both in The Conversation, both written by the scientists themselves. They are used because
  they are the exchange in the authors' own words; the peer-reviewed reply (F11) could be read only
  as an abstract.
- **Quotations reproduce the source's words exactly**, except that curly quotation marks,
  apostrophes and typographic hyphens are written as plain ASCII ones, line-break hyphens left by text extraction are removed,
  and subscripts are written inline (S5 for S with subscript 5). Dashes and symbols such as ~ and ±
  are kept as the source prints them.
- "Layer" sizes in Dunbar's work count **cumulatively**: the 150 layer includes the 50, which
  includes the 15, and so on (F7).

## A. The layers, and what each one is

### F1. Dunbar 1993: the prediction of about 150, and three levels of grouping

R. I. M. Dunbar, "Coevolution of neocortical size, group size and language in humans", *Behavioral
and Brain Sciences* 16(4), December 1993, pp. 681 to 694 (target article; commentaries and reply to
735), published online by Cambridge University Press on 4 February 2010
([cambridge.org, doi 10.1017/S0140525X00032325](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/coevolution-of-neocortical-size-group-size-and-language-in-humans/4290FF4D7362511136B9A15A96E74FEF)).
The published abstract:

> "Group size covaries with relative neocortical volume in nonhuman primates. This regression
> equation predicts a group size for modern humans very similar to that for hunter-gatherer and
> traditional horticulturalist societies."

From the author's preprint ([mnytud.arts.unideb.hu/tananyag/dunbar.htm](https://mnytud.arts.unideb.hu/tananyag/dunbar.htm)):

> "Equation (1) yields a predicted group size for humans of 147.8."

> "the 95% confidence limits around this prediction (from formulae given by Rayner 1985) are
> moderately wide (100.2-231.1)."

On the ethnographic data (his Table 1, hunter-gatherers and swidden horticulturalists):

> "group sizes fall into three quite distinct size classes: small living groups of 30-50
> individuals [...], a large population unit (the tribe or in some cases sub-tribe) that typically
> numbers between 500 and 2500 individuals and an intermediate level of grouping [...] that
> typically contains 100-200 people."

His Table 1 gives means of 37.7 (overnight camp, 8 societies), 148.4 (band or village, 9) and
1154.7 (tribe, 13). **In this 1993 paper there is no separate layer at 500**: 500 is the bottom of
the tribe range.

### F2. Dunbar 1993: the limit of a team, the Hutterites, and Naroll's threshold at 500

Same preprint. On military companies (the preprint gives a mean company size of 179.6 for the
twentieth-century armies it lists):

> "That the smallest independent unit should turn out to have a maximum size of about 200 even in
> modern armies [...] suggests that this upper limit is set by the number of individuals who can
> work effectively together as a coordinated team."

On the Hutterites, citing Hardin (1988):

> "They explicitly state that when the number of individuals is much larger than this, it becomes
> difficult to control their behaviour by means of peer pressure alone (Hardin 1988). Rather than
> create a police force, they prefer to split the community."

On Naroll (1956), an analysis of 30 societies from hunter-gatherers to large-scale agriculturalists:

> "His analyses suggest that there is a critical threshold at a maximum settlement size of 500
> beyond which social cohesion can only be maintained if there is an appropriate number of
> authoritarian officials."

And Dunbar's reading of it: "Bearing in mind that Naroll's threshold is expressed as the maximum
observed settlement size, it seems likely that the equivalent mean settlement size will not be too
far from the value of 150". (Naroll's own paper was not read; see below.)

### F3. Zhou, Sornette, Hill and Dunbar 2005: a hierarchy with a ratio of about three, and the 500 layer

W.-X. Zhou, D. Sornette, R. A. Hill and R. I. M. Dunbar, "Discrete hierarchical organization of
social group sizes", *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 272(1561): 439 to 444, dated 17 February
2005 by PubMed Central
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1634986](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1634986/),
doi 10.1098/rspb.2004.2970). Abstract:

> "humans spontaneously form groups of preferred sizes organized in a geometrical series
> approximating 3-5, 9-15, 30-45, etc."

The innermost layer, from the introduction:

> "the core social grouping is the support clique, defined as the set of individuals from whom the
> respondent would seek personal advice or help in times of severe emotional and financial
> distress; its mean size is typically 3–5 individuals"

**What the paper says the 500 layer is:**

> "Beyond these, at least two larger-scale groupings have been identified in the ethnographic
> literature: the megaband of ca. 500 individuals and the tribe (a linguistic unit, commonly of
> 1000–2000 individuals) (Dunbar 1993)."

The means it computes:

> "Averaging across these grouping levels, the data give mean values of S0=1 (individual or ego),
> S1=4.6, S2=14.3, S3=42.6, S4=132.5, S5=566.6 and S6=1728."

The successive ratios are 4.58, 3.12, 2.98, 3.11, 4.28 and 3.05, mean 3.52. On armies, in the
discussion: "companies (3–4 platoons, ca. 120–150), battalions (usually 3–4 companies plus support
units, ca. 550–800)", which it offers as a possible parallel, phrased as a question. And it is candid
about the gap in understanding: "At present, there is no obvious reason why a ratio of three should
be important."

### F4. Hill and Dunbar 2003: Christmas-card networks

R. A. Hill and R. I. M. Dunbar, "Social network size in humans", *Human Nature* 14(1): 53 to 72,
March 2003 (abstract read through Europe PMC, PMID 26189988, doi 10.1007/s12110-003-1016-y):

> "Maximum network size averaged 153.5 individuals, with a mean network size of 124.9 for those
> individuals explicitly contacted; these values are remarkably close to the group size of 150
> predicted for humans on the basis of the size of their neocortex."

The sample was 43 people by F6's Table 1 (F3 describes it as 42 UK-domiciled subjects). This
measures one person's network, not a community.

### F5. Dunbar 2016: what the 500 and 1,500 layers are

R. I. M. Dunbar, "Do online social media cut through the constraints that limit the size of offline
social networks?", *Royal Society Open Science* 3(1): 150292, published 20 January 2016
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736918](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4736918/)):

> "The two outermost layers (at 500 and 1500) correspond, respectively, to acquaintances (people we
> would not consider as personal friends or family, but know well enough to have a conversation
> with) and to the number of faces we can put names to."

And what the 150 layer is:

> "an individual's social network (defined as all meaningful relationships) is considerably larger
> than this, and typically in the order of 100–200 individuals."

### F6. Dunbar 2020: the layers, the community sizes, households, and the caveats

R. I. M. Dunbar, "Structure and function in human and primate social networks: implications for
diffusion, network stability and health", *Proceedings of the Royal Society A* 476(2240): 20200446,
published 26 August 2020
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7482201](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7482201/)).

Its Table 1 lists community sizes, among them: Domesday Book villages (1087 AD) 150; Italian alpine
communities (1250 to 1800) 176; Hutterite farming communities (51) 107; "Nebraska" Amish parishes
113; company (mean, WW2 armies) 180; average 158.0 ± 28.7 SD. On the original prediction:

> "This prediction had a 95% confidence interval of 100–250, very close to the observed variance in
> the data."

(F1's preprint gives 100.2 to 231.1 for the same prediction; the two do not match exactly.)

The layers each hold about three times the one inside, and contact and closeness fall with each
layer (figure 2: "The number of people included in each circle increases, but the frequency of
contact and emotional closeness declines, with each layer."). On households:

> "One way this structure might arise would be if the basal layer of five people represented, for
> example, a family or household, such that the next layer of 15 consists of three families with an
> especially close relationship"

and the caveat that follows it:

> "in more mobile, post-industrial societies, figure 2 can arise simply a consequence of the
> patterns of interaction between individuals and need have no family-based (or spatial)
> underpinning to it at all."

On variation: "within populations, there is considerable variation between individuals" (by sex, age
and personality). On what holds a group together: "Group-living is often mistaken for a cooperation
problem, but it is in fact a coordination problem." Its Table 2 gives the unit sizes of "modern
armies" across six layers as (4 to 5), 5 to 14, 15 to 45, 80 to 150, 300 to 800 and about 1,500
(special forces, section, platoon, company, battalion, regiment).

From a model of how news spreads face to face through communities of 5, 15, 50, 150, 500, 1,500
and 5,000:

> "The probability of acquiring information reaches an asymptotic value at a community size of
> approximately 1500, with no further gain in the likelihood of hearing about an innovation as
> community size increases beyond this."

and the point where the gain starts to fall away is "at a community size of exactly 50".

### F7. Dunbar 2025: the layers by name

R. I. M. Dunbar, "Why friendship and loneliness affect our health", *Annals of the New York Academy
of Sciences* 1545(1): 52 to 65, published 6 March 2025
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11918532](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11918532/)):

> "These numbers form a series of layers at 1.5 (effectively, intimate friends), 5 (close friends),
> 15 (best friends), 50 (good friends), 150 ("just friends"), extending beyond the 150 layer in
> humans to include layers at 500 (acquaintances), 1500 (familiar faces), and 5000 (known faces)"

And: "Note that these layers count cumulatively: the 15-layer includes the 5-layer, and the 50-layer
includes the 15-layer, and so on."

## B. The dispute over 150

### F8. Lindenfors, Wartel and Lind 2021: the confidence intervals

P. Lindenfors, A. Wartel and J. Lind, "'Dunbar's number' deconstructed", *Biology Letters* 17(5):
20210158, published 5 May 2021
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8103230](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8103230/)).
Abstract:

> "Bayesian and generalized least-squares phylogenetic methods generate approximations of average
> group sizes between 69-109 and 16-42, respectively. However, enormous 95% confidence intervals
> (4-520 and 2-336, respectively) imply that specifying any one number is futile."

> "A cognitive limit on human group size cannot be derived in this manner."

On average against maximum, in the results:

> "Note that these estimates (as was true for Dunbar's original estimated group size) are averages,
> not estimates of upper bounds."

The paper also notes, in its introduction, that the Swedish Tax Authority restructured its offices
"to stay within the 150-person limit".

### F9. Dunbar's reply, 2021 (an essay by the author)

Robin Dunbar, "Dunbar's number: why my theory that humans can only maintain 150 friendships has
withstood 30 years of scrutiny", *The Conversation*, published 12 May 2021
([theconversation.com, 160676](https://theconversation.com/dunbars-number-why-my-theory-that-humans-can-only-maintain-150-friendships-has-withstood-30-years-of-scrutiny-160676)):

> "Most of these claims fail to recognise that Dunbar's number applies to quality relationships,
> not to acquaintances – which account for the more casual outer layers of our social networks,
> beyond our 150 meaningful friendships."

> "For the same dataset, the LSR method used by the Stockholm study predicts 71 (the basis of their
> claim), whereas the RMA method I originally used predicts 158."

(LSR is least-squares regression; RMA is reduced major axis regression, which he argues is the right
method when the brain-size values are themselves uncertain.)

### F10. The critics' rejoinder, 2021 (an essay by two of the authors)

Johan Lind and Patrik Lindenfors, "Why we dispute 'Dunbar's number' – the claim humans can only
maintain 150 friendships", *The Conversation*, published 23 June 2021
([theconversation.com, 161944](https://theconversation.com/why-we-dispute-dunbars-number-the-claim-humans-can-only-maintain-150-friendships-161944)):

> "The new estimate was 289.8, but again with very large 95% confidence intervals between 226.0 and
> 371.6 (in other words, the estimate is highly uncertain)."

(that is, with the RMA method Dunbar proposed; with monkeys and apes only, 404.1, between 300.6 and
543.3), and:

> "Dunbar's method arrives at an estimate of average human group size. But then it is used to
> indicate maximum human group size – two different things."

### F11. Dunbar and Shultz 2023: the peer-reviewed reply (abstract only)

R. I. M. Dunbar and S. Shultz, "Four errors and a fallacy: pitfalls for the unwary in comparative
brain analyses", *Biological Reviews* 98(4): 1278 to 1309, published 31 March 2023 (abstract read
through Europe PMC, PMID 37001905, doi 10.1111/brv.12953). Europe PMC lists it among the papers that
cite F8. Abstract:

> "We argue that most of these disputes arise from a number of conceptual errors and associated
> logical fallacies that are the result of a failure to adopt a biological systems-based approach to
> hypothesis-testing."

Its four error classes include "the use of inappropriate statistical methods". The abstract does
not name F8, and the full text (open access at the publisher) refused automated reading, so what it
says about F8 in particular was not read.

## C. Real communities

### F12. Dunbar and Sosis 2018: communes, Hutterites, kibbutzim, and the threshold at about 500

R. I. M. Dunbar and R. Sosis, "Optimising human community sizes", *Evolution and Human Behavior*
39(1): 106 to 111, January 2018
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5756541](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5756541/)).
It tests 53 nineteenth-century American utopian communes, 97 Hutterite colony fissions (1880 to
1970, from Olsen 1987) and 240 Israeli kibbutzim. Abstract:

> "Community sizes of 50, 150 and 500 are disproportionately more common than other sizes; they also
> have greater longevity."

Its benchmark layers for 20 hunter-gatherer societies, from Lehmann, Lee and Dunbar (2014): bands
42.8 ± 18.0 SD, clans 127.3 ± 43.8, "mega-bands" 566.6 ± 166.2, tribes 1727.9 ± 620.6.

**Hutterites.** Mean size at fission "166.5 ± 25.8SD"; daughter colonies average 76.0 and 91.2;
"communities larger than ~ 180 would fission so frequently (at least once a year) that they would be
socially unstable"; they let colonies run past 150 "but with ~ 200 as the normative upper limit". And
why:

> "The Hutterites split their communities once they are above ~ 150 because, in their experience,
> this is the limit at which community cohesion can be maintained without the need for formal laws
> and a police force to maintain discipline (Olsen, 1987)."

**Communes.** Sizes at foundation fall into clusters "at 49 (41 communities) and 268 (9
communities), with two communities centred at 700" (its Table 1 prints the middle cluster as 278).
"In sum, communities of around 50 or 150 at foundation seem to survive longer than those at other
values." Religious communes lasted far longer than secular ones: "on average, 35.6 ±
32.5SD vs 7.7 ± 8.0 years".

**Kibbutzim.** Geometric mean 468.2. "Two-thirds of the kibbutzim have < 500 residents; only one of
the 240 kibbutzim is below 150 in size and only eight (3.8%) are larger than 1000 residents". They:

> "seem to have a minimum size at foundation of ~ 150 and a long term stable size of ~ 500
> individuals, apparently mainly as a result of trickle emigration (casual emigration by
> individuals) once this value is exceeded."

**The threshold at about 500**, in the discussion:

> "when community size exceeds ~500, the rising intensity of social and other stresses threaten
> community coherence and stability, and cohesion can only be maintained if some mechanism is
> available to suppress or mitigate these disruptive forces (Dunbar, 2012)."

> "Naroll (1956) concluded, from an analysis of maximum village size in 30 small scale societies,
> that there is a critical threshold at ~ 500 individuals beyond which social cohesion depends on
> having a top-down authoritarian organisational structure"

It adds that secular kibbutzim, lacking a religious framework, "have responded by increasing levels
of surveillance over residents in order to enforce conformity", and that "the effective functional
group size is actually the number of adults" (stated as an assumption). On cause: "as yet, we have no
explanation as to why these numbers seem to function socially so much more effectively than other
values."

### F13. Olsen 1987: Hutterite colonies split at about 166

C. L. Olsen, "The Demography of Colony Fission from 1878–1970 Among the Hutterites of North
America", *American Anthropologist* 89(4): 823 to 837, December 1987 (doi 10.1525/aa.1987.89.4.02a00040).
The publisher's page was not read; the abstract was read as indexed by OpenAlex
([api.openalex.org record](https://api.openalex.org/works/https://doi.org/10.1525/aa.1987.89.4.02a00040)):

> "From 1878–1970 fission occurred an average of every 14–15 years when colonies attained a size of
> 166 persons."

The same abstract says emigrating groups held 35% to 55% of the mother colony. This is the primary
source behind F12's Hutterite data, and it does not rest on Dunbar's regression.

### F14. Alberti 2014: where scalar stress becomes critical

Gianmarco Alberti, "Modeling Group Size and Scalar Stress by Logistic Regression from an
Archaeological Perspective", *PLoS ONE* 9(3): e91510, published 13 March 2014
([journals.plos.org, 10.1371/journal.pone.0091510](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0091510)).
A model fitted to the Hutterite fission data (Johnson's "scalar stress" is the rise in conflict that
comes with group size):

> "allows locating a critical scalar stress threshold at community size 127 (95% CI: 122–132), while
> the maximum probability of critical scale stress is predicted at size 158 (95% CI: 147–170)."

And: "Hutterite colonies usually fission at a population threshold that Olsen [63] locates somewhere
between 150 and 175 persons."

### F15. Casari and Tagliapietra 2018: six centuries of Alpine commons

M. Casari and C. Tagliapietra, "Group size in social-ecological systems", *PNAS* 115(11): 2728 to
2733, published 22 February 2018
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5856516/)).
248 groups in Trentino, Italy, that governed shared pasture and forest from 1249 to 1801. From the
significance statement:

> "In the field, the average size of successful villages was around 176 individuals, although the
> variance was considerable."

> "Estimates from multiple perspectives converge on similar figures at around 140–176 members per
> group, or in a larger range between 109 and 215 when performing a sensitivity analysis"

The median was about 140, and "The estimated attraction point is 154." The groups decided face to
face: "All household heads entitled to common property resources in the community were called to
join the assembly at which decisions were undertaken face to face." Group size counts people (the
households at the assembly multiplied by the average household size). The authors separate a group
that decides together from a network of acquaintances:

> "One may conjecture that groups needing collective decision making will, on average, be smaller,
> or at least not larger, than social networks."

### F16. Kordsmeyer, Mac Carron and Dunbar 2017: residential campsites

T. Kordsmeyer, P. Mac Carron and R. I. M. Dunbar, "Sizes of Permanent Campsite Communities Reflect
Constraints on Natural Human Communities", *Current Anthropology* 58(2), April 2017, from page 289,
electronically published 31 January 2017 (doi 10.1086/690731; read from the published PDF in
Maynooth University's repository,
[mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/11647](https://mural.maynoothuniversity.ie/id/eprint/11647/1/MacCarron_Sizes_2017.pdf)):

> "In two separate data sets of different grain, we find consistent evidence for sites with 50, 150,
> 500, and maybe 1,500 residents."

> "Our data do not allow us to say why this pattern emerges"

It names, as an untested prediction, "that large campsites are divided into subcommunities that
interact within themselves especially frequently and between themselves only rarely".

### F17. Hamilton and colleagues 2007: an independent hunter-gatherer hierarchy

M. J. Hamilton, B. T. Milne, R. S. Walker, O. Burger and J. H. Brown, "The complex structure of
hunter-gatherer social networks", *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 274(1622): 2195 to 2203,
published 3 July 2007
([pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2706200](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2706200/)).
1,189 group sizes in 339 hunter-gatherer societies (Binford's 2001 compilation); none of the authors
is Dunbar. Abstract:

> "We show that population structure is indeed self-similar or fractal-like with the number of
> individuals or groups belonging to each successively higher level of organization exhibiting a
> constant ratio close to 4."

Its Table 1, geometric means: family 4.48; dispersed group 15.60; aggregated group 53.66; periodic
aggregation 165.32; population 839.19 (95% limits 736.36 to 954.03). **There is no level near 500
in this dataset**; the level above about 165 is the whole regional population.

### F18. Cohousing: 20 to 40 homes (a practitioners' statement)

Cohousing Alliance, "About" ([cohousingalliance.org/about](https://cohousingalliance.org/about/),
published 15 November 2022, modified 9 June 2026 per the page's own metadata):

> "Most communities find 20-40 units is about the right size to know each other well."

This is the experience of the people who build cohousing, not a study. At typical household sizes,
20 to 40 homes is roughly 50 to 150 people.

### F19. Antarctic stations

U.S. National Science Foundation, Office of Polar Programs, station pages, both "updated: June 5,
2026". McMurdo Station
([nsf.gov/od/opp/ail/mcmurdo-station](https://www.nsf.gov/od/opp/ail/mcmurdo-station)):

> "Up to 1,100 people during a week in the summer season. Up to 200 people during a week in the
> winter season."

Amundsen-Scott South Pole Station
([nsf.gov/od/opp/ail/amundson-scott-south-pole-station](https://www.nsf.gov/od/opp/ail/amundson-scott-south-pole-station)):

> "Up to 150 people during a week in the summer season. Up to 42 people during a week in the winter
> season."

These are logistics capacities, not studies of group size; they show the sizes that isolated,
self-contained crews actually live at.

### F20. The International Space Station

NASA, "Space Station Facts and Figures"
([nasa.gov](https://www.nasa.gov/international-space-station/space-station-facts-and-figures/),
page last updated 16 July 2024):

> "An international crew of seven people live and work while traveling at a speed of five miles per

A crew of seven sits at the support-clique to sympathy-group scale; no source read ties it to group

## What is still unknown, and what would settle it

| Unknown | What would settle it | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Whether 500 is a distinct community size or a by-product of the analyses. ## What it means for this project

### Certain (from the sources)

- In Dunbar's model, about 500 is the layer of acquaintances, people one knows well enough to talk
  with but not as personal friends or family (F5, F7); about 150 is the layer of "all meaningful
  relationships" (F5); about 1,500 is faces one can put names to (F5).
- The working limit Dunbar himself named in 1993 for "individuals who can work effectively together
  as a coordinated team" was about 200, from military companies (F2), not 500.
- Self-governing communities without formal law split between about 150 and 200: Hutterites at a
  mean of 166 (F12, F13), with a critical point modelled at 127 to 158 (F14); Alpine commons at a
  mean of 176, median 140, attraction point 150 to 154 (F15).
- Communities near 500 exist and last: kibbutzim settle near 468 (F12), campsites cluster near 500
  (F16), and the nineteenth-century communes clustered at 49, about 270 and 700 at foundation (F12).
  Above about 500, sources report that cohesion
  needs a mechanism: formal officials and specialist roles (Naroll as reported, F2, F12), a shared
  religious framework, or in secular kibbutzim, surveillance (F12).
- The layers stack at a ratio of about three (F3, F6), or about four in an independent dataset
  (F17); the household is the base, about 4.5 to 5 people (F3, F17).
- The brain-size extrapolation cannot fix any number with confidence (F8, F10), and Dunbar disputes
  the critics' statistics (F9, F11). Both sides agree the figures are averages with wide individual
  variation (F6, F8).

### Judgement calls (ours; a reader may weigh them differently)

- **The operator's 500 is right, read the right way.** It is not the size of one group that works
  together; that is about 150, and the evidence for it is the strongest in this file because it
  comes from real communities (F12 to F15, F17) as well as from the disputed regression. It is the
  largest community in which everyone can still know everyone by sight and hold a conversation, and
  the size past which the record says a community needs a formal structure. That makes it a good
  **cap** for a sector, and a poor size for any single team, assembly or work crew.
- **We weigh the dispute this way:** the 2021 critics are right that the regression alone cannot
  give a number and that an average is not a maximum (F8, F10); Dunbar is right that the community
  evidence does not stand or fall with the regression (F9). The design therefore leans on the
  community evidence and treats every number as a centre with a spread, never as a hard rule.
- **The numbers the design should use**, at a ratio of about three, so that each layer the research
  supports gets a place of its own:

| Layer | People | Built as | Shared places | Why |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Home | 1 to 6, planned at 5 | one home | its own rooms | support clique 3 to 5 (F3); family 4.48 (F17); "basal layer of five" (F6) |

- **Households are about 5, not 15.** The operator's "households of about 5 to 15" spans two layers:
  5 is the home, 15 is a cluster of about three homes that share a door or a court.
- **Grow and split like the Hutterites.** A neighbourhood that would pass about 200, or a sector
  that would pass 500, opens a new one rather than packing more in (F12, F13). Filling a new sector
  one neighbourhood at a time, from about 150, matches how kibbutzim were founded (F12).
- **The sector's mechanism should be open rules and an elected council, never surveillance.** F12
  reports surveillance as what secular kibbutzim used to hold together past 500; the project's
  privacy rules rule that route out. Every adult in a neighbourhood fitting in one room keeps
  face-to-face decisions possible at the 150 scale, as in the Alpine assemblies (F15); the sector
  above it needs representatives.
- **Size places, not people.** A person's own 150 is personal and varies (F6); building
  neighbourhoods of 150 gives that layer somewhere to meet, but no game rule should cap how many
  friends a character can have, and the layers should not be assumed to follow walls (F6, F16).
- **Above 1,500 there is no personal layer.** The ship's larger units (Sector 1 at about 9,400
  people in the master plan, and the operator's 2026-11-07 targets of 1 and 10 billion) are
  institutions built from these blocks. The ladder belongs in a data file with its ratio, so that it
  repeats to any scale (infinite-of-x). The word "sector" now names both the master plan's
  9,400-person drum section and a 500-person community; which word goes with which size is the
  operator's call.
- The 500 layer rests on thinner evidence than 150 (first row of the unknowns table). If a later
  research pass finds it does not hold, the sector cap moves to the nearest supported size, three
  neighbourhoods of about 150, which is where these numbers already land.

This is a reading of public research, not sociological or anthropological advice; nobody who wrote
it is a social scientist.
