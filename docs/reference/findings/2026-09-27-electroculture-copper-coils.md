# Do copper coils, applied electricity or lightning make crops grow better?

**Research date: 2026-09-27.** Every source below was read on 2026-09-27, and the
research was cut short that day by the session's usage limit, so some planned
sources were not reached (listed at the end). This is a reading of public sources,
done so a decision about the game's crop simulation can rest on what the evidence
says. **It is not agronomic, electrical or safety advice**, and nobody who wrote it
is a plant physiologist or an electrical engineer.

**The question.** Do "electroculture" copper coils and antennas, or deliberately
applied electricity, or lightning, measurably increase crop growth or yield, and if
so by how much and under what conditions?

Prepared after the operator said (2026-09-27): "I have seen that some farmers use
copper coils to increase growth but, they either have to power it manually or are
limited to lightning strikes (an Earth only thing for the most part.)" Nothing about
electroculture exists in the game's code on this date; see "What this means for
the game" at the end.

---

## Short answer

- **Passive copper coils and antennas (the product sold today): no controlled
  study found shows a benefit, and the one controlled, peer-reviewed test found
  none.** Chier et al. (2025, PLOS One) grew four vegetables with and without
  copper-wrapped dowels, with a buried-copper control, and found "no consistent
  evidence" of better growth or yield. They measured 2 millivolts entering the soil
  from a rod. Two extension specialists at Washington State University call the
  claims unsupported. Several specific "trial" figures circulating online could not
  be traced to any source at all (A4).
- **Powered electroculture of 1880 to 1936 was a real research programme with
  inconsistent results, and it was abandoned for that reason.** Lemström claimed
  at least 45%. Blackman's British field trials averaged 14% (he estimated 22% from
  the ones he trusted), but the U.S. Department of Agriculture's eight-year test
  under a 50,000-volt overhead network found no increase outside experimental
  error, and Blackman's own pot trials came out 26 up, 21 down, averaging 1%. The
  UK Electro-Culture Committee (1918 to 1936) closed saying the effect could not be
  relied on to recur and "the practical results should be so disappointing".
- **Modern applied-electricity research does report effects, mostly at the
  seed and seedling stage, in the laboratory.** Examples: barley seedlings about
  50% heavier after 15 days on an electrically polarised hydroponic scaffold (0.5 V,
  PNAS 2023); pea yield about 18% higher under a wind-and-rain-powered
  high-voltage field (Nature Food 2022, abstract only); germination and seedling
  gains of roughly 10 to 40% from brief high-voltage seed treatments. None read was
  a replicated whole-season field trial, and all of them use a powered source or
  kilovolt fields, nothing like a copper coil.
- **Lightning adds nitrogen, but very little.** The best estimate of all
  lightning on Earth is about 5 (2 to 8) million tonnes of nitrogen a year. Spread
  evenly over Earth's land, that is roughly 0.3 kg of nitrogen per hectare per year
  (our arithmetic), about a thirtieth of one person-day of urine per 10 square
  metres of garden per year, using the game's own urine figure. It is a chemical
  contribution (nitrate in rain), not electrical stimulation of plants.
- **Safety was only partly researched.** The sources note that the kilovolt
  set-ups need a high-voltage source "that has safety considerations" and that
  copper at high concentration harms plants. Powered DIY coils and electric fences
  were not researched before the session ended (see "Still unknown").

## Findings

### A. Passive copper coils and antennas, as sold today

**A1. Chier, Oakey, Budny and Lemoine (2025), the controlled test.** "Passive
electroculture using copper rods does not improve yield in home container
vegetable gardening", *PLOS One* 20(8): e0329615. Published 2025-08-07. DOI
[10.1371/journal.pone.0329615](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0329615).
Marquette University. Full text read (the PLOS PDF).

The design:

> "a control group with no added copper, exposed copper wire, and buried copper
> wire (n=10 plants per treatment, per species)."

> "The exposed copper wire treatment consisted of a 40cm dowel rod coiled with
> copper wire and inserted 10cm into the soil."

Species: mustard greens, kale, beets, turnips, in one-gallon pots under LED grow
lights, eight weeks, with height, leaf greenness, photosynthesis, water use and
dry biomass measured.

The result:

> "We found no consistent evidence that passive electroculture is beneficial to
> crop growth or yield in container gardens."

Buried copper (the control for copper acting as a fertiliser) did give beets and
turnips more top growth, and turnips more root, but exposed copper, with a similar
amount of copper in the soil, did not, so the authors attribute it to chance:

> "it is therefore likely that this pattern is simply a result of small sample
> sizes."

The electrical measurement:

> "The commonly accepted differential is 100 V per meter, such that the voltage
> differential between the soil and the top of a 40cm copper rod is only 4 V."

> "found that copper rods placed randomly around the room transmit only 2
> millivolts into the soil. When placed within 4cm of a fluorescent light,
> transmittance increased to a maximum of 20 millivolts."

On copper as a nutrient or a harm:

> "At high concentrations, copper becomes photo-inhibitory and forms the base
> metal of many herbicides."

> "Copper likely does not leach from the metal rod fast enough to infiltrate soils
> and plant roots."

Conclusion: "We therefore suggest that both the production and purchase of such
products would waste both financial and natural resources." Funding: "US National
Science Foundation DEB 1941390. The funders took no part in this study." The authors
declare no competing interests.

Limits (our observation, not the authors'): ten plants per group, one room, eight
weeks, containers rather than open ground, and it was indoors, where a building
screens much of the outdoor atmospheric field that proponents say the coil
collects. It tests the product as a container gardener would use it. It is one
study; no replication was found.

**A2. Washington State University Extension, Yakima County (2024).** Sheehan L,
"Electroculture", a Master Gardener column, dated 2024-07-20.
[extension.wsu.edu](https://extension.wsu.edu/yakima/2024/07/20/electroculture/);
[PDF](https://s3.wp.wsu.edu/uploads/sites/2083/2024/07/24-07-20-Electroculture.pdf).
Full text read. It relays WSU Extension Specialist Linda Chalker-Scott:

> "According to Chalker-Scott, none of these promises are backed with any reliable
> scientific evidence."

> "a coiled copper wire inserted into garden soil is not going to bring electricity
> to the soil. Even if it did, the effect on plant growth is purely conjecture."

An extension column, not a study. Its lightning claim is examined under D3.

**A3. Chalker-Scott (2023), Garden Professors blog.** "Electroculture:
rediscovered science or same old CRAP?", posted 2023-09-23. Live page blocked by a
bot check; read from the [Wayback Machine copy of 2026-05-19](http://web.archive.org/web/20260519192221/https://gardenprofessors.com/electroculture-rediscovered-science-or-same-old-crap/).

> "I did a search of the scientific literature through AGRICOLA, CABI, and Web of
> Science/BIOSIS. There were zero publications after 1968."

> "Worldwide, scientists consider electroculture to be a pseudoscience,
> particularly because it does not propose any plausible scientific mechanism to
> explain how electricity would stimulate plant growth."

**Where the sources disagree.** The "zero publications after 1968" search
evidently used the word electroculture: section C below lists peer-reviewed plant
science on applied electric fields from 2011 to 2025, including a 2018 review in
*Scientia Horticulturae* and a 2023 review in *Horticulture, Environment, and
Biotechnology* that uses the word "electro-culture". In our judgement her verdict
holds for the passive coil products she is writing about, and does not hold as a
statement that no modern research on electricity and plants exists. She is a
horticulture professor writing a public blog; weigh it as expert opinion, not as a
study.

**A4. Specific "trial results" that could not be traced.** A web page
("Electroculture Gardening: What Science Says (Not Hype)",
[lifetips.alibaba.com](https://lifetips.alibaba.com/plant-care/electroculture-gardening),
undated, no author) states that "Researchers at the University of Copenhagen tested

(2018–2020)", that "In a 2022 citizen-science project coordinated by the Northeast

and cites "A 2021 study in Plant and Soil". The page gives no citation, link, author
or date for any of them, and web searches for each returned only that page or pages
repeating it. **Not used, and not to be quoted as evidence.** Their conclusions
happen to agree with A1, which is exactly why they are tempting to cite; that is not
a reason to trust them. They look like machine-written text. Listed here so the next
session does not rediscover them and treat them as real.

### B. The older electroculture research, and why it faded

**B1. U.S. Department of Agriculture (1926), the American test and its review of
everyone else's.** Briggs LJ, Campbell AB, Heald RH, Flint LH, "Electroculture",
*USDA Department Bulletin No. 1379*, dated January 1926. Public domain; full OCR text
read from the [Internet Archive scan](https://archive.org/details/electroculture1379brig)
(NAL copy).

The natural field that a passive rod would sit in:

> "the magnitude of the vertical gradient is of the order of 100 volts per meter,
> though it is continually varying."

> "during stormy weather frequently attaining values of 10,000 volts per meter, 100
> times the normal gradient."

(The bulletin also gives the natural air-to-earth current per square metre; its
exponent is illegible in the OCR text read, so it is not reproduced here.)

What the USDA powered, and what it found over eight years of field trials at
Arlington, Virginia, on wheat, rye, oats, corn, soybeans and cowpeas:

> "the standard height of the network was 5 meters, and the potential of the
> network was approximately 50,000 volts."

> "These experiments do not show any well-defined increase in yield due to
> electrical treatment. There is an indication of a slight increase in the yield
> of wheat when grown under a positively charged network, but the observed
> increase is well within the experimental error of field trials."

The bulletin notes it also measured each plot's productivity without treatment,
"a precaution which has not been generally observed by other investigators".

Lemström's own summary, as the bulletin quotes it (Finland, from the 1880s; his
book *Electricity in Agriculture and Horticulture*, 1904):

> "The real increase due to electrical treatment has not yet been exactly
> determined for the different plants, but we are approaching its smallest value
> by fixing it at 45 per cent."

> "On poor soil it is so small as to be scarcely perceptible."

> "Electric treatment when accompanied by hot sunshine is damaging to most
> vegetables, probably to all"

The bulletin's analysis of Blackman's British field trials:

> "Of 33 trials shown in Table 28, 21 indicate an increase for treated areas,
> whereas 12 indicate a decrease."

> "The treated areas return a yield represented by the range 76 to 184 when the
> untreated areas return a yield represented by 100 and give an average increase
> of 14 per cent."

> "Blackman estimates the more reliable experiments as indicative of an average
> increase in yield of about 22 per cent."

It then points out that the treated and control plots' normal productivity was
mostly unknown and that the soil was visibly uneven between them. Of Blackman's pot
trials:

> "Making a comparison, 26 trials out of 47 give positive results, while 21 give
> negative results."

> "the pot-culture trials in their entirety thus furnish no definite evidence of a
> response to the electrical treatment."

Its overall verdict on the literature from the 1740s on: it "does not lend
assurance of great progress."

**B2. The UK Electro-Culture Committee (1918 to 1936), through the one scholarly
account found.** Kinahan D, "Struggling to Take Root: The Work of the
Electro-Culture Committee of the Ministry of Agriculture and Fisheries Between 1918
and 1936 and its Fight for Acceptance", *Reinvention: a Journal of Undergraduate
Research* 2(1), 2009, University of Warwick. The live page is now password
protected; read from the [Wayback Machine copy of 2024-06-17](http://web.archive.org/web/20240617140627/https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/cross_fac/iatl/student-research/reinvention/archive/volume2issue1/kinahan/).
Full text read. Kinahan worked from the Committee's eighteen Interim Reports and the
Ministry files at the National Archives (MAF 33). **The Committee's reports
themselves were not read**; the quotation below is the Final Report as Kinahan
reproduces it, cited by him as "(Snell, J. et al. 1937 3-4)":

> "In spite of the failures of recent years, the field results obtained some years
> ago and the earlier pot culture results would seem to have established the fact
> that the Electro-Culture effect is a real one. It would seem, however, to be of
> little advantage to continue the work either on economic or on scientific
> grounds. Increases of 20 per cent can hardly be considered economic even if
> obtained in most years; experiments, however, demonstrate that the regular
> occurrence of the effect cannot be expected. On the scientific side the erratic
> occurrence of the phenomena to be investigated renders their full study
> impossible… The Committee regret that after so exhaustive a study of this matter
> the practical results should be so disappointing"

The run of results, per Kinahan: by 1920, "twelve experiments, eleven of which found
positive increases in yield, and eight of these were between 30% and 50%"; then wet
and dry seasons ruined 1920 to 1922; "in 1926, 73% of experiments showed a
decrease"; "1932-35 saw no significant increases whatsoever". On the American
result: the USDA study "yielded no positive results whatsoever", and Kinahan reports
two historians arguing it contributed to the Committee's closure.

**Where the sources disagree.** Kinahan's own conclusion is more favourable than
the Committee's:

> "It is concluded that the Electro-Culture effect is a real one, and that the
> Committee was axed as a result of economic pressures, not because the idea was
> wrong."

He also reports a 2006 hypothesis by Andrew Goldsworthy that plants respond to the
strong field under an approaching thunderstorm as a signal of coming rain, so that
electroculture would only work with water supplied soon after, which would explain
some failures. That hypothesis was not read in its original form. Our judgement: the
Committee's words ("the regular occurrence of the effect cannot be expected") and
the USDA's null result are the primary record; Kinahan's reading is a 2009
undergraduate history paper arguing the case should be reopened, worth knowing,
not a new experiment.

**B3. The "not for publication" detail.** Marsh D, "Electroculture", *The Garden
History Blog*, 2021-09-04,
[thegardenhistory.blog](https://thegardenhistory.blog/2021/09/04/electroculture/),
read in full, relays Kinahan's finding that from 1922 the Committee's reports were
marked not for publication, with two copies printed. Kinahan's own paper says the
reports were published between 1918 and 1936; the two statements were not
reconciled here. The blog's quotation of the 1936 conclusion matches B2.

**Why it faded, as the sources give it:** results that would not repeat from season
to season (B1, B2); the cost of high-voltage installations against gains the
Committee called uneconomic even at 20% (B2); a large American null result (B1, B2);
and, per Chalker-Scott (A3, not otherwise checked), the arrival of commercial
fertilisers and better plant physiology.

### C. Modern research on applied electricity, fields and seed treatment

**C1. Oikonomou et al. (2023), low-voltage stimulation of barley seedlings.**
"eSoil: A low-power bioelectronic growth scaffold that enhances crop seedling
growth", *PNAS* 121(2), published 2023-12-26. DOI
[10.1073/pnas.2304135120](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2304135120). Linköping
University and SLU. Full text read ([SLU open copy](https://pub.epsilon.slu.se/33441/1/oikonomou-v-k-et-al-20240425.pdf)).

> "Simply by polarizing the eSoil, seedling growth is accelerated resulting in
> increase of dry weight on average by 50% after 15 d of growth."

The set-up, from the methods: a conductive polymer and cellulose scaffold in
hydroponics, one seed per scaffold, +0.5 V applied from day 5 to day 10, harvest at
day 15; control dry weight 0.074 g against 0.111 g stimulated (21 and 23 plants
across five independent experiments). The effect depended on polarity:

> "we applied -0.5 V for 5 d mirroring the protocol we used previously. In this
> case, growth of the two treatments was not significantly different"

Its own limit:

> "However, our work focused on seedlings, and therefore, more studies are required
> to show whether growth enhancement by stimulation treatment at the early stages
> of growth impacts the whole growth cycle of plants."

On the older kilovolt approach:

> "In this case, a high-voltage source is required that has safety considerations,
> and it is energy demanding while the physics of the system becomes more
> complicated."

**C2. Li et al. (2022), a wind-and-rain-powered field over peas.** "Stimulation of
ambient energy generated electric field on crop plant growth", *Nature Food*
3:133-142, published 2022-01-13. DOI
[10.1038/s43016-021-00449-9](https://doi.org/10.1038/s43016-021-00449-9). Abstract
only (via Europe PMC); the full text is paywalled.

> "Treated by the self-generated high-voltage electric field, the system can
> increase pea seeds germination speed by ~26.3% and pea yield by ~17.9%."

Plot size, replication, season and where the yield was measured were not read. No
replication by another group was found. It is the closest thing found to a modern
field yield result, and it uses a powered high-voltage source (triboelectric
generators), not a passive coil.

**C3. Seed treatments with high-voltage fields.** All abstract only, via Europe
PMC:

- Chili pepper, *Scientific Reports* 14, 2024-03-27, DOI
  [10.1038/s41598-024-57978-z](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-57978-z): best
  setting "the output voltage of 12.08 kV and polarisation time of 30.32 s", after
  which "seed germination potential increased by 9.09%, germination rate increased
  by 20.45%".
- Maize under chilling, *Scientific Reports* 15, 2025-02-01, DOI
  [10.1038/s41598-025-88346-0](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-025-88346-0): "At an
  HVEF strength of 1.6 kV/cm, all indices in the low-temperature group
  significantly improved relative to the control (P < 0.01)", and "Root length,
  shoot length, and dry weight of maize seedlings rose by 20.3%, 19.2%, and 16.6%."
- Maize, *Plant Signaling & Behavior*, 2019-10-25, DOI
  [10.1080/15592324.2019.1681101](https://doi.org/10.1080/15592324.2019.1681101):
  "electrical stimulation with optimal intensity boosted germination rate and
  seedling growth"; the abstract gives no percentages.

These are germination and seedling outcomes over days, not harvests. "Optimal"
settings were chosen from the same experiments that measured them.

**C4. Current through a hydroponic root zone: kale (2020).** "Electric stimulation
promotes growth, mineral uptake, and antioxidant accumulation in kale (Brassica
oleracea var. acephala)", *Bioelectrochemistry*, online 2020-12-21. DOI
[10.1016/j.bioelechem.2020.107727](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.bioelechem.2020.107727).
Abstract only.

> "Three levels of electric current (10, 50, and 100 mA) were applied to the
> nutrient solution of hydroponically grown plants for three weeks."

> "Kale plants subjected to the electric fields, particularly 50 mA, had higher
> fresh and dry weights than the control."

> "The absence of an electric field in a Faraday cage caused a significant decrease
> in shoot and root growth compared with the natural electric field (control)."

The last line is the only source found suggesting the natural atmospheric field
itself matters to growth; it is one hydroponic study, abstract only, and was not
checked further.

**C5. Current applied to harvested tomatoes (2011).** "Effects of
direct-electric-current on secondary plant compounds and antioxidant activity in
harvested tomato fruits", *Food Chemistry*, 2011. DOI
[10.1016/j.foodchem.2010.10.092](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.foodchem.2010.10.092).
Abstract only. "Different intensities of DC (100–500mA) with varied application
times (15–60min) were applied during postharvest", raising lycopene and phenols by
and reports lycopene, beta-carotene, total phenols and antioxidant activity at between about 114% and 140% of the untreated level, depending on the setting and the wait before measuring. This is fruit quality after harvest, not
growth or yield.

**C6. Reviews.** Lee S and Oh M-M, "Electric field: a new environmental factor for
controlling plant growth and development in agriculture", *Horticulture,
Environment, and Biotechnology* 64:955, online 2023-05-31. DOI
[10.1007/s13580-023-00525-y](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13580-023-00525-y). Abstract
only:

> "The application of electricity to plant cultivation has a long and contentious
> history."

Dannehl D, "Effects of electricity on plant responses", *Scientia Horticulturae*
234:382-392 (2018), DOI
[10.1016/j.scienta.2018.02.007](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.scienta.2018.02.007): the
publisher withholds the abstract from every service tried and the page returned
HTTP 403, so it was **not read**. A1 cites it for the range of voltages used in
electroculture studies, 6 to 1200 V with most over 200 V (A1's words restated, as its
dash did not survive extraction).

**C7. China's greenhouse programme (press only).** Chen S, "China is making its
vegetables grow bigger, faster and stronger ... using electricity", *South China
Morning Post*, 2018-09-16
([scmp.com](https://www.scmp.com/news/china/science/article/2164365/electric-plants-powering-chinas-new-agricultural-revolution)).
Only the opening loaded:

> "vegetable greenhouse farms with a combined area of more than 3,600 hectares
> (8,895 acres) have been taking part in an “electro culture” programme funded by
> the Chinese government."

The yield figures in the article body and any peer-reviewed report behind them were
not read. Treat as unverified.

**What effect sizes hold up, in our judgement.** Laboratory and seedling-stage
effects of roughly 10 to 50% are reported repeatedly under powered stimulation,
from millivolt-scale root-zone polarisation to kilovolt seed and air fields. What
has not held up, from 1926 to now in the sources read, is a whole-season field yield
gain that repeats from year to year; the one modern field-yield figure found (C2,
~18% peas) is abstract-only and unreplicated. No source read shows a passive coil
producing any of these conditions.

### D. Lightning and soil nitrogen

**D1. Schumann and Huntrieser (2007), the standard review of how much nitrogen
lightning fixes.** "The global lightning-induced nitrogen oxides source",
*Atmospheric Chemistry and Physics* 7:3823-3907, 2007.
[acp.copernicus.org](https://acp.copernicus.org/articles/7/3823/2007/). Abstract read
on the journal's page (subscripts and superscripts flattened by extraction).

> "a "typical" thunderstorm flash produces 15 (2–40)×10 25 NO molecules per flash,
> equivalent to 250 mol NO x or 3.5 kg of N mass per flash with uncertainty factor
> from 0.13 to 2.7."

> "the best estimate of the annual global LNO x nitrogen mass source and its
> uncertainty range is (5±3) Tg a −1 in this study."

That is 5 teragrams (5 million tonnes) of nitrogen a year, range 2 to 8, for the
whole planet.

**D2. What that means per hectare (our arithmetic, with our assumptions).** Earth's
surface is about 51 billion hectares and its land about 15 billion. Spreading 5
million tonnes evenly over the whole surface gives about 0.1 kg of nitrogen per
hectare per year; putting all of it on land, an overestimate, gives about 0.34
(range 0.13 to 0.54 for the 2 to 8 Tg range). 0.34 kg per hectare is 0.034 g per
square metre, so a 10 square metre garden would receive about 0.34 g of lightning
nitrogen a year. The game's own soil model (`src/systems/farming/soil.rs`) gives one
stored person-day of urine as 10.9 g of nitrogen: lightning's yearly share for that
garden is about a thirtieth of one day's urine. Assumptions: even spreading (real
deposition is uneven and was not researched), and that lightning nitrogen reaches the
ground at all rather than being lost or transformed in the air (not researched). The
per-flash 3.5 kg is made in the air along the lightning channel; whether any
meaningful part lands where the bolt strikes was not researched and should not be
assumed.

**D3. The extension claim about storms.** Both A2 and A3 carry the same sentence:

> "This was undoubtedly responsible for the reported improvement in plant growth
> after electrical storms."

Neither gives a quantity. Set against D2, lightning nitrogen is real but far too
small to explain a visible greening after one storm; the rain itself is a more
obvious candidate. That is our judgement; no source read tested it.

**D4. Clarifying the operator's framing.** The historical powered systems (B1, B2)
charged overhead wires to tens of thousands of volts, and the natural field reaches
about 10,000 V/m under storms (B1). A lightning strike is not a way of "powering" a
coil: what lightning demonstrably contributes to fertility is the nitrate of D1,
which falls everywhere, strike or no strike. What a direct strike does to plants in a
field was not researched.

### E. Safety

What was found:

- The kilovolt approaches need "a high-voltage source ... that has safety
  considerations" (C1). The USDA ran its network at about 50,000 V (B1).
- Copper at high concentration is "photo-inhibitory" and used in herbicides (A1);
  a solid rod is unlikely to release enough to matter (A1).
- Lemström warned that treatment "accompanied by hot sunshine is damaging to most
  vegetables, probably to all" (B1): the historical method could harm crops.

What was **not researched** before the session ended: mains-powered DIY coils and
their electrocution risk, electric-fence energiser standards and incident records,
and whether a metal garden antenna changes lightning risk. No claim is made here
either way.

## What is still unknown, and what would settle it

1. **Whether any passive coil works outdoors, in open ground, over a full season.**
   A1 was indoors, in pots, for eight weeks. What would settle it: a randomised,
   replicated outdoor trial (several plots per treatment, sham rods of wood or
   plastic, yield as the endpoint), the kind a university extension farm runs in
   a season or two.
2. **Whether powered stimulation raises yield over a whole crop cycle, repeatably,
   in the field.** C1 stops at 15 days; C2 is abstract only. Settled by reading C2's
   full text (library access or purchase) and by any independent replication.
3. **The Committee's own reports.** The National Archives MAF 33 files would show
   the raw numbers behind B2. Cost: a visit to Kew or a paid copy.
4. **Dannehl (2018) and the 2024 *Scientia Horticulturae* review** ("Effects,
   physiological response and mechanism of plant under electric field
   application", DOI 10.1016/j.scienta.2024.112992) were not read; either may hold
   a quantitative synthesis.
5. **Where lightning nitrogen deposits,** and how that compares to a farm's other
   nitrogen inputs. A deposition source (for example national deposition networks)
   would settle it.
6. **Safety** (section E, the unresearched list).
7. **The Chinese greenhouse programme's data** (C7): a peer-reviewed yield report,
   if one exists.

## What this means for the game

### Certain

- **The game has no electroculture on this date.** `src/systems/farming/soil.rs`
  lists the household's nitrogen sources as compost, urine and legume fixation,
  and has no atmospheric deposition input. `src/systems/disasters.rs` has a
  `Lightning` disaster type that deals electric damage; it does not touch soil.
- **No source read shows a passive copper coil or antenna improving growth or
  yield,** and the one controlled test found none (A1).
- **Powered electroculture's field yield record is inconsistent** (B1, B2), and the
  body that studied it longest closed saying the effect could not be relied on.
- **Lab and seedling effects of powered stimulation are reported** (C1 to C4), with
  real power sources, from 0.5 V at the roots to kilovolt fields.
- **Lightning's nitrogen is real and tiny,** of order 0.1 to 0.3 kg per hectare
  per year averaged (D1, D2), and it arrives as nitrate, not as electricity.

### Judgement call: options, not a decision

The choice is the operator's. Listed, not ranked.

**1. Model no effect.** Copper coils can exist as an item, and do nothing to crops.
- For: matches the only controlled test (A1) and the extension view (A2, A3); keeps
  the rule that the farm teaches real growing.
- Against: players who have seen the videos may expect something; an in-world note
  or Library entry explaining why (the "say what we will not build, and why" rule)
  would answer that.

**2. Model only lightning nitrogen on Earth-like worlds.** A small atmospheric
nitrate input, scaled to D1 and D2.
- For: true, and it teaches where natural nitrogen comes from.
- Against: at 0.1 to 0.3 kg per hectare per year it is far below anything a player
  could notice next to compost or urine; it may cost more code than it teaches.
  Deposition geography was not researched, and the game's garden is largely
  indoors or in a station, where it would be zero.

**3. Model powered stimulation as an experimental technology, with its caveats.**
For example a seedling-stage boost behind a powered device, with an honest in-game
note that field results have not repeated.
- For: some real, recent lab results support a seedling effect (C1, C3), and the
  project's dual-mode rule allows a realism mode that carries the caveat.
- Against: turning a 15-day seedling result into a whole-crop yield bonus would
  present an unreplicated finding as settled; the numbers to model a full season
  do not exist in the sources read.

**4. Combine 1 and 2 with a Library page** on the history (Lemström, Blackman, the
USDA and the Committee), the physics (2 mV into the soil), and what lightning
actually adds. Teaches the most with no invented mechanic.
