# May a Washington household heat with a portable kerosene or propane heater indoors?

**Research date: 4 October 2026.** Everything below was read on that day. If you
are reading this later, check the date of each source against anything that has
changed since: Washington adopts a new edition of the fire code every few years.

This is a reading of public sources by an AI working on the project, not legal
advice. Nobody who wrote it is a lawyer or a fire official. The people who decide
how the code applies to a particular home are the local fire marshal and building
official (for Silverdale, Central Kitsap Fire & Rescue and Kitsap County).

## The question

The Library guide "Heating a Home Safely" (docs/user/skills/heating_a_home_safely.md)
used to tell readers to "ask" whether a portable unvented heater is allowed. Its
fact check (2026-10-04) found a state rule behind that question. What does
Washington's fire code say about portable unvented fuel-fired heaters (kerosene,
portable propane) inside homes, and where?

## Short answer

Washington adopts the 2021 International Fire Code and does not change its rule
on portable unvented heaters. Under section 605.5 of that code:

- Portable unvented fuel-fired heating equipment is prohibited in assembly,
  educational and institutional buildings, ambulatory care facilities and all the
  residential groups R-1 to R-4 (which include apartments and houses).
- The one household exception: a portable unvented heater listed to UL 647 may be
  used in a one- or two-family dwelling, operated and maintained as its maker
  instructs. UL 647 is the standard for unvented kerosene-fired room heaters and
  portable heaters.
- Section 605.5.1: unvented fuel-fired heating equipment may never be placed in,
  or take its combustion air from, a bedroom, a bathroom, a toilet room or a
  storage closet.

Our reading: a listed kerosene heater may be used in a house or duplex, but never
in a bedroom, bathroom or closet; in an apartment building it is not allowed. A
portable propane heater is not a UL 647 product, so the household exception does
not appear to cover using one indoors; ask the fire marshal before relying on
anything else.

## Findings

**1. Washington adopts the 2021 International Fire Code.** WAC 51-54A-003,
"International Fire Code", https://app.leg.wa.gov/WAC/default.aspx?cite=51-54A-003
(WSR 22-13-093, 23-12-107 and 23-20-027, effective 3/15/24): "The 2021 edition of
the International Fire Code, published by the International Code Council is hereby
adopted by reference with the following additions, deletions, and exceptions."

**2. It took effect statewide on 15 March 2024.** WAC 51-54A-008,
"Implementation", https://app.leg.wa.gov/WAC/default.aspx?cite=51-54A-008 (WSR
23-23-106, filed 11/15/23): "The International Fire Code adopted by chapter
51-54A WAC shall become effective in all counties and cities of this state on
March 15, 2024."

**3. Washington's only change to section 605 is about fuel oil tanks.** WAC
51-54A-0605, "Fuel fired appliances",
https://app.leg.wa.gov/WAC/default.aspx?cite=51-54A-0605 (same filings, effective
3/15/24), amends sections 605.4.1.1, 605.4.2.1 and 605.4.2.2 only; it begins
"605.4.1.1 Approval. Outdoor fuel oil storage tanks shall be in accordance with
UL 142, UL 142A, or UL 2085." Section 605.5 is not amended.

**4. The rule itself, 2021 International Fire Code sections 605.5 and 605.5.1.**
codes.iccsafe.org refused scripted access, so the text was read in the City of
Seattle's published 2021 Seattle Fire Code, chapter 6,
https://www.seattle.gov/documents/Departments/SDCI/Codes/SeattleFireCode/2021SFCChapter6.pdf.
That edition prints the model code with Washington's amendments marked [W] and
Seattle's marked [S]; neither 605.5 nor 605.5.1 carries a mark, so the text is the
model code as Washington adopted it. Section 605.5 makes such heaters
"prohibited in occupancies in Groups A, E, I, R-1, R-2, R-3 and R-4", plus
ambulatory care facilities; its first exception permits heaters listed to UL 647
in one- and two-family dwellings, operated and maintained per the manufacturer's
instructions; its second covers portable outdoor gas-fired heaters (605.5.2).
Section 605.5.1 bars unvented fuel-fired heating equipment from sleeping rooms,
bathrooms, toilet rooms and storage closets, including taking combustion air from
them. (The code is copyrighted by the International Code Council; it is restated
here apart from the one short phrase quoted.)

**5. The occupancy groups.** The same edition's chapter 2,
https://www.seattle.gov/documents/Departments/SDCI/Codes/SeattleFireCode/2021SFCChapter2.pdf:
group A is assembly, E educational, I institutional, R residential. Its "DWELLING"
definition (marked [A] only, so not a Seattle change) is a building that contains
one or two dwelling units. Its Residential Group R-2 entry covers sleeping units or
more than two dwelling units (apartments), and its R-3 entry includes buildings of
no more than two dwelling units. Those two group entries carry Seattle's [S] mark,
so Seattle has amended them; the model code's own wording for them, which is what
applies in Kitsap County, was not read here.

**6. What UL 647 covers.** UL Standards & Engagement's catalogue,
https://www.shopulstandards.com/ProductDetail.aspx?productid=UL647: UL 647 is the
"Standard for Unvented Kerosene-Fired Room Heaters and Portable Heaters", edition
2, published 3 May 1993. Only the title was read, not the standard.

**7. The earlier edition was broader.** The 2018 International Fire Code (section
603.4, as quoted by the Fairfax County Fire Marshal, a secondary source) excepted
listed and approved unvented heaters in one- and two-family dwellings generally.
The 2021 edition names UL 647. The fact check's first suggested wording came from
the 2018 text, which is why the guide uses the 2021 text instead.

## What is still unknown

- Whether Kitsap County or Central Kitsap Fire & Rescue has a local amendment to
  section 605.5. None was found; the county's code pages were not searched for one.
- Whether any portable propane heater for indoor use is listed to UL 647 (the
  standard's title says kerosene) or to another standard the fire marshal accepts.
- How the fire code is enforced inside an owner-occupied house in practice.
