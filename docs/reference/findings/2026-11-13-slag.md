# What is slag from asteroid ore, how much of it is there, and what can a ship use it for?

**Research date: 2026-11-13.** Every source below was read on 2026-11-13 by three research helpers
(their full notes, 88 entries with every quotation, are in
[research/2026-11-13-slag/](../research/2026-11-13-slag/README.md)). This is a reading of public sources so
the game's ship handles its mining waste by real numbers. **It is not engineering advice**, and
nobody who wrote it is a metallurgist, a materials engineer or an aerospace engineer. Anyone
building with slag should go to the primary sources and to a professional.

How the quotations were checked: those marked (pdf) were copied from text extracted from the
source's PDF and are exact; those marked (page) came through a web page reader and were not
compared byte for byte with the page, so check one by eye before quoting it publicly.

## The question

When the fleet's drones bring home stony, carbonaceous and metallic asteroid ore and the smelter
takes the metal out, what is left, how much is left per tonne of metal, and what can a spaceship
do with it?

Asked by the operator on 2026-11-13, verbatim: "We'll have to figure out the various ways we can
use the slag. That way we can justify it as an infinite resource sink." The design that uses the
answers is [docs/design/slag.md](../../design/slag.md).

## Short answer

- **What it is.** Slag is the rock left after metal is melted out of ore; crushed rock that was
  never melted is called tailings (F5). Asteroid slag made without added lime is a **magnesium
  silicate**, about 55% silica and 35% magnesia, close to an ultramafic rock, unlike Earth's
  blast-furnace slag, which is lime-rich because limestone is added (computed from F1, compared
  with F7).
- **How much.** Per tonne of iron-group metal: a stony asteroid leaves about 2.3 to 3.5 t of slag
  if the iron in its minerals is reduced too, or 4.9 to 24 t of residue if only its metal grains are
  taken; a carbonaceous asteroid about 2.2 to 2.4 t (after its water and carbon leave as gas); a
  metallic asteroid only 10 to 15 kg (computed from F1 to F4). Earth's blast furnace leaves 0.25 to
  0.30 t (F6) because its ore is 60 to 66% iron.
- **The bigger waste stream.** Water baked out of carbonaceous rock leaves about 4 to 19 t of dry
  rock per tonne of water (computed from F1 and F2), far more than any smelter.
- **The uses that never fill up** are two: new shielding for new habitat, which NASA's 1975 study
  found swallows more slag than the processing plant makes (F22), and reaction mass thrown
  overboard by a mass driver (F21). Everything else (soil, balance weights, a heat store,
  insulation, filters, binders) is a one-time fill or saturates.
- **Rock outside the water.** Regolith-like rock needs about 1.5 to 2.1 times water's mass for the
  same cosmic-ray dose (the [active-shielding finding](2026-11-13-active-shielding.md)); a 15 cm
  layer of rock-like material stops the gram-class meteoroids a square kilometre of hull meets
  about once a decade (F20).
- **What slag cannot be.** Clear glass (it needs about 72% silica, 14% soda and almost no iron,
  F18); continuous basalt-type fibre without added silica (F16); Portland-blend slag cement without
  lime or magnesia added (F9, by arithmetic on F1).

## The findings

### What asteroid ore is made of

**F1. Chondrite compositions, element by element.** Lodders gives group means for CI, CM, H, L and
LL chondrites (the meteorites that stand for carbonaceous and stony asteroids). Iron: CI 18.6, CM
21.1, H 27.2, L 21.8, LL 19.8 wt%; carbon CI 4.13, CM 2.32 wt%; hydrogen CI 1.86, CM 1.15 wt%
(Table 1).
- Source: K. Lodders, "Relative Atomic Solar System Abundances, Mass Fractions, and Atomic Masses
  of the Elements and Their Isotopes, Composition of the Solar Photosphere, and Compositions of the
  Major Chondritic Meteorite Groups", Space Science Reviews 217:44, accepted manuscript
  https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10642789. Source date: accepted 24 March 2021.
- Quote (pdf): "Table 1 gives the elemental composition by mass of the major chondrite groups in
  parts per million (µg/g)." The values are table only.
- Computed from it (helper A, A2 and A17): the oxides left after all the iron is reduced, normalised,
  for H: SiO2 55.6, MgO 35.3, Al2O3 3.2, CaO 2.6, Na2O 1.3 wt%; CI and CM nearly the same (SiO2
  about 53, MgO 36 to 37). CI holds up to 16.6 wt% water if all its hydrogen sits in water (an upper
  bound), CM 10.3.

**F2. Round numbers per asteroid type.** About 20% metal and 6% troilite in non-carbonaceous
asteroids; about 99% metal in metallic ones; a table of oxides per type after O'Leary et al. 1979
(C1: water 12%, free iron 0.1%; S: free iron 6 to 19%).
- Source: Shane D. Ross, "Near-Earth Asteroid Mining", Space Industry Report, Caltech,
  https://ross.aoe.vt.edu/papers/ross-asteroid-mining-2001.pdf. Source date: 14 December 2001.
- Quotes (pdf): "about 20% metallic iron-nickel alloy; about 6% of the ferrous sulphide mineral
  troilite" and "Iron meteorites, or metallic M-type asteroids, are even more metal-rich; about 99%
  metal."

**F3. Metal in stony meteorites by volume.** Equilibrated H, L and LL chondrites hold 8.4, 4.1 and
2 vol% metal (about 17, 8 and 4 wt% by density, computed).
- Source: P. Vernazza, B. Zanda, T. Nakamura, E. Scott, S. Russell, "The Formation and Evolution of
  Ordinary Chondrite Parent Bodies", Asteroids IV, arXiv:1611.08734,
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/1611.08734. Source date: 26 November 2016.
- Quote (pdf): "They are « Low » in total iron, « Low » in metal, hence the LL denomination." The
  numbers are table only (Table 1); the extracted rows were shifted and re-aligned by meaning, so
  check against the page.

**F4. Iron meteorites and stony-irons.** An average iron meteorite is Fe 90.6, Ni 7.9, Co 0.5 wt%
(Table 5); mesosiderites are about half metal.
- Source: J. A. Sanchez, V. Reddy, W. F. Bottke et al., "Physical Characterization of Metal-rich
  Near-Earth Asteroids 6178 (1986 DA) and 2016 ED85", arXiv:2109.13950,
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/2109.13950. Source date: 30 September 2021.
- Quotes (pdf): "Abundances corresponding to Fe, Ni, Co, and Cu in iron meteorites are from
  Buchwald (1975)." and "Mesosiderites are breccias composed of similar proportions of silicates
  and FeNi metal."
- Precious metals follow the metal, not the slag (helper A's arithmetic on F1 and F3: about 33 ppm
  of platinum-group metals and gold in H-chondrite metal, 72 in LL metal), consistent with Kargel
  1994's "1.2-5.3% Fe-Ni metal containing 50-220 ppm of precious metals" for LL chondrites (page,
  read on a mirror of the abstract, https://stafforini.com/works/kargel-1994-metalliferous-asteroids-potential/;
  JGR 99(E10):21129, 1994).

### What slag is, and how Earth uses it

**F5. Slag and tailings.**
- Source: H. G. van Oss, USGS 2017 Minerals Yearbook, "Slag: Iron and Steel" (advance release),
  https://d9-wret.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/assets/palladium/production/atoms/files/myb1-2017-fesla.pdf.
  Source date: May 2020. Quote (pdf): "Iron and steel (or ferrous) slags are silicate melts that
  result from the addition of slagging agents and fluxes".
- Source: US EPA, "TENORM: Copper Mining and Production Wastes",
  https://www.epa.gov/radiation/tenorm-copper-mining-and-production-wastes. Source date: last
  updated 29 September 2026. Quotes (page): slag is the "Byproduct left after a desired metal is
  thermally separated from its raw ore."; tailings are "The remaining portion of a metal-bearing ore
  after some or all of a metal, such as uranium, has been extracted."

**F6. Earth's ratio and Earth's uses.** A blast furnace makes 0.25 to 0.30 t of slag per tonne of
iron (up to 1.0 to 1.2 for poor ore). US sales in 2017: 16.0 Mt. Air-cooled blast-furnace slag went
47.6% to road bases, 21.4% to ready-mixed concrete, 13.2% to asphalt; granulated (glassy) slag
99.8% to cement; steel slag 40.8% to road bases (Table 3).
- Source: USGS 2017 Minerals Yearbook, as F5. Source date: May 2020.
- Quotes (pdf): "a blast furnace normally will produce about 0.25 to 0.30 metric ton (t) of slag
  per ton of crude or pig iron produced." and "About 80% of total slag sales in 2017 were air-cooled
  blast furnace slag and steel slag. Both slag types are mainly used as construction aggregates."

**F7. Earth's slag, and the fork that decides its use.** Blast-furnace slag (1985 survey means):
CaO 39, SiO2 36, Al2O3 10, MgO 12 wt% (Table 3-2). Quenched in water it becomes glass (the cement
grade); cooled slowly in beds it becomes crystalline rock (the aggregate grade). Air-cooled slag:
compacted unit weight up to 1,940 kg/m3, water absorption up to 6%. Steel slag carrying free lime
or magnesia swells "up to 10 percent or more" when it meets water.
- Source: FHWA, "User Guidelines for Waste and Byproduct Materials in Pavement Construction",
  FHWA-RD-97-148, chapters Blast Furnace Slag and Steel Slag,
  https://www.fhwa.dot.gov/publications/research/infrastructure/structures/97148/bfs1.cfm and
  .../ssa1.cfm. Source date: 1997 (pages last modified 2016).
- Quotes (page): "If the molten slag is cooled and solidified by rapid water quenching to a glassy
  state, little or no crystallization occurs." and "volume changes of up to 10 percent or more
  attributable to the hydration of calcium and magnesium oxides".

### Taking oxygen and metals out of slag

**F8. Molten oxide (regolith) electrolysis.** NASA's 2026 demonstration on 25 kg of simulant
measured 0.3 mol of oxygen per kWh (about 104 kWh per kg of oxygen, computed), the melt held above
1,700 °C. An MIT model of a mature plant: 14 kW makes 1,000 kg of oxygen a year (about 123 kWh/kg
run continuously; 56.5 kW makes 10,000 kg, about 50 kWh/kg). Current efficiency is 30 to 60% while
iron oxides are reduced and near 100% once they are gone, so slag that has already lost its iron
should cost less per kilogram (not measured). Lunar regolith is 40 to 45% oxygen by weight (ESA).
- Source: J. A. Toro Medina, A. Meier, M. Shah, E. Bell, D. Essumang, J. A. Olson, L. Sibille,
  "Large-scale Demonstration of Molten Regolith Electrolysis for Oxygen Production in a Relevant
  Environment", ICES 2026 poster, NTRS 20260006167,
  https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20260006167/downloads/MRE%20GCD%20ICES26_v3.pdf. Source
  date: 2026. Quote (pdf): "Oxygen Production Efficiency: 0.3 Mols O2/kW-hr".
- Source: S. S. Schreiner, MIT S.M. thesis, https://dspace.mit.edu/handle/1721.1/98589. Source
  date: 2015. Quote (page): "a 400 kg, 14 kW MRE-based ISRU system can produce 1,000 kg oxygen per
  year".
- Source: S. S. Schreiner, J. A. Hoffman, G. B. Sanders, K. A. Lee, "Integrated Modeling and
  Optimization of Lunar In-Situ Resource Utilization Systems", 2015 IEEE Aerospace Conference,
  https://sciences.ucf.edu/class/wp-content/uploads/sites/23/2017/01/Muscatello-Integrated-modeling-and-optimization-of-lunar-In-Situ-Resource-Utilization-systems-MRE.pdf.
  Source date: 2015. Quote (pdf): "can range from 30-60% for iron oxides and is near 100% for melts
  once the iron oxide has been depleted".

### Slag as a building material

**F9. The rule a slag must meet to go into cement.** EN 197-1, as quoted in a 2025 product
declaration: CaO + SiO2 + MgO above 66%, (CaO + MgO)/SiO2 above 1, glass content above 66%.
Asteroid slag from F1 has (CaO + MgO)/SiO2 of about 0.68 (computed), so it fails unless lime or
magnesia is added. Granulating one tonne: 446 MJ of non-renewable primary energy and 0.202 m3 of
fresh water net (table only).
- Source: Kiwa-Ecobility Experts, "Environmental Product Declaration: GBFS - Granulated
  Blast-Furnace Slag" (HKM, Duisburg), https://hkm.de/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/EPD-Kiwa-EE-191088-EN.pdf.
  Source date: issued 21 August 2025. Quote (pdf, subscripts lost in extraction): "(CaO + MgO) /
  SiO > 1".

**F10. Grinding glassy slag.** A vertical roller mill measured 28.54 kWh/t at 3,290 Blaine; the
whole grinding shop was estimated at 40 kWh/t.
- Source: CORDIS, EU project IN.-00026-94 (Lafarge pilot of a Loesche mill for slag),
  https://cordis.europa.eu/project/id/IN.-00026-94. Source date: tests March to April 1995.
- Quote (page): "so 28,54 kWh/t in total".

**F11. Slag cement and the alkali route.** Slag cement normally replaces 20 to 80% of Portland
cement (25 to 50% in paving and structures), so a Portland blend still needs limestone, which
asteroids lack. Alkali-activated slag needs no Portland cement: 5.5 to 8% Na2O of the binder,
water to binder about 0.4.
- Source: Slag Cement Association, "FAQs", https://www.slagcement.org/faqs. Source date: no page
  date (footer copyright 2026). Quote (page): "normally ranges from 20 to 80 percent".
- Source: B. Sun, G. Ye, G. de Schutter, "A review: Reaction mechanism and strength of slag and fly
  ash-based alkali-activated materials", Construction and Building Materials 326 (2022) 126843,
  open copy https://repository.tudelft.nl/file/File_ce6118ee-0719-49b2-bbf5-75744280fbcd. Source
  date: 2022. Quote (pdf): "The optimal values of the Na2O/binder ratio of BFS-AAM and FA-AAM are
  between 5.5% and 8%".
- A lime-poor ferronickel slag (MgO 8.9%) still reached 60 MPa alkali-activated when quenched to
  glass; magnesium locked in crystals barely reacted (K. Liu, Z. Liu, J. Sun, Frontiers in
  Materials 8, 7 September 2021, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fmats.2021.748833/full;
  quote (page): "60 MPa compressive strength and 5.5 MPa splitting tensile strength"). No study read
  covers magnesia above about 9%.

**F12. Crushing rock to aggregate.** About 1.6 to 1.8 kWh/t at two quarries' crushers (table only).
- Source: C. Mitchell (British Geological Survey), "Aggregate Carbon Demand: The hunt for low-carbon
  aggregate", slides,
  https://nora.nerc.ac.uk/id/eprint/11458/1/EIG_2010_Aggregate_Carbon_Demand_Clive_Mitchell_BGS.pdf.
  Source date: 2010 (2008 data).

**F13. Cast basalt and cast regolith.** Melt at 1,200 to 1,300 °C, cast, cool slowly; furnace
energy 1.3 x 10^9 J per ton (figure only); cast basalt in the same figure: compressive 550 MPa,
tensile 36 MPa, 2.9 g/cm3. Brittle, so joints are the design problem. Fused cast basalt pipe
linings in industry last more than 30 years.
- Source: W. P. Rogers, S. Sture, "Indigenous lunar construction materials", NASA/USRA, NTRS
  19930019931, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/19930019931/downloads/19930019931.pdf. Source
  date: 1 November 1991. Quote (pdf): "It involves liquification of the regolith at 1200-1300°C,
  casting the liquid into a form, and controlled cooling."
- Source: Kalenborn, "Wear protection for plant components and pipe systems",
  https://www.kalenborn.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/EN_Productbrochure_Wear_Protection.pdf.
  Source date: June 2023. Quote (pdf): "Worldwide ash removal pipes lined with ABRESIST reach
  lifetimes of more than 30 years."

**F14. Sintered and printed blocks.** ESA baked 0.1 mm layers of simulant at 1,000 °C into a
20 x 10 x 3 cm brick in about five hours, "equivalent strength of gypsum". A 2024 review gives
strengths from 2.49 MPa (solar-sintered in air) to 152 MPa (vacuum-sintered at 1,100 °C) and
344.7 MPa (laser-fused in vacuum). No energy per kilogram for sintering was found in any source
read.
- Source: ESA, "Printing bricks from moondust using the Sun's heat",
  https://www.esa.int/Enabling_Support/Space_Engineering_Technology/Printing_bricks_from_moondust_using_the_Sun_s_heat.
  Source date: 3 May 2017. Quote (page): "We can complete a 20 x 10 x 3 cm brick for building in
  around five hours."
- Source: M. Azami et al., "A Comprehensive Review of Lunar-based Manufacturing and Construction",
  arXiv:2408.05823, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.05823. Source date: 11 August 2024. Quote (pdf):
  "vacuum-sintered specimens showcased higher compressive strength at lower temperatures compared to
  those sintered in the air (152 MPa vs. 98 MPa)."

**F15. Slag wool insulation.** Commercial slag wool is about 70% blast-furnace slag with natural
rock; about 80% of North American mineral wool uses iron-ore slag; a wall takes about 5 kg/m2 and a
ceiling about 8 kg/m2 (NIST table); mineral wool insulates at R-3 to R-4 per inch (about 0.036 to
0.048 W/m K, converted).
- Source: NAIMA, "Rock and Slag Wool Insulation: Sustainable Choices for Conserving Energy", N046,
  https://insulationinstitute.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/01/N046-Rock-and-Slag-Wool-Insulation-Sustainable-Choices-for-Conserving-Energy.pdf.
  Source date: September 2006. Quote (pdf): "Typically, slag wool insulation uses approximately 70
  percent blast furnace slag".
- Source: NIST BEES, "Generic Mineral Wool",
  https://ws680.nist.gov/Bees/ProductListFiles/Generic%20Mineral%20Wool.pdf. Source date: 13 August
  2010 (file metadata). Quote (pdf): "about 80 % of North American mineral wool is manufactured using
  iron ore slag."
- Source: R. Aldrich, P. Mantha, S. Puttagunta, "Measure Guideline: Basement Insulation Basics", US
  DOE Building America,
  https://www1.eere.energy.gov/buildings/publications/pdfs/building_america/measure_guide_basement_insul.pdf.
  Source date: October 2012. Quote (pdf): "R-value: 3-4 per inch".

**F16. Continuous basalt fibre.** Industry melts rock at about 1,500 °C; a suitable melt has an
acidity modulus (SiO2 + Al2O3)/(CaO + MgO) of 3 to 8.5 (table, citing Johannesson et al. 2019).
Asteroid slag from F1 is about 1.55 (computed), so it needs a lot of added silica. A manufacturer
gives 3.0 to 3.5 kWh/kg for all-electric melting (trade article, medium to low confidence).
- Source: Y. Ma, Z. Guo, J. Fu, X. Xi, P. Ma, X. Wang, "From Rock to Fiber: The Mechanical
  Properties of Continuous Rock Fibers", Natural Resources Research, open copy
  https://ira.lib.polyu.edu.hk/bitstream/10397/114855/1/s11053-025-10483-0.pdf. Source date: 15
  April 2025. Quote (pdf): "In the industry, rocks are first melted at approximately 1500 °C and held
  for homogenization."
- Source: China Beihai Fiberglass, "Two mainstream continuous basalt fiber preparation
  technologies", https://basaltbest.com/news/two-mainstream-continuous-basalt-fiber-preparation-technologies/.
  Source date: 29 May 2025. Quote (page): "the comprehensive unit power consumption is 3.0-3.5
  kWh/kg".

**F17. Slag as decoration.** Victorian "slag glass" took its name from ground iron-works slag added
as a colourant.
- Source: A. M. Bowey, "Slag glass", Glass Encyclopedia, https://glassencyclopedia.com/slagglass.html.
  Source date: undated page (1998 to 2021). Quote (page): "The name derives from the belief that
  these colours were achieved by adding slag from iron smelting works to the glass."

**F18. Clear glass is not slag.** A standard soda-lime-silica glass: SiO2 72.26, Na2O 13.78, CaO
10.05, Fe2O3 0.021 wt% (table only).
- Source: Deutsche Glastechnische Gesellschaft, "Standard Glass 2 of the DGG",
  https://www.hvg-dgg.de/fileadmin/Standard_Glass_2_DGG.pdf. Source date: 3 February 2022 (PDF).
  Quote (pdf): "Standard Glass 2 of the DGG is a soda-lime-silica glass (close to float glass)."

### Slag as mass: shielding, meteoroids, reaction mass, balance, heat

**F19. The cosmic-ray numbers** are in the [active-shielding finding](2026-11-13-active-shielding.md)
and its [dose chart](../research/2026-11-13-active-shielding/README.md): lunar regolith needs about
13.4 t/m2 for 5 mSv a year against water's about 9.4 (Globus and Strout 2017, NASA OLTARIS).
Nothing new was read for it here.

**F20. Micrometeoroids.** By the Grün model, a square metre in interplanetary space at 1 AU meets
about 6.0 x 10^-4 meteoroids of 1 mg or more a year and 7.0 x 10^-8 of 1 g or more (computed from
the formula); NASA SP-413 gives "about once every 10 years" per square kilometre for 1 g. A NASA
hydrocode fit gives crater depth in concrete or processed regolith of 4.34 times the projectile's
diameter (a 1 g stony meteoroid digs about 7 cm, 10 g about 15 cm, computed); a 15 cm processed
regolith wall with 330 m2 exposed had a 0.99 chance of no penetration over 20 years.
- Source: ESA SPENVIS, "Meteoroids and debris: background", section 2.1.2,
  https://spenvis.oma.be/help/background/metdeb/metdeb.html. Source date: last update 12 March
  2018 (model: Grün et al. 1985). Quote (page): "F(m) = 3.15576x10^7 [F1(m) + F2(m) + F3(m)]".
- Source: R. D. Johnson, C. Holbrow (eds.), NASA SP-413 "Space Settlements: A Design Study",
  Chapter 2, https://settlement.nss.org/75SummerStudy/Chapt.2.html. Source date: 1977. Quote
  (page): "This radiation shield would also protect against meteoroids."
- Source: S. Evans, R. Stallworth, J. Robinson, R. Stellingwerf, C. Engler, "Meteoroid Risk
  Assessment of Lunar Habitat Concepts", NTRS 20060024733,
  https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/20060024733. Source date: 2006. Quote (pdf): "D = 4.34 * d".

**F21. The mass-driver reaction engine.** At 8,000 m/s exhaust, 560 N of thrust from 2.9 MW, 75%
efficient: about 190 N per MW and about 42.7 MJ of electricity per kilogram thrown (computed). The
exhaust must be powder, not pellets, or it endangers other craft.
- Source: G. K. O'Neill, "Mass-driver reaction engine as Shuttle upper-stage", AIAA Paper 77-536,
  NTRS 19770049208, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19770049208. Source date: 1 May 1977. Quote
  (page, abstract): "For a mission requiring an exhaust velocity of 8,000 m/sec, thrust is 560
  newtons," and "power is 2.9 megawatts, and efficiency is 75%."
- Source: Arnold, Bowen, Cohen, Kaplan, Fine, M. Kolm, H. Kolm, Newman, O'Neill, Snow, "Mass Drivers
  III: Engineering", NASA SP-428, https://settlement.nss.org/spaceres/III-3.html. Source date: 1979
  (paper 1977). Quotes (page): "may be rendered harmless if it consists of liquid oxygen or a loose
  aggregate of fine powder" and "If it is a solid pellet, however, it poses a hazard to other
  spacecraft as well as to itself".

**F22. NASA's 1975 design used slag as its shield, and ran out of slag.**
- Source: NASA SP-413, Chapter 4, https://settlement.nss.org/75SummerStudy/Chapt4.html. Source
  date: 1977. Quotes (page): "Large pieces of shield are placed around the completed portions as the
  slag material becomes available" and "the colony's shielding requirements exceed the slag
  production of the processing plant".

**F23. Balancing a spinning habitat.** Kalpana One (also 250 m in radius) controls wobble with
weights on winched cables; passive balancers need no more than 1 to 2% of the spacecraft's mass;
and a spinning hull close to a non-rotating shield is a failure mode no study had addressed.
- Source: A. Globus, N. Arora, A. Bajoria, J. Strout, "The Kalpana One Orbital Space Settlement
  Revised", http://alglobus.net/NASAwork/papers/2007KalpanaOne.pdf. Source date: 2007 (file name).
  Quotes (pdf): "Wobble control is provided by weights attached to cables on motorized winches under
  computer control." and "Contact between these is a catastrophic failure mode not addressed by any
  of the studies."
- Source: Martz, Grantham, "A passive balancer for a class of rotating spacecraft", NASA TN D-6924,
  NTRS 19720026208, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/citations/19720026208. Source date: 1 October 1972. Quote
  (page, abstract): "The ratio of total controller mass to spacecraft mass need not be greater than
  1 to 2 percent."

**F24. Heat.** Lunar soil holds 758 J/kg K at 300 K (about 1,000 J/kg K when hot, by a second
fit); water about 4,190 J/kg K (NIST, computed). A 1,000 t bed of volcanic rock heated to 750 °C
stores 130 MWh, about 0.13 MWh a tonne. Molten slag cooling from about 1,500 °C gives up about
1.8 GJ a tonne.
- Source: K. Wada et al., "Asteroid Ryugu Before the Hayabusa2 Encounter", arXiv:1804.03734,
  https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.03734. Source date: 2018. Quote (pdf): "a value of cp = 758 J kg-1 K-1
  is obtained by evaluating Eq. 14."
- Source: Siemens Gamesa, press release 12 June 2019,
  https://www.siemensgamesa.com/global/en/home/press-releases/190612-siemens-gamesa-inauguration-energy-system-thermal.html.
  Quote (page): "The ETES pilot plant can thus store up to 130 MWh of thermal energy for a week."
- Source: CSIRO, "Dry slag granulation: producing valuable by-products from waste",
  https://www.csiro.au/en/work-with-us/industries/mining-resources/processing/dry-slag-granulation.
  Source date: last updated 2 November 2022. Quote (page): "one tonne of molten slag releases about
  1.8 gigajoules (GJ) of heat."

### Slag in the soil and the water

**F25. Building soil, and topping it up.** Soil is about 45% mineral and 5% organic by volume;
ideal bulk density about 1.33 g/cm3 with mineral particles of 2.65; NASA's 1975 farm grew crops in
soil about 0.3 m deep. So the mineral part of 0.3 m of soil is about 0.22 to 0.36 t/m2 (computed).
Enhanced rock weathering models a baseline of 40 t/ha a year of basalt (4 kg/m2); field trials of
rock powder use 1 to 20 t/ha (the [closed-loop helper notes](../research/2026-11-13-mothership-sizes/E-closed-loop-and-wind.md), E2).
- Source: Cornell SoilNOW, "Soil Science 101", https://blogs.cornell.edu/soilnow/the-science-of-soil.

**F26. Steel slag as lime and silicon for crops.** In a rice paddy, steel-slag fertiliser raised
soil pH from 5.48 to about 6.5 with no build-up of cadmium or lead. (Steel slag is lime-rich;
asteroid slag is not, so this is an analogue. Chromium and vanadium were not reported.)
- Source: Ning, Liang, Liu, Xiao, Duan, "Impacts of Steel-Slag-Based Silicate Fertilizer on Soil
  Acidity and Silicon Availability and Metals-Immobilization in a Paddy Soil", PLoS ONE 11(12):
  e0168163, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5156425/. Source date: 14 December 2016.
  Quote (page): "No significant accumulation of total cadmium (Cd) and lead (Pb) was noted in
  soil."

**F27. Slag filters catch phosphorus.** Steel-slag filters on dairy wastewater removed 70% of the
dissolved phosphorus over 400 days, holding 2.15 g per kg of slag.
- Source: Lee, Drizo, Rizzo, Druschel, Hayden, Twohig, "Evaluating the efficiency and temporal
  variation of pilot-scale constructed wetlands and steel slag phosphorus removing filters for
  treating dairy wastewater", Water Research (2010),
  http://www.uvm.edu/giee/pubpdfs/Lee_2010_Water_Research.pdf. Source date: online 24 May 2010.
  Quote (pdf): "resulting in P retention capacity of 2.15 g DRP/kg slag (Drizo et al., 2008)."

**F28. Slag locks away carbon.** Aqueous carbonation of three steel slags bound 81 to 211 kg of CO2
per tonne.
- Source: Leventaki et al., "Aqueous mineral carbonation of three different industrial steel slags",
  Environmental Research 252, 118903, record https://research.chalmers.se/en/publication/541270.
  Source date: 2024. Quote (page): "reaction yields were in the range of 81–211 kg CO2/ton of slag".

## What is still unknown, and what would settle it

- **The liquidus and viscosity of flux-free magnesium-silicate slag** (what temperature the smelter
  must hold, and whether the melt can be spun into wool). Settled by a phase-diagram source for the
  MgO-SiO2-Al2O3 system at about 55% silica (a slag atlas or a ceramics text), an hour's reading.
- **Whether high-magnesia slag (above about 9%) still activates with alkali, and whether it swells.**
  Settled by reading high-magnesia ferronickel slag studies (Maragkos et al. 2009 is the lead).
- **Electrolysis energy for slag that has already lost its iron.** No measurement found; the 100%
  current efficiency after iron depletion (F8) suggests less than raw regolith. Settled by a
  molten-oxide electrolysis paper on an iron-free silicate melt.
- **The balance accuracy a 250 m drum needs.** Kalpana One calls it a thesis topic; the 1960s NASA
  notes (TN D-1515, D-2749, D-5426) are scans with no text layer. Settled by reading those scans by
  eye.
- **Whether phosphorus-loaded filter slag is a safe fertiliser.** Not sourced. Settled by a study of
  spent slag filter media applied to soil.
- **Chromium, nickel and vanadium in asteroid slag going to food soil.** Settled by the per-element
  partition between metal and silicate in a smelt of chondritic ore, and a soil-metals guideline.
- **The rock's dose behind the water, layer by layer.** One OLTARIS run of the real stack (slag,
  water, gap, steel, soil, air) settles it (the active-shielding finding's question 3).
- **The free-metal weight percent of H, L and LL chondrites straight from Jarosewich 1990.** Our
  figure is converted from volume percent (F3). Settled by the paper's Table 6 (blocked, below).

## What it means for this project

**Certain (from the sources):**
- Asteroid slag is a magnesium silicate, not Earth's lime-rich slag; where lime matters (Portland
  blends, steel-slag fertiliser figures) Earth's numbers are analogues only.
- Stony and carbonaceous ore leave several tonnes of slag or residue per tonne of metal; metallic
  ore leaves almost none.
- Slag cannot make clear glass or continuous fibre without additions, and cannot meet EN 197-1's
  cement rule without lime or magnesia.
- A radiation shield of a few tonnes per square metre makes meteoroids a minor matter (SP-413), and
  NASA's own slag-shielded design ran short of slag.
- A mass driver can throw slag at about 8 km/s for about 42.7 MJ a kilogram, as powder.

**Judgement calls (ours, to accept or reject):**
- That shielding for a growing fleet and reaction mass are the only sinks without an end, and the
  rest are one-time fills (this finding's reading of F19 to F28).
- That the water source decides whether the fleet is short of rock waste or awash in it (computed
  from F1 and F2; depends on how much water comes from carbonaceous rock rather than ice).
- That slag outside the water cuts the dose by a factor of about 0.65 to 0.8 per t/m2 beyond the
  water's 5 mSv (extrapolated from the cited dose curves, not a transport calculation).

This is a reading of public sources, not engineering advice.
