# Research dossier for the fire clothing post (2026-09-24)

### performer-guidance
- [high] Two US city fire departments that issue fire-performer permits go further than the others: performers AND their spotters must wear snug-fitting clothing made of fire-resistant material or natural fibre (wool, leather or cotton are the examples), and synthetic clothing is banned outright. Neither city gives a blend percentage or defines 'synthetic'. (caveat: These are local permit conditions. They are binding only for permitted performances in Seattle and Portland, but they are the most authoritative plain statements found. Seattle's sentence is garbled: it reads 'shall not be worn in the event there is any unintended ... flame spread', which looks like Portland's two sentences run together, and the intent is plainly a ban. Seattle cites 'NFPA 8.5.6.2', presumably NFPA 160, which is paywalled and was not read.)

- [medium] The national standard behind most fire-performance permits, NFPA 160 (flame effects before an audience), is quoted by jurisdictions only as requiring clothing that is 'suitable' for the flame exposure. As quoted, it names no fabrics. (caveat: NFPA 160 itself was not read because it is paywalled. This finding rests on how the jurisdictions paraphrase it. The standard may contain more detail.)
  sources: https://frederickcountymd.gov/4257/Flame-Effects-Before-an-Audience "protected by clothing or other means suitable for their exposure to the flame effects" | https://www.clarkcountynv.gov/adobe/assets/urn:aaid:aem:18f2c05a-c310-4f2a-bb6b-990fd1cd5a81/original/as/fire-performers.pdf "Clothing shall be suitable for the flame exposure of the performance." | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "protected by clothing or other means suitable for their exposure to flame effects"
- [high] The North American Fire Arts Association (NAFAA) publishes voluntary guidelines that local fire districts redistribute. They say costumes should ideally be flame-resistant or high-heat material. Where that is not available, every part of the costume should be checked for flammability and flame-retarded, the performer should rehearse lit in the actual costume several times, and spotters should dress with the same care as performers. NAFAA does not mention 'natural fibres' at all. (caveat: The '800 degrees' has no unit, presumably Fahrenheit. The guidelines are voluntary. NAFAA's own site (nafaa.org) was not checked for a newer revision.)
  sources: https://www.avon.org/DocumentCenter/View/19606/Fire-Performer-Safety-Guidelines "Paraphrase (not verbatim): costumes of flame resistant/high-heat material that withstands 800 degrees for over 3 seconds, if available; otherwise check and flame-retard each part; practise lit in costume; spotters dressed with same care as performer." | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "Paraphrase: Appendix A reprints NAFAA Revision 2.1 with the same costume and spotter clauses."
- [high] The Utah State Fire Marshal's fire performer safety standard gives the most specific official fabric lists found. Avoid: highly flammable, lightweight, lace, loose-weave, flammable stretch or fuzzy fabrics, pleather, nylon, polyester and other 'poly' fabrics, feathers and fur. Reliable: heavy denim, leather, tightly woven natural materials such as cotton and wool, and fire-resistant or treated fabrics. Shoes should be tied tight or covered so falling embers cannot get in. (caveat: The document calls itself a compilation of internet resources and industry practice, not a code. It contradicts itself elsewhere: section 14.8 calls kerosene the safest spinning fuel, while 17.4.11 recommends kerosene for breathing but not for poi or staff. Treat its non-clothing chapters with care.)
  sources: https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "Avoid pleather, nylon, polyester or other poly type fabrics." | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "Tight weave natural materials such as cotton, wool, etc." | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "Never use materials known to be flammable like feathers or fur."
- [high] Fit guidance is consistent across sources. Clothing should be snug and close to the body. Avoid loose or elaborate sleeves, loose necklines, floppy collars, scarves, fringe, tulle, frayed edges, flowing skirts or pants, and anything fuzzy or dangling. The two reasons given are that loose fabric has more oxygen around it and that it can tangle with or catch the prop. One exception: the Utah standard allows loose, floppy or fuzzy clothing for effect only if it has been rehearsed with fire first. (caveat: This is a consensus of practitioners, retailers and the Utah compilation. The Seattle and Portland permit conditions say only 'snug fitting'.)
  sources: https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "Avoid loose clothing, elaborate sleeves, loose neck or sleeve lines" | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "No fluff or looseness or dangling accessories." | https://flowartsinstitute.com/fabric-science-for-pyros/ "Paraphrase: frayed edges, cheesecloth, tulle and fringe ignite very quickly; tight costumes (not spandex) are safer than loose ones." | https://andrewnoske.com/wiki/Fire_Dancing_-_Safety_Manual "Paraphrase: snug fit prevents oxygen build-up; sleeves and pants not too long or floppy; avoid dangling or fuzzy clothes." | https://thespinsterz.com/blogs/resources/fire-safety "Paraphrase: avoid loose sleeves, fringe, scarves and anything that dangles."
- [high] 'Natural' is necessary but not enough. Fabric weight, weave and finish decide how fast a garment catches. In an informal flame test published by a major poi retailer, lightweight cotton or gauze, cheap T-shirt cotton and flax caught within about 3 seconds and light shiny silk within about 5. Heavy tightly woven cotton (khaki-type), unfrayed denim, cotton corduroy and heavy blanket-grade wool held up for 10 seconds or more, and leather curled rather than burning through. Several sources say that fabric thin enough to see light through is too thin, and that a loose weave can soak up fuel and act as a wick. (caveat: The flame test (credited to 'Pele') was informal: one tester, no controlled method, times counted by hand. Treat the numbers as rough comparisons, not measurements.)
  sources: https://www.homeofpoi.com/us/lessons/teach/Fire-Training/Fire-training-safety/Clothing-Costume-Fire-safety "Paraphrase: not all natural fibres are acceptable; the lighter the fabric and looser the weave, the faster it goes up." | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "Avoid lightweight fabrics, lace or loose weave fabrics." | https://flowartsinstitute.com/fire-safety-clothing/ "Paraphrase: if you can see light through the fabric its weave or weight is insufficient; heavier and tighter is more protective." | https://www.freezerburn.org/fire-performer-safety "Paraphrase: a weave of the right fibres can still be loose enough to hold fuel and act like a wick; unfinished cotton edges light easily."
- [high] Guidelines forbid synthetics because they MELT and stick to skin, holding heat and causing deeper burns with plastic left in the wound. They do not necessarily catch fire faster: flame-contact figures reprinted in the Utah standard show polyester (10.0 s) and 50/50 poly-cotton (9.2 s) taking longer to ignite than cotton (4.8 s) or rayon (3.0 s). Those same notes say blends can be more dangerous than either fibre alone because they shrink and stick as they burn. (caveat: The table's credit to the US Consumer Product Safety Commission could not be traced to an original CPSC document in this pass. The textile-science research strand should verify it.)
  sources: https://www.freezerburn.org/fire-performer-safety "If the clothing melts, as synthetics will do, it is not a safe fabric." | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "Paraphrase (Appendix B table, attributed to US CPSC): polyester/nylon may be slower to ignite but melt; residue holds heat, causes deep burns; acrylic drips molten; blends can be worse and stick to skin." | https://flowartsinstitute.com/fabric-science-for-pyros/ "Paraphrase: synthetic garment burns leave blisters and scar tissue with synthetic material embedded in the skin."
- [high] The fabrics performer guidelines recommend most often are thick or tightly woven cotton (including denim and canvas-weight cotton such as khaki), wool (heavy grades do best), and leather or suede, plus purpose-made flame-resistant aramids such as Nomex or Kevlar. Silk, hemp and linen appear on some lists, with the warning that lightweight versions catch quickly. (caveat: Most of these sources are retailers and community groups, not standards bodies. Home of Poi warns that flame-resistant clothing buys only a few seconds, 3 to 4 at best.)
  sources: https://www.freezerburn.org/fire-performer-safety "Paraphrase: cotton, denim, leather, suede and Nomex work really well." | https://www.specialtyinsuranceagency.com/sites/default/files/2021-01/safety-requirements-for-fire-performers.pdf "Paraphrase: wear natural fibres such as cotton, silk or hemp." | https://flowartsinstitute.com/fire-safety-clothing/ "Paraphrase: aramid fabrics are strong, hard to ignite and do not melt; natural fibres turn to ash rather than melting." | https://bonoboflow.com/fire-safety-for-flow-arts/ "Paraphrase: only 100% natural fibres (cotton, wool, leather, denim) or synthetics specifically sold as fire-safe are recommended."
- [high] Hair: tie up, braid, cover or wet long hair. Do not perform with hairspray, gel or oily hair products, because alcohol-based products make hair combustible. The Utah standard makes covering mandatory for loose, long or hair-sprayed hair, and says the covering should be the same safe fabric as the costume, without tassels or long ties, and easy to pull off quickly. (caveat: Utah treats wetting as reassurance and still requires covering for long or sprayed hair. Some retailers present wetting as an alternative to covering.)
  sources: https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "loose, long or sprayed with hair spray the head must be covered" | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "hair covering should be of the same type as those used in costuming" | https://www.specialtyinsuranceagency.com/sites/default/files/2021-01/safety-requirements-for-fire-performers.pdf "Paraphrase: no gel or hairspray in hair; products containing alcohol make hair combustible." | https://www.firetoys.com/blogs/fire/fire-safety-for-fire-spinners "Paraphrase: tie up long hair under a thick non-synthetic hat; dampening helps; avoid flammable hair products." | https://www.homeofpoi.com/pdf/Fire-Safety-Sheet.pdf "Paraphrase: wear tight-fitting natural fibre clothing; conceal or wet long hair."
- [medium] Fuel on clothing burns whatever the fabric, so guidance says to keep fuel off your clothes and skin, remove excess fuel from wicks before lighting, and take off or change fuel-soaked clothing. The fuel manufacturer's safety data sheet says to remove fuel-soaked clothing and launder it before wearing it again. Retailers add: change out of fuel-soaked clothes before going near any flame, and be aware that fuel sprayed on you during spin-off can carry fire onto you. (caveat: None of the fire codes or official standards found explicitly requires changing clothes after fuelling. That advice comes from retailers, and the data sheet's reasons are skin irritation and general fire hazard. By analogy only: a UK fire service warns that washing does not fully remove oily skin-cream residue from fabric, which then stays flammable ('does not remove it completely and the danger may remain', https://www.westsussex.gov.uk/fire-emergencies-and-crime/west-sussex-fire-and-rescue-service/home-fire-safety/emollients-and-paraffin-based-skin-creams, undated). That source is about emollients, not fuel.)
  sources: https://www.oldtowncoleman.com/safety/sds-coleman-kerosene-fuel.pdf "Paraphrase: immediately remove soaked/contaminated clothing and wash before reuse; remove fuel-soaked clothing and launder before reuse." | https://thespinsterz.com/blogs/resources/fire-safety "Paraphrase: wash hands and change out of fuel-soaked clothing before going near a cigarette, grill or campfire." | https://bonoboflow.com/fire-safety-for-flow-arts/ "Paraphrase: be aware when fuel sprays on your skin or clothes before lighting, as it causes fuel transfers." | https://andrewnoske.com/wiki/Fire_Dancing_-_Safety_Manual "Paraphrase: fire-retardant sprays help a little, but fuel on you will still burn." | https://www.homeofpoi.com/pdf/Fire-Safety-Sheet.pdf "Paraphrase: when dipping, try not to get the rest of the equipment covered in fuel."
- [high] All fuelling rules agree: fuel in a separate, supervised area away from the audience and from flames, and remove excess fuel before lighting by spinning into a spin-off bucket or squeezing it back into the dip can. Clark County requires the fuelling and spin-off area to be at least 25 feet from the audience. Burning Man's Fire Conclave bans spinning excess fuel onto the playa. (caveat: Distances differ: Clark County says 25 ft, while NAFAA and Utah say audience and smoking kept 30 ft from the fuel station.)
  sources: https://www.portland.gov/sites/default/files/2020-06/fir-3.07-fire-performance-art-080218-755010.pdf "A “spin out bucket” shall be utilized to spin off any excess fuel" | https://www.clarkcountynv.gov/adobe/assets/urn:aaid:aem:18f2c05a-c310-4f2a-bb6b-990fd1cd5a81/original/as/fire-performers.pdf "fueling and spin-off area shall be at least 25 feet away from the audience" | https://fireconclave.org/resources/03-create/fire-tools-safety/ "Paraphrase: do not spin or shake off excess fuel on the playa; use a spin-off bucket or zip-lock bags." | https://www.firetoys.com/blogs/fire/fire-safety-for-fire-spinners "Paraphrase: ideally squeeze excess fuel back into the dip pot with thick non-absorbent gloves; open spin-off can spray people and surfaces."
- [medium] Flame-retardant sprays and labels buy only seconds. Unlabelled fabric should be treated as flammable, retardant treatments wear off and need reapplying, and the Utah standard suggests a 3-second vertical lighter test on costume materials. Replace or re-treat anything that keeps burning. (caveat: Utah also calls fire retardants 'generally ... non-toxic'. That claim was not checked, and the chemistry varies by product.)
  sources: https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "All fire retardants, however, deplete and reduce their effectiveness in time." | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "a lighter and a three second vertical test" | https://www.homeofpoi.com/us/lessons/teach/Fire-Training/Fire-training-safety/Clothing-Costume-Fire-safety "Paraphrase: unless labelled flame resistant/retardant treat all fabric as highly flammable; 'better protection' means 3 to 4 seconds at best."
- [high] The safety person, not the fabric, is the main protection. Guidelines require a sober, dedicated safety with wet natural-fibre cloth (a cotton towel) or a wool or Nomex blanket, or duvetyne, ready for a costume fire. Where the rules name spotters' clothing, they apply the same rules as for performers. (caveat: Home of Poi's own safety sheet notes that performers may not know their back is on fire, which is why someone must watch them.)

- [high] The smoke from burning costume materials can be toxic, and not only from plastics. The US poisons agency (ATSDR) lists hydrogen cyanide as a combustion by-product of nitrogen-containing materials including wool, silk and plastics. Nylon, acrylic and polyurethane foam also contain nitrogen according to fire-service sources. Burning PVC (vinyl, and many 'pleathers' and 'patent' finishes) mainly gives off hydrogen chloride, an irritant to the eyes, nose and lungs, plus carbon monoxide, according to NIST. The Utah standard warns that burning synthetics such as nylon carpet or foam padding can produce gases fatal in very small amounts. (caveat: These sources describe fires in general (building fires, furnishings), not a brief flare-up on a costume. No source found puts a number on the exposure from a small costume fire. The honest message is 'avoid breathing smoke from burning fabric or plastic; it can be toxic', not a specific dose. 'Natural fibres give harmless smoke' is NOT supported: wool and silk smoke can contain cyanide too.)
  sources: https://wwwn.cdc.gov/TSP/MMG/MMGDetails.aspx?mmgid=1141&toxid=249 "a combustion by-product of nitrogen-containing materials such as wool, silk, and plastics" | https://www.nist.gov/publications/toxicity-pyrolysis-and-combustion-products-polyvinyl-chlorides-literature-assessment "hydrogen chloride (a sensory and pulmonary irritant) and carbon monoxide (an asphyxiant)" | https://firemarshal.utah.gov/wp-content/uploads/Final-Artisans-Safety-Manual-pages.pdf "can produce highly toxic gases that can be fatal in very small amounts" | https://bonoboflow.com/fire-safety-for-flow-arts/ "Paraphrase: synthetic clothing will melt and produce some nasty chemicals."
- [high] The fuel smoke itself is a toxic exposure. NAFAA says petroleum fuels burnt on open wicks always produce toxic fumes, worse in enclosed spaces, and that semi-enclosed spots with little wind can be as bad as indoors. It advises limiting petroleum fuels indoors and preferring the most purified fuel. The kerosene data sheet lists toxic levels of carbon monoxide and irritating aldehydes and ketones among the products released when it breaks down in heat. (caveat: NAFAA's advice to use high-proof alcohols indoors instead of petroleum fuels is about fuel choice, which falls outside the clothing question.)
  sources: https://www.avon.org/DocumentCenter/View/19606/Fire-Performer-Safety-Guidelines "Paraphrase: petrol fuels burnt on open wicks always produce toxic fumes, augmented in enclosed spaces; low-wind semi-enclosed areas can be as hazardous as indoors." | https://www.oldtowncoleman.com/safety/sds-coleman-kerosene-fuel.pdf "Paraphrase: hazardous decomposition products are toxic levels of carbon monoxide, carbon dioxide, irritating aldehydes and ketones."
- [medium] For prop builders, one source says some metals give off toxic fumes when heated. ### fibre-science
- [high] How the limiting oxygen index (LOI) works: normal air is about 21% oxygen. A fibre with an LOI below 21 keeps burning in ordinary air; roughly 21 to 25 burns slowly; about 26 and above counts as flame-retardant. LOI is a lab test in which a sample burns downward like a candle, so it ranks fibres but does not by itself tell you whether a garment is safe. (caveat: The Salmeia quote came through a summarising fetch tool, and the original wording may differ slightly. Tarasafe says 'materials with an LOI higher than 21% are self-extinguishing in air', which is looser than the 26 threshold in the academic review.)
  sources: https://www.jamesheal.com/flammability-testing-a-burning-issue "any fibre having a LOI below 21% will burn more readily" | https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6432008/ "LOI values beyond 26 vol % suggest flame retardant features." | https://blog.tarasafe.com/2025/10/03/role-of-loi-in-determining-fabric-flame-resistance/ "it's not the sole determinant of fire safety"
- [high] Published LOI numbers for the same fibre differ between sources by 2 to 4 points. Cotton appears as 16-18, 18.4, 18-20 and 20. Wool appears as 24-25, 25.2 and 26. All sources agree on the order (cotton well below 21, wool above it), but no single exact value should be quoted as definitive. (caveat: The spread comes from fibre versus fabric testing, fabric construction, and test method. Woolmark is the wool industry's own body, although its figures cite CSIRO.)
  sources: https://www.woolmark.com/globalassets/_06-new-woolmark/_industry/research/factsheets/gd2405-wool-is-fire-resistant.pdf "wool requires an oxygen level (25.2%) that is higher than in the natural atmosphere" | https://blog.tarasafe.com/2025/10/03/role-of-loi-in-determining-fabric-flame-resistance/ "Cotton: 16-18% ... Wool: 24-25%" | https://www.havep.com/en/blog/what-limiting-oxygen-index-loi-and-why-it-important "Untreated cotton ... LOI: ~18–20%" | https://www.jamesheal.com/flammability-testing-a-burning-issue "Cotton has an LOI of 20%, and wool has an LOI of 26%"
- [high] Cotton catches fire easily, burns fast, and can keep glowing (smouldering) after the flame is out. It leaves a soft ash and does NOT melt. The clearest synthetic-specific hazard is hydrogen chloride from PVC or vinyl, which is found in plastisol prints, PVC vinyl transfers and vinyl 'leather'. Hydrogen cyanide can come from nylon, acrylic and polyurethane, and also from wool and silk. Smouldering cotton makes the irritant acrolein. All smoke should be avoided. / BUTTONS: the common advice to avoid 'plastic buttons' is only partly right. Most buttons are polyester resin, a thermoset that cannot re-melt, while nylon, ABS and acrylic buttons are thermoplastic and will melt. No source tested buttons in flame, so natural (horn, corozo, wood, shell, bone) or metal buttons are the cautious choice. / ZIPPER melting figure: LenZip says 'nylon or acetal plastic melts at roughly 350 °F'. That fits acetal, but nylon melts higher (nylon 6 around 215°C, nylon 6,6 around 260°C). Either way, plastic zippers and the usual polyester zipper tape melt; a metal zipper is not all-metal. / Single-source or unverified claims to flag or leave out: Flow Arts Institute says synthetics' static electricity is an ignition risk (not verified elsewhere). Fire in Your Face says 'There is no known fire-safe mascara' and that flame-retardant chemicals may be toxic through sweat (neither confirmed by a primary source). No source was found that metal hardware such as zippers, rivets or underwire burns skin by conducting heat. / LEATHER vs FAUX LEATHER: many fire-arts sources recommend real leather and suede. 'Pleather', 'PU leather', leatherette and 'vegan leather' are PVC- or polyurethane-coated fabric, which is plastic, so they must not be confused with real leather.

### smoke
- [high] All smoke, including smoke from natural materials like wood, is a mixture of gases and fine particles. The fine particles (PM2.5) are the main everyday health concern because they get deep into the lungs and cause burning eyes, runny nose, bronchitis and asthma attacks. (caveat: The EPA pages are about wood and wildfire smoke, not fabric. They are used here for the general point that smoke from anything, natural materials included, is harmful to breathe.)

- [high] Every material that burns makes smoke, and carbon monoxide comes from both smouldering and open flame. Carbon monoxide has no colour or smell, so you cannot tell you are breathing it. - [high] Natural PROTEIN fibres (wool, silk) give off hydrogen cyanide as well as carbon monoxide when they burn or smoulder incompletely, because they contain nitrogen. - [high] PVC and vinyl (vinyl costume pieces, 'pleather', and plastisol prints) give off hydrogen chloride when overheated, starting at about 225-275 C, which is before they catch fire.
