## Plain-words answer first

- Rock powder gives minerals (calcium, magnesium, iron, some potassium and phosphorus, trace elements). It gives almost no nitrogen and no carbon from living matter. So "only space rock powder" is not enough by itself: the loop needs a one-time seed of living matter (soil, microbes, seeds, bees, worms) AND a nitrogen, carbon and water source.
- Carbonaceous (CI/CM-type) asteroid rock is the ship's real input, not basalt. Bennu is close to CI chondrite and carries carbon, ammonia and clay-bound water. Plain basalt is only the mineral part.
- Nitrogen comes in over time two ways: from the asteroid rock itself (ammonia and nitrogen-rich organics, about 0.2 percent by mass for Bennu-like rock, low confidence) and from biological fixation (legumes, cyanobacteria, Azolla). The loop recycles most nitrogen but not all (BIOS-3 kept only about 40 percent of nitrogen in its third run).
- Helpers: bumblebees for tomato-type flowers (honey bees for open flowers), earthworms plus the soil's own fungi and bacteria as decomposers, and a small set of predators and parasitic wasps for pests. Biosphere 2 shows what happens without them: pollinating insects vanished, ants and cockroaches boomed, and a mite wrecked a crop.
- Trees need moving air. The mechanism is firmly published (shorter, thicker, more root). The "Biosphere 2 trees fell over for lack of wind" story is only in popular press and blogs; I could not find it in the project's own papers.

## 1. What rock powder supplies and cannot

### E1. Basalt composition (one measured example)
- The number(s): MgO 12.9, CaO 10.8, Fe2O3 11.7, P2O5 0.9, K2O 0.7 (mass percent oxides, XRF) for the basalt in one 99-day soil experiment. Micronutrients not reported there.
- Source: "Basalt amendment significantly decreased nitrogen leaching" mesocosm paper, Frontiers in Climate, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/climate/articles/10.3389/fclim.2022.869456/full, source's own date: 2022 (article number 869456)
- Quotation: none (numbers were in Table 1 only; page read by WebFetch summary of the table, so treat as paraphrase). Sentence from the same page: "Average N leaching decreased with about 45% upon basalt amendment."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium. One basalt; basalt composition varies by flow.
- Use for the ship: Treat ground basalt as roughly 10 percent calcium oxide, 10 to 13 percent magnesium oxide, 12 percent iron oxide, about 1 percent each of phosphorus and potassium oxide. That is a low-potassium, low-phosphorus fertiliser: a slow liming and trace-element source, not a full fertiliser. (Per element: Ca 7-10, Mg 5-6, Fe 7-13 mass percent was reported in a 2026 review via a search summary only, paraphrase, low confidence.)

### E2. Rock powders are slow, work best on acid tropical soils, and basalt-type rocks do best
- The number(s): review of 48 crop trials; typical applications 1 to 20 t/ha; 50 to 100 t/ha can cause nutrient imbalance.
- Source: Swoboda, Doering, Hamer, "Remineralizing soils? The agricultural usage of silicate rock powders: A review", Science of the Total Environment, doi 10.1016/j.scitotenv.2021.150976 (PDF read at remineralize.org), source's own date: 2022 (journal volume)
- Quotation: "Beneficial results prevail for mafic and ultramafic rocks like basalts and rocks containing nepheline or glauconite."
- Also: "Most of the trials reviewed in Section 3 applied amounts in the range of 1 to 20 t ha-1."
- Also: "Very high application amounts in the range of 50-100 t ha-1 can lead to nutrient imbalances"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for the pattern (peer-reviewed review); medium for transfer to a ship.
- Use for the ship: Mafic rock (basalt-like) is the right powder; fine milling speeds release ("Ten minutes of high-energy milling produced a feldspar powder that had dissolution rates similar to K2SO4"). Plan 1 to 20 t/ha one-off then small top-ups; the ship does not need weathering to be fast because the rock is a lifetime input.

### E3. Rock powder has no nitrogen and no organic carbon (negative finding, partly inferred)
- The number(s): none. No source I read states "basalt contains zero nitrogen" in a sentence.
- Source: Swoboda et al. (above) lists nutrients from rock minerals (K, Ca, Mg, P, Si, micronutrients) and treats nitrogen only as something rock powder can reduce losses of ("Reduction of nitrogenous emissions"; "Nitrogen leaching was significantly lower in the basalt treatment than in the control" from E1).
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium. Chemistry of igneous silicate rock (no nitrogen-fixing or organic phase) is standard, but I did not find a quoted ppm figure.
- Use for the ship: Count basalt as 0 for nitrogen and 0 for organic carbon in the model. Do not expect the dust to feed the first crop's nitrogen.

### E4. Carbonaceous chondrite (CI-like) supplies the missing carbon, nitrogen and water: Bennu
- The number(s): bulk C/N ratio about 20. Bennu samples "like the Ivuna-type (CI) carbonaceous chondrites". Ammonia and nitrogen-rich organics are present. Water is held in clay minerals. Bulk carbon about 4.4 mass percent, nitrogen about 0.1 to 0.25 mass percent (search-summary figures, see below).
- Source: Sandford et al., "Nitrogen- and oxygen-rich organic material indicative of polymerization in pre-aqueous cryochemistry on Bennu's parent body", Nature Astronomy 9(12), 2025, doi 10.1038/s41550-025-02694-5 (open copy at OSTI/eScholarship), source's own date: 2025. Also NASA Goddard summary of Glavin and Dworkin et al. 2025 (Nature Astronomy) and McCoy et al. 2025 (Nature).
- Quotation: "like the Ivuna-type (CI) carbonaceous chondrites2,10,11, with a bulk C/N ratio of ~20" (superscripts are citation numbers). NASA summary: "a surprisingly high abundance of nitrogen in the form of ammonia"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for "CI-like, C/N about 20, ammonia present"; low for the exact bulk wt percent (the carbon 4.42 and 4.45 wt percent, nitrogen 882 and 1,246 ppm, and the 3.54-4.70 wt percent C with 0.145-0.250 wt percent N figures came only from a web-search summary of other papers, not a page I could read; they disagree on N by about 2 to 3 times). Glavin and Dworkin's own statement (search summary): "Typical carbonaceous chondrites, including CI types, contain ~1-3 wt% total carbon, with rare instances up to ~5 wt%".
- Use for the ship: Plan with CI-like rock at roughly 3 to 5 percent carbon and 0.1 to 0.25 percent nitrogen by mass (C/N about 20), plus water in clay (see E5). One tonne of such rock then holds about 1 to 2.5 kg of nitrogen and 30 to 50 kg of carbon. That is why a stream of asteroid rock can top up losses, but the ship's nitrogen mostly has to be conserved and fixed biologically.

### E5. Water and the carbon form in CI-like returned samples (Ryugu)
- The number(s): saponite clay formed holding about 7 mass percent interlayer water, of which Ryugu kept 0.3 mass percent; CI meteorites on Earth keep more (possible Earth contamination). Ryugu's structural (bound) water is retained.
- Source: Yokoyama et al., "The first returned samples from a C-type asteroid show kinship to the chemically most primitive meteorites" (Science 2022, Hayabusa2), NASA NTRS copy, https://ntrs.nasa.gov/api/citations/20220009715/downloads/MainText_rev1_2_light.pdf, source's own date: 2022
- Quotation: "Unlike in CI chondrites, phyllosilicates in Ryugu have lost most of their interlayer water, but retained structural water."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium. I did not extract a single bulk "water wt percent" for CI rock; search-result text only gave Ryugu carbon 3.8 to 4.63 mass percent (Rigaku Journal, paraphrase).
- Use for the ship: The water in these rocks is bound in clay and must be baked out (hundreds of degrees C). Treat asteroid water as a mined input, not as part of a small closed loop. Gap: no sourced bulk water percentage for Orgueil or Murchison found in readable text (CI literature value is commonly quoted near 10-20 percent but I could not source it here).

## 2. Nitrogen into the loop

### E6. Legume fixation, per hectare
- The number(s): Australian pasture legumes fix "from <10 to >250 kg N/ha.year"; lucerne (alfalfa) hay systems 200 to 300 kg N/ha/yr; rule of thumb about 20 kg shoot-N per tonne of herbage dry matter (Peoples and Baldock 2001: 20 to 25). Global agricultural fixation 50 to 70 Tg N/yr.
- Source: (a) Herridge, Peoples, Boddey, "Global inputs of biological nitrogen fixation in agricultural systems", Plant and Soil 311:1-18, 2008, https://rune.une.edu.au/web/handle/1959.11/16181 (abstract read); (b) Crop and Pasture Science 2012 paper CP12123 on Australian pasture legumes (search-result quotation only; publisher page returned 404)
- Quotation (a): "provides an overall estimate of 50-70 Tg N fixed biologically in agricultural systems." (a) also: "We calculate soybean to fix 16.4 Tg N annually, representing 77% of the N fixed by the crop legumes."
- Quotation (b) as relayed by the search tool, not verified on the page: "range from <10 to >250 kg N/ha.year"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium. The 2008 abstract has no per-hectare figure; the per-hectare range is second-hand.
- Use for the ship: Plan legumes at about 50 to 150 kg N/ha/yr in a managed rotation (conservative middle of the range); a well-grown alfalfa or clover plot can reach 200+. This is the main nitrogen "engine".

### E7. Cyanobacteria, Azolla and free-living fixers
- The number(s): Azolla (a floating fern with cyanobacteria) "an additional 30-120 kg of nitrogen per hectare" (per rice crop). Free-living cyanobacteria in rice fields: a few kg up to about 80 kg N/ha per crop. Associative bacteria (Azotobacter type): about 1.3 to 7.7 kg N/ha per crop; soil rhizosphere about 5 to 10 kg N/ha/yr (1979-era reports).
- Source: Review of Azolla-cyanobacteria symbiosis in lowland rice, Asian Online Journals (World Scientific Research), https://asianonlinejournals.com/index.php/WSR/article/view/7994, source's own date: undated on the page read; free-living and associative figures from search-result summary of Watanabe's table and Granhall et al. (paraphrase).
- Quotation: "Notably, it can produce an additional 30–120 kg of nitrogen per hectare, thereby significantly increasing overall rice yield."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium for Azolla; low for the free-living figures (old, second-hand).
- Use for the ship: An Azolla pond or rice paddy is a good second nitrogen source and also feeds ducks, fish and compost. Do not count on free-living soil fixers for more than 5 to 10 kg N/ha/yr.

### E8. How much nitrogen a person turns over per year
- The number(s): safe adult protein intake 0.83 g protein per kg body weight per day; protein = nitrogen x 6.25 (nitrogen about 16 percent of protein). For a 70 kg adult: 58 g protein/day = 9.3 g N/day = 3.4 kg N/year at the minimum. At a normal 90 g protein/day: 14.4 g N/day = about 5.3 kg N/year. For a sector of 500 people: about 1.7 to 2.7 tonnes N/year passing through food and back out in urine and faeces.
- Source: WHO/FAO/UNU 2007 as summarised in a nitrogen-balance meta-analysis and an EFSA review (search-result text; pages not opened: pubmed 12499330, cambridge.org S0007114512002450), source's own dates: 2003 meta-analysis, 2007 WHO/FAO/UNU, 2012 EFSA
- Quotation: none read directly. Paraphrase of search text: "the safe individual intake was identified as 0.83 g/kg/d" and "a general nitrogen percentage of 16% is assumed. This results in a conversion factor of 6.25"
- Read: 2026-11-12 (summary only)
- Confidence: medium (well-established numbers; my arithmetic is simple and shown).
- Use for the ship: Size nitrogen as roughly 4 to 5 kg per person per year circulating through the food cycle, which must be returned to soil from human and animal waste. This is the key stock to protect.

### E9. Closed loops recycle most nitrogen, not all
- The number(s): BIOS-3 third crewed run: "closure of nitrogen was approximately 40%"; mineral closure about 20 percent on average; macro elements K, Na, Ca, Mg, P, S inputs and outputs "nearly balanced"; for microelements, "output exceeded input". Major loss route: burning inedible straw, whose ash was never returned.
- Source: Salisbury, Gitelson, Lisovsky, "Bios-3: Siberian experiments in bioregenerative life support", BioScience 47(9), 1997 (PDF from simoc.space; text by pdftotext, OCR noisy), source's own date: 1997
- Quotation (OCR, light noise): "although closure of nitrogen was approximately 40%." Also: "None of the mineral elements in the ash was returned to the nutrient solution"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium. The run was a few months with human waste mostly exported, so it is a lower bound on what a full loop can do.
- Use for the ship: A fully recycled loop (waste composted, ash returned, urine returned to soil) should do far better than BIOS-3's 40 percent, but denitrification and leakage will still lose a few percent a year. Plan a small continuing nitrogen input (fixation plus a trickle of CI-like rock), and return all ash and waste (BIOS-3 called unavoidable removal "deadlock substances"). MELiSSA's nitrifying compartment held "ammonium conversion ... maintained at 99% during most of the experiment" (E13).

## 3. The helpers

### E10. Pollinators: bumblebees for tomatoes, honey bees for open flowers
- The number(s): 2,000 bee trips per hectare per day was more than adequate for tomatoes, reached with 7 to 15 bumblebee colonies per hectare (surveyed 7.6 to 19.8, mean 11.6). A 2012 Mexican greenhouse trial used 2 colonies of 70 to 90 workers per 1,000 m2 greenhouse. Extension (Michigan State) guidance: 1 to 3 bumblebee colonies per quarter acre (about 1,000 m2); honey bee colony about 20,000-60,000 bees, bumblebee 75-200.
- Source: Morandin et al. 2001, Journal of Economic Entomology 94(2):462 (abstract via search result; page returned empty); Vergara and Fonseca-Buendia, Journal of Pollination Ecology 7(4):27-30, 2012, https://pollinationecology.org/index.php/jpe/article/download/163/41/302 (read); Michigan State Extension (search result only; page returned empty)
- Quotation (Vergara 2012): "have subsequently replaced manual pollination" (about Bombus terrestris colonies in European tomato greenhouses since 1987). Also, from the same paper: "Because tomato flowers do not produce nectar" colonies need a sugar/pollen supply.
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium (one peer-reviewed field study read in full; the density figures and the MSU numbers are search-summary paraphrase).
- Use for the ship: Plan 5 to 15 bumblebee colonies per hectare of buzz-pollinated crop (tomato, pepper, eggplant), honey bees for orchards and open flowers. Both need a continuing supply of nectar and pollen plants, and queens or colonies must be bred aboard (no source gives a closed-loop colony recipe: gap). Bees also need airflow through the house for flight, but not strong gusts.

### E11. Pest predators and parasitic wasps
- The number(s): Encarsia formosa: 1 per plant preventive, 2 to 6 per plant curative (greenhouse whitefly). Delphastus catalinae (a small whitefly-eating lady beetle): 0.5 per plant. Phytoseiulus persimilis (spider-mite predator): "1+ Phytoseiulus persimilis per infested leaf". Encarsia works best 15 to 27 C and not well on sweet potato whitefly (Eretmocerus used instead) (supplier text via search). Aphidoletes midge: 5 per 10 sq ft at hot spots every 2 weeks (supplier text via search).
- Source: UConn IPM, "Biological Pest Control Success in Greenhouse Tomatoes", https://ipm.cahnr.uconn.edu/?p=1972 (read), source's own date: not shown
- Quotation: "1/plant preventive" and "2-6/plant curative" (table cells; table layout loose, pest-to-row pairing inferred)
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium (extension table; rates are for commercial greenhouses with outside resupply).
- Use for the ship: Run a breeding "insectary" aboard for the main predators, because the ship cannot reorder them. Avoid broad-spectrum pesticides, which kill the helpers (University of Kentucky and UConn guidance, via search summary). Lacewings (Chrysoperla) and ladybirds: no usable rate found in a page I could read.

### E12. Decomposers: earthworms and the soil community
- The number(s): Eisenia fetida (red wigglers); about 1 lb of worms eats about 1/4 to 1/2 lb of scraps per day (extension range varies 25 to 50 percent of body weight per day). The Alabama extension guide: "a bin with two pounds of worms can consume between one-half pound and one pound of food scraps per day." Best 59 to 77 F (15 to 25 C); avoid below 40 F (4 C) or above 95 F (35 C); bin moisture 60 to 90 percent.
- Source: Alabama Cooperative Extension, ANR-1469 "Vermicomposting", https://www.aces.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/ANR-1469_Vermincomposting_042523L-G.pdf (read via pdftotext), source's own date: file stamped 2023 (042523)
- Quotation: "optimum range (59 to 77 degrees F)"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for worm bin practice. No readable source found for springtails or soil fungi numbers (gap).
- Use for the ship: Worm beds convert crop residue and food waste to castings; keep soil at 15 to 25 C and moist. For the orchard and field soil, seed the soil with the whole community (fungi, bacteria, springtails, worms) from the first Earth-sourced soil; powder cannot supply them.

### E13. MELiSSA's own nitrogen step
- The number(s): ammonium conversion held at 99 percent for most of a 276-day connected run (nitrifying bioreactor to cyanobacteria reactor); nitrate yields above 90 percent; gas link to rat "crew" for 110 days.
- Source: Frontiers in Astronomy and Space Sciences, 2021, doi 10.3389/fspas.2021.750616, https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fspas.2021.750616 (read, first 100,000 characters)
- Quotation: "The ammonium conversion is maintained at 99% during most of the experiment"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for the experiment; it says nothing about long-run nitrogen losses.
- Use for the ship: A dedicated nitrification stage (urine and ammonia to nitrate, which plants take up) is a proven part of a loop; nitrate, not ammonia, goes to the plants ("all the nitrogen supplied to compartment 4 is in form of NO3-").

### E14. Pests arrive anyway, and closed systems drift
- The number(s): none. Biosphere 2: "Insect and disease problems were caused by two types of mite, mealy bug aphid, powdery mildew, root knot nematode and cockroaches." Broad mites forced a switch from white to sweet potatoes. BIOS-3: UV treatment of phytotrons and gauze masks were used, and runs began in winter "to minimize pathogen invasion from the outside".
- Source: Nelson and Dempster, "Living in Space: results from Biosphere 2's initial closure" (Biosphere 2 crew-member authors), gwern.net copy of 1996 paper (PDF read), source's own date: 1996; Salisbury et al. 1997 (E9)
- Quotation: "The most serious of these proved to be the broad mite, which proved resistant to a number of control techniques."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high.
- Use for the ship: Expect pests to arrive with seeds, people and soil; plan quarantine and a clean-seed route, but also plan an insectary and resistant crop choices, rather than assuming a sterile system.

## 4. Lessons from earlier closed systems

### E15. Biosphere 2: oxygen fall and its cause
- The number(s): oxygen "dropping from an initial 20.9% (ambient) to around 14% after 16 months of closure". CO2 ranged "from under 1000 ppm to over 4000 ppm". Scrubber explained about 1.6 percent of the oxygen loss; "about 12% of atmospheric carbon dioxide disappeared", leaving "more than a 10% loss of atmospheric oxygen unaccounted for"; carbon isotopes showed the interior concrete had taken up the CO2. Soil was too rich in organic matter ("highly enriched with organic matter which promotes rapid microbial respiration"), and glass reduced light.
- Source: Nelson and Dempster, 1996, as in E14; the underlying paper is Severinghaus, Broecker, Dempster, MacCallum, Wahlen, "Oxygen Loss in Biosphere 2", EOS 75(3), 33-37, 18 Jan 1994 (cited there; the Eos page not read)
- Quotation: "Oxygen was depleted from the atmosphere by reactions with organic C in the systems' soils"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high.
- Use for the ship: Do not start a loop with rich soil in a small air volume; start soils lean and rock-based and let organic matter build. Seal or coat structural concrete (or use a non-carbonating structure) so it does not become a CO2 sink. The ship's air volume is large (a 250 m radius drum), which buffers swings far better than Biosphere 2's 180,000 cubic metres.

### E16. Biosphere 2: food, species loss and what boomed
- The number(s): 2,000 square metres of crops gave "about 80% of the eight person's nutritional requirements" (about 250 m2 per person at 80 percent food; average intake about 2,200 kcal, 73 g protein). Species: "noticeable decline in flying insects during the two year closure and loss of two bird species"; "Explosion of some populations including a species of ant and cockroaches".
- Source: Nelson and Dempster 1996 (E14). Secondary popular statement (an opinion piece in The Scientist, not a primary source): "Most of the vertebrate species and all of the pollinating insects died within a year" and "pests like cockroaches and ants boomed" (relayed by the fetch tool, the full sentence was longer). The Cohen and Tilman 1996 Science Perspective (Science 274:1150) is the underlying source; the Science page returned 403 and the abstract was not read.
- Quotation: "Explosion of some populations including a species of ant and cockroaches has stimulated further research and potential control methods"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for the crew-member paper's wording; low for the "all pollinators died" claim (secondary).
- Use for the ship: Bees, hoverflies and other pollinators are exactly what a small closed system loses first. Keep a large, diverse flowering habitat and several pollinator species with spare colonies. Plan a predator for ants and cockroaches.

### E17. Biosphere 2: weak trees and vines
- The number(s): none. Trees grew fast ("Many Leuceana glauca trees ... grew to be 12-16 m tall"); vines (morning glory, passionfruit) overran other plants and needed pruning.
- Source: Nelson and Dempster 1996 (E14). The "stress wood" claim: searched, not in this paper (it never mentions wind). Popular accounts (blogs, a textbook chapter at macmillan, a Wikipedia mirror) say the trees lacked wind, grew fast and fell over; none is a primary study and one blog misnames it "reaction bark".
- Quotation: "This rapid development of tree canopies continued throughout the two year closure in rainforest, savannah and marsh biomes." (primary), nothing quotable for the fall-over claim.
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high that trees grew fast and vines needed control; the "fell over from missing stress wood" story is unverified (press and blog only). I could not locate a peer-reviewed report of it.
- Use for the ship: Treat the story as a plausible illustration, not a citation. The wind science itself (E20 to E23) is solid.

### E18. BIOS-3 (Krasnoyarsk)
- The number(s): built 1972; underground; crew of 2 to 3; longest run six months (winter 1972-73, two men and one woman); third run two men for five months (Nov 1983 to Apr 1984). Crop growing area "The total growing area was 63 m2" (OCR). Water and oxygen recycled "100%"; crops met about half of the crew's food needs; animal products (about 25 percent of food mass) came as freeze-dried meat from outside; human waste mostly exported. Early step: 1968, water recycling raised closure to "80%-85%".
- Source: Salisbury, Gitelson, Lisovsky 1997 (E9) and Nelson and Dempster 1996 (cites "hydroponic cropping area of about 400 square feet" for crews of two or three)
- Quotation: "In 1968, the Krasnoyarsk scientists achieved 80%-85% closure by recycling water." (OCR dropped the hyphen to read "80%85%")
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium (OCR text; areas differ by source: 63 m2 versus about 37 m2).
- Use for the ship: About 20 to 30 m2 of lit hydroponic crop per person fed half a diet; Biosphere 2's soil farm needed about 250 m2 per person for 80 percent. The ship's farms should be sized from sector food models, not these.

### E19. MELiSSA compartments and pilot plant
- The number(s): five compartments: three break down waste by fermentation, one grows algae or plants, the fifth is the crew; crew mock-up 40 rats (about one person's oxygen); target "close to 100% efficiency"; Barcelona pilot plant inaugurated 2009 (ESA text).
- Source: ESA, "Life support pilot plant paves the way to Moon and beyond", and ESA "MELiSSA life support project..." (both read), source's own dates: 2009 (archived)
- Quotation: "The target of the work is the creation of a closed loop life support system with close to 100% efficiency"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium (2009 statements; they set a goal, not a measured closure).
- Use for the ship: MELiSSA's lesson is separate compartments with one microbe community each, linked by gas and liquid lines. For the ship, compost and waste digestion plus a nitrification step should be separate from the farms, so a failure in one does not collapse the others.

## 5. Trees and wind

### E20. What mechanical stress does (Telewski 2021)
- The number(s): none (qualitative).
- Source: Telewski, mini-review on mechanical perturbation and thigmomorphogenesis (term from Jaffe 1973), Frontiers in Forests and Global Change, doi 10.3389/ffgc.2020.574096, https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/forests-and-global-change/articles/10.3389/ffgc.2020.574096/full, source's own date: 2021 (article id 2020)
- Quotation: "the response of trees and other plants to bending is a reduction in extension/height growth". Also: "an increase in radial growth (secondary growth), which increases stem taper"; "a reallocation of growth from shoots to roots increasing the root-to-shoot ratio"; "The reaction to MP by the mechanosensing response network has been shown to be dose dependent". Gap: "no studies have investigated the role of mechanoperception and growth regulation in the development of roots in trees".
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high (peer-reviewed review). Note the review itself says root response in trees is understudied.
- Use for the ship: Trees get shorter, thicker, tapered and root-heavy with regular flexing; without it they are tall, thin and weak. Both the benefit and the dose dependence are published.

### E21. A dose-response experiment on trees: American elm
- The number(s): Six-week-old elm seedlings flexed 0 (staked), minimal, 5, 10, 20, 40 or 80 times per day for 3 weeks under greenhouse conditions. Staked seedlings ended up taller and thinner than every flexed group; height growth fell off roughly exponentially with more flexing; every flexing level thickened stems; 40 and 80 flexures per day also significantly cut leaf area (a cost).
- Source: Telewski and Pruyn, "Thigmomorphogenesis: a dose response to flexing in Ulmus americana seedlings", Tree Physiology 18:65-68, 1998, doi 10.1093/treephys/18.1.65 (abstract via search result; full text not read)
- Quotation: none (paraphrase of abstract from the search tool)
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium (one species, seedlings, abstract only).
- Use for the ship: Even 5 to 20 flexes a day produced the thickening; 40 to 80 started to hurt leaf area. So orchards do not need hurricane winds; regular gentle flexing is the aim.

### E22. Other published flexing and rubbing doses
- The number(s): daily bending of bean plants for 1 minute reduced height response, observed "after 29 days of daily application" (Suge 1978, in Telewski's review). A 1970s-era review (search summary, paraphrase) says rubbing internodes for about 10 s once or twice a day slowed elongation in several species. Neel and Harris, Science 173:58-59, 1971 (sweetgum, shaken), only the citation was confirmed; the shaking protocol detail is unverified.
- Source: Telewski 2021 (E20); search summaries of Jaffe (1973) and Neel and Harris (1971)
- Quotation: "MP in P. vulgaris was observed to decrease after 29 days of daily application of bending for 1 min" (exact phrase in the review, species abbreviated as shown)
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: low to medium for the dose sizes (small plants in labs).

## Recommendations

### Closed loop inputs (what the ship must bring in)
1. One-time seed stock of living matter that no rock can supply: soil with its community (fungi, bacteria, springtails, worms), seeds, bumblebee and honey bee colonies, the insectary predators, fish and livestock. (Basis: E3, E12, E14, E16.)
2. Continuing mineral input: milled basalt-type rock at about 1 to 20 t/ha initially, then small top-ups (E1, E2). It supplies Ca, Mg, Fe, some P and K, and trace elements.
3. Carbon, nitrogen and water: CI/CM-type asteroid rock, about 3 to 5 percent C and 0.1 to 0.25 percent N by mass, with water locked in clay needing heat to release (E4, E5). Low confidence on exact percentages; get a better figure from a CI chondrite compilation before sizing.
4. Biological nitrogen fixation as the main nitrogen engine: legume rotations at about 50 to 150 kg N/ha/yr, an Azolla pond at 30 to 120 kg N/ha per crop (E6, E7). A person turns over about 3.4 to 5.3 kg N/yr (E8), so a 500-person sector circulates about 1.7 to 2.7 t N/yr.
5. Return everything: compost wastes, return urine and ash. BIOS-3 lost most minerals through ash and exported waste and kept only about 40 percent of nitrogen (E9).

### Living helpers
- Pollinators: bumblebees (5 to 15 colonies per hectare of tomato-type crops) and honey bees for orchards; a big flowering habitat and spare queens (E10, E16).
- Decomposers: worm beds (Eisenia fetida at 15 to 25 C) plus whole-soil fungi and bacteria (E12).
- Predators: insectary-bred Encarsia, Phytoseiulus, Aphidoletes, Delphastus, lacewings; no broad-spectrum sprays (E11).
- Separate compost, nitrification and farms into compartments, as MELiSSA does (E13, E19).
- Expect pests and plan for them; start soils lean, seal or avoid carbonating concrete, and keep a large air volume (E14, E15).

### Air movement for trees
- Two-tier design: (a) a steady background of about 0.3 to 0.5 m/s horizontal flow through crop and bee houses (E24); (b) for orchards and parks, add scheduled gusts so crowns visibly sway, because steady gentle flow does not flex trunks.
- No source I read gives a wind speed for trees. As engineering judgement, not a sourced number: gusts of about 3 to 6 m/s at canopy height for 10 to 30 minutes, several times a day, varying direction and timing; all-year, starting from the day saplings go in. Reason: dose-response work on small trees found thickening at 5 to 20 flexes per day and damage to leaf area at 40 to 80 (E21); a gust cycle that makes each tree flex tens of times a day fits that range. Treat as a starting value to test in the game, not a published figure.
- Consider mechanical flexing for young trees (staking loose rather than rigid) in the first years (E20 to E22).
