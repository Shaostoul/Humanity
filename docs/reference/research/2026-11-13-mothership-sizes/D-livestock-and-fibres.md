## TOPIC 1: LIVESTOCK

### D1. Laying hens, Certified Humane "Pasture Raised": outdoor space
- The number(s): 2.5 acres (1 hectare) per 1,000 birds = 10 m2 (108 sq ft) of pasture per hen. Land used for cropping (other than grass or hay) does not count.
- Source: Certified Humane (Humane Farm Animal Care) Animal Care Standards, Egg Laying Hens, Edition 21 (the 2023 file), https://certifiedhumane.org/wp-content/uploads/Standard_LayingHens-2023.pdf, source's own date: Edition 21, file dated 2023
- Quotation: "The minimum outdoor space requirement is 2.5 acres (1 hectare)/1000 birds."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high (the certifier's own standard text, read from the PDF)
- Use for the ship: this is the main benchmark for "good life" hens. 10 m2 of range per hen. The standard also requires rotation (see D3).

### D2. Laying hens, Certified Humane: indoor space (house) and the pasture-raised mobile house
- The number(s): single-level litter house 1.5 sq ft (0.14 m2) per hen; litter plus raised slats 1.2 sq ft (0.11 m2); multi-tier house 1 sq ft (0.09 m2); pasture-raised mobile house with fully perforated floor 1 sq ft (0.09 m2). Nests and elevated perches do not count.
- Source: same as D1, section on stocking density
- Quotation: "a minimum of 1.5 sq. ft. (0.14 sq. m) per hen must" (continues "be allocated to allow normal behavior and dilute the feces"); "the minimum indoor space requirement is 1 sq. ft. (0.09 sq. m) per hen."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high
- Use for the ship: house floor of 0.14 to 0.2 m2 per hen (the AGW figure in D4 is 0.16 m2). Hens sleep inside at night and are kept in only a few days at a time; the standard bars more than 14 consecutive days indoors.

### D3. Certified Humane pasture rotation and free-range figure (older post)
- The number(s): free range 2 sq ft (0.19 m2) per bird of outdoor area; pasture raised 108 sq ft per bird "based on a rotational grazing system"; up to one fifth of the land in use at one time, the rest resting.
- Source: "Truth in Labeling" and a legacy post by Adele Douglass, Certified Humane, https://certifiedhumane.org/?p=1650 and https://certifiedhumane.org/truth-in-labeling/, source's own date: posted May 6, 2014 (the site warns older posts may be out of date)
- Quotation: "There is a 108 sq. ft/bird requirement, which is based on a rotational grazing system"; "up to 1/5th of the land may be used at one time to allow the rest of the land to rest, recover and prevent disease."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium (2014 text; the 2023 standard in D1 confirms the 2.5 acre / 1,000 bird figure and gives free range as 2 square feet uncovered outdoor area)
- Use for the ship: five paddocks per flock, hens on one at a time; that is the rotation to build into the farm band.

### D4. Laying hens, A Greener World "Animal Welfare Approved"
- The number(s): indoor 1.8 sq ft (0.16 m2) per bird; outdoor at least 4 sq ft (0.37 m2) of range per bird; no hens-per-acre figure; rotation recommended, moving the flock before land is damaged is required.
- Source: Animal Welfare Approved by AGW, laying hen standards page (items 8.1.3, 7.3.9, 7.0.4, 7.3.7), https://agreenerworld.org/certifications/animal-welfare-approved/standards/laying-hen-standards/, source's own date: undated (current page)
- Quotation: "After the brooding period each bird must have continuous access to at least 4 sq ft (0.37 sq m) range and foraging area." (from the reader's extract of 7.3.9; the figure itself is as printed)
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium (page read through a summarizing reader; numbers match the other standards' order of magnitude)
- Use for the ship: AGW's 0.37 m2 range is a floor, far below Certified Humane pasture raised. Use AGW only for the indoor 0.16 m2.

### D5. Laying hens, EU organic rules
- The number(s): maximum 6 birds per m2 of usable indoor area (0.17 m2 per hen); minimum 4 m2 of outdoor area per bird.
- Source: Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/464, Annex I Part IV, as adopted (text on legislation.gov.uk), https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2020/464/annex/I/part/4/adopted, source's own date: 26 March 2020
- Quotation: "Maximum number of birds per m2 of usable area of the indoor area of the poultry house" (value 6); "Minimum m2 per bird of the outdoor area" (value 4)
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for the adopted text; later EU amendments were not checked
- Use for the ship: EU organic outdoor (4 m2) is 40 percent of the Certified Humane pasture figure (10 m2). Pick 10 m2.

### D6. Eggs per hen per year
- The number(s): 290 eggs per layer in 2024 (U.S. commercial average, all housing types).
- Source: Chickens and Eggs 2024 Summary, USDA NASS, https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/1v53jw96n/4b29d373g/4455b918n/ckegan25.pdf, source's own date: February 2025
- Quotation: "The annual average production per layer on hand in 2024 was 290 eggs, up 1 percent from 2023."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for the commercial number; I found no sourced figure for pasture flocks
- Use for the ship: plan 250 eggs per hen per year for pasture hens (my assumption, about 14 percent below the commercial number to allow for foraging, weather and moult; flagged as unsourced).

### D7. Feed for a laying hen
- The number(s): 100 to 150 g per day, about 0.25 lb; roughly 36 to 55 kg per year (my arithmetic).
- Source: Alabama Cooperative Extension, Backyard small poultry flock management series: feeding, https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/farming/backyard-small-poultry-flock-management-series-feeding/, source's own date: September 7, 2022
- Quotation: "A laying hen will consume between 100 and 150 grams of feed per day or roughly 0.25 pounds."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for the daily figure
- Use for the ship: 45 kg of feed per hen per year; foraging on range cuts the bought-in share, but aboard everything is grown, so budget the full amount (grain, legumes, insects, kitchen waste).

### D8. Goats (dairy), A Greener World: indoor space and outdoor access
- The number(s): by body weight, bedded lying area plus separate loafing area. A 60.1 to 80 kg goat: lying 13.5 sq ft (1.25 m2) plus loafing 22 sq ft (2.0 m2) = 3.25 m2. Over 80 kg: 16 sq ft (1.5 m2) plus 27 sq ft (2.5 m2) = 4.0 m2. A 40 to 60 kg goat: 1.0 m2 plus 1.5 m2. Kidding pen 25 sq ft (2.32 m2). Continuous pasture access required; no more than 20 percent of the pasture may be denuded. No stocking rate (animals per acre) is given.
- Source: Dairy Goat Standards, Animal Welfare Approved by AGW (sections 7.0.1, 7.1.1, 8.1.1, 4.3.10), https://agreenerworld.org/certifications/animal-welfare-approved/standards/dairy-goat-standards/, source's own date: undated (current page)
- Quotation: "Continuous outdoor pasture access is required for all goats." ; "The activity of the animals must not cause more than 20% of the pasture area they are kept on to be denuded."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high (certifier's own page; tables read as printed)
- Use for the ship: 3.25 to 4 m2 of covered or semi-covered space per adult doe (lying plus loafing), with raised platforms for when pasture is closed.

### D9. Goats and sheep, EU organic rules

### D10. Goat milk per lactation

### D11. Feed for a dairy goat
- The number(s): a lactating dairy doe eats about 4 to 5 percent of body weight as dry matter per day. For a 60 kg doe at 4 percent: 2.4 kg per day, about 880 kg dry matter per year (my arithmetic).
- Source: Oregon State University course material on goats (feed intake table: dry doe 2.8 percent, low-producing lactating 4.0, high-producing 5.0 percent body weight) and a goat extension guide, via search; page not opened. https://courses.ecampus.oregonstate.edu/ans312/nine/goats_4_trans.htm, source's own date: undated
- Quotation: paraphrase, no exact quotation
- Read: 2026-11-12 (search summary only)
- Confidence: low to medium (planning range, not a read table; Langston University's calculator gives a lower 2.5 to 2.7 kg per day for a 50 kg doe giving 4 kg milk)
- Use for the ship: 800 to 900 kg dry matter per goat-year, mostly browse, hay and orchard-floor forage.

### D18. Fish: EU organic maximum stocking densities
- The number(s): rainbow trout and brown trout in fresh water 25 kg/m3; salmon 20 kg/m3; Arctic charr 25 kg/m3; tilapia (Oreochromis) 20 kg/m3; sturgeon 30 kg/m3; pangasius 10 kg/m3. Carp in polyculture: no kg/m3 figure, production limited to 1,500 kg of fish per hectare per year.
- Source: Regulation (EU) 2020/464, Annex II (organic aquaculture), https://legislation.gov.uk/eur/2020/464/annex/II/adopted/data.xht, source's own date: 26 March 2020
- Quotation: "Brown trout and Rainbow trout 25 kg/m3"; "Oreochromis: 20 kg/m3"; "The total production of species is limited to 1 500 kg of fish per hectare per year."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for these as legal maximums; a legal maximum is the most fish allowed, not a measure of a pleasant life
- Use for the ship: cap tanks at 20 kg/m3 for tilapia and 25 for trout; I recommend running at about half of the cap (10 to 12 kg/m3) as the game's "good life" setting (my choice, unsourced). Note also tilapia is a warm-water fish, trout need cold water (below 18 C), carp tolerate both.

### D19. Fish: tank stocking and feed conversion for tilapia

- Source: Global Seafood Alliance (Advocate) summaries of tilapia cage and recirculating-tank studies, https://www.globalseafood.org/advocate/performance-economics-of-tilapia-cage-culture-in-brazil/ (not opened; seen in search summaries)

- Read: 2026-11-12 (search summary only)
- Confidence: low (cage and flow-through numbers applied to tanks; summary not read in full)
- Use for the ship: feed per kg of tilapia about 1.5 to 1.9 kg (a vegetable-fed fish aboard needs the farm to supply it); tilapia grow to 600 to 900 g in tanks in months.

### D26. A small herd for one 500-person sector (my arithmetic; every consumption target is an assumption)
- Hens: 400. Eggs 400 x 250 = 100,000 per year = 200 per person-year (about 0.55 a day). Range 4,000 m2 (0.4 ha), house 80 m2. Feed 18 t per year.
- Dairy goats: 50 milking does, 1,000 kg each = 50,000 kg milk = 100 kg per person-year (about 0.27 kg a day), before kids are raised; shelter 200 m2; browse about 5 ha. Feed about 44 t dry matter per year. About 25 kids per year would be eaten or grown out (add 12 does of other age classes).
- Sheep: 80, giving 256 kg greasy wool = 0.5 kg per person-year; shelter 260 m2; pasture about 8 ha.
- Rabbits: 20 breeding does + growers; about 600 market rabbits a year (about 1.1 t live weight); house plus run 60 m2 for does and about 200 m2 for growers.
- Fish: tilapia tank of about 100 m3 at 10 to 12 kg/m3 holds about 1.1 t; with one harvest per year (about a 50 percent turnover guess) that is 1 kg of fish per person-year or more. The size of the fish system is an open design question.
- Bees: 10 colonies for honey, plus 4 per hectare of orchard.
- Total outdoor land (hens 0.4 + goats 5 + sheep 8 + rabbits 0.3 = about 14 ha) is large against a sector's farm band (unknown to me), so the sheep count in particular is the first thing to trim; hens, goats and rabbits can graze under orchard trees on land that is also growing fruit.

## TOPIC 2: CLOTHING FIBRES

### D27. Basalt fibre maker's safety data sheet
- The number(s): nominal fibre diameter 10 to 22 micron; dust may cause temporary mechanical irritation; skin irritation is possible; the maker recommends avoiding tight-fitting garments; not classed carcinogenic under the EU rules cited by the maker. Intended use listed: "Industrial applications."
- Source: Safety Data Sheet, Continuous basalt fiber products, Basfiber, https://basfiber.com/system/storage/download/Safety_Data_Sheet.pdf, source's own date: issued 30 September 2015, revised 25 October 2017
- Quotation: "At skin contact, skin irritations are possible."; "have a nominal diameter of 10 - 22 micron."; "avoid wearing tight-fitting garments."
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for this product (a manufacturer's own sheet), though an older sheet; other makers' sheets seen in search show similar wording
- Use for the ship: basalt filament is a coarse (10 to 22 micron) mineral thread that itches and may release dust; the maker itself tells workers to wear loose clothing and gloves around it. That is a plain "not for next to skin" signal.

### D28. Basalt fibre: respirability
- The number(s): a respirable fibre is thin; sheets for glass fibre cite fibres of 3 microns or more (WHO) or 3.5 microns or more (NIOSH) as non-respirable. Basalt filaments at 10 to 22 micron are far above this, so intact filaments are not respirable; cutting, grinding or abrading can still make dust and shorter airborne fibres.
- Source: manufacturer safety data sheets for basalt and glass filament seen via search (https://basfiber.com/system/storage/download/Safety_Data_Sheet.pdf for the diameter; the 3 and 3.5 micron thresholds came from a glass-fibre sheet I did not open)
- Quotation: paraphrase, no exact quotation for the 3 and 3.5 micron thresholds
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: medium
- Use for the ship: cloth of 10 to 22 micron continuous basalt is not an inhalation fibre hazard in normal wear, but sewing, fraying and washing wear it down; this is an extra reason to keep it out of everyday clothes.

### D29. IARC classification of the relevant fibres
- The number(s): continuous glass filament, insulation glass wool, rock (stone) wool and slag wool are Group 3 (not classifiable as to carcinogenicity to humans); special-purpose glass fibres such as E-glass and '475' glass fibres are Group 2B (possibly carcinogenic to humans). Basalt fibre is not named in the monograph.
- Source: IARC Monographs Volume 81, Man-made vitreous fibres, Summary and evaluation, 2002 (inchem.org copy), https://inchem.org/documents/iarc/vol81/81.html, source's own date: 2002
- Quotation: "Special-purpose glass fibres such as E-glass and '475' glass fibres are possibly carcinogenic to humans (Group 2B)." and "Insulation glass wool, continuous glass filament, rock (stone) wool and slag wool are" not classifiable as to their carcinogenicity to humans (Group 3).
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for the IARC text; applying it to basalt is my inference (basalt is the rock that rock wool is made from; continuous filament is the glass-filament analogue), so the match is by similarity, not a named entry
- Use for the ship: no cancer-hazard flag for woven continuous basalt filament, by analogy with Group 3. The concern for clothing is irritation and dust, not cancer.

### D30. What basalt fibre is used for today
- The number(s): none (a list of uses). Reviews report composites, fire insulation, concrete reinforcement, thermal protection and filtration; one review says industrial adoption is still limited "mainly due to inadequate data and lower production volumes leading to higher cost."
- Source: A review on basalt fibre and its composites (Queen's University Belfast), https://pure.qub.ac.uk/en/publications/a-review-on-basalt-fibre-and-its-composites/ and other reviews listed in a search (not opened); Developments and industrial applications of basalt fibre reinforced composite materials, https://pearl.plymouth.ac.uk/handle/10026.1/20036 (not opened)
- Quotation: paraphrase, no exact quotation (the adoption sentence is from a search summary)
- Read: 2026-11-12 (search summaries only)
- Confidence: low to medium
- Use for the ship: use basalt as fireproofing and reinforcement (fire blankets, outer fire-protection layers, rebar, panels, filter cloth), not as shirts. I found no source showing basalt fibre in ordinary next-to-skin clothing. One search summary said basalt fabric is "often used in clothing" but that was a passing line in an unnamed source, and the only maker document I read (D27) says the opposite for skin contact.

### D31. Flax (linen): fibre yield per hectare
- The number(s): long scutched fibre about 1.0 t/ha in 2025 and 1.3 t/ha in 2024 (on 5 to 5.5 t of straw per hectare); a poor 2023 year gave 0.4 to 0.5 t/ha. Europe in 2024: 185,000 ha of flax and 116,000 t of long fibre, which is 0.63 t/ha averaged over all the hectares (my arithmetic, since not all hectares were scutched in that year). Short fibre (tow) is extra and not counted here.
- Source: Flax-Linen and Hemp Economic Observatory, CELC (European Confederation of Flax and Hemp), https://allianceflaxlinenhemp.eu/en/flax-linen-hemp-economic-observatory/flax-linen-and-hemp-economic-observatory-figures-2025, source's own date: releases of May 2025 and 19 December 2025
- Quotation: "estimated yields of 5-5.5 tonnes of straw/hectare, and about 1.0 tonnes long fibre/hectare"; "a yield of long fiber of 400kg to 500kg per hectare on the majority of plots" (2023 page)
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high (industry trade body, current)
- Use for the ship: plan 0.8 t/ha long fibre (a mid value between a good and a poor year) plus about the same weight again of lower-grade fibre. That is 1 kg of linen thread from 12.5 m2 of field per year.

### D33. Cotton: lint yield
- The number(s): world cotton yield 798 kg of lint per hectare for 2022/23 (the best on record was 804 kg/ha in 2017/18); China 2,177 kg/ha, India 420 kg/ha.
- Source: Cotton and Wool Outlook: April 2023, USDA Economic Research Service (CWS-23d), saved PDF, https://esmis.nal.usda.gov/sites/default/release-files/n870zq801/kw52kp99w/9593w845g/CWS-23d.pdf, source's own date: April 13, 2023
- Quotation: "The 2022/23 world cotton yield is projected at 798 kilograms (kg) per hectare (712 pounds per acre)"
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: high for a forecast; cotton aboard would need warm, humid, long-season growing and heavy water
- Use for the ship: 0.8 t/ha lint (1 kg per 12.5 m2), similar to flax per hectare but with a much higher water use (not sourced here). A drum with controlled climate could grow it, but it earns no land advantage over linen.

### D34. Wool per sheep, in land terms
- The number(s): 3.2 kg greasy fleece per sheep-year (D12). Land per sheep from my planning figure in D25: about 1,000 m2 of pasture plus 3.25 m2 shelter, so about 310 m2 of pasture per kg of greasy wool and roughly twice that per kg of clean wool.
- Source: derived from D12 and D25
- Quotation: none (derived)
- Read: 2026-11-12
- Confidence: low (the pasture figure is not from a certifier)
- Use for the ship: wool costs about 25 times the land per kg of fibre that flax does (310 m2 against 12.5 m2), so use it only where it is hard to replace (socks, jumpers, blankets, felt). It is a by-product of sheep kept for other reasons.

### D35. How much fibre one person uses per year
- The number(s): global fibre production 124 million tonnes in 2023 (116 in 2022); cotton 24.4 Mt; synthetics 75 Mt; polyester 57 percent; man-made cellulosics 7.9 Mt. Divided by about 8.0 billion people that is about 15.5 kg per person per year for ALL uses (clothing, home textiles, footwear, some industrial), my arithmetic.
- Source: Textile Exchange Materials Market Report 2024 (11th edition, September 2024), as reported by renewable-carbon.eu (nova-Institute), https://renewable-carbon.eu/news/materials-market-report-2024/, source's own date: September 2024. The primary Textile Exchange PDF was too large for my reader (over 10 MB).
- Quotation: "Global fiber production increased from around 116 million tonnes in 2022 to a record 124 million tonnes in 2023."
- Read: 2026-11-12 (second-hand quotation of the report; the sentence is the report's wording as shown in a search summary)
- Confidence: medium (headline total; per-person figure is my division and includes waste and more consumption than a ship would have)
- Use for the ship: 15.5 kg per person-year is the world's production, not a need. Ship design number: 8 kg of fibre per person-year (clothes, bedding, towels, shop-floor use), about half the world average because fast-fashion turnover is not wanted (an assumption). I did not find a sourced figure for fibre per person actually worn.

### D37. Recommended everyday cloth and area per person
- Recommendation: make linen and hemp the ship's everyday cloth. Of 8 kg per person-year: 4 kg flax (50 m2 of field), 2.5 kg hemp (12.5 m2), 1 kg wool (310 m2 of pasture, but shared with the sheep's other uses), 0.5 kg cotton or none. Without wool pasture the cropland need is about 65 m2 of field per person-year (0.65 ha per hundred people, about 3 ha per 500-person sector), and a 1 kg wool allowance would add about 15 ha of pasture per sector, which is why wool is kept small (my arithmetic).
- Fibre growing area is on top of food; neither flax nor hemp feeds a person directly, though flax seed (linseed) and hemp seed are edible by-products (not sourced here).
- Both flax and hemp need retting (soaking or dew-rotting) and scutching or decortication, work and water the ship must also plan.
- Confidence: medium; the fibre per person and the hemp yield are the soft numbers.

### D38. Basalt, plainly
- Recommendation: do not make everyday clothes of basalt fibre. Use basalt for fire blankets, outer fire layers and fireproof gloves, rebar, composite panels and filters. Reasons: filaments of 10 to 22 micron are coarse, the maker warns of skin irritation and tells workers to avoid tight clothing (D27); abrasion makes dust (D27, D28); the fibre is classed with the Group 3 vitreous fibres by analogy (D29), so cancer is not the issue; and I found no source of basalt used in ordinary next-to-skin clothing (D30). Brittleness: I did not find a published number for basalt filament flex-fatigue or fracture; the search for it returned nothing (see gaps).
