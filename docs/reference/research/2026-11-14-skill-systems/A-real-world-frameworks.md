# A. Real-world skill frameworks

Notes for the HumanityOS skills design: how real frameworks group skills, how a level is earned, and
what a level means. All reading done 2026-11-14. Each site's robots.txt was read first, in a fetch of
its own. Quotations are exact and under 15 words; everything else is paraphrase. "Source's date" is
the date printed on the source itself, never a guess.

## A1. O*NET Content Model (US Department of Labor)

- **Source:** "The O*NET Content Model", O*NET Resource Center, National Center for O*NET Development
  for the US Department of Labor, https://www.onetcenter.org/content.html ; supporting: "Scales
  Reference" and "Level Scale Anchors", O*NET 31.0 Data Dictionary,
  https://www.onetcenter.org/dictionary/31.0/excel/scales_reference.html and
  https://www.onetcenter.org/dictionary/31.0/excel/level_scale_anchors.html ; "Appendix 4. Content
  Updates", O*NET 30.3 Data Dictionary,
  https://www.onetcenter.org/dictionary/30.3/text/appendix_updates.html ; Tsacoumis and Willison,
  "O*NET Analyst Occupational Skill Ratings: Procedures" (HumRRO report FR-08-70),
  https://www.onetcenter.org/dl_files/AOSkills_Proc.pdf
- **Source's date:** Content Model page: no date on page. Data dictionary pages: "Site updated
  October 6, 2026", data release 31.0 (no month given). The terminology change is listed under
  release 30.3 (no month given). Rating procedures report: March 2010. ; **Read:** 2026-11-14
- **Quote:** "Skills you need for most jobs such as reading, writing, math, and learning." (the
  page's line for Essential Skills)
- **How it organises skills:** Worker Requirements are split into Skills and Knowledge, which O*NET
  keeps apart: knowledge is information and understanding, a skill is the ability to do. Skills come
  in two groups: Essential Skills (10 skills needed in most jobs, in two sub-groups, "Foundational
  Areas" such as reading, listening, writing, speaking, mathematics and science, and "Ways of Working"
  such as critical thinking, active learning, learning strategies and monitoring) and Transferable
  Skills (25 skills usable across jobs: social, complex problem solving, technical, systems, and
  resource management skills). Knowledge has 10 top-level areas: Business and Management;
  Manufacturing and Production; Engineering and Technology; Mathematics and Science; Health Services;
  Education and Training; Arts and Humanities; Law and Public Safety; Communications; Transportation.
  The 30.3 data dictionary says terminology was simplified in that release; search results (not read
  as pages) indicate the older names were "Basic Skills" and "Cross-Functional Skills".
- **How a level is earned or assessed:** O*NET does not certify people; it rates occupations. Each
  skill gets two ratings per occupation: Importance on a 1 to 5 scale (Not Important, Somewhat
  Important, Important, Very Important, Extremely Important) and Level on a 0 to 7 scale. The Level
  scale is anchored with element-specific sample activities at particular points (the 31.0 dictionary
  describes these as sample activities at specific points along the scale), and the anchors rise in
  the amount of skill needed from 1 to 7. Analysts give a Level of 0 only when Importance is 1.
- **What a level gives / means:** A Level number means "how much of this skill the job needs",
  calibrated by a concrete example activity at that point on the scale, not a reward. Importance is a
  separate axis: a skill can be needed at a high level yet matter little to a job, or the reverse.
- **Fit for a game teaching the real skill:** Borrow the knowledge-versus-skill split (knowing about
  soil chemistry is not the same as growing a crop) and, above all, behaviour anchors: every level of
  every skill described by one real example task at that point ("level 2: ...; level 4: ...; level 6:
  ..."). The Essential/Transferable grouping is a useful model for a few shared skills (measuring,
  reading instructions, monitoring, safety) that feed every family. Do not borrow the occupational
  framing or the 35-skill list as the game's skill list: it is built for job matching, not for
  hands-on crafts such as smithing or poi.
- **Confidence:** high for the groups, counts, knowledge areas and the 1-5 and 0-7 scales (read on the
  page and in the March 2010 procedures report); medium for the old-to-new naming (seen only in search
  results; the appendix page confirms a terminology simplification but does not name the old labels).

## A2. ESCO (European Skills, Competences, Qualifications and Occupations)

- **Source:** "Skills & competences" (the skills pillar) and Escopedia entries "Skill", "Skill
  reusability level" and "Transversal knowledge, skills and competences", European Commission,
  https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/classification/skill_main ,
  https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/about-esco/escopedia/escopedia/skill ,
  https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/about-esco/escopedia/escopedia/skill-reusability-level ,
  https://esco.ec.europa.eu/en/about-esco/escopedia/escopedia/transversal-knowledge-skills-and-competences
- **Source's date:** each page prints "Last update 10/12/2025" for ESCO v1.2.1, Release v6.2.0 (date
  as printed; European day/month order would make it 10 December 2025). ; **Read:** 2026-11-14
- **Quote:** "the ability to apply knowledge and use know-how to complete tasks and solve problems"
  (ESCO's definition of skill, which it says it shares with the European Qualifications Framework)
- **How it organises skills:** The skills pillar has four sub-classifications: Knowledge, Language
  skills and knowledge, Skills, and Transversal skills, and every concept is tagged as either a
  skill/competence concept or a knowledge concept. ESCO makes no distinction between skill and
  competence; it describes competence as the broader ability to apply knowledge and skills
  independently in new situations. Each concept carries a reusability level: transversal (useful in
  almost any job, e.g. working in teams), cross-sector (several sectors, e.g. animal welfare),
  sector-specific (one sector, several occupations, e.g. monitoring livestock) and
  occupation-specific (one occupation, e.g. milking operations). Only the transversal skills form a
  strict hierarchy: six main categories, 24 groups, 96 single skills (a structure revised by an expert
  group from 2020).
- **How a level is earned or assessed:** ESCO does not assess or grade anyone and has no proficiency
  scale; it is a vocabulary linking skills to occupations and qualifications. Assessment and grading
  are left to the qualifications it links to.
- **What a level gives / means:** ESCO's "levels" are levels of reusability (how widely a skill
  transfers), not levels of mastery.
- **Fit for a game teaching the real skill:** The reusability idea is a strong shape for skill
  families: a few transversal roots (safety, measuring, planning, teamwork), cross-sector skills
  under them (animal welfare, tool care), then sector and specialist skills (milking under husbandry),
  so practising a specialist skill can also feed its parents. ESCO's "skill contextualisation" (a
  general skill made specific by its context) suggests one generic skill shown in several domains
  rather than duplicated. Do not borrow any of it for levelling: it has no grades at all.
- **Confidence:** high (all statements read on ESCO's own pages; the six main transversal categories
  were not named on the page I read, only counted).

## A3. Apprenticeship occupational standards (England: Skills England, formerly the Institute for Apprenticeships and Technical Education)

- **Source:** "Developing an occupational standard", Skills England, GOV.UK,
  https://www.gov.uk/guidance/developing-an-occupational-standard ; "End-point assessment guide for
  apprentices", GOV.UK,
  https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/end-point-assessment-guide-for-apprentices/end-point-assessment-guide-for-apprentices
  ; worked example "Farrier" standard ST0172, Skills England,
  https://skillsengland.education.gov.uk/apprenticeship-standards/st0172?view=epa and its
  "End-point assessment plan for Farrier apprenticeship standard" ST0172/V1.4,
  https://skillsengland.education.gov.uk/media/logp1mc0/st0172_farrier_l3_epa_v14-june-2024.pdf
- **Source's date:** "Developing an occupational standard": published 2 June 2025. EPA guide: last
  updated 11 September 2026. Farrier standard page: version 1.6, approved for delivery 4 July 2019,
  updated 16/08/2024. EPA plan read: ST0172/V1.4, Crown copyright 2024, file named June 2024 (the
  standard page now links a V1.5 plan, which I did not read). ; **Read:** 2026-11-14
- **Quote:** "All assessment methods must be a distinction to gain a distinction overall." (Farrier
  EPA plan V1.4)
- **How it organises skills:** Each occupation starts from a profile of its duties, and every
  standard states the knowledge, skills and behaviours (KSBs) needed to be competent in those duties,
  each KSB traced back to a duty. Skills England's guidance asks for about 15 to 20 knowledge
  statements, 15 to 20 skill statements and 5 to 6 behaviour statements, and says skill statements
  should start with an active verb (not "can" or "be able to"). Behaviours (attitudes such as safety
  and professionalism) are listed alongside, not inside, the skills.
- **How a level is earned or assessed:** After on-programme training (farrier: typically 42 months,
  minimum 12), the apprentice reaches a "gateway": the employer confirms they consistently work at the
  standard and set evidence is ready (farrier: a portfolio plus a "shoe board" of roughly 8 to 12
  shoes they made, and level 2 English and maths). Then an independent end-point assessment by an
  approved organisation, made of several separate methods; for the farrier, a written test, a
  professional discussion and two practical assessments (an unshod trim and a shoeing test), each
  marked by independent assessors and weighted equally. Each method is graded fail, pass or
  distinction; a fail in any method fails the whole; pass needs every method at least a pass;
  distinction needs every method at distinction. A failed method can be re-sat (no further learning)
  or re-taken (after further learning), but a re-sit is never offered to raise a pass to a
  distinction, and the EPA guide adds that you cannot re-sit a part already passed to improve it.
- **What a level gives / means:** Passing certifies occupational competence for that standard at its
  level (farrier is level 3); a distinction marks performance above the pass criteria in every method.
  A completion certificate is not issued without it.
- **Fit for a game teaching the real skill:** The richest model for a "level-up exam" in a real
  skill: separate knowledge (quiz), practical demonstration (do the task, observed against criteria),
  a portfolio of things the player actually made (the shoe board is a perfect game mechanic), and a
  discussion (explain why). The all-methods-must-pass rule stops a strong quiz score hiding weak hands,
  and "no re-sit to upgrade" keeps grades honest. Borrow the weakest-link combination and the portfolio
  gateway; do not borrow the multi-year duration or the bureaucracy (EPAO approval, funding bands).
  The US Department of Labor's Competency Model Clearinghouse pyramid could not be read (see below).
- **Confidence:** high for the farrier plan (read in full text from the official PDF) and the KSB
  guidance; medium for the general rules, which vary by standard (each standard publishes its own
  plan; an electrician standard, seen only in search results, grades pass, merit and distinction by
  percentage).

## A4. The Dreyfus model of skill acquisition

- **Source:** A. Peña, "The Dreyfus model of clinical problem-solving skills acquisition: a critical
  perspective", Medical Education Online, vol. 15 (open access, peer reviewed), PubMed Central,
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2887319/ . It cites the original: S. Dreyfus and H.
  Dreyfus, "A five stage model of the mental activities involved in directed skill acquisition",
  University of California, Berkeley, Operations Research Center, 1980. The 1980 report itself (DTIC
  accession ADA084551, dated February 1980 per search results) and Stuart Dreyfus's 2004 summary
  article could not be read (see below).
- **Source's date:** Peña article: published 2010 Jun 14 (as printed on the page). Original report:
  1980 (as cited by Peña). ; **Read:** 2026-11-14
- **Quote:** "Dreyfus' main idea that intuition is a major aspect of expertise" (Peña, abstract)
- **How it organises skills:** It does not group skills; it describes five stages any one skill
  passes through, as Peña presents them: novice (follows context-free rules, takes no responsibility
  beyond applying them), advanced beginner (with real experience starts to notice situational
  features and learns maxims), competent (after much experience, chooses plans and guidelines and
  becomes emotionally invested in the outcome), proficient (recognises what is happening intuitively
  and builds personal rules to decide what to do), expert (fluid, mostly unconscious performance on
  implicit knowledge, falling back on analysis when intuition fails). Stage labels differ between
  versions: search results say the 1980 report ended with a "mastery" stage and later versions ended
  at expert, while Peña treats advanced beginner as original and mentions later additions ("master",
  "practical wisdom"). I could not check the 1980 wording myself.
- **How a level is earned or assessed:** The model has no test. A person moves up through accumulated
  concrete experience, and the change is in how they perceive and decide (from rules to recognition),
  not in how much they know.
- **What a level gives / means:** Each stage means a different relationship to rules: the novice
  needs them spelled out, the expert sees what to do and needs rules only when stuck. Peña's critique:
  the original Air Force reports cite few studies, the model leans on phenomenology rather than
  measured evidence, and experts also reason analytically, so its fit for clinical (diagnostic)
  skills is debatable.
- **Fit for a game teaching the real skill:** The best idea here is what a level GIVES: scaffolding
  that fades. A novice gets explicit step-by-step guidance and context-free rules; higher stages get
  fewer prompts and more situational cues, ending with no prompts at all. Use the five names as the
  plain-language meaning of level bands, not as a scoring formula. Do not treat it as validated
  science: Peña finds the empirical support thin, so present it as a teaching heuristic.
- **Confidence:** medium (an authoritative peer-reviewed secondary source read in full; the primary
  1980 report was not readable, and sources disagree on the original stage names).

## A5. Scouting badges (Scouting America could not be read; Scouts Victoria's Outdoor Adventure Skills used instead)

- **Source:** "About the Outdoor Adventure Skills" and "Outdoor Adventure Skills FAQ's", Scouts
  Victoria (the Victorian branch of Scouts Australia),
  https://scoutsvictoria.com.au/activities-events/outdoor-adventure-skills/ and
  https://scoutsvictoria.com.au/activities-events/outdoor-adventure-skills/oas-faqs/ . Scouting
  America's own site (scouting.org) refused automated reading, so its merit badge counselor process
  is NOT covered from a primary source.
- **Source's date:** both pages show only "Copyright © 2017"; the FAQ links a Peak Awards factsheet
  whose file name refers to February 2023. ; **Read:** 2026-11-14
- **Quote:** "show competency in all the I-statements of the lower Stages" (FAQ page)
- **How it organises skills:** Nine skill areas: three core (Bushcraft, Bushwalking, Camping) and six
  specialist (land: Alpine, Cycling, Vertical, which covers abseiling, caving and climbing; water:
  Aquatic, Boating, Paddling). Each area has stages numbered 1 to 9, and each stage is a list of
  "I" statements (things the member can do or knows). The framework is for all ages and abilities;
  stages are not tied to the age sections.
- **How a level is earned or assessed:** The member works through one stage's statements at a time,
  demonstrating each, and a higher stage never excuses the lower stages' statements. Verification is
  "two down": a verifier must be at least two stages above the member, and can be another youth
  member, a leader holding a Certificate of Proficiency (core stages 1 to 5, specialist 1 to 4), an
  adventurous activity leader or guide for stage 5 and above, or an outside specialist whose
  qualifications are checked. Skills gained outside Scouting can be claimed by self-assessment plus
  evidence (photos, video, witness sign-offs) reviewed by the unit council.
- **What a level gives / means:** A stage completed is a badge-level record of what the member can
  do in that area; stage progressions are tallied toward each section's peak award (the top award),
  and progressions beyond one section's need carry into the next section's award.
- **Fit for a game teaching the real skill:** Very close to what a game needs: per-skill stages open
  to any age, written as "I can" statements a player actually performs, all lower statements
  required, and progressions that roll up into a broader award (the scouting badge-to-rank link).
  The "two down" rule is a ready-made multiplayer teaching loop: a player two stages ahead (or an NPC
  mentor) can sign off a learner, and higher stages need a qualified specialist. Borrow the evidence
  route for skills learned in real life (photo or video, reviewed), which suits the game's real-life
  twin.
- **Confidence:** medium (Scouts Victoria pages read directly; the stage count and verification rules
  are clear, but this is a state branch, and it substitutes for the merit badge source the brief named).

## A6. Martial arts grading (British Judo Association)

- **Source:** "Kyu Grade Scheme", British Judo Association,
  https://www.britishjudo.org.uk/get-started/grading/kyu-grade-scheme/ ; "British Judo Association Dan
  Grade Promotion Syllabus",
  https://www.britishjudo.org.uk/wp-content/uploads/2025/07/Dan-Grade-Promotion-Syllabus-July-2025-V2.pdf
- **Source's date:** Dan syllabus: "Issued July 2025", implementation date 1st January 2026. Kyu
  scheme page: no date on page; it links a "Kyu Grade Syllabus Booklet (As of February 2025)".
  ; **Read:** 2026-11-14
- **Quote:** "the emphasis shifts away from the acquisition of technique on to its effective
  application" (Dan syllabus, on the kyu-to-dan boundary)
- **How it organises skills:** Two tiers: six kyu (student) grades with coloured belts, then dan
  (advanced) grades, black to 5th dan, red and white from 6th, red at 9th and 10th; the BJA awards to
  5th dan and the International Judo Federation awards 7th dan and above. Each grade has a set of
  named techniques, Japanese terms (and their meanings) and, at dan level, a named kata (a fixed
  demonstration form); the dan syllabus lists Nage-no-kata for 1st dan through Kodokan Goshin-jutsu
  for 5th, with six kata required for 7th dan and above.
- **How a level is earned or assessed:** Kyu grades: candidates practically demonstrate the grade's
  techniques, know the terms, and at higher grades explain their choice of technique and grip;
  gradings up to 4th kyu are run in the club by a licensed coach, and most people finish the kyu
  system in about three years. Dan grades, competitive route: 100 to 120 contest points (from
  promotion examinations or competition), plus a minimum time in grade that grows with each step (1
  year as 1st kyu, then 2, 3, 4, 5 years for 2nd to 5th dan) and a minimum age (15 for 1st dan, rising
  to 29 for 5th), plus a technical and kata examination. A technical route examines knowledge and
  demonstration with longer time in grade, and there are routes by outstanding performance
  (international competitors, referees, coaches, officials) and a once-only "qualified by
  experience" award for judoka aged 70 and over. Time in grade only counts while BJA membership is
  current.
- **What a level gives / means:** A belt shows tested ability within the grade system; the syllabus
  describes the grades filling with judoka sorted by proven ability. Higher dan grades also mark
  long service, and high-grade colour belts are worn mainly when instructing, coaching or at
  ceremonies.
- **Fit for a game teaching the real skill:** Borrow three things: a demonstration-based grading
  where you perform named techniques and explain your choices; a minimum time (practice) in grade
  that lengthens at each step, so high levels cannot be rushed; and several routes to the same grade
  (contest results, technical demonstration, service as a coach or referee). The kyu-to-dan shift
  (from learning the technique to applying it under pressure) is a good line between a skill's
  learning levels and its mastery levels. Do not copy minimum ages or membership rules.
- **Confidence:** high (the dan syllabus was read in full text from the official PDF; the kyu page was
  read directly). The IJF's own robots.txt allowed reading, but no IJF grading page was read.

## A7. Flow arts and prop manipulation grading

- **Source:** "Skills" (Skill Level Achievement Program), World Juggling Federation,
  https://www.thewjf.com/skills ; "What is the U.S. PreTrials program?", United States Twirling
  Association, https://ustwirling.com/news/individual-stories/2022/2-4/pretrials-program/index.html ;
  "Levels Flow arts", University of Bern university sport (ZSSW),
  https://www.zssw.unibe.ch/usp/zms/niveau/242/en/Niveaubeschrieb_Flow%20Arts_EN.pdf ; also the
  International Jugglers' Association programs page, https://www.juggle.org/programs/
- **Source's date:** WJF page: "Skill Level Achievement Program V1.0 2021 Edition" (page footer
  copyright 2026). USTA article: no date on page (its image path says 2022, not a reliable date).
  Bern level sheet: no date on document. IJA programs page: copyright 2026. ; **Read:** 2026-11-14
- **Quote:** "Level certification is an all or nothing certification." (WJF skills page)
- **How it organises skills:** Juggling (WJF): 13 levels, Future Olympian then 1A to 1C, 2A to 2C,
  3A to 3C and 4A to 4C, across balls, rings and clubs; from 3A a juggler can certify overall or per
  prop. Baton twirling (USTA): competition levels A, AA, AAA and Elite, plus a ladder of
  "Compulsories" and "Movement Technique" test levels running from C upward. Poi, staff and other
  flow arts: I found no grading body or published level scheme. The University of Bern's sport
  programme states plainly that no performance levels have been defined for flow arts, and the
  search turned up only per-school course labels (beginner, intermediate) and the IJA's youth
  achievement programme, whose levels are not described on its page.
- **How a level is earned or assessed:** WJF: each level is a list of measurable feats (for example
  six catches of a three-ball cascade without a drop at the entry level, up to 100-catch six-ball and
  50-catch seven-ball runs at 4C), proved by a video submission judged for drops, foot movement,
  reaching, collisions and uneven patterns; a level is passed whole or not at all, and a failed
  attempt comes back with the reasons and advice. USTA: individuals must pass every Compulsories and
  Movement Technique level from C up to their competition level, and AAA and Elite twirlers also need
  a qualifying score at a regional competition.
- **What a level gives / means:** WJF: a "stamped" video on its channel, a certificate and the name
  listed at that level; only the top level (4C) must be reapplied for each year. USTA: entry to
  higher competition (U.S. PreTrials).
- **Fit for a game teaching the real skill:** For poi and staff the game would be building the first
  graded scheme, and should say so. WJF shows the pattern to copy: levels as countable feats (catches,
  rotations, transitions without a drop), all-or-nothing, judged on clear faults, with feedback on
  failure, which a game can check from motion data or a recorded video. The USTA split between
  technique tests and competition results suggests two tracks: a skill ladder anyone can pass, and
  performance or competition standing on top.
- **Confidence:** medium (WJF and the Bern sheet read directly; the USTA test content and who judges
  it live in a members-only manual, and the USTA judge page refused automated reading; "no poi or
  staff grading exists" rests on one search and one university statement, so it is an absence of
  evidence, not proof).

## A8. Certificates that expire: first aid and a trade licence

- **Source:** "Are you a first-aider?" and "Frequently asked questions on first aid", Health and
  Safety Executive (UK regulator), https://www.hse.gov.uk/firstaid/first-aider.htm and
  https://www.hse.gov.uk/firstaid/faqs.htm ; trade example: "Electrician", Washington State Department
  of Labor & Industries,
  https://lni.wa.gov/licensing-permits/electrical/electrical-licensing-exams-education/electrician .
  (The American Red Cross site refused automated reading.)
- **Source's date:** HSE first-aider page: shows 2025-03-21 (not labelled as updated or reviewed). HSE
  FAQ page: 2025-09-04. L&I electrician page: no date on page. ; **Read:** 2026-11-14
- **Quote:** "Certificates for the purposes of first aid at work last for three years." (HSE)
- **How it organises skills:** HSE recognises courses rather than skill levels: a three-day First Aid
  at Work course and a one-day Emergency First Aid at Work course, with syllabus guidance for each.
  Washington licenses electricians by certificate type (trainee, journey-level, specialty and so on),
  each with its own renewal rules.
- **How a level is earned or assessed:** First aid: a training course; the certificate lasts three
  years, the first-aider must take a requalification course before it expires to get another three
  years, and once it has expired the person is no longer considered competent to act as a workplace
  first-aider. HSE strongly recommends (but does not require) an annual refresher so first-aiders keep
  their basic skills current and keep up with changed procedures. Electrician: the certificate
  expires on the holder's birthday every three years; renewal needs 24 hours of approved continuing
  education (8 on code updates, 4 on the state's electrical laws and rules); fees double after
  expiry; a certificate expired more than 90 days cannot be renewed and the holder must retest; paying
  without the hours leaves the certificate inactive.
- **What a level gives / means:** The certificate means "currently competent", not "once trained":
  it is a dated claim that lapses, and lapsing removes the right to act in that role until requalified
  (or, after a long lapse, re-examined).
- **Fit for a game teaching the real skill:** Separate two things the real world separates:
  competence (the level, earned once) and currency (recent practice and refreshers, which run out).
  A game can keep the level permanently but show a "last practised" or "current until" date that a
  short refresher renews, and on the real-life side track real certificates with expiry reminders.
  Hard loss of the level after a long lapse (the 90-day retest rule) is realistic but punishing;
  better offered in the full-realism mode only, per the project's dual-mode rule.
- **Confidence:** high (both regulators' own pages read directly; the HSE pages do not mention any
  grace period, and the L&I page carries no date).
