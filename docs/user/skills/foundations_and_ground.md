# Foundations and Ground

Every load in a building ends up in the ground. The roof, the walls, the
people and the snow all travel down through the frame, as [How a
Building Stands Up](how_a_building_stands_up.md) describes, and the
foundation's job is to hand that load to the soil in a way the soil can
take. Get it right and nobody ever thinks about it again. Get it wrong
and the house settles unevenly, the doors stick, the walls crack, the
floor goes out of level, and fixing it means lifting the building.

A foundation does four things:

- **It spreads the load** over enough ground that the ground does not
  sink under it.
- **It reaches ground that does not move**: below the soft topsoil,
  below loose fill, and below the depth where the ground freezes.
- **It keeps the building's wood away from wet ground**, where it would
  rot and where termites live.
- **It holds the building in place** against wind trying to lift it and
  earthquakes trying to slide it.

The Army's carpentry manual sums up the starting point: "Foundations
vary according to their use, the soil-bearing capacity, and the type of
material available." This guide is about the middle term of that
sentence, the ground, and what it asks of the foundation.

Its sources are the federal rules for installing manufactured homes (24
CFR Part 3285, from the Department of Housing and Urban Development,
which gives plain numbers for soil and footings), the US Army's manuals on carpentry (FM 5-426) and soils
engineering (FM 5-410), the Forest Service's handbook on wood-frame
house construction, the Environmental Protection Agency's (EPA) guidance
on moisture in buildings, the Occupational Safety and Health
Administration's (OSHA) excavation rules, the Pipeline and Hazardous
Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA), and a guide prepared for HUD on
inspecting older houses. All are United States government publications.
Where something is arithmetic, general practice or our own reading, the
text says so.

Before you build anything, two other guides come first. [Choosing Where
to Build](choosing_where_to_build.md) is about picking ground that will
hold a building at all: slopes, flooding, wet ground. [Your Soil,
Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) shows how to look up what the
soil survey says about your exact ground. This guide assumes you have a
site and want to put something on it.

## First: where foundation work hurts people

Foundation work is digging, lifting and concrete, usually in a hole.
Each of those can hurt you.

### Before you dig

PHMSA, the federal pipeline safety agency, says: "Every digging project
requires that you call the one-call center before digging", even small
projects like planting trees or shrubs. In the United States you dial
811, the call and the marking are free, and the companies that
own buried lines must mark them with flags or paint before you dig;
state laws set how much notice you must give. "Call 811 before you dig,
every time." Lines that you or an earlier owner put in, to a shed, a
well, a septic tank or a propane tank, are generally not marked, so find
them from your own records first (general practice). [Square, Level and
Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md) covers the call and what the marks
mean in more detail.

### Trenches and holes

OSHA's excavation rule (29 CFR 1926.652) requires every worker in an
excavation to be protected from cave-ins by a designed protective
system, such as shoring or sloped sides, unless the excavation is
entirely in stable rock or is less than 5 feet (1.52 m) deep and a
trained "competent person" has examined the ground and found no sign of
a cave-in. A homeowner is not that person. **Do not get into any trench
or hole deeper than your waist** (general practice, set well below
OSHA's 5 feet for that reason). Keep the dug soil, and your tools and
materials, at least 2 feet (0.61 m) back from the edge, as OSHA's rule
requires (29 CFR 1926.651(j)(2)), so they cannot fall or roll in.

### Digging beside a foundation that is already there

A footing is held in place partly by the soil around and beside it, and
if that soil is taken away the footing can slide or tip towards the hole
(our explanation; HUD's inspection guide describes a footing rotating,
and the wall above it buckling, when water washes the earth away along
one side of it). OSHA's rule forbids excavation "below the level of the base or
footing of any foundation or retaining wall that could be reasonably
expected to pose a hazard to employees" unless the structure is held up
by a support system such as underpinning, the excavation is in stable
rock, or a registered professional engineer has approved it (29 CFR
1926.651(i)(2)). The same rule says sidewalks and pavements shall not be
undermined without protection against their collapse. At home: never dig
below the bottom of a footing beside it, yours or your neighbour's,
without an engineer (our reading of the rule).

### Concrete and cement

Wet cement and lime can burn skin and eyes. [Ratios and
Mixing](ratios_and_mixing.md) has OSHA's advice on gloves, boots, eye
protection and first aid, and lifting heavy stone and block safely
is in [Stone, Clay and Earth](stone_clay_and_earth.md). Read both before
you mix or lay anything.

### Forms and shoring

Concrete is poured into forms, usually of wood, and the Army's manual
lists the cautions for form work:

- "Watch for protruding nails. They are the principal cause of accidents
  on form work."
- "Place mud sills under shoring that rests on the ground."
- "Do not raise large form panels in heavy gusts of wind."
- "Brace all shoring securely to prevent collapse of form work."
- Do not remove forms until the concrete has set.

### Lifting a building

Levelling a sagging floor or a settled house means jacking it up, and
everything in [Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md) about
jacks applies, above all never being under a load held only by a jack.
HUD's guide to inspecting older houses says that jacking a sagging floor
back towards level, where it is possible at all, must be done slowly and
carefully, and that a roof that has spread should be looked at by a
structural engineer before anyone tries to jack it back (restated).
Lifting or underpinning a house is professional work (general practice).

## What the ground can carry

Soil is not all the same, and the difference is large. The federal rule
for installing manufactured homes gives a clear public statement of how
large. It starts with the ground itself (24 CFR 3285.201): "To help
prevent settling or sagging, the foundation must be constructed on firm,
undisturbed soil or fill compacted to at least 90 percent of its maximum
relative density. All organic material such as grass, roots, twigs, and
wood scraps must be removed in areas where footings are to be placed."

It then requires the bearing capacity of the soil to be found before the
foundation is built (24 CFR 3285.202), by soil tests, by the soil records
of the local authority, by a pocket penetrometer, or, where the type of
soil can be identified, from its table of allowable pressures. In
outline (the table's own wording is longer):

| Soil | Allowable pressure (pounds per square foot) | About (kilopascals) |
|---|---|---|
| Rock or hard pan | 4,000 and more | 190 and more |
| Sandy gravel and gravel; very dense or cemented sands | 2,000 | 96 |
| Sand, silty sand, clayey sand, silty gravel; very stiff silts and sandy clays | 1,500 | 72 |
| Loose to medium dense sands; loose sands; firm to stiff clays and silts; alluvial fills | 1,000 | 48 |
| Uncompacted fill, peat, organic clays | not given: a professional decides | |

(The kilopascal column is our conversion.) Three more parts of the rule
matter as much as the table:

- **A default.** Instead of the other methods, "an allowable pressure of
  1,500 psf may be used, unless the site-specific information requires
  the use of lower values based on soil classification and type."
- **A stop.** "If the soil appears to be composed of peat, organic
  clays, or uncompacted fill, or appears to have unusual conditions, a
  registered professional geologist, registered professional engineer,
  or registered architect must determine the soil classification and
  maximum allowable soil bearing capacity."
- **A warning about the numbers.** The table's note says its values
  "have not been adjusted for overburden pressure, embedment depth,
  water table height, or settlement problems."

That table is the rule for manufactured homes. A house built on site is
governed by the building code where you live, which has its own table
(general practice: ask the building department). The point to take from
it is the range: rock carries four times what the weakest listed soil
carries, and some ground is not to be built on without an
expert at all.

### Ground that will not hold

The Army's soils manual (FM 5-410) describes peat and other highly
organic soils as having "many undesirable engineering characteristics",
and says they "can be easily identified in the field by their
distinctive color and odor, spongy feel, and frequently fibrous
texture." It also warns that soils with a high clay content may swell a
great deal if they are packed down dry and then get wet. The soil
survey rates this as shrink-swell, and [Your Soil,
Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) shows where to read your soil's
ratings for dwellings with and without basements and for shallow
excavations.

Fill is the other trap. The Forest Service's 1955 handbook on wood-frame
houses says that where fill has been used, "the foundations should
extend below the fill to undisturbed earth", and the manufactured home
rule allows fill only if it is compacted to 90 percent of its maximum
density. Ground that someone has dug and put back, or levelled with
spoil, is not the same ground it was, and on a site that has been built
on before, the only way to know is to dig and look (general practice).

## Footings: spreading the load

A footing is the wide base at the bottom of a foundation wall or under a
post. The 1955 handbook gives its job: "The footings act as the base of
the foundation and transmit the superimposed load to the soil." The
arithmetic behind its size is simple: **the pressure on the soil is the
load divided by the area of the footing**, so a footing must be big
enough that the pressure stays below what the soil can carry (our
statement of the arithmetic).

**Worked example (our arithmetic).** A post carries 2,000 pounds, a
number chosen to show the method, not a design load. On soil allowed
1,500 pounds per square foot, the footing needs at least 2,000 / 1,500 =
1.33 square feet, which is a square about 14 inches (36 cm) on a side.
On soil allowed 1,000 pounds per square foot, it needs 2 square feet,
about 17 inches (43 cm) on a side.

The manufactured home rule's footing table (24 CFR 3285.312) does the
same sum with the footing's own weight taken off, since the soil has to
carry the concrete as well (its notes say the capacities "have been
reduced by the dead load of the concrete footing"). Some of its figures
for unreinforced poured footings under an 8 by 16 inch pier:

| Soil (psf) | 16 x 16 in footing | 20 x 20 in footing | 24 x 24 in footing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 | 1,600 lb | 2,600 lb | 3,700 lb |
| 1,500 | 2,500 lb | 4,000 lb | 5,600 lb |
| 2,000 | 3,400 lb | 5,300 lb | 7,600 lb |

Read across a row: a footing 1.25 times as wide carries a little over
1.5 times as much, because its area grows with the square of its width
(our arithmetic). Read down a column: the same footing on better soil
carries more, roughly in proportion. And check the rule's arithmetic for
yourself: a 16 inch square is 1.78 square feet, which on 1,000 psf soil
is 1,778 pounds; a 6 inch thick concrete pad that size weighs about 130
pounds; the difference is about 1,650, which the table rounds to 1,600
(our arithmetic, taking concrete at about 150 pounds per cubic foot).
So the 2,000 pound post in the example above needs the 20 inch footing
on the weaker soil and the 16 inch one on the better.

The same rule sets minimums: poured concrete footings at least 6 inches
thick with a 28-day compressive strength of at least 3,000 pounds per
square inch, footings on undisturbed soil or properly compacted fill,
and "A footing must support every pier." The 1955 handbook adds two
rules worth keeping: footings at least 6 inches thick, and if a footing
trench is dug too deep, fill the extra depth with concrete, never with
dirt put back. A footing that sits on loose soil it was meant to bear on
undisturbed ground will settle (our explanation).

For walls on firm soil, the 1955 handbook gives a rule of thumb it
describes as one method often used: a footing as deep as the wall is
thick, sticking out on each side by half the wall's thickness. It says
in the same breath that footing sizes "should comply with local code
requirements", and that soil of low bearing capacity may need wider,
reinforced footings. Treat it as a way to check a drawing, not a design
(our reading).

The Army's carpentry manual adds three points for its own simple
buildings: wall foundations are used where heavy loads are carried or
"where the earth has low supporting strength"; posts and columns are
usually spaced 6 to 10 feet apart depending on the weight; and "When
wood posts extend 3 feet or more above the ground, braces are
necessary". It also says "Steel-rod reinforcements should be used in all
concrete walls."

## Frost

Water expands when it freezes, and in some soils the freezing does far
more than that. The Army's soils manual explains that "two conditions
must be present simultaneously before frost action is a major
consideration": a source of water during the freezing period, and a
long enough freezing period for the cold to get into the ground. Water
drawn up to the freezing layer builds lenses of ice that lift the ground
above them (our explanation; the manual names the ice lenses). Some
soils are much worse than others: "In general, the silts and fine silty
sands are most susceptible to frost. Coarse-grained
materials with little or no fines are affected only slightly or not at
all." Clays are affected too.

The answer is to put the bottom of the footing below the depth the
ground freezes, so the freezing happens above it. The manual says it
directly of retaining walls: "As with other structures, the bottom of a
retaining wall should be located beneath the line of frost penetration."
The manufactured home rule (24 CFR 3285.312(b)) requires footings in
freezing climates to be placed below the frost line depth for the site,
and says that where the local authority cannot give the frost line
depth, a registered professional engineer, architect or geologist must
determine it. Frost depth is local: get the number for your site from
the building department (general practice).

Two other designs can sit above the frost line, a monolithic slab and an
insulated foundation, and the same rule allows them only when they are
designed by a registered professional engineer or architect to prevent
frost heave. We read that as a sign of how much can go wrong with them.

What frost heave looks like in an old building: HUD's inspection guide
says a footing or pier that is too shallow, or has no proper footing,
can be lifted or tipped by frost, with movement in the structure above
it like that caused by settlement (restated).

## Water: the foundation's other enemy

The EPA's guidance on moisture control in buildings (EPA 402-F-13053,
December 2013) explains why foundations get wet: rain and leaks run
downhill to them, they are cooler than summer air so water can condense
on them, and "Crawl spaces and basements are holes in the ground and
have more extensive contact with soil than slab-on-grade foundations."
Its guidance, for slabs, crawl spaces and basements alike:

- **Slope the ground away.** It specifies a fall of 5 percent, which is
  6 inches over 10 feet, in the finished grade away from the foundation,
  or a stricter local code requirement, and says that applying the slope
  over the first 6 to 10 feet is generally acceptable. (The manufactured
  home rule asks for at least half an inch per foot for the first ten
  feet, about 4 percent; checking a fall is in [Square, Level and
  Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md).)
- **Keep the foundation out of the ground.** "Design the foundation and
  surrounding grade so there is a minimum of 8 inches of exposed
  foundation after the final grading."
- **Break the wick.** Water climbs through concrete and masonry the way
  it climbs a paper towel (our comparison), so the guidance calls for
  capillary breaks to keep it from wicking into the framing:
  a sill seal, flashing or damp-proof course between the concrete and the
  wood framing on top of it; a layer of coarse gravel with no fines, a
  membrane or foam board between the earth and a floor slab; and a
  coating between the top of a footing and the wall on it. "While coarse
  stone will provide a capillary break, a vapor barrier directly beneath
  the slab is required to manage water vapor migration."
- **Drain below the floor.** For basements and crawl spaces, a curtain
  of free-draining material against the wall and a perforated drain
  below the top of the footing or the bottom of the slab, led to
  daylight or a sump.
- **Stay above the water.** It says to design a basement or crawl space
  so its floor is above the 100-year flood level and the local water
  table.

The Army's manual makes the same point about slabs in fewer words: "A
concrete floor is likely to be damp unless it is protected."

For a crawl space closed in with skirting, the manufactured home rule
(24 CFR 3285.204) requires the ground under the home to be covered with
a vapour retarder of at least six mil polyethylene sheeting (about 0.15
mm), with joints overlapped at least 12 inches, unless the site is in an
arid region with dry soil. The 1955 handbook asks for at least 18 inches
between the ground and the underside of the floor joists in a crawl
space, so the space can be inspected, and foundation walls high enough
above the ground that termite tubes running from soil to wood can be
seen before they do damage. [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md) covers what moisture under a house
does to the air above it, and [The Ground Under You](the_ground_under_you.md)
covers radon, a gas from the ground that enters through foundations.

### What moves a foundation

HUD's inspection guide lists the usual causes of uneven settlement in a
house (restated): soil compressing under the footings; soil shrinking as
nearby trees and large plants draw the moisture out of it; soil swelling
when surface or house drainage is poor or blocked; heaving from frost or
heavy root growth; clay soils creeping slowly downhill on slopes; changes
in the water table; soil washed out from around footings by poor
drainage, faulty drains or leaking water pipes; and compaction or
movement from vibration, heavy traffic or earthquakes. Much of that list
is water, which is why the drainage above is foundation work (our
reading).

## Kinds of foundation

The Army's manual divides foundations into wall foundations, which are
"solid their total length", and column or pier foundations, posts or
piers carrying the building at points. Among ordinary houses, the
common kinds are (general practice):

- **A slab on the ground**, concrete poured directly on prepared ground,
  thickened at the edges or set on a wall foundation, with a capillary
  break and vapour barrier under it.
- **A crawl space**, a short foundation wall or a ring of piers that
  holds the floor framing above the ground.
- **A basement**, a full-height foundation wall that is also a room.
  With a crawl space, the EPA notes, it has more contact with the soil
  than a slab does.
- **Piers or posts** on separate footings, the usual choice for sheds,
  decks and porches.

### Wood in or on the ground

Wood that touches soil or stays wet decays. The *Wood Handbook*'s
chapter on preservatives (FPL-GTR-282, 2021) describes the use
categories that treated wood is sold under: "UC3 is for exterior wood
used above ground; UC4 is for wood used in ground contact in exterior
applications." It adds that the end tag on treated lumber should show
the preservative, the retention (how much is in the wood) and the
intended use category, and that items with a high replacement cost,
house foundations among them, are required to be treated to higher
retention levels. A post set in the ground needs lumber whose tag says
ground contact; a board sold for above-ground use is not the same thing
(our reading). [Rust, Rot and Decay](rust_rot_and_decay.md) covers
reading treated wood and why decay starts where wood stays wet.

### Holding the building down

The Army's manual says the sill plate, the first piece of wood on a
foundation, "is anchored to the foundation wall", and suggests a sill
sealer, and a termite shield if wanted, underneath. Anchoring is not a
detail. HUD's guide warns that in earthquake country, wood-frame houses
not anchored to their foundations can shift or slide (restated), and
the Department of Energy's Building America guide to the load path lists
"wall-to-foundation connections" among the connections a building needs
to resist wind uplift and sideways loads. How many anchor bolts, what
size and how deep is set by the building code (general practice).

## Reading a foundation you already have

HUD's inspection guide (restated throughout this section) gives a way to
read the foundation of an existing house.

- **Signs of uneven settlement**: walls cracked or pushed out of plumb,
  door and window openings out of square, sloping floors, doors and
  windows that stick.
- **When it happens.** Settlement most often happens early in a
  building's life, or when conditions underground change sharply. It
  often slows soon after construction and stops.
- **What a crack tells you.** A crack that tapers, wide at one end and
  hairline at the other, points to settlement. A clean crack suggests
  recent movement; a dirty or previously filled one may be inactive.
  Compare a crack's width with the building's age: half an inch in a new
  house may be rapid settlement, while in a fifty-year-old house it may
  be very slow movement, about a hundredth of an inch a year. Measure it
  over months before deciding.
- **Not every crack is settlement.** Many masonry cracks blamed on
  settlement come from moisture and temperature movement, and cracks
  that open and close with the seasons should be sealed with a flexible
  sealant, not mortar.
- **Piers.** Check them for plumb, for settling or rotation of their
  footings, for frost heave (most common under porches), and for
  vertical cracking or bulging, which are signs of overstress.
- **Who to call.** HUD's guide advises a structural engineer for masonry
  and foundation problems, and a soils engineer as well if settlement is
  the cause.

## Worked example: posts for a garden shed

You are putting an 8 by 12 foot shed on six posts, three down each long
side, 6 feet apart. This is a procedure; the loads are numbers chosen
for the arithmetic.

1. **Call 811** and wait for the marks. Find your own buried lines.
2. **Look up your soil** in the soil survey ([Your Soil,
   Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md)), and dig a test hole to the
   footing depth to see it. Peat, organic soil, a high water table or
   fill means stop and ask.
3. **Find the frost depth** for your site from the building department.
4. **Work out each post's share.** Each middle post carries the floor
   and roof for 3 feet either side of it along the wall, and half the
   width of the shed: 6 feet by 4 feet, 24 square feet. Each corner post
   carries 3 feet by 4 feet, 12 square feet (our arithmetic). The middle
   posts carry twice what the corners do, which is the pattern wherever
   supports are evenly spaced.
5. **Turn area into load.** Suppose, for the example, that the shed,
   its contents and the design snow together come to 80 pounds per
   square foot. A middle post then carries 24 x 80 = 1,920 pounds, a
   corner post 960 (our arithmetic). Your building department, or a span
   and load table it accepts, gives the real figure for your place.
6. **Size the footings.** Using the HUD table as a guide to the
   arithmetic: on soil allowed 1,500 pounds per square foot, its 16 inch
   square footing carries 2,500 pounds, enough for every post. On soil allowed only 1,000, the 16 inch footing
   carries 1,600: enough for the corners, not the middle posts, which
   need the 20 inch footing at 2,600.
7. **Set them deep enough**, with their bottoms below the frost depth,
   on undisturbed ground, in a hole you never had to get into above
   your waist.
8. **Use ground-contact lumber** for anything in or near the soil, keep
   the wood off the ground, slope the ground away, and anchor the shed
   to its posts, because wind lifts sheds.

## Know where your own work stops

A shed, a deck footing or a garden wall on good ground is work you can
learn. These are past this guide:

- **A foundation for anything people live or sleep in.** Its design
  depends on the soil, the loads, the frost depth and the code where you
  live; it needs the building department, and often an engineer (general
  practice).
- **Peat, organic clay, uncompacted fill or unusual ground.** The
  manufactured home rule requires a registered professional to decide
  the bearing capacity, and so should you.
- **Swelling clays, high water tables, slopes and flood zones.** See
  [Choosing Where to Build](choosing_where_to_build.md); these are
  engineering questions.
- **Slabs or insulated foundations above the frost line.** The federal
  rule requires an engineer or architect to design them.
- **Digging below an existing footing, underpinning, lifting a building,
  or repairing a settled foundation.** OSHA's rule and HUD's guide both
  point to engineers.
- **Any trench or hole deeper than your waist.**

## Traps

- **"The ground looks firm."** Fill, peat and clay that swells can all
  look firm in a dry summer (general practice).
- **Putting the dirt back.** A footing hole dug too deep gets concrete,
  not loose soil.
- **Ignoring frost because the winter was mild.** The footing has to
  survive the hard winters too.
- **Wood touching soil.** Untreated wood on the ground, or above-ground
  lumber in it, rots.
- **Water running towards the house.** Much of the trouble on HUD's
  list is water.
- **Undermining a neighbour.** A hole beside an existing footing can
  take the footing with it.

## How the game models it

The game has foundations, but not ground that matters to them.

- **Two foundations.** From the Crafting page you can build a Wood
  Foundation, 4 by 4 metres and 0.2 m thick, from 8 Wood Planks, or a
  Stone Foundation, 4 by 4 metres and 0.3 m thick, from 12 Stone Bricks
  (`data/blueprints/basic.ron`). A wall or a piece of furniture placed
  over a foundation rests on its top.
- **A foundation is optional.** A wall, a roof or a bed placed where
  there is no foundation simply stands on the floor or the ground
  (`src/systems/construction/placement.rs`).
- **The ground does nothing to it.** On a planet, a piece is set at the
  height of the ground where you aim, on the metre grid. Nothing is dug,
  levelled or packed underneath, so on sloping ground part of a 4 metre
  foundation is in the ground and part is above it. There is no soil
  bearing capacity, no frost, no settlement, no water under the house
  and no rot; nothing you build ever sinks or tilts. The one rule about
  ground is that a piece cannot be built on water
  (`src/engine/planet_build.rs`).
- **No digging.** There are no footings, no trenches and no buried
  lines, which is also why there is no 811 to call.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, crafting
  takes no materials, so you can cut planks or bricks for nothing, but
  building a foundation from the Crafting page still takes its 8 planks
  or 12 bricks from your pack or the home's storage, and on a planet only
  from what you carry.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches, so
  what you built starts over at the next launch. Turn the setting off to
  keep it.

What to practise outside the game: everything in this guide. The game's
foundation is a level slab you can drop almost anywhere; a real one is the part
of the building you design first and can least afford to get wrong.

## You own this when

- You call 811 before you dig, find your own buried lines, keep spoil 2
  feet back, never get into a hole deeper than your waist, and never dig
  below a footing beside it.
- You can say what a foundation does: spread the load, reach stable
  ground below frost and fill, keep wood dry, and hold the building down.
- You can name the soils that carry the most and the least, and you
  know which ground needs a professional before anything is built on it.
- You can size a footing from a load and a soil pressure, and you know
  the footing's own weight counts.
- You put footings below the frost depth you got from the building
  department, on undisturbed ground, and you fill an over-dug hole with
  concrete.
- Water runs away from everything you build, with the foundation clear
  of the soil and a capillary break between concrete and wood.
- You use ground-contact treated wood in the ground, and you read the
  tag.
- You can read cracks, sloping floors and sticking doors in an old house
  as possible signs of settlement, and you know who to call.
