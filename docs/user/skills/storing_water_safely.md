# Storing Water Safely

If the tap stopped working tonight, how long could your household drink,
cook, and wash? Storing water is one of the cheapest, simplest preparedness
skills there is, and it is very hard to get wrong if you follow a few exact
rules from public health agencies. This guide uses the numbers published by
the CDC (the US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) and the EPA
(the US Environmental Protection Agency), plus university extension
services. Where a number matters, it is theirs, not ours.

This guide is for emergency preparedness. It is not a substitute for
guidance from your local health department or water utility. If they issue
instructions during a real emergency (a boil-water advisory, for example),
follow their instructions first.

## How much water to store

The CDC's rule: store at least 1 gallon (about 4 liters) of water per
person per day, for at least 3 days. That gallon covers drinking, cooking,
and basic washing. If you can, aim for a 2-week supply.

Store more than that if anyone in the household is pregnant or sick, or if
you live in a hot climate. Ohio State University Extension suggests about
1 quart (roughly a liter) a day for a small pet.

Quick math for a family of four: 4 people x 1 gallon x 14 days = 56
gallons for the full 2-week goal. That sounds like a lot, but it is
fourteen 4-gallon stackable containers, or a mix of jugs and bottled
water built up a little at a time. Start with 3 days (12 gallons) and
grow from there.

## What you need (cheap or free options first)

- Unopened store-bought bottled water. The CDC calls this the safest and
  most reliable emergency water. Watch the expiration date on the label.
- Empty 2-liter soda bottles, washed well. Utah State University Extension
  says these are acceptable because they are less likely to leak or hold
  food residues.
- Food-grade water containers (the blue 5 to 7 gallon jugs from camping or
  surplus stores). "Food grade" means the plastic is made to hold food or
  water without transferring harmful substances into it.
- One bottle of plain unscented liquid household chlorine bleach. The label
  should say it contains between 5% and 9% sodium hypochlorite (the active
  chlorine chemical). No scents, no "splashless," no added cleaners.
- A medicine dropper (for measuring bleach by the drop), a permanent
  marker, and some masking tape for labels.

### Containers to never use

- Milk jugs and juice bottles. Milk proteins and fruit sugars soak into
  the plastic and cannot be fully washed out; they feed bacteria in your
  stored water. The thin plastic also cracks and leaks over time.
- Anything that ever held a toxic chemical: bleach bottles, pesticide
  jugs, fuel cans, cleaning product containers. The CDC says do not use
  these, period. No amount of rinsing makes them safe.
- Glass works but breaks; the CDC recommends durable, unbreakable
  containers with a tight-closing lid, ideally with a narrow neck so you
  pour instead of dipping.

## Step 1: Clean and sanitize the container

This is the CDC's exact procedure. "Sanitize" means killing the germs that
washing alone leaves behind.

1. Wash the container with soap and water, then rinse completely.
2. Mix a sanitizing solution: 1 teaspoon of unscented 5% to 9% household
   bleach in 1 quart (about 1 liter) of water. This mix is for rinsing
   the container only; it is far too strong to drink. Disinfecting
   drinking water uses only DROPS of bleach (see the disinfection
   section further down).
3. Pour the solution into the container, close the lid tightly, and shake
   well so the solution touches every inside surface.
4. Wait at least 30 seconds, then pour the solution out.
5. Let the container air-dry, or rinse it with water that is already safe
   (treated tap water or bottled water). Do not rinse with untreated water,
   which would re-contaminate it.

## Step 2: Fill it

- From a treated municipal supply (city tap water): fill the sanitized
  container straight from the tap and cap it tightly. City water is
  already disinfected, so nothing more is needed.
- From any other source (well, spring, rain barrel, or a tap under a
  boil-water advisory): treat the water FIRST using the disinfection
  section below, then fill.

## Step 3: Store it right

- Label every container "drinking water" with the date you filled it.
- Keep it cool: the CDC recommends 50 to 70 degrees Fahrenheit (10 to 21
  degrees Celsius). A closet, basement, or under-bed space works.
- Keep it dark. Never store water in direct sunlight; light feeds algae
  growth and ages plastic.
- Keep it away from gasoline, pesticides, and other chemicals. Vapors can
  pass slowly through plastic.
- Keep it off bare concrete. Utah State University Extension recommends
  storing containers off the ground and off cement; a scrap board or
  wooden pallet costs nothing and also protects against temperature
  swings from the slab.
- Freezer bonus: a few bottles in the freezer (leave 2 to 3 inches of air
  space so they do not burst) give you ice during a power outage and
  become drinking water as they melt.

## Rotation: the 6-month rule

Replace water you filled yourself every 6 months. This is the CDC's
number, and Utah State and Ohio State extensions give the same figure.
Rotation day is a good time to re-sanitize containers and to replace your
stored bleach bottle too, since bleach quietly loses strength as it ages.
Store-bought bottled water just follows its printed expiration date.

An easy habit: rotate when the clocks change, or on New Year's Day and the
Fourth of July. Old stored water is not wasted; use it on plants or for
cleaning.

## How to tell stored water has gone bad

Look and smell before you drink:

- Cloudiness or floating particles that were not there when you filled it
- Green tinge or slime (algae) on the water or container walls
- A rotten or sour smell
- A bulging or leaking container

If you see any of these, do not drink it. Dump it, re-sanitize the
container (Step 1), and refill. Perfectly stored water can still taste
flat or faintly of plastic; that alone does not mean it is unsafe (see
troubleshooting below).

## Disinfecting questionable water

Use this when you must drink water you are not sure about: an untreated
source, or stored water during an emergency when you cannot simply dump
and refill. If the water is cloudy, first let it settle and pour the clear
part through a clean cloth, paper towel, or coffee filter.

Boiling (the most reliable method):

- Bring water to a rolling boil for at least 1 minute.
- At high altitude, boil for 3 minutes. The EPA sets this cutoff at
  altitudes above 5,000 feet; the CDC uses 6,500 feet. The agencies
  differ, so if you live above 5,000 feet the safe choice is the full
  3 minutes.
- Let it cool before drinking.

Bleach (when you cannot boil), the EPA's exact table, using plain
unscented household bleach:

- If the label says 6% sodium hypochlorite: 2 drops per quart or liter,
  8 drops per gallon.
- If the label says 8.25% sodium hypochlorite: 2 drops per quart or
  liter, 6 drops per gallon.
- Double the amount if the water is still cloudy or colored after
  settling and filtering, or if it is very cold.
- Stir, cover, and let it stand for 30 minutes. The water should have a
  slight chlorine smell. If it does not, repeat the dose and let it stand
  another 15 minutes before use.

The CDC's simplified version is consistent: 8 drops (a little less than
1/8 teaspoon) of 5% to 9% bleach per gallon of clear water, 16 drops
(1/4 teaspoon) if cloudy, stand at least 30 minutes. (For 8.25% bleach
the CDC rounds slightly higher than the EPA table; either dose is safe.)

Note what bleach and boiling do NOT fix: chemical contamination (fuel,
pesticides, industrial spills). If water smells of chemicals, no home
treatment makes it drinkable; find another source.

## What can go wrong (honest troubleshooting)

- Plastic or flat taste. Common and usually harmless; water stored
  without air goes flat. Pour it back and forth between two clean
  containers to put air back in (a standard extension-service tip for
  boiled or stored water). If a
  strong chemical taste persists, the container may not be food grade;
  discard the water and retire that container from water duty.
- Green growth. Algae got light. Dump the water, re-sanitize the
  container, refill, and move it somewhere truly dark. Do not just top
  it off; the growth clings to the walls.
- Cloudy stored water. Something got in, often from dipping hands or a
  dirty scoop. Dump, re-sanitize, refill. In future, pour instead of
  dipping, and never touch the inside of the container or lid.
- You forgot to rotate and it is 14 months old. Do not panic and do not
  drink it untreated. If it looks and smells fine it is probably safe,
  but the cautious move outside an emergency is to rotate it now. In an
  actual emergency, disinfect it (previous section) before drinking.

Your first attempt may go sideways: a leaky jug, a forgotten label, a
bottle that tastes like a pool toy. That is normal and useful. Every
failure teaches you before the day it matters.

## How you know it worked

- You can point to at least 1 gallon per person per day for 3 or more
  days, in labeled, dated, tightly capped containers.
- The water is clear, smells like nothing (or very faintly of chlorine),
  and the containers show no growth or leaks.
- Every label is less than 6 months old.
- A bottle of unscented 5% to 9% bleach and a dropper sit next to the
  water, ready for disinfection if you ever need it.

When all four are true, your household has real water security for the
most common emergencies. Quiet peace of mind, earned in an afternoon.
