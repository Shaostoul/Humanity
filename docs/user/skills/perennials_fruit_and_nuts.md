# Perennials, Fruit and Nuts

Most of a vegetable garden starts again from seed every spring. A fruit
tree, a blueberry bush, an asparagus bed or a rhubarb crown is planted once
and then feeds you for decades. The price is patience: years of care before
the first real harvest, and a handful of decisions at planting time (where,
which variety, on which rootstock, next to which partner) that you live
with for as long as the plant does. Get those right and the yearly work is
pruning, thinning and picking. Get them wrong and a tree can take up a
corner of the garden for ten years and give almost nothing.

This guide is about those decisions: why perennials are worth the wait,
what your climate allows (hardiness and chill), how the rootstock sets a
tree's size, why many apples and sweet cherries need a partner, the berries
and their soil, the two great perennial vegetables, nut trees, planting and
pruning, and what yield you can honestly expect from each plant.

Units: the sources are American, so distances are in feet and inches and
yields in pounds, with metric alongside. Temperatures are in degrees
Fahrenheit with Celsius in brackets. The worked examples use Silverdale,
Washington, the same place as [The Growing Calendar](the_growing_calendar.md);
its hardiness zone is 8b (the USDA ZIP code table, as
[Choosing Where to Build](choosing_where_to_build.md) records).

## What your climate allows

### Winter cold: the hardiness zone

The USDA Plant Hardiness Zone Map is the standard for which perennials will
survive where. In USDA's own words, it "is based on the average annual
extreme minimum winter temperature, displayed as 10-degree F zones and
5-degree F half zones." The 2023 edition uses the 30 years from 1991 to
2020, and USDA says it is generally about a quarter zone warmer than the
2012 map across much of the country, because of the more recent period.
Silverdale's 8b means an average coldest night of the year between 15 and
20 F (minus 9.4 and minus 6.7 C).

Washington State University Extension's home orchard chapter (Sallato and
DuPont, in its Pacific Northwest Gardener's Handbook) gives the winter cold
the common tree fruits can take while dormant:

| Fruit | Survives down to about |
|---|---|
| Apple, tart cherry, American plum | minus 30 F (minus 34 C) |
| Pear, European plum | minus 20 F (minus 29 C) |
| Sweet cherry, Japanese plum, apricot | minus 10 to minus 15 F (minus 23 to minus 26 C) |
| Peach and nectarine | injured at minus 8 to minus 12 F (minus 22 to minus 24 C) |

**Spring frost matters as much as winter cold.** WSU notes that swelling
flower buds are the most tender part of the tree, that a late frost below
28 F (minus 2.2 C) can kill them, and that where late frosts are common you
should choose late-blooming varieties. It also says most fruit trees need at
least 150 frost-free days; in Washington the season runs from 90 days north
of Spokane to 180 along the coast. [The Growing
Calendar](the_growing_calendar.md) shows how to read your own frost dates.

### Winter chill: the other half of the climate

Deciduous fruit trees need cold as well as surviving it. A University of
California Master Gardener sheet (Contra Costa County, February 2020)
defines chill hours as the total hours below 45 F (7.2 C) that fruit and nut
trees need to produce fruit, counted from 1 November to the end of February.
Without enough of them, it says, trees leaf out late, bloom over a long
straggling period, drop their buds and make few if any flowers, and with no
flowers there is no fruit.

The count is a simplification. Mississippi State University Extension
(Stafne, Publication 3067) explains that temperatures between 32 and 50 F
(0 and 10 C) help, that 32 to 45 F are the most effective, that very little
chilling happens below freezing, and that long spells above 60 to 70 F (16
to 21 C) can undo it. Its table of typical needs, for crops grown in
Mississippi, shows how much varieties differ:

| Crop | Chill hours needed, by variety |
|---|---|
| Apple | 200 to 1,000 |
| Blueberry | 150 to 700 |
| Peach | 200 to 800 |
| Pear | 400 to 900 |
| Plum | 400 to 700 |
| Strawberry | 200 to 400 |

The practical rule: a variety's chill need has to fit your winters, so
check it on the label or with the nursery, and in a mild-winter place look
for low-chill varieties. WSU counts sufficient winter chilling among the
resources that give Washington its exceptional conditions for tree fruit,
alongside water, suitable soil, long warm summer days and cool nights.

### Rain, and the fruits that dislike it

Cool, wet weather is its own limit. For nut trees, WSU names the cool, wet
growing season as the most limiting factor in western Washington. Oregon
State is blunt about peaches: they bloom early, when it is often too cool
and wet for good pollination, and wet weather brings peach leaf curl,
coryneum blight and brown rot; without frequent spraying, it says, peach
trees in cool, wet climates soon die. It names Frost and Rosydawn as
varieties resistant to leaf curl.

### The site

- **Sun.** WSU puts full sun for most of the day first; Minnesota wants at
  least 8 hours a day for apples; the Pacific Northwest pruning guide (PNW
  400) says trees should get at least six.
- **Deep, well-drained soil.** Oregon State says the soil must let roots
  reach at least 3 feet (0.9 m); WSU says most trees do better with at least
  4 feet (1.2 m) of well-drained soil, and that dwarfing rootstocks manage in
  shallower ground. WSU suggests digging a pit 3 feet deep before planting
  to find hard layers or poor drainage, and a soil test from 8 to 12 inches
  (20 to 30 cm) down. [Testing and Correcting Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md)
  covers the test.
- **Room.** A tree's spread at maturity is set mostly by its rootstock
  (below). Measure before you buy.

## Fruit trees: the rootstock sets the size

Almost every fruit tree you can buy is two plants joined together. The
upper part, the scion, is the variety you chose (Honeycrisp, Bartlett,
Bing); the root is a rootstock chosen for how big it lets the tree grow,
how soon it bears and how well it stands in your soil. WSU's advice to home
growers is to choose dwarfing rootstocks whenever possible: the tree bears
sooner, and needs less ladder work to prune, spray and pick.

Oregon State's table for apples (EC 819, Table 2):

| Apple rootstock type | Spread | Height | Years to first bearing | Yield per tree | Rootstocks |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Vigorous | 30 to 40 ft (9 to 12 m) | 25 to 60 ft (7.6 to 18 m) | 7 to 10 | 300 to 400 lb (136 to 181 kg) | none named (usually a seedling, WSU) |
| Semidwarf | 15 to 25 ft (4.6 to 7.6 m) | 15 to 25 ft | 5 to 7 | 180 to 300 lb (82 to 136 kg) | MM-106, MM-111, M-7 |
| Dwarf | 5 to 10 ft (1.5 to 3 m) | 6 to 15 ft (1.8 to 4.6 m) | 3 to 5 | 50 to 150 lb (23 to 68 kg) | M-9, M-26, Bud 9 |
| More dwarf | 4 to 6 ft (1.2 to 1.8 m) | 5 to 7 ft (1.5 to 2.1 m) | 3 to 5 | 25 to 50 lb (11 to 23 kg) | M-27 |

What the other sources add:

- **Dwarfs need support.** Oregon State says trees on M-9, M-26 and
  sometimes M-7 may need a trellis or post. Minnesota says M.9's brittle
  roots and heavy cropping mean it needs staking for the life of the tree,
  and M.26 for the first few years, or for life on a windy site.
- **WSU's choice for a home garden:** M.9, Bud 9, G.11, G.41 or M.26, which
  keep a tree at a workable 6 to 8 feet (1.8 to 2.4 m); M.27, the most
  dwarfing, suits a container. A tree grown on a seedling root can easily
  reach 30 feet (9 m) or more.
- **Cherries** in Oregon and Washington are mostly grown on vigorous Mazzard
  roots. WSU names the Gisela series as the common dwarfing roots, Gisela 3
  the most dwarfing, then 5, 6 and 12, and says trees on dwarfing roots bear
  sooner.
- **Peaches** usually grow on their own seedling roots, and WSU says their
  size is best kept down by pruning.
- **Spacing**, from WSU: 12 to 24 feet (3.7 to 7.3 m) between free-standing
  trees on larger roots, 8 to 12 feet (2.4 to 3.7 m) on semidwarf and dwarf
  roots, and as close as 1 to 5 feet on a trellis, which needs more exact
  pruning.

## Pollination partners

A fruit forms only from a pollinated flower, and many fruit trees cannot
pollinate themselves. WSU explains the terms: a self-fertile (self-compatible)
variety sets a crop alone; a self-sterile (self-incompatible) one sets
nothing without pollen from a different variety. That second variety, the
pollinizer, has to do two things: flower at the same time, and carry
compatible pollen. Bees do the carrying.

**Which fruits need a partner** (WSU, Oregon State and Minnesota):

- **Most apples.** Minnesota says two varieties are required, and one of
  them can be a crabapple.
- **Most pears.** WSU notes that Bartlett may set a few seedless pears
  alone but sets more with d'Anjou, and that Bosc and Comice pollinate each
  other.
- **Many sweet cherries.** Oregon State says the three main varieties, Bing,
  Lambert and Royal Ann, will not pollinate one another. Self-fruitful sweet
  cherries do exist: Oregon State names Stella, Sweetheart and Lapins, and
  WSU names Lapins, Benton, Skeena, Sweetheart and Sandra Rose.
- **Some plums** (Oregon State's table: "Some varieties").
- **Hazelnuts and chestnuts** (Oregon State): every hazelnut variety needs
  another variety. Oregon State's table marks chestnuts as needing a partner
  too, and its text adds that Chinese chestnuts bear some nuts on their own
  pollen but often more and bigger ones with a second tree.

**Which usually do not** (WSU and Oregon State): sour (pie) cherries, most
peaches and nectarines, most European plums, and, among nuts, black and
English walnuts.

**The rules for a partner:**

- **Close enough.** WSU and Minnesota both say within 100 feet (30 m).
  Minnesota adds that a crabapple in a neighbour's garden can serve.
- **Flowering together.** WSU asks for bloom periods that overlap by at
  least several days. Its western Washington fruit handbook (EB0937, 2008)
  says that in that region's cool springs the window can be as long as 10
  days, so usually only the very earliest and very latest bloomers fail to
  pollinate each other.
- **Good pollen.** Some apples, including Gravenstein and Jonagold, are
  pollen-sterile, usually because they are triploids (three sets of
  chromosomes instead of two). EB0937 says a pollen-sterile variety will not
  pollinate other apples, so if you plant one you need two other
  pollinizers. It recommends white-flowering crabapples as pollinizers, and
  names Evereste as especially suited to western Washington because it is
  immune to apple scab.
- **When space is short,** EB0937 suggests a tree with three to five
  varieties grafted onto one trunk, and WSU suggests a bouquet of flowering
  branches from a compatible variety in a bucket of water beside your tree
  during bloom.

**Berries are easier.** WSU says blueberries are largely self-fertile but
set bigger berries when two or more varieties grow near each other;
raspberries and strawberries are self-fertile.

## Planting a tree

From WSU's orchard chapter, with Minnesota's figure for the graft:

1. **Dig wide, not deep.** Make the hole two to three times wider than the
   roots and only as deep as needed, so the tree sits at the same soil level
   it grew at in the nursery or pot. Roughen the sides of the hole in clay.
2. **Refill with the native soil only.** WSU warns against filling the hole
   with planting mix or amendments: when the soil in the hole differs from
   the soil around it, roots may not grow out, and the tree can decline
   three or four years later. The one exception it names is phosphorus, when
   a soil test shows it is low, mixed in below the roots with a layer of soil
   between.
3. **Keep the graft union above ground.** The graft union is the swelling
   where variety meets rootstock. Minnesota puts it about 4 inches (10 cm)
   above the soil line. If it is buried, the variety grows its own roots and
   the dwarfing effect is lost. Rub off any shoots that come from below the
   graft; WSU says these root suckers bear poor fruit and compete with the
   tree.
4. **Spread the roots** of a bare-root tree over a low mound, and cut any
   roots circling a potted tree's root ball.
5. **Water at once** to settle the soil around the roots.
6. **Protect the trunk** with white water-based latex paint or a trunk wrap
   against sunscald and animals.
7. **Keep grass and weeds off** the ground within about 3 feet (0.9 m) of the
   trunk for the first three to five years (Minnesota), with a few inches of
   mulch kept a few inches clear of the trunk.

## The first years: grow roots, not fruit

A young plant that fruits early does it at the cost of its roots and frame.
University of Missouri Extension (G6005) says to remove all flowers or fruit
during the first growing season and the year after, and calls this critical
for apples, pears, peaches, plums, cherries, grapes and blueberries; allow a
light crop in the third season, then adjust the crop each year. WSU gives
the same advice for blueberries (no fruit for the first two years) and for
new June-bearing strawberries (remove the blossoms the first year). Oregon
State's newer blueberry guide (EC 1304, May 2025) is a little less strict:
prune off every flower bud at planting so there is no fruit the first
season, then prune so a small first crop comes in the second growing
season. The sources agree on the part that matters, which is no fruit in
the planting year.

## Pruning and thinning

**When.** The Pacific Northwest guide to training and pruning home orchards
(PNW 400, Olsen and Bell, revised February 2025) says to prune once the
danger of a fall or early winter freeze has passed but before growth begins
in spring. Sweet cherries are the exception: they may be pruned in August,
when there is less risk of bacterial infection.

**How.** Cut back to the branch collar, the raised ring at the base of a
branch, and leave no stub: PNW 400 warns that stubs do not heal and invite
wood rot.

**Two shapes.**

- **Open center (vase):** three or four main limbs spreading from a short
  trunk, with the middle kept open to the sun. PNW 400 uses it for peaches
  and sweet cherries (three to five limbs for cherries).
- **Central leader:** one upright trunk with tiers of side branches. PNW
  400 recommends it for fully dwarf apples on a post or trellis, and says
  most commercial pears in the Pacific Northwest are grown with a central
  or modified central leader.

Either takes about four winters to establish.

**Know where the fruit grows.** PNW 400 explains that peaches bloom only on
one-year-old shoots, so a peach must be pruned every year to grow new
fruiting wood, while apples, pears, cherries and plums fruit mostly on
spurs, short stubby shoots on wood two years old or more, which Oregon
State describes as long-lived. WSU draws the lesson: trees that fruit on
one-year-old wood, such as peaches and hazelnuts, need harder pruning to
renew it, and trees that fruit on spurs should be kept open so light
reaches them.

**Thin the fruit.** A tree left to carry everything it sets gives small
fruit and, Minnesota warns, often a heavy crop one year and almost none the
next (biennial bearing). Minnesota's rule for apples: once the fruit is
marble-sized (late June or early July there, after the natural drop), leave
one or two fruits per cluster, or for the best fruit about 4 to 6 inches
(10 to 15 cm) between fruits along a branch.

## Berries

### Blueberries

Blueberries need acid soil, and getting that right is most of the job. WSU's berry chapter (DeVetter and Hoheisel):

- **pH 4.5 to 5.5.** Above pH 6.0 the plants turn yellow and stunted from
  iron deficiency, and other nutrients run short too.
- **Acidify a year ahead.** Test the soil 12 months before planting and work
  elemental sulfur into the top 8 inches (20 cm): 2 to 5 pounds per 100
  square feet (about 100 to 240 g per square metre) in heavy clay, or 1 to 3
  pounds (about 50 to 150 g per square metre) in sandy soil. If the pH rises
  later, a fertilizer of ammonium sulfate helps, and a little more sulfur can
  go on the surface in late summer or early fall.
- **Feed with ammonium, not nitrate,** and avoid manures and composts, whose
  pH and salts can harm blueberries. Douglas-fir sawdust or peat are the
  preferred organic matter.
- **Shallow roots,** 12 to 18 inches (30 to 46 cm) deep. WSU renews a
  mulch of sawdust or wood chips 2 to 3 inches (5 to 8 cm) deep every two
  or three years.

If your soil is too acid instead (below pH 4.5), Oregon State (EC 1304,
revised May 2025) says to work in finely ground limestone, about 5 to 10
pounds per 100 square feet, toward pH 5.5. [Testing and Correcting
Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) explains pH.

**What to expect:** WSU says young plants bear 4 to 5 pounds (about 2 kg)
and plants six years old or more up to 15 pounds (6.8 kg), sometimes 20 or
more. Oregon State gives 9 to 15 pounds (4 to 7 kg) a bush for mature
northern highbush varieties.

### Raspberries

- **Soil:** WSU gives an ideal pH of 6.0 to 6.5, with lime in the fall if
  it is below 5.5. Drainage matters most of all: WSU says root rot, worst on
  poorly drained sites, can be the most limiting problem, and little can be
  done once it is in a planting. Plant on raised beds or berms if water
  stands.
- **Two kinds** (WSU). **Summer-bearing** (floricane) varieties grow a
  cane one year and fruit on it the next spring and summer, and then that
  cane dies; the canes live two years, but the roots are perennial.
  **Everbearing** (primocane-fruiting, fall-bearing) varieties also fruit on
  the new canes in their first year, from about August until the fall
  rains.
- **Pruning summer-bearers:** after harvest and leaf drop (October to
  February), cut out the brown, spent fruiting canes and keep 8 to 15 of the
  healthiest green new canes.
- **Pruning everbearers:** cutting every cane to the ground in winter gives
  one fall crop. Cutting off only the top half of the canes that fruited
  gives a summer crop the next June from the lower half as well.
- **Yield:** Oregon State (EC 1306, revised June 2021) expects 18 to 27
  pounds (8 to 12 kg) for every 10 feet (3 m) of row from mature
  summer-bearing red or yellow raspberries, depending on the region, and
  slightly less from everbearing and black raspberries. WSU gives 15 to 30
  pounds (7 to 14 kg) per 10 feet of row, without saying which type.

### Strawberries

- **Soil:** full sun and well-drained, slightly acid soil, pH 5.6 to 6.5
  (WSU); a raised bed 6 to 10 inches (15 to 25 cm) high if drainage is poor,
  and away from frost pockets.
- **Three kinds** (WSU). **June-bearing** plants form flower buds in late
  summer and fall and fruit once, in late spring or early summer; a well
  weeded planting lasts four to five years or more. **Everbearers** give a
  small fall crop and an early spring one. **Day-neutrals** fruit whenever the
  temperature suits, from May into October in parts of western Washington,
  but their yield falls after one or two years, so many people grow them as
  annuals.
- **The first year:** remove the blossoms of new June-bearers so the plants
  build healthy roots and shoots (WSU).
- **Renovate June-bearers** two weeks after fruiting: mow off
  the old leaves, leaving about 2 inches (5 cm) above the crowns, and narrow
  the matted row to 10 to 12 inches (25 to 30 cm). Do not renovate
  day-neutrals or everbearers.

## Perennial vegetables

### Asparagus

An asparagus bed is a fifteen-year decision, so place it with care: Minnesota
suggests the north end of the garden, where the tall ferns will not shade
other crops, and a site that is not a frost hollow, since frost kills
emerging spears.

- **Soil:** well-drained, pH 6.5 to 7.0 (Minnesota).
- **Planting:** crowns go in a trench 6 to 12 inches (15 to 30 cm) deep,
  shallower in heavy clay (6 to 8 inches) and deeper in sand (10 to 12
  inches), covered lightly at first and filled in gradually as the ferns
  grow (Minnesota).
- **The first harvests are short on purpose.** Both sources take nothing
  until the third growing season, counting the planting year as the first.
  Minnesota: no spears at all the first spring after planting; the second
  spring, if the plants grew well, pick for two weeks only; after that,
  pick until about 1 July (in Minnesota). Utah State allows up to 4 weeks
  in that third year, 6 weeks in the fourth and up to 8 weeks once the bed
  has had five years of growth.
- **When to stop each spring:** Utah State says stop when most spears are
  thinner than a pencil. After that, let the ferns grow all summer: they
  feed the crown for next year's crop.
- **Yield:** Utah State says a mature plant gives about three quarters of a
  pound (0.34 kg) of spears over the season, and that 25 plants are enough
  to eat fresh and to put some by.
- **Putting it by:** Minnesota notes that home-canned asparagus must be
  processed in a pressure canner. [Why Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md)
  explains why.

### Rhubarb

Rhubarb is the other long-lived vegetable, and it comes with one firm rule:
**eat the stalks, never the leaves.** Minnesota says rhubarb leaves are
toxic and not to be eaten, and to cut the leaf blades off the stalks as soon
as you pick them. Iowa State explains why the line falls where it does: the
leaves and the stalks both contain some oxalic acid, harmful in large
amounts, but the leaves contain more, so they are not eaten. Iowa State adds
that the leaves are safe to put in the compost. Minnesota also notes that
people with gout, kidney disorders or rheumatoid arthritis may want to
avoid foods high in oxalic acid and should ask their doctor about rhubarb.

- **Two myths.** Minnesota says it is not true that the whole plant becomes
  toxic later in summer; stalks pulled then are tougher, not dangerous. And
  after a freeze, Iowa State says a hard freeze (mid-twenties Fahrenheit or
  lower, about minus 4 C) blackens and shrivels the leaves and turns the
  stalks soft and limp; pull and discard any stalks that look like that, but
  if the plant shows no damage two or three days after the freeze, it is
  safe to harvest, and new stalks that grow afterwards are too.
- **When to start:** Minnesota says wait until the second season (the third
  if grown from seed); Iowa State says take nothing for the first two years,
  harvest for four weeks in the third, and 8 to 10 weeks from the fourth
  year on. If in doubt, wait the extra year.
- **How to pick:** hold the stalk near its base and pull and twist, rather
  than cutting (Minnesota; Iowa State says pull up and slightly to one side).
  Never take more than half the grown stalks from a plant at once (Iowa
  State). Stop by late June so the leaves can rebuild the plant.
- **Care:** cut off flower stalks as soon as they appear; give each plant a
  space about 3 feet (0.9 m) square; divide a crown in early spring once it
  is crowded and making many thin stalks, which Iowa State says often
  happens after eight to ten years. Minnesota says one plant is usually enough for a family, and a plant can
  live 15 years or more.

## Nut trees

Nut trees include the longest waits and the biggest trees in this guide.
From Oregon State's table:

| Nut | Space per tree | Needs a partner? | Years to bearing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hazelnut (filbert) | 15 to 20 ft (4.6 to 6.1 m) | yes | 5 to 6 |
| Chestnut | 20 to 40 ft (6 to 12 m) | yes | 5 to 7 |
| Black walnut | 30 to 40 ft (9 to 12 m) | no | 10 to 12 |
| English walnut | 40 to 50 ft (12 to 15 m) | no | 10 to 12 |

- **Hazelnuts** need the least room of the nuts here, and even so give a
  modest crop: Oregon State says a mature tree using 20 feet or more of space
  gives only 15 to 20 pounds (7 to 9 kg) of dried nuts, less if jays and
  squirrels take their share. It also warns that eastern filbert blight, a
  fungus, is killing many hazelnuts in the Willamette Valley, and advises
  against planting susceptible varieties in western Oregon; ask for a
  resistant variety.
- **Walnuts and chestnuts** make good shade trees, Oregon State says, but
  are harder to prune and spray than smaller trees. PNW 400 says a fully
  grown chestnut reaches about 40 feet (12 m).
- **Black walnut poisons some neighbours.** A WSU Master Gardener sheet
  (Spokane County, C032, revised 2021) explains that black walnut makes a
  chemical called juglone, strongest in its buds, leaves, nut husks and
  roots, which builds up in the soil under the tree and kills or stunts
  sensitive plants. Its sensitive list includes apple, blueberry,
  blackberry, strawberry, tomato, potato, pepper, eggplant, cabbage and
  rhododendron; beans, beets, corn, onions, cherry and most maples are among
  the resistant. Keep sensitive plants well away from any walnut, do not
  mulch them with walnut leaves, hulls, sawdust or chips, and compost walnut
  leaves separately: the sheet says juglone breaks down completely in a
  compost pile within two months, and that you can test doubtful compost by
  planting tomato seedlings in it.

## What to expect from each plant

Yields vary with variety, soil, weather and care, so read these as the
range a well kept, mature plant gives. The Missouri table notes that its
higher figures are the most productive varieties in their best years.

| Plant | Yield | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Apple, dwarf | 50 to 150 lb (23 to 68 kg) a tree | Oregon State; Missouri |
| Apple, semidwarf | 180 to 300 lb (82 to 136 kg) a tree | Oregon State |
| Apple, vigorous | 300 to 400 lb (136 to 181 kg) a tree | Oregon State |
| Peach | 150 to 250 lb (68 to 113 kg) a tree | Missouri |
| Plum; sour cherry | 40 to 120 lb (18 to 54 kg) a tree | Missouri |
| Blueberry | 5 to 15 lb (2 to 7 kg) a bush; 9 to 15 lb (4 to 7 kg) for mature northern highbush | Missouri; Oregon State |

| Strawberry | 30 to 65 lb (14 to 29 kg) per 50 feet (15 m) of row | Missouri |

| Asparagus | about 0.75 lb (0.34 kg) a plant | Utah State |
| Hazelnut | 15 to 20 lb (7 to 9 kg) of dried nuts a tree | Oregon State |

(Missouri's table also gives 400 to 650 lb for a pear tree without saying
what size of tree; Oregon State's table has no pear yield.)

## Sprays and the harvest interval

Fruit trees attract pests, and in some places controlling them is not
optional. WSU's orchard chapter says that in Washington State home
orchardists have a legal responsibility to control the insects and diseases
on their fruit and nut trees, because the pests spread to neighbours' and
commercial orchards, and that for most fruit and nut trees this will mean
some pesticide use alongside integrated pest management. [Pests and
Disease](pests_and_disease.md) covers the methods.

If you spray, the label decides everything. The EPA puts it plainly:
pesticide labels are legally enforceable, and all of them carry the
statement "It is a violation of Federal law to use this product in a manner
inconsistent with its labeling." And: "In other words, the label is the
law." The EPA's manual for writing labels lists what the directions for use
must state, including the sites (crops) the product may be used on and,
where one applies, the **pre-harvest interval**, given in days or weeks:
the wait between the last application and picking. Its sample label text
reads "BROCCOLI (7-day PHI)" and "Do not apply within 7 days of harvest."
WSU's advice on cherry fruit fly shows how that works in practice: spray
according to the label from the time the cherries start to turn from green
to yellow until the pre-harvest interval stated on the label, and then
stop. So:

- use only a product whose label lists your crop;
- mix and apply it only as the label says;
- note the pre-harvest interval, and count the days from your last spray
  before you pick.

## A backyard orchard, worked

A Silverdale garden (zone 8b, in western Washington's cool, wet climate)
with room for a small planting. Every yield is from the table above, and the
arithmetic is worked here, not quoted from anyone.

- **Two dwarf apple trees** of two different varieties that bloom together,
  neither of them a pollen-sterile triploid, about 8 to 12 feet (2.4 to 3.7
  m) apart, each staked. First fruit in about 3 to 5 years; at full bearing,
  100 to 300 lb (45 to 136 kg) between them, which is a lot of apples to
  store, sauce or share.
- **Four blueberry bushes** of at least two varieties, in a bed tested and
  acidified with sulfur a year before planting. Blossoms off for two years; mature after
  about seven; then 36 to 60 lb (16 to 27 kg) a year at Oregon State's 9 to
  15 lb a bush.
- **Twenty feet (6 m) of summer-bearing raspberries** on a well-drained
  berm: 36 to 54 lb (16 to 24 kg) a year from mature plants.
- **Twenty-five asparagus crowns** at the north end of the vegetable garden:
  no harvest the first spring, a short one after, and about 19 lb (8.5 kg) a
  spring from mature plants.
- **One rhubarb plant**, which Minnesota says is usually enough for a
  family.

Peaches are left out on purpose: in a cool, wet climate they need leaf curl
resistant varieties and regular spraying to survive. A pair of hazelnut
trees of different, blight-resistant varieties would begin bearing after
five or six years and, once mature, add 30 to 40 lb (14 to 18 kg) of dried
nuts a year, for about 20 feet of space each.

## How the game models it

The garden in this project grows these plants, but it models only part of
what this guide teaches, and the gaps are worth knowing.

- **The waiting is real.** Each crop's days to first harvest in
  `data/plants.csv` count from what it is planted from, so a perennial
  carries its establishment years: asparagus and rhubarb 730 days from the
  crown, blueberry 730 from a cutting, apple, pear and cherry 1,460 from
  seed, peach, plum and hazelnut 1,095, walnut and chestnut 1,825, raspberry
  365.
- **Soil pH is modelled** (`data/garden/soil_ph.ron`). Garden loam
  starts at pH 6.5, and blueberry's window is 4.5 to 5.5, so a blueberry in
  an untreated bed has its health capped (at 70 percent, one pH unit
  outside its window at 30 percent a unit). Garden sulfur lowers the pH over
  months as it reacts, with a limit on how much one application may use,
  after Oregon State's advice (its EC 1560, quoted in that file) that two
  small applications a year apart beat one large one; lime and wood ash
  raise it.
- **Strawberries are picked through a season.** A strawberry plant is picked
  daily over a 112 day window and yields a pound (0.45 kg) a plant, the
  University of Minnesota's figure for day-neutral plants in a high tunnel
  (`data/garden/harvest_windows.ron`, `data/garden/yields.ron`), and
  indoors it needs its flowers pollinated, by hand, bees or a device
  (`data/garden/pollination.ron`).

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game:

- Every perennial except the strawberry is harvested **once and then is
  spent**, leaving its space empty, as if it were an annual. A real apple
  tree, asparagus bed or rhubarb crown crops year after year for decades.
- There is **no chill and no hardiness**: a crop only grows more slowly
  outside its temperature range, frost never kills it, and no variety needs
  winter cold to flower.
- There are **no varieties, rootstocks or pollination partners** for tree
  fruit: `pollination.ron` covers the strawberry among the perennials, so a
  single apple tree sets a full crop on its own.
- The **tree fruit yields are older figures without a source**. The apple,
  for one, gives 20 to 80 apples of 0.2 kg, 4 to 16 kg a tree, well under
  Oregon State's 23 to 68 kg for even a dwarf. The sourced yields in
  `data/garden/yields.ron` do not yet reach the fruit trees.
- There is **no pruning, thinning, fruit removal in the first years,
  renovation or juglone**. The walnut's list of plants it harms is in
  `data/plants.csv`, but the garden does not read it.
- There are **no pesticides or harvest intervals**.
