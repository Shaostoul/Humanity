# Chickens and Eggs

A few hens are often the first animals a household keeps for food. They
turn feed, safe kitchen scraps and garden insects into eggs, their
droppings feed a compost heap, and a small flock fits on a town lot
where a goat or a cow never could. They also need somebody every single
day: feed, clean water, a door shut at dusk and opened in the morning,
eggs collected, and a house kept clean and dry. A flock that is missed
for a weekend is a flock that may not be there on Monday.

This guide is about keeping a small flock, three to a dozen hens, for
eggs, through a whole year. Its safety rules come from the Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the US Department of Agriculture's
Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS) and the Food and Drug
Administration (FDA), and from federal regulations; those are United
States government publications in the public domain. Its husbandry
numbers come from university extension services in Florida, Georgia,
Maryland, North Dakota and the Pacific Northwest, from a Cornell
University soil project and from a county fire marshal. Those are
copyrighted, so their facts are restated here in our own words, never
quoted. Where something is general practice rather than a published
rule, the text says so.

Four other guides sit beside this one. [Handwashing and
Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) covers the habit that does most to
keep a flock from making you ill. [Telling Spoiled From
Safe](telling_spoiled_from_safe.md) covers food that has been kept too
long or too warm. [Your First Compost](your_first_compost.md) is where
the litter from the coop goes. [Testing and Correcting
Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) explains how to find out whether
the ground your hens will scratch in carries lead. Killing and dressing
birds for meat is its own topic, not yet written, and this guide does
not teach it.

## First: find out whether you may keep them

Before buying a single chick, find out what your town or county allows.
The University of Florida's extension service lists the usual limits:
how many birds you may keep, how far the coop must stand from the
neighbour's property, and a ban on roosters, with roaming birds
generally forbidden and the rules often enforced after a neighbour
complains. The CDC adds that many cities forbid roosters because their
crowing breaks noise ordinances. Coops can need a permit or a setback of
their own, and the same Florida publication notes that killing birds
for meat is generally prohibited in residential areas. Your city or
county planning and zoning office can tell you what applies to your lot.

You do not need a rooster for eggs. Both the Florida and Georgia
extension services make the point: a hen lays whether or not a male is
present, and a rooster is needed only if you want eggs that will hatch.

## The ways a flock makes people ill, and how to stop it

The CDC lists the diseases backyard poultry can pass to people: bird
flu, Campylobacter, E. coli, histoplasmosis and Salmonella. Young
children, older adults and people with weakened immune systems are the
most likely to fall ill. The part that surprises new keepers is that a
healthy-looking bird is no guarantee. In the CDC's words: "Remember that
poultry can appear healthy and clean, but still spread harmful germs
that make people sick."

The CDC's rules, which are the core of safe keeping:

- **Wash your hands with soap and running water** after touching the
  birds or anything in the area where they live, including after
  collecting eggs and after handling the feeder or the drinker. Hand
  sanitizer will do if there is no soap and water nearby, and the CDC
  suggests keeping some by the coop. Adults should watch young children
  wash.
- **Do not kiss or snuggle the birds** and then touch your face or mouth.
- **Keep the birds outdoors.** Never let them into the house, above all
  where food or drink is made, served or stored, and do not eat or drink
  where they live.
- **Keep a pair of shoes for the coop**, and leave those shoes outside.
- **Clean their equipment outdoors**, never indoors. A feeder rinsed in
  the kitchen sink brings the coop into the kitchen.
- **Wear gloves to clean up droppings**, never pick them up bare-handed,
  and wash your hands afterwards.
- **Children under 5 should not handle chicks, ducklings or other
  poultry**, and chicks should not be given to young children as gifts.
- **Wash any peck, bite or scratch at once** with soap and warm water.
  The CDC says to see a doctor, and to say a bird did it, if the bird
  seems sick, the wound is serious, the wound becomes red, painful, warm
  or swollen, or your last tetanus shot was more than 5 years ago.

### Bird flu

The CDC explains that backyard flocks can catch bird flu from wild
birds, and "Poultry can get very sick from bird flu and usually die from
their infection." Owners who handle sick birds are then at higher risk
themselves. Its rule is plain: do not touch sick or dead birds, their
droppings or litter, or anything their saliva or droppings may have
reached, without personal protective equipment such as gloves.

APHIS lists the signs to watch for: sudden deaths or more deaths than
usual; birds with no energy or appetite; fewer eggs, or soft-shelled or
misshapen ones; swelling of the head, neck, eyelids, comb, wattles or
lower legs, or purple colouring of the comb, wattles or legs; gasping,
sneezing or a runny nose; stumbling, falling or a twisted neck; and
greenish, watery diarrhoea. If you see sudden or large changes like
these, APHIS says to report them right away: "Report any sick or dying
birds to your State veterinarian or to USDA by calling toll-free
1-866-536-7593". Write that number inside the coop door before you need
it (general practice).

The same agencies describe the everyday habits, which APHIS calls
biosecurity, that keep disease out: clean clothes, shoes and tools
before you go into the bird area; wash your hands; keep visitors to
people who need to be there; protect the birds from pests and wildlife;
and, from the CDC, do not share tools or equipment with neighbours who
keep birds. New birds, even healthy-looking
ones, are kept apart from the flock for at least 30 days, and the CDC
suggests separate gloves, boots and clothes for each group during that
time, caring for the existing flock first.

### Fire: the heat lamp

Chicks need warmth for their first weeks, and the usual way to give it
is an infrared heat lamp. That lamp is the fire risk of chicken keeping.
In May 2021 Clark County, Washington, and its fire marshal warned that
heat lamps used to keep animals warm on cold spring nights had caused
multiple fires in the county, and passed on these tips from the National
Fire Protection Association: secure the lamp properly, because its
spring clamp alone will not stop it falling; stand a space heater on a
firm, steady surface where it cannot be knocked over; keep lamps and
heaters away from anything that can burn; brush dust and cobwebs off
fixtures and outlets; fit bulbs with covers that keep dirt off them; use
electrical equipment made for farm or commercial use; and do not run
extension cords into a coop.

North Dakota State University's extension service, writing about lamps
for young livestock in March 2023, adds detail: hang the lamp from
something that cannot burn, such as a chain with a locking connector,
never twine or rope, and fix it as if it were permanent; buy heavy-duty
lamps with thick glass bulbs; clean off dust, cobwebs and dead insects
and look for bare wires, loose sockets or broken bulbs before each use;
plug the lamp straight into an outlet, not an extension cord, on a
circuit protected against both ground faults (a GFCI) and arc faults (an
AFCI), without putting more on that circuit than it can carry; keep a
10-pound ABC fire extinguisher by the door; and check the
lamp several times a day, because an animal knocking it or chewing its
cord can start a fire in minutes.

Brooders are often set up in a garage, a shed or a spare room close to
the house, which is exactly where a fire matters most. Adding a new
outlet or circuit for a coop is a job for an electrician (general
practice).

### Lead in the run

Chickens scratch and peck at bare soil all day, and in older towns that
soil can hold lead from old paint and decades of traffic. A project of
Cornell University, the New York State Department of Health and New York
City's community garden programme tested soil and eggs from chicken
runs in the city's community gardens. Its notes to gardeners (dated
October 2012) say the United States has no health-based standard for
lead in chicken eggs. For a run whose soil tested above 400 parts per
million of lead, it advises: watch children in the run and have them
wash their hands; keep run soil out of the house by cleaning tools and
changing shoes; and cover the run with clean soil, mulch or other clean
material. Where the soil is high in lead or its level is not well known,
it says to give feed, grit and calcium in feeders rather than scattering
feed or scraps on bare ground. For gardens whose eggs carried more lead,
it adds more: keep the hens away from buildings painted with lead paint
and off the soil with more lead in it, do not feed them unwashed scraps
from that ground, brush dust and soil off the eggshells before using the
eggs, and, because lead can collect more in the yolk than in the white,
think about giving young children fewer yolks. Have the soil tested
before the birds go in (general practice); [Testing and Correcting
Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) explains where lead is likely and
how to sample for it.

## Getting your first birds

The CDC advises buying chicks and adult birds from hatcheries that take
part in the USDA's National Poultry Improvement Plan (NPIP), a programme
meant to reduce Salmonella in baby poultry, and choosing birds that are
bright, alert and active, with smooth, clean feathers. A bird that is
sluggish or dirty may be ill.

A few words you will meet. Pullets are young females that have not yet
started to lay. "Straight run" chicks have not been sorted by sex, and
the Florida extension service says to expect about half of them to be
males. If roosters are not allowed where you live, buy pullets that have
been sorted as female (general practice).

The extension services describe the breeds in three groups. Leghorns lay
the most eggs but are small birds with little meat. Dual-purpose breeds,
among them the Rhode Island Red, Plymouth Rock, New Hampshire, Wyandotte
and Orpington, lay somewhat fewer eggs and carry enough meat to be worth
eating. Florida's guide gives a range of breeds from about 150 to about
300 eggs a year.

## Chicks: the first weeks

**Warmth.** The Florida extension service gives day-old chicks 90 to
95 F (32 to 35 C) at chick level, lowered by 5 F (about 3 C) each week
until the chicks are about four weeks old or fully feathered; Georgia's
gives 90 F for the first week and the same weekly drop, ending at five
weeks. Florida calls a thermometer the most accurate check, hung at the
height of the chicks, not your eyes, and the chicks' own behaviour a good
indicator: chicks huddled close to the lamp are cold, chicks staying
away from it are too hot, and quiet chicks spread evenly are
comfortable.
Georgia suggests a ring of cardboard to keep chicks near the heat, with
room inside it to move away from the lamp. The temperature is changed by
raising or lowering the lamp, which is why the lamp must hang securely.

**Feed.** Florida's guide sets out the stages: a starter feed with 20 to
24 percent protein from hatching to six weeks (at least 4 pounds a
bird), a grower feed of 16 to 20 percent protein from six to eighteen
weeks, and a layer feed of 14 to 16 percent protein, with about 3.5 to
4 percent calcium, from eighteen weeks on. The order matters. The same
guide says chicks given layer feed develop kidney problems and rickets,
because they cannot use the extra calcium, and layers given starter or
grower feed do not get enough calcium to make eggs. The Pacific Northwest
publication gives one more reason to keep starter feed away from laying
hens: the drugs in medicated starter can leave residues in their eggs.
Some starter feed is medicated against
coccidiosis, an intestinal parasite; Florida notes that medicated feed
works against the live coccidiosis vaccine, so ask the hatchery whether
the chicks were vaccinated before you choose.

**Water**, clean and constant, from the first day.

## The house and the run

**Room.** Florida and Georgia both work from about 3 square feet
(0.28 m2) of floor inside the coop for each laying hen, and Florida
suggests 8 to 10 square feet (0.74 to 0.93 m2) per bird in an outdoor
run. For six hens that is a coop of about 18 square feet, say 4 by 5
feet, and a run of 48 to 60 square feet, say 6 by 10 feet (our
arithmetic). Florida adds that even a few birds will quickly turn a
fixed run to bare ground.

**Nest boxes.** Georgia and Nebraska both give one nest box for every
four to five hens, and Florida at least one for every five; about 12
inches (30 cm) each way, half filled with straw or wood shavings, set
about 2 feet (60 cm) off the ground, and in place by the time the
pullets are 18 to 20 weeks old.

**Litter and cleaning.** Georgia recommends an absorbent litter such as
pine shavings, rice hulls or peanut shells, with soiled litter replaced
as it gets dirty. Georgia advises against hardwood shavings: mould sometimes
grows in hardwood shavings that have begun to compost in storage, and
breathing it in can cause a serious brain infection, in chicks and in
the people who look after them. Florida suggests cleaning the nest boxes weekly and
cleaning and disinfecting the whole coop at least once a year, following
the disinfectant's label. The CDC's method for any cleaning: scrub off
droppings and dirt with soap and warm water first, because most
disinfectants do not work on a dirty surface; then apply the diluted
disinfectant, leave it for the time on its label, rinse and let it dry.

**Air.** The Pacific Northwest extension publication on laying lists
adequate ventilation in closed houses among the things that reduce
stress and keep hens laying, and the CDC describes a draft-free shelter
for winter. A coop needs fresh air
moving above the birds without a draught across them where they roost
(general practice).

**Predators.** The University of Maryland's extension service calls the
house the most important defence, and its advice is specific:

- Shut the birds in a predator-proof house every night.
- Bury the run's fence at least a foot (30 cm) deep and turn it outward
  for another 8 to 10 inches (20 to 25 cm), so an animal digging at the
  fence meets wire. Florida gives 12 to 18 inches in an L-shape.
- Raccoons climb, reach through wire to grab birds, and can open simple
  latches, so lock the doors. Window screens stop few predators; open
  windows need strong wire over them.
- Mink and weasels get through small holes, so keep the house in good
  repair.
- Hawks, eagles and owls are protected by federal law, the Migratory
  Bird Treaty Act, so the answer is a covered run, not killing them.
- Dogs are among the most common poultry predators.

Florida adds that hardware cloth, a stiff mesh with smaller openings
than chicken wire, better protects birds from smaller animals such as
snakes and rats, and that the top of a run should be covered against
animals that fly or climb. Maryland notes that dropped feed and untidy
ground near the coop attract rats and wild birds, and Georgia that rats
and mice carry disease and that controlling them is the keeper's job.

## Feed and water, every day

The Pacific Northwest publication's formula for laying hens is a layer
feed available all day, crushed oyster shell (calcium for the shells)
available all day in a separate feeder, and treats limited
to what the hens will clean up in about 15 minutes (Georgia says 10 to
20), because scraps, scratch grain and garden waste unbalance the diet.
Georgia calls feed the greatest cost of keeping chickens, and Florida
puts it at about 70 percent of the cost.

Florida separates two things that are often confused. Oyster shell or
limestone is calcium, dissolved and used to make shells. Grit is small
insoluble stone that sits in the gizzard and grinds food. Birds on
commercial feed may need little grit; birds that forage may need more.

Florida lists foods never to give the flock: raw green potato peels;
the leaves of rhubarb, peppers, potatoes, eggplant and tomatoes; raw,
dried or undercooked beans; avocado skin and pits; sweets and
chocolate; and alcohol and tobacco. It also warns that hens can take in
pesticides from a treated lawn or garden, and suggests throwing away
their eggs for several days if they may have.

**Water.** Florida says 25 hens drink about a gallon (3.8 L) a day, and
Georgia about a gallon for 20 hens in cool weather; both say the amount
rises sharply in hot weather. The Pacific Northwest publication names a
lack of feed or water among the stresses that can stop hens laying.
Georgia's advice is to scrub drinkers daily.

## Eggs: how many, and when

The Pacific Northwest publication gives the shape of a hen's laying
life. A pullet starts at 18 to 20 weeks, reaches her peak at about 35
weeks, when she lays more than 9 eggs in 10 days, holds that for about
10 weeks and then slowly declines. A commercial White Leghorn lays about
265 eggs a year; backyard breeds lay fewer. Florida expects about 200 to
240 eggs a year from each hen in a small backyard flock.

So six backyard hens might give about 1,200 to 1,440 eggs a year, an
average of about 23 to 28 a week (our arithmetic). The average hides the
seasons: more in spring and summer, far fewer in late autumn and winter,
for the reasons below.

**Daylight.** Shortening days slow laying and set off the moult. The
three extension services agree that 14 to 16 hours of light a day keeps
hens laying, which in winter means a light on a timer in the coop. The
Pacific Northwest publication says the light need only be bright enough
to read a newspaper, and that once you start a lighting programme you
must keep it going, because even one missed day can cut production.
Florida adds that the light should be off for part of every night, to
reduce the risk of stress-related cannibalism, hens pecking each other.
The Pacific Northwest publication also suggests letting hens moult
properly in their second winter by leaving the extra light off for
about six weeks.

**The moult.** Usually in autumn, though stress can bring it on at any
time, a hen drops and regrows her feathers and lays few or no eggs while
she does. Both Florida and the Pacific Northwest publication call it
natural, and Florida says the hen usually returns to laying when it is
over.

**Broodiness.** Sometimes a hen stops laying and sits on the nest to
hatch eggs. The Pacific Northwest publication says a nest full of eggs
usually sets it off, so collect eggs every day; if a hen will not leave
the nest, keep her away from it for several days and she will usually
return to laying.

**Age.** The same publication says hens lay well until they are 2 to 3
years old and then decline, with longer moults and more problems. A hen
that pushes part of her oviduct outside her body (a prolapse) must be
taken away from the flock at once, because the others will peck at it.
Florida notes that an older hen's feed can cost more than her eggs are
worth; some keepers keep her anyway, and others replace her. Decide
which you will do before you need to.

**Fewer eggs as a warning.** The Pacific Northwest publication says a
drop in laying is often the first sign of disease, and that moving,
handling, overheating, fright and missing feed or water can all stop a
hen laying. A sudden drop across the flock is a reason to look closely
at the birds, and at the APHIS signs above.

## Collecting, cleaning and keeping eggs

1. **Collect often.** The CDC says to collect eggs often, because eggs
   left in the nest get dirty or break; Florida says at least once a
   day, and Georgia suggests checking twice a day. Eggs left in the nest
   also encourage a hen to go broody.
2. **Start with clean eggs.** Clean nests make clean eggs. The CDC
   advises cleaning the coop, floor, nests and perches regularly to keep
   eggs clean.
3. **Throw away cracked eggs.** The CDC's reason: bacteria on the shell
   enter more easily through a crack.
4. **Clean dirt off dry if you can.** The CDC suggests fine sandpaper, a
   brush or a cloth.
5. **If you must wash, wash warm, never cold.** The CDC warns: "Don't
   wash warm, fresh eggs because colder water can pull bacteria into the
   egg." The federal rule for egg-packing plants (7 CFR 56.76(f)) sets
   wash water at 90 F (32 C) or higher and at least 20 F (11 C) warmer
   than the inside of the eggs, and says: "Eggs shall not be allowed to
   stand or soak in water." Florida's guide applies the same two numbers
   to backyard eggs and adds: dry them before they go in the
   refrigerator.
6. **Refrigerate at once.** The FDA's advice is to store eggs promptly in
   a clean refrigerator at 40 F (4 C) or below, checked with a
   refrigerator thermometer, in their carton, and to use them within 3
   weeks for best quality. Writing the date on the carton and using the
   oldest first is Florida's suggestion.
7. **Cook them through.** The FDA and the CDC both say to cook eggs until
   the yolk and the white are firm, and dishes containing eggs to 160 F
   (71 C). For anything served raw or lightly cooked, such as a homemade
   dressing, the FDA says to use eggs treated to kill Salmonella, by
   pasteurisation, for example. Your own hens' eggs are not treated, so
   keep them out of raw recipes (our reading of the FDA's advice).

**Selling or giving away eggs.** The CDC says to know and follow your
local rules and licences before selling eggs. The FDA's federal egg
safety rule covers farms with 3,000 or more laying hens that do not sell
all their eggs directly to consumers (21 CFR 118.1), so a small flock
answers to state and local rules instead; ask your state's department of
agriculture (general practice).

## The litter and the compost heap

Used litter and droppings make good compost, and they also carry the
germs this guide began with. The National Organic Program's standard
(7 CFR 205.203(c)), which certified organic farms must follow, makes a
useful benchmark for a home garden (our suggestion). It allows raw
manure on land for food crops only if it is worked into the soil at
least 120 days before harvesting a crop whose edible part touches the
soil, or 90 days before one whose edible part does not. Composted manure
must start with a carbon to nitrogen ratio between 25:1 and 40:1, and
must have held 131 to 170 F (55 to 77 C) for 3 days in an enclosed vessel
or a static aerated pile, or for 15 days in a windrow turned at least
five times in that period. Florida's advice for a
heap whose temperature you have not checked is to use that compost on
flowers and shrubs rather than food plants, and to keep the coop and the
heap at least 50 feet (15 m) from any stream, pond or lake, because
runoff carries the nutrients into the water. [Your First
Compost](your_first_compost.md) explains how to build a heap that gets
hot.

## A year with a small flock

- **Spring.** Chicks arrive; Florida notes that feed stores have them
  most often in spring. The brooder and its heat lamp are the main
  hazards of the year (above). Lengthening days bring the hens back into
  lay (the daylight rule above).
- **Summer.** Florida says water use rises sharply in hot weather, and
  the Pacific Northwest publication names overheating as a stress that
  stops laying. Give shade and moving air, and check the drinkers more
  than once on hot days (general practice).
- **Autumn.** Days shorten and the moult begins. Fewer eggs is normal.
  Decide now whether you will light the coop through the winter.
- **Winter.** The CDC describes keeping poultry warm outdoors with a
  draft-free shelter or a safe heat source; the fire warnings above
  apply to any heater, including a space heater on a steady surface
  where it cannot be knocked over and a circuit not overloaded. Water freezes, so check it morning and evening,
  or use a heated drinker made for poultry (general practice).
- **Every day, all year.** Feed, water, eggs collected, the door shut at
  dusk and opened in the morning, a look at every bird.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Sick or dying birds.** Report them as APHIS says (above), and work
  with a veterinarian who treats poultry; the CDC suggests calling ahead,
  because not every vet does. If birds fall sick or die soon after you
  bought them, the CDC says to tell the feed store or hatchery, to have a
  vet or extension agent look into the cause, to clean and disinfect the
  enclosure before using it again, and to consider waiting at least 30
  days before replacing the birds.
- **Wiring for lights and heat** in a coop: an electrician (general
  practice).
- **Killing a bird.** Check the local rules first, as the Florida guide
  says; the method belongs to the butchery topic, not this one. The same
  guide notes that disposal of a dead bird is set locally too, with
  Florida law, for example, requiring a burial two feet deep.
- **Selling eggs**: the state's rules (above).

## Traps

- **"They look healthy, so they are safe."** The CDC says otherwise. Wash
  your hands every time.
- **Thinking any wire keeps predators out.** Maryland's guide says
  raccoons reach through wire to grab birds and open simple latches.
  Lock the house every night.
- **The heat lamp on a spring clamp.** Hang it from a chain.
- **Washing eggs in cold water.** It draws bacteria in. Wash warm or dry
  clean.
- **Too many treats.** Scraps unbalance the diet and cut laying.
- **Starting the winter light and then forgetting it.** A lapse can stop
  laying.

## How the game models it

The game keeps chickens in the simplest way that still lets you collect
eggs.

- **Three hens at home.** Your home starts with three chickens wandering
  near the grain field in its fields room, set in
  `data/entities/livestock.ron`. Look at one up close and the prompt
  reads "[E] collect Egg (Chicken)" when she has one, or how many seconds
  until the next. Press E to take it.
- **An egg every five minutes.** Each hen gives one egg, then needs 300
  seconds on the game clock before the next (`renewable_product` in
  `data/creatures.csv`). At the default Realistic time speed a game
  second is a real second, so that is an egg every five minutes from
  each hen, hundreds a day, where a real hen at her peak lays a little
  under one. A hen holds one egg until you collect it.
- **Eggs spoil.** An egg spoils after 504 game hours (21 days) at room
  temperature, in your pack or in the home's storage, which the game's
  food data describes as the low end of 3 to 5 weeks in a refrigerator.
  The game has no refrigerator yet, so at its room temperature an egg
  keeps as long as a real egg would in a refrigerator, and in the home's
  Freezer twenty times as long (`data/food_system.ron`,
  `data/containers/types.csv`).
- **Raw eggs can make you ill.** Eating an egg raw carries a 15 percent
  chance of food poisoning, and eating a spoiled one always poisons you.
  Cooked dishes made with eggs, such as an omelette, a cake, cookies,
  cooked pasta or protein bars, carry no such risk
  (`data/food_system.ron`, `data/recipes.csv`).
- **The hens run from predators** that come within 12 metres, and they
  can be killed, which ends their eggs.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, materials
  are free while the Creative mode switch on the Inventory page is on,
  which it is unless you turn it off, so the cooking recipes need no
  eggs. Collecting eggs, spoilage and the risk of raw eggs work the same
  in every mode.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches. The
  eggs you collected are gone at the next launch, and the three hens
  start it with an egg each, ready.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: its hens
need no feed, no water, no house, no light and no cleaning; they never
fall ill, never moult, never go broody, never age and never stop laying;
there are no chicks, no roosters and no heat lamps; and nothing you do
with a hen can make you ill. Real chickens are the other way round, and
nearly all of this guide is the work the game leaves out.

## You own this when

- You know what your town allows, and whether you may keep a rooster.
- You wash your hands after every visit to the birds, keep coop shoes
  outside, keep the birds out of the house, clean their equipment
  outdoors, and keep children under 5 from handling them.
- You know the APHIS signs of serious illness and have the USDA number
  written where you can find it, and you never handle a sick or dead
  bird bare-handed.
- You hang a heat lamp from a chain, away from anything that burns,
  plugged straight into a protected outlet, and check it several times a
  day.
- You have tested the run's soil for lead if you live where it is
  likely, and you keep the hens away from buildings with lead paint.
- You buy from an NPIP hatchery and keep new birds apart for 30 days.
- You can set up a brooder at the right temperature, feed starter,
  grower and layer feed at the right ages, and read chicks by where they
  stand.
- Your house gives about 3 square feet a bird and a nest box for every
  four or five hens, the run's fence is buried and turned out, the top is
  covered, and the door is locked every night.
- You feed layer ration with oyster shell on the side, keep treats small,
  and never let the water run out.
- You collect eggs at least daily, throw away cracked ones, clean them
  dry or in warm water, refrigerate them at 40 F or below, and cook them
  through.
- You compost the litter hot, or keep it off food plants.
- You know which jobs belong to a vet, the state veterinarian, an
  electrician and your local rules.
