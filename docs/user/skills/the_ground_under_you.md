# The Ground Under You

Dig down far enough anywhere and the soil gives way to the material it
was made from: solid rock in some places, and in many more, a deep pile
of sand, gravel, silt or clay left by an ice sheet, a river, a lake or
the wind. That material, and the history of how it got there, decides a
surprising amount about a household's life. It sets what the soil is
like, where the ground water is and what is dissolved in it, whether a
radioactive gas seeps into the basement, whether the ground can sink or
slide, and what you could build with if you had to.

This guide explains the kinds of rock and sediment, how to read the
geologic time a map is written in, how to find out what is actually
under your own ground, and what each answer means for your water, your
air, your foundations and your garden. It ends with a worked example,
Silverdale, Washington, where almost everything at the surface was left
by a glacier.

Its facts come from the United States Geological Survey (USGS), the
Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the Mine Safety and Health
Administration (MSHA), the Occupational Safety and Health Administration
(OSHA), the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration
(PHMSA), the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the Department
of Agriculture, the armed services' hand tool manual and federal
regulations. All are United States government
publications, in the public domain. Where something is general practice
or our own reading, the text says so.

## First, where the ground can hurt you

Geology is mostly a matter of looking. The few ways it can kill you
come from digging into it, going under it, standing below or above
steep parts of it, and breathing what comes off it.

### Before you dig

- **Call 811 first, every time.** PHMSA says every digging project
  needs a call to the one-call centre before you start, even planting a
  tree or a shrub; that all states require the call 48 to 72 hours
  before digging; that the utility companies then mark their lines with
  flags or paint; and that the call and the marking cost you nothing.
  Then, in PHMSA's words, "wait the required time for facilities to be
  located and marked, respect the marks and dig with care".
- **The marks show only the operators' own lines.** PHMSA says the call
  lets the affected operators "locate and mark their underground
  facilities". Lines that belong to you or an earlier owner, such as
  power to a garage or shed, a feed to a well pump, a pipe to a propane
  tank or a sprinkler line, are not theirs, so expect them to be
  unmarked, and find them from your own records or with a private
  locating service before you dig (our reading of PHMSA's description,
  and general practice). Where a gas company does not look after a
  customer's own buried gas piping, the federal pipeline rule (49 CFR
  192.16) has it tell the customer so in writing, and tell them: "When
  excavating near buried gas piping, the piping should be located in
  advance, and the excavation done by hand." Dig by hand near any mark
  and any line you know of (general practice;
  [Square, Level and Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md) says the same).
- **Never get into a trench whose walls could fall on you.** OSHA's rule
  for workplaces (29 CFR 1926.652(a)(1)) requires every worker in an
  excavation to be protected from cave-ins by a designed protective
  system, unless the excavation is entirely in stable rock, or is less
  than 5 feet deep and a competent person has examined the ground and
  found no sign of a possible cave-in. Shallow is not the same as safe.
  [Square, Level and Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md) covers trenches
  and spoil piles in more detail.

### Mines, quarries and pits

MSHA runs a national campaign whose whole message is its name: "STAY
OUT - STAY ALIVE". Its list of what waits in old and active mine sites:

- vertical shafts that can be hundreds of feet deep and completely
  unprotected or hidden by vegetation, debris, dirt, rock or water;
- gases that can displace oxygen with no visible sign, causing
  suffocation;
- tunnel roofs and walls that decay and collapse;
- old explosives that can become unstable and explode on their own;
- miles of tunnel in which people get lost, with public maps often
  out of date;
- water-filled quarries and pits that hide rock ledges, machinery and
  other hazards. In MSHA's words, "The water may look refreshing but
  can be deceptively deep and dangerously cold. Steep, slippery walls
  can make exiting the water difficult."

MSHA's safety alert on abandoned mines gives the rules: do not enter any
mine opening, shaft or fenced or posted area, and treat every opening as
deadly; never swim or dive in quarries or pit lakes; no exploring,
drones or rope descents into mines, because recreational gear does not
protect against bad air, collapses or hidden shafts; report open shafts
and missing signs to the land manager, the state abandoned mine lands
office or the property owner; and note that entering sealed or posted
mine structures is trespassing. Teach children this one first (our
suggestion).

### Bluffs, cliffs and road cuts

Steep ground fails, and the person at the top and the person at the
bottom are both in the way. The USGS's study of landslides in Kitsap
County, Washington (the worked example below) found them concentrated
along the coastal bluffs. [Choosing Where to
Build](choosing_where_to_build.md) and [The Hazards Where You
Live](the_hazards_where_you_live.md) cover slope hazards for a home. For
looking at rock: study a bluff, cliff or road cut from a safe distance,
do not climb or dig into its face, and stay off the bottom of it after
heavy rain (general practice). Keep well back from the top edge too,
whether you are looking at the view or at the rock (general practice):
the same USGS report found that, in the north of the county, many of
the smaller landslide deposits are mostly debris topples and falls
"that initiate at the tops of coastal bluffs", and that their
escarpments often run from the bluff top to the high-tide mark.

### Breaking and cutting rock

- **Protect your eyes.** The armed services' hand tool manual (TM
  9-243) says eye protection is needed because of flying objects such as
  chipped concrete, among other hazards, and that it is "absolutely
  necessary" to use eye protection during operations that endanger the
  eyes. Breaking a rock with a hammer is one (our application).
- **Do not breathe the dust.** OSHA says crystalline silica is a common
  mineral found in the earth's crust, present in sand, stone, concrete
  and mortar, and that the very fine, breathable dust is created when
  stone, rock, concrete, brick and mortar are cut, sawed, ground,
  drilled or crushed. Breathing it raises the risk of silicosis, which
  OSHA calls "an incurable lung disease that can lead to disability and
  death". Its construction rule (29 CFR 1926.1153) shows the household
  answer: for a handheld power saw it requires one with a built-in
  water feed that continuously wets the blade, plus a respirator when
  cutting indoors (or outdoors for more than four hours in a shift);
  and it forbids dry sweeping or dry brushing silica dust where wet
  sweeping or a HEPA-filtered vacuum would work. So cut
  stone and concrete wet, outdoors if you can, with a respirator, and
  clean up wet (general practice following the rule, which is written
  for workplaces).
- **Water and a corded saw need a GFCI.** Wet cutting puts water next
  to mains electricity. Use a saw made to cut wet, the rule's
  "integrated water delivery system", rather than a dry saw with a hose
  turned on it (general practice), and plug it into a ground-fault
  circuit interrupter (GFCI): an outlet, breaker or portable plug-in
  unit that cuts the power before a shock can kill. The Consumer
  Product Safety Commission (CPSC) says GFCIs matter most "where
  electrical equipment is near water", that portable ones give
  protection "even if a GFCI is not installed on the circuit", and
  advises them "With electric tools (drills, saws, sanders, etc.) for
  do-it-yourself work in and around the house". Test the GFCI before
  you start (our advice; CPSC says to test every GFCI at least once a
  month and according to the manufacturer's instructions).

### Collecting rocks: whose ground is it?

What you may take depends on who owns the land.

- **Public lands managed by the Bureau of Land Management.** The rule
  (43 CFR 8365.1-5) allows anyone to collect "reasonable amounts" of
  rocks, mineral specimens and semiprecious gemstones for
  noncommercial purposes, except on developed recreation sites or where
  collecting is prohibited and posted. It does not allow explosives or
  motorised or mechanical devices (metal detectors excepted) to help,
  and collecting to sell needs a contract or permit.
- **National parks.** The rule (36 CFR 2.1) prohibits possessing,
  removing, digging or disturbing "a mineral resource or cave formation
  or the parts thereof". Leave rocks where they are.
- **Other public land and private land.** Rules differ; ask the
  managing office or the owner first (general practice).

## Rock comes in three kinds

Every rock belongs to one of three families, named for how it formed.
The USGS describes them this way.

**Igneous rock** forms when molten rock cools and hardens. If it cools
slowly deep underground, over many thousands or millions of years, the
mineral crystals have time to grow large and the rock is coarse-grained:
granite, diorite and gabbro are examples. If it erupts and cools fast at
the surface, the crystals stay tiny or do not form at all, so the rock
is fine-grained or glassy, often with gas bubbles trapped in it: basalt,
andesite, rhyolite and obsidian.

**Sedimentary rock** forms from pieces of older rocks, or of
once-living things, that piled up at the surface, were buried, lost
their water and became cemented. It is often visibly layered. Its
commonest kinds are named by grain size, smallest first: shale (clay),
siltstone (silt), sandstone (sand) and conglomerate (pebbles larger
than 2 millimetres in a sandy or muddy matrix). Those are the same size
words as the soil textures in [What Soil Is](what_soil_is.md), and the
same 2 millimetre line divides sand from anything coarser in both.
Limestone forms from the remains of living things or by precipitating
out of water.

**Metamorphic rock** started as one of the other kinds and was changed
by heat, pressure or hot mineral-rich fluids without melting, usually
deep underground or where tectonic plates meet. The USGS describes such
rocks as often "squished, smeared out, and folded". Common examples are
phyllite, schist, gneiss, quartzite and marble; some are banded or
split into sheets because pressure lined their minerals up.

**Why a household cares.** The rock a soil formed from leaves its mark
on that soil. NRCS's sheet on soil pH contrasts the pH of soils formed
in calcareous (lime-rich) material with those formed in granite. The
USGS's booklet on radon says that, in general, a soil holds about the
same amount of uranium as the rock it came from. Some rocks dissolve,
which is how sinkholes form. And some rocks, as the next sections show,
put things into your water and your air.

## Most people live on sediment, not bedrock

In many places, the worked example below among them, solid rock is far
below. What you dig into, build on and drill a well through is loose
sediment: sand, gravel, silt and
clay laid down by ice, rivers, lakes, the sea or the wind, and not yet
turned to stone. Soil scientists call whatever a soil formed in, rock
or sediment, its parent material, and the deepest, least changed layer
of a soil is made of it ([What Soil Is](what_soil_is.md) shows where it
sits in a soil profile).

Sediment matters because its grain size controls how water and air move
through it. Coarse sand and gravel let water and gas through quickly;
clay lets them through far more slowly. The USGS's radon booklet uses
exactly that contrast, and so does its explanation of why the coastal
bluffs in the worked example below slide.

## Reading geologic time

Geologic maps and reports date the ground in millions and thousands of
years, with names and letter codes. The USGS's chart of geologic time
(Fact Sheet 2018-3054, August 2018) gives the ones a householder meets
most:

- **ka** means thousands of years ago and **Ma** millions of years ago.
- The **Holocene** is the last 11,700 years (0.0117 Ma).
- The **Pleistocene**, the age of the great ice sheets, ran from 2.58 Ma
  to the start of the Holocene. Together they make the **Quaternary**,
  map symbol **Q**.
- The **Tertiary**, map symbol **T**, ran from 66 Ma to 2.58 Ma. The fact
  sheet notes that "T" and "Q" have been used on geologic maps for more
  than a century.
- The Earth itself is about 4,600 million years old.

So a map unit whose symbol starts with Q dates from the ice ages or
since, and one that starts with T is older (our reading of the
symbols). For example, the USGS's Kitsap landslide report describes an
earlier USGS map of the area's surficial deposits (Yount and others,
1993) that "shows composite historic and prehistoric landslide
deposits as a distinct map unit (Qls)" (Quaternary, landslide, in our
reading of the letters).

## How to find out what is under you

Work from the most local and easiest to the more technical.

1. **Your soil survey.** The USDA soil survey describes, for each
   mapped soil, the material it formed in and what lies beneath it.
   [Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) walks through
   looking up your ground in Web Soil Survey and reading the official
   description of your soil series.
2. **A geologic map.** The USGS's National Geologic Map Database is, in
   its own description, "the authoritative, comprehensive National
   Archive for maps and reports about the Nation's geology", holding
   maps from the USGS and the state geological surveys. Its mapView
   site shows geologic maps on a zoomable map
   (https://ngmdb.usgs.gov/mapview/). Look up your
   place, note the unit, and read its description in the map's legend
   (general practice). Your state's geological survey usually publishes
   more detailed local maps (general practice).
3. **Look at exposures.** Road cuts, stream banks, quarry rims seen
   from outside the fence, and coastal bluffs show the layers in
   section. The USGS's Kitsap report notes that older deposits there
   are exposed mainly in the lower parts of coastal bluffs. Look from a
   safe place, as above.
4. **Ask about wells.** Well drillers and the state agency that
   records wells know what drillers found at depth in your area
   (general practice; [Where Water Comes
   From](where_water_comes_from.md) covers private wells).

## What the ground decides for you

### What is in your soil

The rock or sediment sets the soil's starting texture and chemistry:
sand from a sandy outwash, clay from a lake bed, a lime-rich soil over
limestone, an acid one over granite or under heavy rain. NRCS's pH sheet
says soil parent material influences soil properties including pH.
[Soil Chemistry](soil_chemistry.md) explains why that matters for what
plants can take up.

### What is in your water

Ground water picks up what it flows through. The USGS, with the Centers
for Disease Control and Prevention, estimated in 2017 that about 2.1
million people in the United States may be drinking from private wells
with high concentrations of arsenic, presumed to be from natural
sources, and said that nearly all of the arsenic in the ground water
they tested is likely from natural sources, "presumed to be coming
primarily from rocks and minerals through which the water flows". Its
list of places where the most people may be affected begins with much of
the West, Washington included. Its hydrologist's conclusion was blunt:
"the only way for them to be certain of what's in their water is to
have it tested."

Radon dissolves in ground water too. The USGS's radon booklet says
areas with high uranium in the underlying rock are the ones most likely
to have radon in ground water, with granites in various parts of the
country as its example. [Testing Water](testing_water.md) covers what
to test a well for and how to read the results.

### What is in the air of your house: radon

EPA estimates that nearly 1 in every 15 homes in the United States has
an elevated radon level, and nobody can sense it.

EPA calls radon "a cancer-causing, radioactive gas" and says you cannot
see, smell or taste it. It comes from the natural breakdown of uranium
in soil, rock and water, and it usually rises through the ground and
enters a house through cracks and other openings in the foundation,
where the house traps it. EPA says the Surgeon General has warned that
radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the United States,
after smoking, and estimates it causes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths
a year. Smoking and radon together are an especially serious risk.

The geology explains where it is more likely. The USGS's booklet says
most rocks hold 1 to 3 parts per million of uranium, but that some,
including light-coloured volcanic rocks, granites, dark shales,
phosphate-bearing sedimentary rocks and the metamorphic rocks made from
them, can hold as much as 100. It says radon moves more easily through
permeable ground, such as coarse sand and gravel, than through clay,
and lists coarse glacial deposits and fractured or cavernous bedrock
among the places where homes may have high indoor radon. But the
booklet's own preface says it is important that all houses be measured,
and EPA puts it plainly: "Any home may have a radon problem", old or
new, sealed or drafty, with or without a basement.

What to do, from EPA's *A Citizen's Guide to Radon*:

1. **Test.** EPA and the Surgeon General recommend testing all homes
   below the third floor. Low-cost do-it-yourself kits exist, or you
   can hire a qualified tester. Place the kit in the lowest lived-in
   level, in a room used regularly but not the kitchen or bathroom, at
   least 20 inches above the floor, away from drafts, heat, humidity
   and outside walls. For a short test, keep windows and outside doors
   closed as much as possible, and for a 2 or 3 day test close them at
   least 12 hours before you start.
2. **Read the number.** EPA recommends fixing a home at 4 picocuries per
   litre (pCi/L) or more, and, on its radon publications page, says to
   consider fixing between 2 and 4 pCi/L, adding that there is no known
   safe level of exposure. The guide puts the average indoor level at
   about 1.3 pCi/L and outdoor air at about 0.4.
3. **Confirm before you fix.** If a first short-term test reads 4 or
   more, follow it with a long-term test (more than 90 days) or a second
   short-term test. Fix the home if the long-term result is 4 or more,
   or consider fixing if the average of the two short-term tests is 4 or
   more.
4. **Fix it properly.** The usual fix is a vent pipe and fan that draw
   radon from beneath the house and vent it outside. EPA says lowering
   high radon levels requires technical knowledge and special skills,
   and that you should use a contractor trained to fix radon problems;
   your state radon office can list qualified contractors.
5. **Test again.** After the fix, and every two years after that.

If your air test shows a problem and your water comes from a well,
EPA says to have the water tested for radon too, though radon from the
soil is usually a much larger risk than radon from water.

### Ground that sinks or slides

**Sinkholes.** The USGS says sinkholes are common where the rock below
the surface is limestone, other carbonate rock, salt beds, or other
rocks that ground water can dissolve, leaving spaces that eventually
cannot hold up the land above. It names Florida, Texas, Alabama,
Missouri, Kentucky, Tennessee and Pennsylvania as the states where most
sinkhole damage tends to occur. Most sinkholes form slowly, but some
collapse within hours. The USGS adds that people cause new ones, by
pumping ground water, by construction, and by changing natural
drainage: in January 2010, more than 110 sinkholes opened around Dover,
Florida, after farmers pumped ground water to protect crops from a
freeze. If your geologic map shows limestone or other soluble rock, ask
about sinkholes before you buy or build (general practice).

**Landslides and earthquakes.** These are covered for a home in [The
Hazards Where You Live](the_hazards_where_you_live.md) and [Choosing
Where to Build](choosing_where_to_build.md). The geology behind one
kind of landslide is in the worked example below.

## Worked example: Silverdale, Washington

Silverdale sits on the Kitsap Peninsula, on the west side of Puget
Sound. The USGS's lidar study of landslides in Kitsap County (Open-File
Report 2008-1292) summarises its geology, and almost all of it is one
story: ice.

**Shaped by an ice sheet.** The report says most of the county's
landforms are glacially sculpted and mostly record the youngest of
several advances and retreats of the Puget lobe of the Cordilleran ice
sheet during the Pleistocene, with the youngest advance and retreat
across the Puget Lowland between about 18,000 and 16,000 years ago. Its
lidar images show flutes, troughs and outwash channels running mostly
north to south, which now form the area's north-trending hills, U-shaped
valleys and plateaus.

**Buried under what the ice left.** Most of the county, the report
says, is directly underlain by Vashon till, a dense, gravelly and
bouldery deposit left by the ice. Beneath the till lies advance
outwash, the sand and gravel washed out in front of the
advancing glacier, exposed mainly in the sides of drainages and along
coastal slopes; and beneath that, in many areas, a fine-grained unit.
Around Seattle, the layer that commonly lies under the advance outwash
is the Lawton Clay, which the report describes as a laminated silt- to
clay-rich deposit laid down in lakes in front of the advancing glacier. For Kitsap it is
more careful: more recent mapping in parts of the county, it says,
suggests that the fine-grained unit there "may be, or may correlate
with, the Lawton Clay Member of the Seattle area".
Older Pleistocene deposits and Tertiary bedrock show only along the
lower parts of the coastal bluffs, in some deep valleys, and in the
Gold Mountain and Green Mountain hills in the southwest of the county,
which are made of basaltic bedrock and rise to about 500 metres. Since the ice, only
relatively minor beach, lake, bog and stream deposits, landslides and
slope debris have been added.

**What that means for a household there.**

- **The soil.** This project's Silverdale soil data, drawn from the
  USDA soil survey, finds that about 64 percent of the area is a thin
  gravelly soil over dense glacial till, the Alderwood series alone
  covering 49.9 percent with its till roughly 50 to 100 cm down; and
  that the local limit on building is not bedrock but that till, with
  water perched on it through the wet season. [Your Soil,
  Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) works through what that
  means for a garden.
- **The bluffs.** The USGS report found that about 77 percent of the
  mapped landslides involve the advance outwash, and describes the
  explanation first made for Seattle: water sinks through the permeable
  outwash sand, is stopped by the fine-grained layer beneath, and builds
  pressure in the sand above it until the slope fails. It also notes a
  later study arguing that erosion at the base of the bluffs matters
  even more. [Choosing Where to Build](choosing_where_to_build.md) has
  what it means for a building site.
- **Radon.** The USGS radon booklet's national overview describes the
  volcanic basalts of the Pacific Northwest coastal mountains as low in
  uranium, but Silverdale's surface is glacial sediment, and the same
  booklet lists coarse glacial deposits among the permeable ground where
  homes may have high indoor radon. No map settles it for your house;
  EPA's advice to test every home applies here as everywhere (our
  reading of the two sources together).
- **Well water.** The USGS's 2017 arsenic estimate lists Washington
  among the places where the most people may have high arsenic in
  private wells. If you drink from a well, test it ([Testing
  Water](testing_water.md)).
- **Building stone.** There is little solid rock at the surface to
  quarry; what the ground offers is gravel and sand in the outwash, and
  the till, which is hard to dig (our reading of the report and the
  soil data).

## Where your work stops

- **Building on a slope, a bluff, fill, or soluble rock:** a licensed
  geotechnical engineer or engineering geologist, before you buy or
  build (general practice; [Choosing Where to
  Build](choosing_where_to_build.md) covers whom to ask).
- **Fixing radon:** a contractor trained in radon reduction, found
  through your state radon office (EPA).
- **Testing a well:** a certified laboratory ([Testing
  Water](testing_water.md)).
- **Any trench a person must stand in:** protection designed for it, or
  do not go in (OSHA's rule above).
- **Mines, shafts, quarry water and anything that looks like old
  explosives:** never. Leave, and report it (MSHA).

## Traps

- **"The neighbours tested, so we know our radon level."** Radon varies
  from house to house; the USGS booklet notes that houses on
  uranium-poor soils can still have high indoor radon. Test your own.
- **"Clear, cold well water is clean."** Arsenic and radon have no taste
  or colour. Test it.
- **"It is only a few feet deep."** Trenches collapse; OSHA's shallow
  exception depends on someone competent examining the ground.
- **"That quarry is a great swimming hole."** MSHA says never to swim
  or dive in quarries or pit lakes, and warns that what is under the
  surface and the shock of cold water kill even strong swimmers.
- **Taking rocks home from a national park.** It is prohibited.
- **Dry-cutting stone or concrete indoors.** Silica dust is the reason
  the rule calls for water and a respirator.
- **"811 marked everything."** It marked the operators' lines. The
  cable to your shed and the pipe to your propane tank are yours to
  find.

## How the game models it

- **The shape of the Earth is real, coarsely.** The game's Earth is
  drawn from NOAA's ETOPO 2022 global relief model, averaged to cells of
  0.05 degrees, about 5.5 km apart, so mountain ranges, valleys and
  coasts are where they are on the real planet
  (`data/planets/earth_heightmap.bin`). Finer tiles of about 460 m can
  be added, but they are not part of the standard data folder.
- **What is under the surface is not.** There are no rock types,
  sediments or ground water beneath the terrain, and nothing to dig,
  drill or test. The ground's surface materials, such as grass, dirt,
  rock and sand, are textures chosen for how they look
  (`data/ground/materials.ron`). There is no radon, arsenic, sinkhole
  or quarry in the game.
- **A geology file waits to be used.** The data folder carries
  `data/geology.ron`, listing rock types (granite, basalt and others in
  the three families of this guide), ore veins and soil types, but in
  the current build nothing reads it during play.
- **Mining happens in space.** The Mining section of the Inventory page
  sends a drone to one of three test asteroids (a metallic, a stony and
  a carbon-rich one) placed for the mining loop during development. It
  brings back ores such as iron, nickel, copper and graphite, and every
  unit of ore it delivers adds one point of experience to your Mining
  skill (`src/systems/mining.rs`).

**Settings that change this.** Settings > Gameplay > "Start every session
from the default home" is off by default, so the asteroids stay as you
mined them, and the ore you brought home and the Mining experience you
earned are kept between launches. With the setting on, only your
character (name, look and outfit) carries between launches: the
asteroids start full again, and the ore and the experience are not
kept.

## You own this when

- You call 811 before every hole, find your own private lines, dig by
  hand near the marks, and never stand in a trench whose walls could
  fall on you.
- You and your children know to stay out of mines, shafts and quarry
  water.
- You wear eye protection to break rock, and cut stone wet, with a
  respirator, on a saw made for water and plugged into a tested GFCI.
- You look at bluffs from well back from the top edge and stay off
  their base after heavy rain.
- You can name the three rock families and say how each forms.
- You can read "Q", "T", "ka" and "Ma" on a geologic map, and you have
  looked up your own ground in a soil survey and on a geologic map.
- You can say what your ground is likely to put into your water and
  your air, and you have tested your home for radon and, if you have a
  well, your water.
- You know whether your area has soluble rock, steep bluffs or deep
  fill, and who to ask before building on them.
- You know where you may and may not collect rocks.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- Teaching children the mine rule first is our suggestion.
- Looking at bluffs and cuts from a safe distance, keeping well back
  from the top edge, and staying off their base after heavy rain are
  general practice; the USGS report supplies the reason for the top
  edge.
- That the 811 marks leave out lines the household owns is our reading
  of PHMSA's description; finding those lines from records or with a
  private locating service, and digging by hand near any mark or known
  line, are general practice (49 CFR 192.16 says the hand digging for
  customer-owned gas piping).
- Breaking rock as an eye-hazard operation is our application of TM
  9-243; cutting stone wet, outdoors, with a respirator, and cleaning up
  wet, are general practice following OSHA's workplace rule.
- Using a saw made for wet cutting rather than adding water to a dry
  saw is general practice; testing the GFCI before each job is our
  advice, beside CPSC's monthly test.
- Asking the managing office or owner before collecting on other land
  is general practice.
- Reading "Qls" as Quaternary landslide deposit is our reading of the
  map-symbol convention; the unit itself is on the 1993 map by Yount
  and others, as the Kitsap report describes it.
- Reading a geologic map's legend, state geological surveys' local
  maps, and asking well drillers and the state well-records agency are
  general practice.
- Asking about sinkholes where maps show soluble rock, and hiring a
  geotechnical engineer or engineering geologist for difficult ground,
  are general practice.
- The radon, building-stone and bluff conclusions for Silverdale are
  our reading of the USGS and EPA sources together with this project's
  soil data.
