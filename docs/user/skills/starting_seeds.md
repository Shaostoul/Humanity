# Starting Seeds

If you have grown one plant from a purchased start, the next skill is
raising plants from a seed packet yourself. It costs a fraction as much,
it opens up hundreds of varieties a store will never stock, and it moves
the start of your growing season indoors, onto a windowsill or a shelf,
weeks before the weather allows anything outside.

One honest thing first: seed starting is the fussiest stage of growing.
Seeds fail to sprout, seedlings flop over dead, whole trays go moldy.
Every one of those failures has a boring, fixable cause, and all of them
are covered below. Losing a tray costs a few cents and two weeks; that
is the whole downside.

## Why seeds: the math

A packet of seeds typically costs about $1 to $3 and can hold 100 or
more seeds. A single young plant (a "transplant" or "start") at a
nursery or big-box store runs about $2 to $4, sometimes more. So one
packet, even if only half the seeds grow, can produce more plants than
most gardens have room for, for less than the price of one bought
plant. Prices drift year to year and store to store; the ratio is the
point. Leftover seeds keep for later seasons if stored cool and dry.

## What to start indoors, what to sow straight outside

Not everything wants an indoor head start. The packet will say which
camp a crop is in; here is the pattern:

- Start indoors: crops that need a long warm season, longer than most
  climates provide outdoors. Tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, broccoli,
  cabbage, cauliflower, and onions are the classic list.
- Sow direct (straight in the garden): beans, cucumbers, and melons do
  not transplant well and should go into the ground where they will
  grow. Carrots and other root crops also go straight in the ground,
  since the root you eat forms badly if disturbed. Peas are quick and
  cold-tolerant and are sown outside early. Squash is usually sown
  direct too, though it can be transplanted young where summers are
  short.

This guide is about the first camp: the indoor start.

## When to start: count backward from your last frost

Everything is timed from your area's average last spring frost date.
To find yours, search your state extension service's name plus "last
frost date," ask your county extension office, or use the National
Gardening Association's nationwide zip-code frost date lookup
(garden.org/apps/frost-dates). It is an average, not a promise; a late
frost can land after it.

Then count backward. Weeks of indoor growing before transplant time,
by crop:

- Lettuce and other leafy greens: 2 to 4 weeks
- Cucumber, squash, melon (indoor-start only where summers are too
  short for direct sowing; disturb the roots as little as possible at
  transplant): 3 to 5 weeks
- Cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower: 4 to 6 weeks
- Tomato: 5 to 6 weeks
- Pepper, eggplant, onion: 8 to 10 weeks

Starting too early is the beginner's classic mistake: you get huge,
starved, tangled plants and nowhere to put them. When in doubt, start
later.

## What you need

Cheapest options first.

- Containers, 2 to 3 inches deep. Recycled ones are fine: yogurt cups,
  cut-down milk jugs, the clear boxes salad greens come in. Two rules:
  every container must have drainage holes punched in the bottom, and
  any container that held plants before must be sterilized first (see
  damping-off below for how). A solid tray underneath, like a baking
  sheet, catches water and enables bottom watering.
- A bag of seed-starting mix. This is not the same as potting mix: it
  is a finer, lighter, soil-free blend (usually peat and vermiculite)
  that tiny roots can push through and that starts out free of the
  organisms that kill seedlings. Buy it new. Never use garden soil or
  compost for seed starting, and never reuse last year's mix; both
  carry the damping-off organisms described below.
- Seed packets. The packet is your instruction sheet: depth, spacing,
  days to germinate, and indoor-vs-direct advice are printed on it.
- Light. Read the light section below before assuming a window will
  do. An inexpensive LED or fluorescent shop light on a cheap outlet
  timer is the reliable answer.
- Optional but helpful: a waterproof seedling heat mat (made for damp
  trays; do not substitute a household heating pad, which is not built
  to live under wet containers), and labels, because you will not
  remember which cup is which.

## Sowing: the steps

1. Moisten the mix before filling: dump it in a bucket, add water, and
   stir until it is damp as a wrung-out sponge, not dripping.
2. Fill the containers, tap them to settle the mix, and set them on
   the tray.
3. Plant at the right depth. The rule of thumb: a seed goes about
   twice as deep as it is wide, deep enough that one more seed could
   sit directly on top of it. Big seeds go a knuckle deep; dust-fine
   seeds are barely covered or just pressed onto the surface. Too deep
   is a common cause of nothing coming up. Drop 2 or 3 seeds per
   container; you will thin to one later.
4. Label each container with the crop and the date.
5. Keep them warm. A potting mix temperature of about 65 to 75 F suits
   most vegetable seeds; a heat mat holding the mix at 70 to 75 F
   speeds things up and is itself a damping-off preventer. The top of
   a refrigerator is the traditional free warm spot.
6. Keep the mix damp, never soggy, and wait. The packet's "days to
   germinate" number tells you when to expect sprouts and when to
   start worrying.

## Light: the sunny-window honesty

The day sprouts appear, they need strong light, and this is where most
first attempts go wrong. A windowsill, even a good south-facing one,
usually delivers too few hours of weak, one-sided late-winter light.
The result is "leggy" seedlings: tall, pale, thin stems flopping
toward the glass. Leggy seedlings are weak seedlings.

The fix costs little: a plain LED or fluorescent shop light, hung on
adjustable chains or propped so you can raise it. Extension guidance:
keep the light close, 2 to 6 inches above the leaf tops, and closer is
better (Minnesota Extension advises no more than 4; raise it as the
plants grow), and run it 12 to 16 hours a day. An outlet timer makes the hours automatic.

Electricity and watering share a shelf here, so name the hazard: keep
plugs, timers, and connections up out of any possible drips, water
with dry hands, and unplug the light before adjusting its height. If
your setup involves rewiring a fixture or an outlet, that is a job for
a qualified electrician, not a guess.

Seedlings also like it cooler than germinating seeds: roughly 55 to
60 F at night and 65 to 70 F by day grows stockier plants than
constant warmth.

## Watering from below

Overhead watering flattens seedlings and washes seeds around. Instead,
pour water into the solid tray underneath and let the mix drink it up
through the drainage holes. When the surface turns dark and damp, pour
off whatever still stands in the tray; containers must not sit in a
puddle. Water again only when the surface has dried out. Constantly
soggy mix is the single biggest invitation to damping-off.

## Thinning: the emotional hurdle

You planted 2 or 3 seeds per container, and if you were lucky they all
came up. Now cut down all but the strongest one, snipping the extras
off at the soil line with scissors. Do not pull them out; pulling
tears the roots of the seedling you are keeping.

Everyone hates this step. Sparing the extras feels kind and is not:
crowded seedlings starve each other into weakness, all of them. One
strong plant per container is the goal. Snip, do not mourn.

## Feeding, briefly

Seed-starting mix contains little or no food, but a seed carries its
own lunchbox, so nothing is needed at first. Once a seedling has
several sets of "true leaves" (the adult-shaped leaves that appear
after the first plain pair), feed weekly with a water-soluble
fertilizer mixed at one quarter of the package strength. Full-strength
fertilizer burns seedlings. If your bag is a potting mix with
fertilizer already in it, it can carry a seedling for at least 6 weeks
with no additions.

## Damping-off: the classic seedling killer

Damping-off is a group of soil-dwelling molds and fungi that attack
seeds and new seedlings. You will know it when you see it: seeds that
never come up, or seedlings that looked fine yesterday now collapsed
flat, the stem pinched, brown, and water-soaked at the soil line.
Sometimes a fluffy white cobweb growth appears on the mix. There is no
cure once a seedling falls; prevention is the whole game:

- Use new, bagged seed-starting or potting mix, never garden soil,
  compost, or last year's mix.
- Sterilize previously used pots and trays: soak 30 minutes in a 10%
  bleach solution (1 part plain household bleach to 9 parts water),
  then rinse well. Handle with care: this solution is for tools only,
  never for drinking or skin contact, keep it away from children and
  pets, mix it in a ventilated space, and never combine bleach with
  other cleaners.
- Water with clean, room-temperature water, and never let containers
  sit soggy; water from below and pour off the excess.
- Keep germination warm (the 70 to 75 F mat) so seedlings sprint
  through their vulnerable stage, and give sprouted seedlings their
  full 12 to 16 hours of light.
- Gentle air movement from a small fan on low, out of splash range of
  the watering tray, helps the surface dry between waterings.

## Hardening off: the two-week goodbye

An indoor seedling has never met wind, direct sun, or a cold night; put
it straight into the garden and it scalds and stalls. "Hardening off"
is a one-to-two-week transition that toughens it up. Beginning about
two weeks before transplant day:

1. Days 1 and 2: set the plants outside in a shaded, wind-protected
   spot for a few hours (two or three) on a mild day, then bring them
   back in.
2. Each following day: leave them out a little longer and let them
   have a little more direct sun, morning sun first.
3. Bring them in every night, and skip any day that is windy or below
   45 F; cold and wind undo the work.
4. Water a bit less during these weeks (never to the point of
   wilting); slight thirst also toughens.
5. By the end of the transition they can stand a full day of sun and
   are ready for the ground.

## Transplant timing

Cool-season crops (broccoli, cabbage, lettuce) can go out a few weeks
before the last frost date. Warm-season crops (tomatoes, peppers,
eggplant, squash) wait until all danger of frost is past and the soil
has warmed; their roots grow poorly in cold soil, and frost kills them
outright. Watch the forecast, not just the calendar: the frost date is
an average, and one cold night can erase two months of work. If frost
threatens after transplanting, cover the plants overnight.

## What can go wrong

- Nothing germinated: usually one of four causes. Too cold (the mix,
  not the room, must reach the 65 to 75 F range); planted too deep
  (the twice-the-seed's-width rule); moisture failure (the mix dried
  out mid-sprout, or stayed so wet the seeds rotted); or old, dead
  seed. Check the packet's days-to-germinate before declaring failure,
  add warmth, and resow shallower. Two weeks and a resow is all a
  total loss costs.
- Mold or green film on the soil surface: the surface is staying wet
  and the air is still. Water less often, always from below, pour off
  standing water, and add the small fan. Surface mold is a warning
  shot, not yet a dead tray; damping-off follows the same conditions.
- Leggy, pale seedlings: not enough light, full stop. Move the light
  down to a few inches above the leaves, run it 12 to 16 hours, and
  cooler days will help. Leggy tomato seedlings get one mercy: at
  transplant time, bury the extra stem, and it grows roots.
- Seedlings collapsed at the base: that is damping-off (see above).
  Remove the fallen and their containers from the tray, let the
  survivors' surface dry, add air movement, and review the prevention
  list; next sowing, start with clean everything.

## How you know it worked

Stout, short-stemmed seedlings with deep green true leaves, standing
straight without leaning; roots that hold the mix together in one
piece when you slide a plant out; and after hardening off, plants that
shrug off a full day outdoors. If, some weeks after transplant, you
are eating from a plant that existed only as a dry speck in an
envelope last winter, you have the whole skill, and every packet on
the seed rack is now an open door.
