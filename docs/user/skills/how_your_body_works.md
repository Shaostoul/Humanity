# How Your Body Works

Your body is running dozens of jobs at once, all the time, without asking
you. It pumps blood, moves air, keeps its temperature steady, turns food
into fuel, clears out waste and fights off germs. Most days you notice
none of it. But when one of those jobs starts to fail, the body nearly
always says so: a pain in a particular place, a change in breathing, a
face that droops on one side, a thirst that will not go away. The skill
this guide teaches is listening: knowing the systems well enough to
recognise a warning, knowing what is normal for you, and knowing how
fast to act on what you notice.

On a homestead, or anywhere far from a hospital, that skill matters more,
not less. The minutes between noticing and calling for help are the
minutes you control.

This guide takes its picture of the body from the National Cancer
Institute's SEER Training Modules, an anatomy course the National
Institutes of Health (NIH) publishes for people who keep cancer records.
Its warning signs come from the Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention (CDC), the National Cancer Institute (NCI), the Consumer
Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the Substance Abuse and Mental Health
Services Administration (SAMHSA) and the Health Resources and Services
Administration (HRSA). All of those are United States government works,
in the public domain. A few facts about normal readings and allergic
reactions come from MedlinePlus's medical encyclopedia, which is
copyrighted, so those facts are restated here in our own words and never
quoted. Where something is general practice or our own reading, the text
says so.

Several other guides go deeper into one part of this one. [Cold and
Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) and [Heat and How It
Moves](heat_and_how_it_moves.md) cover keeping your temperature;
[Bleeding and Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md) and [Treating
Burns](treating_burns.md) cover injuries; [Nutrition That Keeps You
Working](nutrition_that_keeps_you_working.md) covers food; and
[Handwashing and Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) is the cheapest
protection against infection there is. The same ideas applied to
livestock are in [How Animals Work](how_animals_work.md).

## What this guide is, and what it is not

This guide will help you notice that something is wrong, measure it, write
it down and get the right help at the right speed. It will not teach you
to diagnose an illness or to treat one, and it does not recommend any
medicine. Those belong to trained people.

Three numbers belong on the wall of every home in the United States,
before you need them:

- **911** for an emergency: someone who has collapsed, cannot breathe, has
  the signs of a heart attack or a stroke, or is getting worse fast.
- **1-800-222-1222**, the Poison Help line. HRSA, which funds it, says it
  connects you to your local poison center and that you can call it
  anytime, anywhere in the United States.
- **988**, the Suicide and Crisis Lifeline. SAMHSA describes it as "24/7
  judgment-free support for mental health, substance use, and more", by
  text, call or chat.

Outside the United States the numbers differ; find yours before you need
it. And take a first-aid and CPR class if you can. The CDC notes that
bystanders trained in CPR or in using a defibrillator may be able to help
someone whose heart has stopped until emergency medical personnel arrive.
A class is where you learn the hands-on part; no page can teach it.

## One body, many systems

The SEER course describes the body as built from four kinds of unit, each
more complex than the last:

- **Cells**, the simplest units of living matter that can keep themselves
  alive and reproduce.
- **Tissues**, many similar cells organised together.
- **Organs**, several kinds of tissue arranged to do one job. Its example
  is the stomach: muscle and connective tissue form the wall, epithelial
  and connective tissue the lining, and nervous tissue runs through both.
- **Systems**, groups of organs that together do complex work for the
  whole body.

It names ten major systems: skeletal, muscular, nervous, endocrine,
cardiovascular, lymphatic, respiratory, digestive, urinary and
reproductive.

The idea that ties them together is **homeostasis**: keeping the inside
of the body relatively constant while the world outside changes. The
course traces it to the French physiologist Claude Bernard, who noticed
that the body's cells stay healthy only when the temperature, pressure
and chemistry around them stay roughly the same. "Relatively" is the
important word. A healthy body is not fixed; it wanders a little and
pulls itself back. Illness is often a body that can no longer pull
back.

Two more points from the course are worth carrying around:

- **Life depends on water, oxygen, nutrients, heat and pressure** from the
  environment, as well as on the body's own processes. Every one of the
  emergencies below is one of those going wrong.
- **The body works least well at both ends of life**, in infancy and in
  old age. Babies and older people deserve the closest watching, and the
  lowest threshold for calling for help. (That conclusion is our reading
  of the course.)

## What each system does, and how it tells you it is failing

The descriptions of what each system does come from the SEER course.
The warning signs come from the CDC, the NCI and the other sources
named; they are collected here and explained in the sections that
follow.

### Heart and blood vessels

The cardiovascular system is a pump, the heart, and a closed circuit of
arteries, veins and capillaries. The work that keeps you alive happens in
the capillaries, which reach every tissue: nutrients pass out of the blood
to the cells there, and waste passes back in. Left to itself, the heart's
natural pacemaker, the sinoatrial node, fires 70 to 80 times a minute; the
brain speeds it up or slows it down as the body needs.

Blood carries oxygen from the lungs, food from digestion, hormones from
glands, disease-fighting cells to the tissues and waste to the kidneys.
The course's summary is plain: "Without blood, the human body would stop
working."

**How it fails:** a heart attack, when part of the heart muscle does not
get enough blood; cardiac arrest, when the heart stops; and high blood
pressure, which damages the heart, brain, kidneys and eyes for years
without saying a word.

### Lungs and breathing

The respiratory system works with the circulation to bring oxygen in and
take carbon dioxide out, and it also helps keep the blood's acidity (its
pH) right. The SEER course says that every 3 to 5 seconds nerve impulses
start the next breath: the diaphragm contracts, the chest gets bigger,
and air flows in because the pressure inside the lungs has dropped.

**How it fails:** shortness of breath, wheezing, or breathing that is
fast, noisy or a struggle. Shortness of breath is on the CDC's lists for
heart attack and for sepsis, and trouble breathing is the most dangerous
sign of a severe allergic reaction. And because every breath goes straight
into the blood, air that carries carbon monoxide poisons you without any
warning smell.

### Brain and nerves

The nervous system is the body's main control and communication system,
the seat of thought, learning and memory. The SEER course splits its work
into three: sensing (receptors inside and outside the body), integrating
(the brain making sense of it and deciding) and acting (signals to muscles
and glands).

**How it fails:** a stroke, when the blood supply to part of the brain is
blocked or a vessel bursts; confusion, which is a warning sign in sepsis,
heatstroke, dehydration and carbon monoxide poisoning; and the slow
changes of mood and thinking that are as real an illness as any other.

### Hormones

The endocrine system regulates the body with chemical messengers,
hormones, carried in the blood. The SEER course contrasts it with the
nerves: a nerve signal acts in seconds and in one place, a hormone over
minutes, hours or weeks and across the whole body. Hormones influence
growth, development and how the body uses its fuel.

**How it fails:** slowly, and often silently. Diabetes, in which the body
cannot manage its blood sugar, is the common example, and its signs are
below.

### Digestion

The digestive tract is one long tube from the mouth to the anus, with the
salivary glands, liver, gallbladder and pancreas pouring fluids into it.
Food is broken down mechanically (chewing, the churning of the stomach)
and chemically (enzymes and water), absorbed through the lining of the
small intestine into the blood and lymph, and what cannot be used is
eliminated.

**How it fails:** vomiting and diarrhoea, which can dehydrate you fast;
and slower changes such as blood in the stools, a change in bowel habits,
trouble swallowing or pain after eating that does not go away, which the
NCI lists among the symptoms to see a doctor about.

### Kidneys and urine

The urinary system keeps the volume and make-up of the body's fluids
within normal limits: how much water, which salts, and the blood's
acidity. The kidneys filter the blood and send the waste out as urine.
They also make a hormone that controls red blood cell production and an
enzyme that helps hold blood pressure. Other organs excrete some waste
too (the lungs breathe out carbon dioxide and water, the skin sweats),
but the SEER course is blunt that if the urinary system fails, the other
organs cannot take over and compensate adequately.

**How it fails:** dark urine and less of it are signs of dehydration;
peeing much more often than usual is on the CDC's list of diabetes
symptoms; and blood in the urine, pain when urinating or trouble
urinating are on the NCI's list. The urinary tract is also one of the
places the CDC says the infections that lead to sepsis most often start.

### Skin

The SEER course counts the skin among the body's excretory organs, getting
rid of waste through the sweat glands, and sweat is also how you shed
heat. The skin is the outer wall against germs, which is why the guides
on wounds and burns spend so long on keeping it clean.

**How it fails:** the skin is where several emergencies first show.
Clammy or sweaty skin is on the CDC's sepsis list; hot, red, dry or damp
skin goes with heatstroke; cold, pale and clammy skin with heat
exhaustion; hives and swelling with a severe allergic reaction. And the
NCI lists a sore that does not heal, a new mole or a change in an
existing one, and yellowing of the skin and the whites of the eyes
(jaundice) among the changes to show a doctor.

### The lymphatic system and defence

The lymphatic system has three jobs in the SEER course: it collects the
fluid that leaks out of the capillaries into the tissues and returns it
to the blood; it absorbs fats from the gut; and, best known, it defends
the body, with lymph nodes that filter out germs and lymphocytes that
destroy them. When fluid is not returned, it builds up in the tissues as
swelling, which the course calls edema.

**How it fails:** an infection the body cannot contain can turn into
sepsis, the body's extreme response to an infection, which the CDC calls
a life-threatening medical emergency. Swelling or lumps in the neck,
armpit, belly or groin are on the NCI's list of changes to see a doctor
about.

### Muscles and bones

The SEER course says the skeleton, with its cartilage, ligaments and
tendons, is about 20 percent of body weight. Bones hold the body up,
protect the brain, spinal cord, heart and lungs, store calcium (and
release it into the blood when the blood runs short), and in the red
marrow make the blood cells. Muscles do almost all the moving, hold your
posture, keep joints stable, and make heat: nearly 85 percent of the heat
the body produces comes from muscle contraction, which is why shivering
warms you.

**How it fails:** injuries are covered in their own guides. Weakness or
numbness, especially on one side of the body, is not a muscle problem; it
is a warning sign of stroke.

### Reproduction

The reproductive system makes the next generation. Its warning signs are
mostly beyond this guide; the NCI lists breast changes (a lump or firm
feeling in the breast or under the arm, nipple changes or discharge, and
skin that is itchy, red, scaly, dimpled or puckered) among the changes to
see a doctor about.

## Know your own normal

The normal ranges printed in books are wide, because people differ. Your
own normal is much narrower, and the change from it is the useful signal.
MedlinePlus makes this point about the pulse: a change from your usual
heart rate can mean a health problem, and a fast pulse can be a sign of
infection or dehydration. The only way to know your own normal is to
measure it on ordinary days, while you are well, and write it down.

### Your pulse

The pulse is your heartbeats per minute, felt where an artery runs close
to the skin. MedlinePlus describes the two easiest places:

- **At the wrist.** Put the tips of your index and middle fingers on the
  underside of the other wrist, just below the base of the thumb, and
  press with flat fingers until you feel it. Use your fingers, not your
  thumb (general practice: the thumb has a pulse of its own that can
  confuse the count).
- **At the neck.** Put the same two fingers just to the side of the
  Adam's apple, in the soft hollow, and press gently. **Sit or lie down
  first, and never press on both sides of the neck at once.** MedlinePlus
  warns that the neck arteries in some people are sensitive to pressure,
  so pressing can cause fainting or slow the heart, and pressing both
  sides at once can slow the blood flow to the head and cause fainting.

Count the beats for a full minute, or for 30 seconds and double it; a
15-second count times four is usually accurate too. For your resting
rate, rest for at least 10 minutes first.

MedlinePlus gives a resting rate of 60 to 100 beats a minute for adults
and children from 10 years old, and 40 to 60 for well-trained athletes.
Younger children run faster, and a newborn's normal range reaches up to
190. A resting rate that is high all the time, or below the normal range,
is worth raising with a health care provider, and so is an irregular
pulse.

### Your breathing

MedlinePlus puts a resting adult's breathing at 12 to 18 breaths a
minute, which fits the SEER course's figure of a breath every 3 to 5
seconds. Count it for a full minute. People change how they breathe when
they know they are being watched, so count someone else's breathing while
you seem to be doing something else, such as holding their wrist for the
pulse (general practice).

### Your temperature

MedlinePlus gives a normal temperature for a resting adult as about 97.7
to 99.1 F (36.5 to 37.3 C), averaging 98.6 F (37 C). It varies through the
day and between people. Two thresholds from the CDC matter most: below
95 F (35 C) means hypothermia and a need for medical attention at once,
and 103 F (39.4 C) or higher is a sign of heatstroke. Both are in the
section below.

### Your blood pressure

The CDC defines normal blood pressure as less than 120/80 mm Hg (the first
number is the pressure while the heart beats, the second while it rests
between beats), and high blood pressure as "consistently at or above
130/80 mm Hg". The word "consistently" matters, because the CDC also says
your blood pressure changes through the day with what you are doing. One
high reading is not a diagnosis; a pattern is.

The most important fact about it, again from the CDC: high blood pressure
usually has no warning signs or symptoms, many people do not know they
have it, and measuring it is the only way to know. A home cuff, or a
pharmacy machine, is one of the few instruments that can find a silent
problem years before it does damage (the home cuff is general practice;
ask a health care provider how to use and check yours).

### Your weight, your urine and your energy

- **Weight.** Losing weight without trying is on the CDC's list of
  diabetes symptoms, and weight loss or gain for no known reason is on
  the NCI's list. You can only notice it if you weigh yourself now and
  then.
- **Urine.** MedlinePlus lists dark-coloured urine and urinating less
  than usual among the signs of dehydration in adults. Pale urine in
  ordinary amounts is the everyday check that you are drinking enough
  (general practice).
- **Energy.** Tiredness that is severe and lasts is on the NCI's list;
  unusual or unexplained tiredness is on the CDC's heart attack list,
  especially for women; and fatigue is on its diabetes list. Ordinary
  tiredness after work is normal. Tiredness that is new, heavy and has no
  reason is a reading worth writing down.

### Worked example: a body log

Write your readings into a notebook the same way [Keeping
Records](keeping_records.md) suggests for a machine: the date, the time,
what you measured, and anything unusual about the day. A month of calm
mornings might look like this (an invented example):

| Date | Time | Resting pulse | Breaths a minute | Temperature | Note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 3 May | 7:10 | 62 | 14 | 97.9 F | normal day |
| 10 May | 7:05 | 64 | 14 | 98.1 F | |
| 17 May | 7:15 | 61 | 13 | 97.8 F | |
| 24 May | 7:20 | 88 | 19 | 99.4 F | sore throat, tired |

Every one of the 24 May readings is inside, or barely outside, the
printed normal range. For this person, though, a resting pulse 25 beats
higher than usual, with a temperature up and breathing faster, is a clear
change, and that is the point of the log: it turns "I feel a bit off"
into something you can describe to a nurse on the telephone. That reading
of the example is ours; the ranges are MedlinePlus's.

## Signs that mean act now

Some failures give you weeks and some give you minutes. The rest of this
guide is sorted by how fast you must move.

### Minutes: call 911

**Someone has collapsed and is not breathing normally.** The CDC says a
person may be in cardiac arrest if they collapse suddenly and lose
consciousness, are not breathing or are only gasping, do not respond to
shouting or shaking, and have no pulse. Its instruction: call 911 right
away, look for an automated external defibrillator (AED), and give CPR
until medical professionals arrive. Do not delay CPR to hunt for a pulse:
someone who has collapsed and is not breathing normally needs it now, and
the 911 call-taker can talk you through it (general practice). The CDC
puts out-of-hospital cardiac arrests in the United States at more than
356,000 a year, with about 60 to 80 percent of those people dying before
they reach the hospital. That
is why the class matters: a trained person on the spot is often the only
help in time.

**Heart attack.** The CDC's major symptoms:

- "Chest pain or discomfort", usually in the centre or left side of the
  chest, lasting more than a few minutes or going away and coming back.
  It can feel like pressure, squeezing, fullness or pain.
- Feeling weak, light-headed or faint, perhaps with a cold sweat.
- Pain or discomfort in the jaw, neck or back.
- Pain or discomfort in one or both arms or shoulders.
- Shortness of breath, with the chest discomfort or before it.

Unusual or unexplained tiredness and nausea or vomiting can be symptoms
too, and the CDC says women are more likely to have these. Its rule:
"Call 9-1-1 if you notice symptoms of a heart attack", because the sooner
treatment starts, the less heart muscle is lost. Do not wait to see
whether it passes.

**Stroke.** The CDC's signs, every one of them sudden:

- trouble walking, dizziness, loss of balance or coordination;
- trouble seeing;
- numbness or weakness in the face, arm or leg, especially on one side;
- confusion, trouble speaking or understanding speech;
- a severe headache with no known cause.

The CDC's quick test is **B.E. F.A.S.T.**: **B**alance (ask if they feel
off-balance or dizzy), **E**yes (ask if they have trouble seeing), **F**ace
(ask them to smile; does one side droop?), **A**rms (ask them to raise both
arms; does one drift down?), **S**peech (ask them to repeat a simple
phrase; is it slurred or strange?), **T**ime (if you see any of these,
call 911 right away). Then do two more things the CDC asks:

- **Note the time the symptoms started.** The treatments that work best
  are available only if the stroke is recognised and diagnosed within 3
  hours of the first symptoms, and the time is how doctors decide.
- **Do not drive.** "Do not drive to the hospital or let someone else
  drive you." Call 911 for an ambulance, so treatment starts on the way.

If the symptoms go away after a few minutes, that may have been a
transient ischemic attack, a "mini-stroke". The CDC says it is a sign of
a serious condition that will not go away without medical help, and that
many people ignore one because it clears up. Tell a health care team
about it right away.

**A severe allergic reaction (anaphylaxis).** MedlinePlus describes it as
a whole-body allergic reaction that usually comes on within seconds or
minutes of the trigger, commonly a medicine, a food or an insect sting.
Signs can include trouble breathing, wheezing, coughing or a high-pitched
sound when breathing in; swelling of the face, eyes or tongue; hives,
itching or red skin; dizziness; trouble swallowing; slurred speech; and
loss of consciousness. A very hoarse or whispered voice can mean the
throat is swelling. What MedlinePlus says to do, restated: call 911 at
once; if the person carries emergency allergy medicine such as an
epinephrine injector, help them use it right away; and if they are having
trouble breathing, give them nothing by mouth and do not put a pillow
under their head, which can block the airway. A sting left in the skin
should be scraped off with something firm such as a fingernail or a card,
not pinched with tweezers, which squeeze in more venom.

**Heatstroke.** The CDC's heat sheet lists a body temperature of 103 F or
higher; hot, red, dry or damp skin; a fast, strong pulse; headache,
dizziness, nausea and confusion; and passing out. Its steps: call 911
right away, because "heat stroke is a medical emergency"; move the person
somewhere cooler; lower their temperature with cool cloths or a cool
bath; and **do not give them anything to drink.**

**Hypothermia.** The CDC calls hypothermia a medical emergency: if a
person's temperature is below 95 F (35 C), get medical attention
immediately. It warns that a body that is too cold affects the brain, so
the person may not know it is happening, and that it can happen at cool
temperatures above 40 F if someone is wet from rain, sweat or cold water.
[Cold and Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) covers how to recognise it
and how to rewarm someone without hurting them.

**An infection that is getting worse: sepsis.** The CDC calls sepsis the
body's extreme response to an infection and "a life-threatening medical
emergency". It says almost any infection can lead to it, most often
infections that start in the gut, the lungs, the skin or the urinary
tract, and that without fast treatment it can quickly lead to tissue
damage, organ failure and death. Its signs:

- clammy or sweaty skin;
- confusion or disorientation;
- extreme pain or discomfort;
- fever, shivering or feeling very cold;
- a high heart rate or a weak pulse;
- shortness of breath.

The CDC says a person with sepsis "might have one or more" of these signs,
so do not wait for several. Its patient sheet says that if you or someone
you care for has an infection that is not getting better or is getting
worse, act right away: ask a doctor "Could I have sepsis?" and whether you
should go to the emergency room, and if the signs are severe, call 911
immediately.

**Carbon monoxide.** The CDC calls carbon monoxide an odorless, colorless
gas that "kills without warning", made whenever fuel burns: in furnaces,
generators, stoves, grills, fireplaces, engines and charcoal. Its most
common symptoms are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach,
vomiting, chest pain and confusion, often described as "flu-like", and
the CDC warns that people who are asleep, drunk or under the influence of
other substances can die from it before they have any symptoms. The CPSC
adds that the early symptoms are like the flu without the fever, and that
a high level can make people confused and unable to move before any
milder symptom. Two clues are worth knowing (our reading of those
descriptions): several people in the house feeling ill at once, and
feeling better away from home.

The CPSC's steps when a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, or when you think
you have the symptoms: **get outside to fresh air immediately**; call 911
or the fire department, from outside or from a neighbour's home; count
heads so you know everyone is out; and do not go back in until the
emergency responders say you may. Do not try to find the source yourself.
Then tell a doctor you suspect carbon monoxide. Prevention, alarms and
venting are in [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md).

**Poisoning.** For anything swallowed, breathed in, splashed in the eyes
or on the skin, the EPA's emergency advice for pesticides holds as a
household rule: call 911 if the person is unconscious, having trouble
breathing or having convulsions; otherwise check the product label for
first aid and call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222, with the container in
your hand. (Using the pesticide advice for every household product is our
extension of it.)

### Hours: get medical care today

**Heat exhaustion.** The CDC sheet lists heavy sweating; cold, pale,
clammy skin; a fast, weak pulse; nausea or vomiting; muscle cramps;
tiredness or weakness; dizziness; headache; and fainting. Move to a cool
place, loosen clothes, put cool wet cloths on the body or take a cool
bath, and sip water. Get medical help right away if the person is
throwing up, the symptoms get worse, or they last longer than 1 hour.
Left alone, heat exhaustion can become heatstroke.

**Dehydration.** MedlinePlus describes the adult signs as feeling very
thirsty, a dry mouth, urinating and sweating less than usual, dark urine,
dry skin, tiredness and dizziness. In babies and young children the signs
include a dry mouth and tongue, crying without tears, no wet nappies for 3
hours or more, a high fever, unusual sleepiness, irritability and sunken
eyes. It says to get medical help right away if there is also confusion,
fainting, no urination, a rapid heartbeat, rapid breathing or shock.
Diarrhoea, vomiting, fever, heavy sweating and hot work are the usual
causes, and older adults, who may lose some of their sense of thirst, and
infants and young children, are the most likely to be caught by it.

### Weeks: make an appointment

Some failures are slow, and the danger is getting used to them.

**Changes that do not go away.** The NCI's advice is to see a doctor about
symptoms that last a couple of weeks or do not get better after a few
weeks. It is careful to add that these symptoms are most often caused by
something other than cancer, such as illness, injury or a harmless
growth, and that cancer often does not cause pain, so you should not wait
for pain. Among the changes it lists:

- a lump or swelling anywhere, such as the neck, armpit, belly, groin or
  breast;
- a sore that does not heal, a new mole, or a change in an existing one;
- a cough or hoarseness that does not go away;
- blood in the stools or the urine, or bleeding or bruising for no known
  reason;
- a change in bowel habits, trouble swallowing, or heartburn or
  indigestion that does not go away;
- fatigue that is severe and lasts;
- fever or night sweats for no known reason;
- weight gain or loss for no known reason.

**Diabetes.** The CDC lists frequent urination, increased thirst and
hunger, losing weight without trying, tiredness, feeling irritable,
blurry vision, and frequent urinary tract or yeast infections, and, more
often in type 2 diabetes, cuts and sores that heal very slowly, dark
patches of skin around the neck, armpits or groin, and numbness or
tingling in the hands or feet. It warns that type 2 symptoms often take
several years to develop and that some people notice none at all, so it
says to know the risk factors (a test is how you find out; that part is
general practice).

**Type 1 can be faster.** The CDC says that as type 1 diabetes progresses,
its symptoms can appear suddenly, in a few weeks or months, and can be
severe, and it adds nausea, vomiting, stomach pains and diabetic
ketoacidosis (DKA). In children, unexplained bed-wetting or more accidents
can be a sign. With nausea, vomiting or stomach pains in someone who has
those other signs, get medical care the same day, not an appointment in a
few weeks (general practice).

**Blood pressure.** No symptoms, as above, so measure it.

### The mind is part of the body

The SEER course places all mental activity in the nervous system. A mind
that is failing is a body system failing, and it deserves the same
seriousness: a doctor for a change in mood or thinking that lasts, and
988 at once for a crisis. SAMHSA says the line offers free and
confidential support around the clock to people in suicidal crisis or
emotional distress, by call, text or chat, including for Deaf and hard of
hearing callers and in Spanish. If someone is in immediate danger, call
911.

## Where your own work stops

The work in this guide is yours: knowing the systems, knowing your own
normal, noticing a change, measuring it, writing it down and choosing how
fast to get help. Everything after that is a professional's:

- **Diagnosis.** The same symptom has many causes, and the NCI's
  reminder that most of its warning signs are not cancer cuts both ways:
  neither your worry nor your hope is a diagnosis. A health care
  provider examines, tests and decides.
- **Treatment and medicine.** This guide recommends none. Even for
  ordinary illnesses, ask a pharmacist or a provider before mixing
  medicines (general practice).
- **Transport in an emergency.** For a stroke, the CDC says not to drive
  or be driven; call an ambulance. The same reasoning applies to a
  heart attack: the crew starts treatment on the way, and a car cannot
  (our reading).
- **Babies, older people and anyone with a long-term illness.** Their
  bodies have the least room to cope, so call earlier for them, not
  later (our reading of the SEER course and of the at-risk groups the
  CDC and MedlinePlus name).

## Traps

- **Telling yourself it is probably nothing.** For the sudden signs,
  minutes count and a false alarm costs little. A stroke that clears in
  minutes still needs a doctor the same day.
- **Waiting for pain.** The NCI says cancer often does not cause pain, and
  high blood pressure causes nothing at all you can feel.
- **Driving yourself in.** The CDC says not to drive to the hospital with
  a stroke or let someone else drive you.
- **Reading the range instead of your normal.** A resting pulse of 88 is
  "normal" on paper. If yours is usually 62, it is a change.
- **Trusting one reading.** Blood pressure changes through the day. Look
  for a pattern before you worry, and see a provider when the pattern is
  there.
- **Ignoring a household of "flu".** Several people ill at once in a house
  with something burning fuel is a carbon monoxide question first.

## How the game models it

The game gives your character a simple body that runs on real-world
timescales, and leaves out nearly everything that can go wrong inside one.

- **Health and the vitals.** You have Health out of 100, and on the
  Inventory page six more readings: Satiation (how fed you are), Hydration,
  Energy, Oxygen, Waste and Body temp. Settings > Gameplay > "Survival bars
  on the HUD" decides how many show under the health bar. The default,
  "When low", shows food, water and energy once they fall below half,
  Waste once it is more than half full, air whenever you are out of
  breathable air or it is not full, and body temperature whenever it is
  outside the safe range; "Always" shows them all and "Off (health only)"
  shows only health.
- **First Aid.** The First Aid ability puts 35 health back at once for 15
  energy and can be used again 10 seconds later, cast with its button on
  the Profile page or a number key on the HUD (`data/abilities.csv`,
  `src/systems/abilities.rs`); real first aid restores nothing instantly,
  as [First Aid Until Help Arrives](first_aid_until_help_arrives.md)
  explains.
- **Thirst and hunger run on real time.** With the default Vitals drain
  of 1.0 (Settings > Gameplay), Hydration empties in about two days and
  Satiation in about a week, and below a quarter you become Thirsty or
  Hungry, which slows you. Only when one is completely empty does your
  health start to drain: over about a day for thirst and about two weeks
  for hunger, so thirst is by far the faster killer, as it is in life.
  Sweating in the heat costs extra water. The numbers are in
  `src/systems/food.rs`, and the slowdowns in `data/status_effects.csv`.
- **Tiredness and sleep.** Energy runs down through the waking day, and
  after about sixteen waking hours it falls below a quarter and you are
  Fatigued and slower. A night in a bed you have built restores it; the
  game sleeps you 8 hours.
- **Air.** Out of breathable air, your Oxygen falls in about 40 seconds;
  below half you have Hypoxia, which slows you hard, and at zero
  Suffocation drains your health fast.
- **Body heat.** Your core temperature is a real heat balance of your
  body, your clothes and the weather, with Hypothermia below 35 C and Heat
  Exhaustion and Heatstroke above 39 and 40 C. The default Body heat
  setting is Forgiving, which swings your temperature half as far; Realistic
  is the real balance. [Heat and How It Moves](heat_and_how_it_moves.md)
  has the detail.
- **Food poisoning.** Spoiled food always gives you food poisoning, and
  several raw foods carry a chance of it (`data/food_system.ron`); it
  drains your health for a while.
- **Acceleration.** Aboard a ship under a hard burn, you feel extra
  weight. Above 1.5 g you get High Gravity, which halves your speed, and
  your health drains, faster the harder the burn: at 4 g, about 50 seconds
  takes you from full health to none (`data/ship/flight.ron`).

**What changes with the settings.** The default Dev play mode (Settings >
Gameplay > Play mode) makes materials free, but it does not pause your
body: hunger, thirst, tiredness, air and body heat run the same in every
play mode. The Vitals drain slider changes the pace of hunger, thirst and
tiredness (0 pauses them), and the Dev page's fly mode, a developer tool,
suspends the danger of airless space and the weather while it is on.
Your health and vitals are not saved at all, so every launch starts you
at the starting levels, fed, watered and rested, whatever the save; and
while "Start every session from the default home" is on, which it is by
default during development, only your character's name, look and clothes
carry over, so the food and water in your pack go too.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there are
no heart attacks, strokes, allergies, infections, sepsis, diabetes or
carbon monoxide, and nothing about you has a pulse, a breathing rate or a
blood pressure to measure. The game's data includes a catalogue of
injuries and illnesses (`data/medical.ron`) and disease effects such as
flu and infection in `data/status_effects.csv`, but nothing in the game
gives them to you yet. In the game, a body fails in a handful of clear
ways (hunger, thirst, air, heat and cold, food poisoning and hard
acceleration), and each one warns you with a bar or a named condition. A
real one fails in hundreds, and tells you only if you know how to listen.

## You own this when

- You have 911, Poison Help (1-800-222-1222) and 988 where everyone in the
  house can find them, and you know your own country's numbers if you are
  elsewhere.
- You can name the body's main systems and say what each one does.
- You can take your own pulse at the wrist, and at the neck sitting down
  and one side only, and count breaths without the person noticing.
- You know your own resting pulse, breathing rate and temperature, and
  you have written them down on ordinary days.
- You know your blood pressure, and you check it now and then because
  high blood pressure has no symptoms.
- You can run B.E. F.A.S.T., you note the time, and you call an ambulance
  instead of driving.
- You know the CDC's heart attack signs, including the ones women are more
  likely to have, and you call 911 rather than waiting.
- You know that someone who collapses and is not breathing normally needs
  911, an AED and CPR, and you have taken, or plan to take, a class.
- You recognise heatstroke, heat exhaustion, dehydration and sepsis, and
  you know which needs 911 and which needs care today.
- When a carbon monoxide alarm sounds, everyone goes outside first and
  the call is made from there.
- You see a doctor about any change that has lasted a couple of weeks,
  without waiting for pain.
