# Wells and Groundwater

Leave aside the water frozen in ice caps and glaciers, and nearly all
of the world's fresh water is not in rivers or lakes. It is in the
ground, filling the spaces between grains of sand and gravel and the
cracks in rock, and a well is how you reach it. The US Geological
Survey (USGS) puts over 68 percent of Earth's fresh water in ice and
glaciers, and then: "Another 30 percent of freshwater is in the
ground." Rivers and lakes hold only about 1/150th of one percent of all
the water on Earth. For about half the people in the United States, and
nearly everyone in the countryside, the ground is where drinking water
comes from: the USGS puts groundwater as the source of drinking water
for about half the total population and nearly all of the rural
population.

This guide is about that water: where it sits, why a well works or
fails, who is allowed to take it, what keeps a well clean, and the
handful of ways a well can hurt somebody. It does not teach you to
drill one. In Washington, this guide's worked example, building a well
is licensed work, and the EPA's advice everywhere is to hire a
certified well driller; the guide says where the line is.

It sits beside three other water guides. [Where Water Comes
From](where_water_comes_from.md) compares a private well with every
other source and explains dug, driven and drilled wells. [Testing
Water](testing_water.md) is the annual test and how to read it.
[Moving Water Without Power](moving_water_without_power.md) covers hand
pumps for when the power is out. This guide does not repeat them.

Its sources are the USGS, the Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention (CDC), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the
National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), the
Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) and the Consumer
Product Safety Commission (CPSC), all US federal and public domain,
and, for the worked example, Washington State law, restated in our own
words. Where something is general practice rather than a published
rule, the text says so.

## First, where a well can hurt you

A well is a narrow hole full of water, wired to the house. These are the
ways that can hurt people, worst first.

### Never go down a well

A dug well, a well pit, a cistern or a spring box fits NIOSH's
description of a confined space (our reading; its own examples include
pits and storage tanks): a space with limited openings for getting in
and out, poor natural ventilation, and not designed for anyone to stay
in. In such a space the air can be very different from the air
outside. NIOSH lists the ways the oxygen gets used up or pushed out:
rusting, bacterial action, and displacement by another gas such as
carbon dioxide. Its
alert on confined space deaths says that deaths in confined spaces often
occur because the atmosphere is oxygen deficient or toxic, and adds the
number that matters most: "More than 60% of confined space fatalities
occur among would-be rescuers".

OSHA's rule for excavations at work says the same thing in regulation
form: where air with less than 19.5 percent oxygen, or another
hazardous atmosphere, could reasonably be expected, the air in an
excavation deeper than 4 feet must be tested before workers go in (29
CFR 1926.651(g)). OSHA's rules bind employers and protect their
workers, so they are not a household's legal duty; the air in a hole is
the same whoever goes into it (our reading).

So:

- **Do not climb into a well, a well pit or a cistern,** even a dry one,
  even for a minute, even to fetch something you dropped. NIOSH's guide
  to confined spaces warns never to trust your senses here: you cannot
  see or smell many toxic gases, nor tell how much oxygen there is.
- **If somebody has collapsed down there, do not go after them.** Call
  911, keep everyone else out, and tell the dispatcher it is a well. The
  same guide says an unplanned rescue, someone instinctively rushing in
  to help, can easily end in two deaths instead of one; NIOSH's 60
  percent is those rescuers.
- Work that genuinely needs someone inside (cleaning or deepening a dug
  well) is for a contractor with gas-testing and rescue equipment. That
  is our reading of the NIOSH and OSHA material for a household, which
  has neither.

### Keep every well covered, and close the ones you are not using

The CDC's advice on retiring an old well puts the danger plainly:
"People or animals can also get hurt if they fall into a forgotten,
crumbling well." An old dug well under rotten boards, in long grass, is
exactly that well. Washington's well rules require any well that is not
in use, or is temporarily out of service, to be securely capped so
nothing can get in, and say that capping on its own is not the same as
closing a well for good (WAC 173-160-301). A well that is unusable,
abandoned or a safety hazard has to be decommissioned: filled and sealed
by the method the rules set out (WAC 173-160-381).

If you find an old well on a property: keep people and animals away
from it, cover it with something that cannot slide off or rot through
and that will carry the weight of anyone who steps on it, mark it, and
call your state's well program (general practice; the rules above are
Washington's).

### The pump is electric, and so is the water around it

A household well pump runs on mains electricity, and the pump, its
pressure switch and its controls sit in or next to water. Treat it like
any other wiring: switch it off at the breaker, lock or label the
breaker, and prove it dead with a tester before you touch anything. The
full procedure is in [Working Out Why Something
Broke](working_out_why_something_broke.md). The pressure tank holds
water under pressure even with the power off; let the pressure out at a
tap before you open the plumbing (general practice, and the reason is in
[Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md)).

After a flood, the EPA's well guidance starts with a warning in capital
letters: "DO NOT TURN ON THE PUMP". In its words, "There is danger of
electrical shock and damage to your well or pump if they have been
flooded." Once the water has gone down and the pump and wiring have
dried, the EPA says not to switch the equipment on until the wiring has
been checked by a qualified electrician, well contractor or pump
contractor, and to get a well or pump contractor's help turning the
pump back on. A dry pump is not yet a safe pump: silt and sand have to
be cleaned out of it, and the EPA warns that "If pumps are not cleaned
and properly lubricated they can burn out." Its current advice puts the
contractor first: "Get assistance from a well or pump contractor to
clean and disinfect your well before turning on the pump." And it warns
that "Rubber boots and gloves are not adequate protection from electric
shock." The rubber gloves in the steps below are for the bleach, not
for the electricity.

### A generator to run the pump goes in through a transfer switch

An electric well pump stops when the power goes out, and a generator is
the obvious way to run it. The CPSC's warning about doing that the easy
way:
never power the house wiring by plugging a generator into a wall outlet,
a practice called backfeeding. "This is extremely dangerous and presents
an electrocution risk to utility workers and neighbors served by the
same utility transformer." A generator that is to run a well pump is
connected through a transfer switch that an electrician installs
(general practice). The CPSC's rules for running a generator at all
(outdoors only, far from windows and doors) are in [Keeping Things
Working](keeping_things_working.md).

### Bleach is a strong chemical

Shock chlorinating a well (below) means pouring household bleach into
it. The EPA's list of what you need for the job includes rubber gloves
and eye protection. The CDC's warning about bleach applies here as
everywhere: "Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners. This will
produce a poisonous gas." And nobody drinks, cooks with or washes in the
water while the chlorine is in the pipes.

### Digging by hand is digging a trench straight down

A hand-dug well is a deep, narrow excavation, and soil walls collapse.
OSHA's rule for work in excavations requires every worker in an
excavation to be protected from cave-ins, with two exceptions: an
excavation cut entirely in stable rock, and one less than 5 feet deep
where a competent person has examined the ground and found no sign of a
possible cave-in (29 CFR 1926.652(a)(1)). The rule is written for
employers, but soil falls the same way on anyone (our reading). A
household digging a well has no shoring, no gas tester and no rescue
plan, which is the main reason this guide does not teach digging one
(our reading).

## What groundwater is

Dig a hole at the beach and it fills with water from the sand around
it. The USGS uses that hole to explain all of groundwater. Below a
certain depth every space between the grains is full of water: that is
the saturated zone, and its top surface is the water table. Above it
the ground is damp but not full. A layer of rock or sediment that holds
water and lets it move easily enough to supply a well or a spring is an
aquifer.

Three things about aquifers explain almost everything a well owner
runs into.

**Water in the ground moves, slowly.** The USGS: "In some permeable
materials groundwater may move several meters in a day; in other places,
it moves only a few centimeters in a century." It moves toward lower
ground and comes out in springs, seeps into streams, or is pumped out of
wells. Rain and snowmelt soaking in from above (recharge) refill it, and
the USGS points out that the rate of recharge is not the same for every
aquifer.

**Some aquifers are confined.** Where an aquifer lies between layers of
clay or shale that water passes through poorly, the water can be under
pressure, and a well drilled into it may rise above the aquifer or even
flow out at the top without a pump. The USGS calls this artesian
pressure. A well into an unconfined aquifer, one whose top is the water
table itself, is open to whatever soaks down from the surface; [Where
Water Comes From](where_water_comes_from.md) quotes the USGS on why
that makes it easier to contaminate.

**Pumping pulls the water table down around the well.** When a well
pumps, the water level around it drops in a shape the USGS calls a cone
of depression, and water flows toward the well from the sides. If water
is taken out faster than it comes back, the water table falls; how far
and for how long depends on the aquifer, from a short dip to a decline
that lasts decades. The consequence the USGS spells out for well
owners: "pumping your well too much can even cause your neighbor's well
to run dry if you both are pumping from the same aquifer."

### What happens when an area pumps too much

The USGS's page on groundwater decline lists what long-term overpumping
does: wells drying up, less water in streams and lakes, worse water
quality, higher pumping costs, and land sinking. Two of those matter to
almost every household:

- **Streams.** Some, and often a great deal, of the water in a river
  arrives as groundwater seeping into the streambed, and pumping can
  intercept it before it gets there. That is why, in many places, a new
  well and a stream's summer flow are treated as the same water (our
  reading).
- **Salt water.** Much of the very deep groundwater, and the water under
  the oceans, is salty, and the USGS warns that pumping "can cause
  saltwater to migrate inland and upward, resulting in saltwater
  contamination of the water supply." The early warning is a rising
  chloride reading, which [Testing Water](testing_water.md) covers.

## Who owns the water under your land

Owning land does not always mean owning the right to pump unlimited
water from under it. Every state sets its own rules, and [Where Water
Comes From](where_water_comes_from.md) explains the two broad traditions
and why the answer differs between them. What follows is Washington's
version, as the worked example, restated from the statutes (read
2026-10-04).

- **A household well usually needs no permit.** Washington requires a
  permit before groundwater is withdrawn, except for small uses: stock
  watering, a lawn or noncommercial garden of half an acre or less, and
  domestic use of up to 5,000 gallons a day (RCW 90.44.050). It is still
  a water right: the same statute says that such a withdrawal, as far as
  it is regularly put to use, carries a right equal to one granted by a
  permit. Washington's groundwater law extends its surface water law to
  groundwater (RCW 90.44.020), and that law ranks rights by age, the
  earlier before the later (RCW 90.03.010). So an exempt well ranks by
  its date like any other right, behind older ones (our reading of the
  three together).
- **Some watersheds have tighter limits.** In eight named watersheds,
  Kitsap among them, a new permit-exempt domestic well pays a $500 fee,
  may take an annual average of no more than 950 gallons a day per
  connection, and can be cut back to 350 gallons a day for indoor use
  only under a drought emergency order (RCW 90.94.030). The statute says
  these interim rules apply until rules are adopted that say otherwise,
  so check the current rule before relying on the number.
- **Drilling without a needed permit is itself unlawful** (RCW
  18.104.030).

Elsewhere, ask your state's water resources agency the same three
questions: do I need a permit, how much may I take, and can it be cut
back in a drought.

## Who may build a well

In Washington, drilling or building a well is licensed work. A licensed
well contractor does it, with one exception: you may build a well
yourself on land you own or lease, for farm or single-family use only,
but you must still meet every construction standard, pay the fees and
give notice, and you may do this no more than once every two years (RCW
18.104.180). The property owner, or someone acting for them, must tell
the Department of Ecology at least 72 hours before a well is built,
rebuilt or decommissioned, and pay the fees (RCW 18.104.048), and
whoever builds or decommissions the well must file a report on it
within thirty days of finishing (WAC 173-160-141). That report records
how the well was built and, for a well that needs no water right
permit, the result of a pump test run at a steady rate for at least an
hour (WAC 173-160-321). If
you own a well, find its report: it is the closest thing your well has
to a manual (general practice).

The EPA's general advice, written for the whole country, is shorter:
hire a certified well driller for any new well, any change to a well,
and closing one.

## What makes a well safe

A well stays clean when surface water cannot run down the outside of
it, nothing can fall or crawl in the top, and it is far enough from the
things that pollute groundwater. Each of those is written into
Washington's construction rules (WAC 173-160, read 2026-10-04), which
make a useful picture of a good well even if you live elsewhere.

- **A seal down the outside.** Every new well has a surface seal of
  cement or bentonite clay filling the gap between the hole and the
  casing, at least 18 feet deep in most wells, so rain cannot run down
  the outside of the pipe into the aquifer (WAC 173-160-231). A dug well
  is sealed to at least 18 feet too, or to within 3 feet of the bottom if
  it is shallower than 21 feet, and carries a cap that stays on except
  during maintenance (WAC 173-160-261).
- **A top that stands up and is capped.** The casing ends at least 6
  inches above the ground, at least 2 feet above the 100-year flood level
  where flooding is possible, and a well in a pit is not allowed. A
  drilled well is closed with a cap made for the purpose and fixed to the
  casing (WAC 173-160-291).
- **Distance from pollution.** For a household well, Washington sets
  minimum distances: 5 feet from a building (and never inside a garage,
  barn, shed or house), 50 feet from a septic tank or a sewer line, 100
  feet from the edge of a drainfield and from other sources of
  contamination, and 1,000 feet from a landfill. The site must not be in
  a floodway or where water ponds, and must be protected from a 100-year
  flood (WAC 173-160-171).

The CDC's national list of distances is shorter than Washington's in
one place: it says at least 50 feet from septic tanks and from septic
leach fields, 50 feet from livestock yards, 100 feet from petroleum
tanks, from liquid-tight manure storage and from where fertilizer is
stored or handled, and 250 feet from manure stacks. Where the two
disagree, the rule where you live is the one that counts, and in
Washington the drainfield distance is 100 feet (our reading of the two
side by side).

## Looking after a well

None of this is technical. It is mostly looking.

- **Once a year, have the water tested and the well checked.** The CDC:
  "Get a well checkup every year, and test your water and check your well
  if you ever suspect a problem." The EPA's annual list is total coliform
  bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids and pH, with an immediate
  test after flooding or land disturbance nearby, after any repair to the
  well system, and when the water changes in taste, colour or smell.
  [Testing Water](testing_water.md) has the detail and what to do with
  the results.
- **Look at the top of the well.** The EPA lists what to look for:
  cracked, corroded or damaged casing; a broken or missing cap; and
  surface seals that have settled or cracked.
- **Keep the ground around it right.** The EPA: "Slope the area around
  the well to drain surface runoff away from the well." Keep pesticides,
  fertilizers, fuels and degreasers away from it, and never mix or use
  them near it.
- **Never use an old well as a drain.** The EPA: "Do not dispose of
  wastes in dry wells or in abandoned wells." A well is a direct line into
  the water you, and your neighbours, drink.
- **Never bury the casing.** The EPA: "Do not cut off the well casing
  below the land surface." A casing below the ground is a well that
  surface water can run into.
- **Keep records.** The EPA suggests records of maintenance such as
  disinfection; [Keeping Records](keeping_records.md) gives the shape of
  a good log.

### Disinfecting a well (shock chlorination)

After a flood, or when a test finds bacteria, a well may need
disinfecting: a strong dose of chlorine down it and through every pipe.
The EPA lists disinfection among the ways a high bacteria count can
sometimes be controlled, and says to ask the health department for the
specific steps. Its emergency procedure for a flooded well, in short:

1. Check the well first. If there is exposed or damaged wiring, call a
   professional before going any further. The steps below run the pump,
   so after a flood they wait until the wiring has been checked and the
   pump cleaned, with a well or pump contractor's help to turn it back
   on, as above. The EPA's current advice is to have that contractor
   help clean and disinfect a flooded well before the pump runs at all;
   these steps are what that work involves.
2. If the water is muddy, run an outside hose until it runs clear.
3. Wearing rubber gloves and eye protection against the bleach (they are
   no protection from electric shock), pour one gallon of
   unscented household liquid bleach into the well casing, through the
   vent or plug of a sealed well or under the lifted cover of a dug or
   bored well. While a dug well's cover is off it is an open hole: keep
   children and animals away, and put the cover back as soon as you
   can (general practice).
4. Run water from an outside hose back into the casing until you smell
   chlorine at the hose.
5. Open every cold tap in and around the house until each one smells of
   chlorine, then close them. Put any water treatment system on bypass
   first.
6. Wait 6 to 24 hours, without drinking, cooking, bathing or washing
   with the water.
7. Run an outside hose to a place where the chlorinated water will not
   harm plants, lakes, streams or the septic tank, until the smell is
   gone.
8. Have the water tested for bacteria 7 to 10 days later.

Two cautions from the same EPA sheet. First: "Drilled, driven or bored
wells are best disinfected by a well or pump contractor, because it is
difficult for the private owner to thoroughly disinfect these wells."
Second, after a flood, "Wells that are more than 10 years old or less
than 50 feet deep are likely to be contaminated, even if there is no
apparent damage." Disinfection kills what is in the well on that day; it
does nothing about a cracked seal or a nearby source, which is why the
test afterwards matters (our reading).

## Worked example: a household well near Silverdale, Washington

The project's canonical place is Silverdale, on the Kitsap Peninsula
(see [Silverdale, Washington](../locale/silverdale_wa.md)), and it is a
good example because groundwater there is not one option among several.
The USGS study of the peninsula says it "is surrounded by saltwater and
the hydrologic setting is similar to that of an island." There is no
river coming in from somewhere else.

**What is underground.** The same study (Scientific Investigations
Report 2014-5106) describes thick glacial deposits: aquifers of sand,
gravel and cobbles, often in separate, patchy bodies of very variable
thickness, with glacial till over much of the peninsula, so confined
aquifers are common. Groundwater flows outward, off the peninsula,
toward Puget Sound and Hood Canal.

**How deep the wells are.** Of the 3,856 wells in Kitsap County with a
depth recorded in the USGS's national inventory, half are deeper than
109 feet, a quarter are shallower than 47 feet and a quarter deeper than
228 feet (our count from the USGS site inventory, 2026-10-04). A shallow
well here is more likely to be in an unconfined layer near the surface,
the kind the USGS and EPA describe as easier to contaminate.

**How much the water level moves.** Water levels in wells in the glacial
deposits rose and fell by 1 to about 20 feet with the seasons over the
study's monitoring period (2011 to 2012), and by 33 feet in one well in
bedrock. A pump set just below the winter water level can be above the
water by late summer (our reading).

**Where the water goes.** In 2012, an above-average year for rain, about
two thirds of the water soaking into the peninsula's ground came out in
its streams, only about 4 percent was pumped from wells, and the rest
seeped out to Hood Canal and Puget Sound (USGS). Since groundwater is
what keeps those creeks running, more pumping here would mostly come out
of the creeks, in the season they are lowest (our reading, following the
USGS page on groundwater decline).

**What the law allows.** Kitsap is one of the eight watersheds with the
tighter limits above: a new household well here may average no more than
950 gallons a day (RCW 90.94.030). That is still a lot of water for a
house, but it is not a farm.

**What to test for.** Being near salt water, a shoreline well should be
tested for chloride as well as the annual list (see [Testing
Water](testing_water.md)); the USGS warning about salt water moving
inland is about exactly this kind of place.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Drilling, deepening and closing wells** belong to a licensed well
  contractor, apart from the narrow self-build exemption in states that
  have one (Washington's is above). Closing a well properly is a sealing
  job with its own rules, not a matter of filling it with rubble.
- **Anything inside the well,** pulling the pump included, is for a well
  or pump contractor (general practice). It is heavy, it is deep, and it
  puts people at the top of an open hole.
- **The pump's wiring and controls** are electrical work, and after a
  flood the EPA wants them checked by an electrician or well contractor,
  and a well or pump contractor's help cleaning the pump and turning it
  back on, before the pump runs.
- **Disinfecting a drilled, driven or bored well** is, in the EPA's
  words, best done by a well or pump contractor.
- **Entering a well, pit or cistern:** never.

Yours to do: looking at the well every year, keeping the cap on and the
ground sloping away, keeping chemicals away, testing the water, keeping
records, and knowing whom to call.

## Traps

- **"The water is clear, so the well is fine."** [Where Water Comes
  From](where_water_comes_from.md) explains why clear water tells you
  almost nothing. Test it.
- **"It is our water, under our land."** In many states, including
  Washington, how much you may take is set by law, and it can be cut in a
  drought.
- **"The old well is fine under those boards."** Boards rot. Cap it
  properly or have it closed.
- **Going in after someone.** The rescuer is the most common victim.
- **A well used as a drain.** Anything poured down it goes into the
  aquifer.
- **A burned-out pump after a flood.** Running it before it is dry,
  cleaned and checked can destroy it and electrocute someone, and rubber
  boots and gloves do not protect you from the shock.

## How the game models it

The game has a well, but not groundwater.

- **The home's water comes from a powered pump.** The household's Water
  pump draws 10 watts and delivers 2 litres a minute through an outlet
  the game labels "well lift"; it moves water only while it has power,
  and the home's plumbing shares that water with every connected tank and
  machine. The pump is `water_pump` in `data/machines/home.ron`; the rule
  that its water needs power is in `src/engine/home_spawn.rs` and
  `src/systems/plumbing.rs`. [Moving Water Without
  Power](moving_water_without_power.md) describes the rest of the home's
  plumbing.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no aquifer behind the pump. The well never runs low, its yield never
falls in summer, it has no depth, no water table and no cone of
depression, it cannot be contaminated, it needs no water right, and it
never affects a neighbour or a stream. Code for a groundwater model
exists in `src/systems/hydrology.rs`, but it is not running in the game.
The Silverdale locale data (`data/locales/silverdale_wa/water.json`)
does record the real aquifer, the local well depths and the legal limits
used in this guide, ready for when it is.

**Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
every session from the default home" is on, which is the default during
development, only your character carries between launches, so the pump,
the tanks and everything in them start each session as they are in the
default home.

## You own this when

- You will not enter a well, pit or cistern, and you would call 911
  rather than go in after someone.
- Every well on your land is capped, and any you do not use is marked
  and on its way to being properly closed.
- You switch off, lock or label, and test before touching the pump's
  wiring, and you leave a flooded pump off until its wiring has been
  checked and a well or pump contractor has helped clean it and turn it
  back on.
- You would run a generator to the pump only through a transfer switch.
- You can explain the water table, an aquifer, and why pumping one well
  can lower another.
- You know your state's limit on a household well and whether it can be
  cut in a drought.
- You know where your well's report is, how deep the well is and when it
  was last tested.
- You check the cap, casing and seal every year, keep the ground sloping
  away and chemicals clear, and test the water every year.
- You can shock-chlorinate a dug well safely, and you know when to call a
  contractor instead.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- That a dug well, well pit, cistern or spring box is a confined space
  in NIOSH's sense, that a contractor with gas testing and rescue
  equipment, not a household, does any work inside a well, that a
  household digging a well has none of the protection OSHA requires, and
  that the air and the soil are as dangerous to a household as to the
  workers OSHA's rules protect, are our reading of the NIOSH and OSHA
  material.
- That an exempt Washington well ranks by its date behind older rights
  is our reading of RCW 90.44.050, 90.44.020 and 90.03.010 together.
- That nearly all fresh water outside ice and glaciers is in the ground
  is our arithmetic from the USGS's figures.
- How to make an old well safe until it is closed (keep people away, a
  cover that cannot slide or rot through and will carry a person, a mark,
  a call to the state program) is general practice.
- Releasing the pressure tank before opening the plumbing, connecting a
  generator through a transfer switch, keeping children and animals away
  from a dug well while its cover is off, pulling a pump being a
  contractor's job, and keeping and reading the well report are general
  practice.
- That the local rule wins where the CDC and Washington's distances
  differ, that disinfection does not fix a cracked seal or a nearby
  source, that a pump set near the winter water level may be above the
  water by late summer, that new wells and summer stream flow are often
  treated as the same water, and that more pumping on the Kitsap
  Peninsula would come mostly out of its creeks are our reading.
- The Kitsap well depths are our own count from the USGS inventory.
