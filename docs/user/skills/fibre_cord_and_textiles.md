# Fibre, Cord and Textiles

String, rope, thread and cloth are all the same idea: short, weak
fibres made long and strong by twisting them together, and then
twisting or weaving the result again. A flax stalk, a sheep's fleece and
a plastic bottle can each end up as a rope, a shirt or a sack.

This guide is about choosing and using them well: which fibre suits
which job, how to make cord from plants, how strong a rope really is
and what weakens it, which knots to trust, and how to read a clothing
label and keep cloth from being eaten. It is also about the two ways
fibre hurts people, which are a rope that breaks and clothing that
burns, and where your own work should stop.

It takes its rope and knot advice from the Army's rigging manual (FM
5-125) and survival manual (FM 21-76), its rules for retiring a rope
from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), its
account of how plant fibre is freed from the stalk from the US
Department of Agriculture (USDA), its clothing safety from the Consumer
Product Safety Commission (CPSC) and the National Park Service, and its
label rules from the Federal Trade Commission (FTC). All are United
States government publications, in the public domain. One source, Penn
State Extension, is not; its facts are restated in our own words. Where
something is general practice rather than a published rule, the text
says so.

Its neighbours: [Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](force_levers_and_mechanical_advantage.md) covers pulleys and
why "the rope is the weak link" in any lift. [Clothing for Fire
Performance](../making/fire_performance_clothing.md) goes deep into which
fabrics melt or burn. [Cold and Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md)
covers dressing for cold and wet. [Identifying Wild
Plants](identifying_wild_plants.md) matters before you gather any plant.

## First: how rope and cloth hurt people

### A rope that breaks

Every rope has a load that breaks it, and a lot of things lower that
load without you noticing. The Army's rigging manual says a factor of
safety is always needed because a rope's breaking strength falls with
use and weather, and because "shock loading, knots, sharp bends, and
other stresses" can cut its strength by as much as 50 percent. It adds
that overloading a rope may break it, with possible serious injury to
the people around it.

Four rules follow:

- **Stay out from under, and out of the way.** OSHA's rule for lifting
  slings (29 CFR 1910.184(c)(9)) says everyone is kept clear of loads
  about to be lifted and of loads that are hanging. A rope under heavy
  strain stores energy; if it or its anchor lets go, the ends can fly
  back. Never stand in line with a rope pulling something heavy, such as
  a stuck vehicle or a tree (general practice).
- **Choose a sound anchor.** The rigging manual warns against dead or
  rotten trees and stumps as anchors, because they are likely to snap
  suddenly when a strain is put on the line, and says to tie low on a
  tree or stump.
- **Never trust a rope you made, or a rope with no rating, with a
  person's weight.** Climbing, lowering someone, swings, tree work and
  fall protection need rope made and rated for it, used by people
  trained to use it (general practice).
- **Retire a damaged rope.** The signs are below. A rope that fails
  inspection is cut up for other uses, never kept back for light jobs
  in the same coil as good rope (general practice; the rigging manual's
  own rule is below).

### Clothing that catches fire

The CPSC reports that older adults are 16 percent of the population but
77 percent of deaths from clothing fires; that in their clothing fire
injuries the item most often ignited is pants, then shirts, nightgowns
and robes; and that about a fifth of those injuries (22 percent) come
from cooking fires. Its advice: keep all clothing away from flames, and "Keep sleeves and dangling clothing
components away from the cooktop." If your clothes catch fire: "Stop
(don't run), drop and roll. Cover your face. Roll until the fire is
out." Running fans the flames (general knowledge).

Which fabrics melt onto skin and which burn without melting is in
[Clothing for Fire Performance](../making/fire_performance_clothing.md).

### Cotton in cold rain

The National Park Service's advice at Rocky Mountain National Park:
"Avoid clothing that will absorb and hold water, like cotton and
jeans." Wet cotton against your skin in wind and rain can chill you
dangerously; the reasons, and what to wear instead, are in
[Cold and Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md).

### Fibre dust

Processing raw fibre makes dust, and dust from cotton is a recognised
lung hazard. OSHA has a whole standard for cotton dust (29 CFR
1910.1043) covering yarn manufacturing and weaving, and it requires
regular medical checks of exposed workers, graded by a classification
of byssinosis, the lung damage named on the warning sign the same
standard requires: "COTTON DUST CAUSES DAMAGE TO LUNGS (BYSSINOSIS)".
Home spinning makes far less dust than a mill (our reading), but card,
comb and break fibres outdoors or in a well-ventilated space, and wear a dust mask for
dusty work (general practice).

### Gathering plants

Nettles sting, and vines you strip for fibre can be poison ivy. Wear
gloves, and learn to recognise poison ivy and its relatives before you
gather (general practice). Wash your hands after handling milkweed, and
keep its milky sap away from your eyes (general practice). [Identifying
Wild Plants](identifying_wild_plants.md) explains how to be certain what
a plant is before you handle it.

Gathering is not always yours to do, either. On private land you need
the landowner's permission (general practice). In national parks, the
federal rule (36 CFR 2.1(a)(1)(ii)) prohibits possessing, removing,
digging or disturbing "Plants or the parts or products thereof". The
same rule lets a park's superintendent allow hand gathering of certain
fruits, berries, nuts and seashells, a list that does not include
stems, leaves or bark, so treat nettles, milkweed, yucca and bark in a
park as not yours to take (our reading). Other public lands have their
own rules, so ask the agency before you gather (general practice).

## What fibres are

### From plants

The rigging manual lists the main plant fibres in rope: abaca (sold as
Manila), sisal, hemp, and sometimes coir, cotton and jute. What it says
of each:

- **Manila** comes from the leaf stems of the abaca plant, a relative of
  the banana, in fibres 4 to 15 feet long, which give the rope "relatively
  high elasticity, strength, and resistance to wear and deterioration."
  It is the standard rope the Army issues.
- **Sisal** rope is about 80 percent as strong as high-quality Manila,
  easy to get, and stands up to sea water well.
- **Hemp** was used widely before Manila; the manual says its main use
  now is in small lines such as marline, which are tarred to make them
  more water-resistant, and tarred hemp has about 80 percent of the
  strength of untarred.
- **Coir**, from coconut husks, is elastic and rough, about a quarter as
  strong as hemp, and light enough to float.
- **Cotton** makes a smooth white rope that takes a lot of bending.
- **Jute** goes chiefly into sacking, burlap and cheap twine and rope.

Hemp and flax are **bast** fibres: they grow in the stem, around a
woody core, and have to be freed from it. Cotton grows as hairs on the
seed (general knowledge).

### From animals

Wool, hair, fur and silk are animal fibres. So is sinew, which the
Army's survival manual calls the best natural material for lashing
small objects: it is moistened and twisted into strands, sticks to
itself, and hardens as it dries.

### Made by people

The rigging manual calls nylon the principal synthetic fibre for rope:
nearly three times as strong as Manila, waterproof, able to stretch and
absorb shocks and return to its length, and resistant to abrasion, rot,
decay and fungus. Many other synthetic ropes and fabrics exist; for any
of them, the maker's label is the authority on strength (general
practice).

## From plant to fibre

Bast fibre is held to the stalk by the plant's own glue, and getting it
off is the oldest part of the craft. The USDA's Economic Research
Service, describing hemp (2000), explains that for textiles the bast
fibres must be separated from the rest of the stalk, and that **retting**
is "a microbial process that breaks the chemical bonds that hold the
stem together." In plain words, you let it partly rot.

It describes the two traditional ways:

- **Field or dew retting.** The stems are cut or pulled and left lying
  in the field to rot, watched closely so the fibre comes free from the
  core without much loss of quality. It needs moisture for the microbes,
  then dry weather for the stalks to dry.
- **Water retting.** The stems are soaked in rivers, ponds or tanks. It
  gives more even fibre, but it is labour-intensive, and the large
  volume of water it uses must be treated before it is let out. Keep
  retting well away from any water anyone drinks (our reading).

After retting and drying, the report describes **breaking**, crushing
the woody core into short pieces, and **scutching**, beating them out to
leave the long fibre. It says these machine steps are "functionally
identical to traditional hand methods" of preparing hemp for twine,
rope or yarn.

**Growing hemp is licensed in the United States.** The USDA says that
to produce hemp you first need to be licensed or authorized under a
state, tribal or USDA hemp program. Check before you plant it. Flax,
nettles and the other fibre plants in this guide need no such licence
(general knowledge), but some plants are listed as noxious weeds in some
places; [The Species Where You Live](the_species_where_you_live.md)
explains why the local list matters.

## Twisting fibre into cord

A single fibre is short and weak. Twisted together, fibres grip each
other by friction, so a long yarn holds even though no fibre runs its
whole length (general knowledge). The rigging manual explains how rope
keeps from untwisting: fibres are twisted into yarns, yarns twisted the
other way into strands, and strands twisted the other way again into
rope. "Twisting each element in the opposite direction puts the rope in
balance and prevents its elements from unlaying when a load is
suspended on it."

### Testing a material

The Army's survival manual gives three quick tests before you make
cord from anything: pull on a length to test its strength; twist it
between your fingers and roll the fibres together; and if it survives
that without snapping, tie an overhand knot in it and gently tighten.
If the knot does not break, the material is usable.

Its list of plants commonly used for cord includes the stems of nettles
and milkweeds, yucca, and the inner bark of trees such as the linden.
Elsewhere it lists inner bark from linden, elm, hickory, white oak,
mulberry, chestnut, and red and white cedar, shredded and braided, and
says to test the finished cord to be sure it is strong enough, braiding
several strands together for more strength.

Take bark from fallen or cut branches, never in a ring around a living
trunk, which can kill the tree (general practice).

### Worked example: two-ply cord

This is the method in the survival manual's drawing, the same opposite
twist that the rigging manual describes.

1. Take two thin strands of prepared fibre and tie them together firmly
   at one end with a knot, as the manual's drawing shows. (Folding one
   bundle unevenly, so its two halves run out at different places, is a
   common alternative; general practice.)
2. Twist each strand clockwise, tightly.
3. Twist the two strands around each other the other way,
   counterclockwise. Each strand's own twist wants to unwind, and the
   opposite twist of the pair locks it in.
4. Before one strand runs out, lay new fibre alongside it and keep
   twisting; staggering the joins keeps the cord even (general
   practice).
5. Test it with the survival manual's pull and knot test.

Cord you make is for tying, lashing, bundling and sewing. It has no
rated strength, so never hang anything over a person on it or trust it
with anyone's weight (general practice).

## Rope: how strong, and what weakens it

### Size and safe load

The rigging manual explains two numbers. **Breaking strength** is the
greatest stress a rope can take without breaking. The **safe working
capacity** is the most that should be put on it, the breaking strength
divided by a factor of safety; it uses a minimum of 4. Its example: a
new 1-inch Number 1 Manila rope breaks at 9,000 pounds, so its safe
working capacity is 2,250 pounds.

When there is no table, the manual's rule of thumb for fibre rope: the
safe working capacity, in tons, is about the square of the diameter in
inches. A half-inch rope gives a quarter of a ton. The rule builds in a
factor of safety of about 4. In metric (arithmetic): a 12 mm rope is
about half an inch, and a quarter of a US ton is 500 pounds, about 230
kg.

For modern synthetic rope, use the working load on the maker's label,
not this rule (general practice).

### Knots weaken rope

The rigging manual says the overhand knot "reduces the strength of a
straight rope by 55 percent." Every knot puts sharp bends in the rope,
which is part of why the factor of safety exists (the manual names knots
and sharp bends among the stresses it covers). OSHA's rule for lifting
slings goes further: slings "shall not be shortened with knots or bolts
or other makeshift devices", and in
a fibre rope sling "Knots shall not be used in lieu of splices." For
anything that lifts, use a properly made sling, not a knotted rope
(our reading of the rule, which is written for workplaces).

### Angles multiply the pull

A rope pulled nearly flat carries far more than the weight hung on it.
The rigging manual explains it for slings: the tension in each leg can
be more than the weight of the load, and it grows as the legs spread
toward the horizontal. Its formula: the tension in one leg is the
weight divided by the number of legs, multiplied by the leg's length
divided by the vertical height it covers. Its example: a 1,800-pound
load on a two-legged sling, each leg 8 feet long and covering 6 feet
of height, puts 1,200 pounds on each leg. "When using slings, stay above
the critical angle", the angle at which each leg carries the whole load.

**Worked example: a hammock** (arithmetic, using the manual's formula).
A person weighing 180 pounds lies in a hammock hung by two ropes. If
each rope is 6 feet long and drops 3 feet from the tree to the hammock,
each carries 90 x 6 / 3 = 180 pounds, the person's whole weight. Hang
the hammock flatter, so each 6-foot rope drops only 1 foot, and each
carries 90 x 6 / 1 = 540 pounds, three times the person's weight. A
person getting in, or flopping down, adds a shock load on top. Hang
hammocks and washing lines with plenty of sag, and use rope rated well
above the load (general practice following the arithmetic).

### Caring for rope

The rigging manual's guidelines for fibre rope:

- Make sure it is dry, then store it in a cool, dry place, against
  mildew and rot; coil it or hang it so air can circulate.
- Do not drag it through sand or dirt or over sharp edges; grit between
  the fibres cuts them.
- Slacken a taut line before rain or damp, because wet rope shrinks and
  may break. Thaw a frozen rope completely before using it.
- Keep it from heat and chemical fumes; it says heat or boiling water
  cuts a rope's strength by about 20 percent.

Before cutting a rope, it says to put two whippings on it, an inch or
two apart, tight wraps of small cord, and cut between them, so neither
cut end unravels.

### Inspecting rope

The manual warns that the outside of a fibre rope is not a good guide
to its inside. Untwist the strands slightly and look in:

- Mildewed rope smells musty, and the inner fibres look dark and stained.
- Dirt and powdery, sawdust-like material inside means it has been
  chafing.
- In a rope with a core, a core that breaks away in small pieces means
  it has been overstrained.
- If it passes everything else, pull out two fibres and try to break
  them; sound fibres resist.

OSHA's list for retiring a natural or synthetic fibre rope sling at once
(29 CFR 1910.184(h)(5)) is a good checklist for any rope: abnormal wear;
powdered fibre between strands; broken or cut fibres; variations in the
size or roundness of strands; discolouration or rotting; and distortion
of hardware in the sling. When a rope fails, the rigging manual says to
destroy it or cut it into short pieces so it cannot be used for
hoisting; the short pieces can do other jobs.

## Knots worth knowing

The rigging manual's test of a good knot: it "must be easy to tie, must
hold without slipping, and must be easy to untie." Learn these, with the
manual's drawings, until you can tie them without looking (the selection
is ours; the descriptions are the manual's):

- **Overhand knot.** The simplest; stops a rope sliding through a hole
  or unravelling. It cuts the rope's strength by more than half.
- **Figure-eight knot.** A bigger stopper knot than the overhand, and
  easy to untie.
- **Square knot.** Ties two ropes of equal size together, but "will not
  hold if the ropes are wet or if they are of different sizes."
- **Sheet bend.** For joining two ropes of unequal size, or tying a
  rope to an eye; it can loosen when slack. The double sheet bend holds
  better, for equal or unequal ropes and for wet ones.
- **Bowline.** A loop that will not tighten or slip under strain.
- **Clove hitch.** Fastens a rope to a post, pipe or timber.
- **Round turn and two half hitches.** Fastens a rope to a pole or
  timber and does not jam.
- **Timber hitch, with a half hitch.** For dragging or lifting logs and
  poles; the manual warns that a timber hitch used alone may come untied
  when slack or under a sudden strain.

## Thread, cloth and clothes

### Reading the label

Two federal rules put useful facts on every garment. The FTC explains
that under the Textile Act, the fibre names and the percentage by
weight of each fibre must be listed, in order from the most to the
least. And its Care Labeling Rule requires makers and importers to
attach care instructions to clothing, which, if followed, will not
cause substantial harm to the garment, and which warn about procedures
a buyer might assume are fine but would harm it. A label that says
"cotton 60%, polyester 40%" and "Do not tumble dry" is the cheapest
fibre lesson there is (our example).

### Choosing cloth for the job

- **Cotton** is cool and absorbent, which is good in heat and bad in
  cold rain (the Park Service's warning above).
- **Synthetics** dry fast, but many melt when they burn; see [Clothing
  for Fire Performance](../making/fire_performance_clothing.md) before
  wearing them near a stove, a fire or a forge.
- **Wool**, like fur and other animal fibres, is what clothes moths
  mainly eat, and one kind eats hemp too (below).

That list is general practice except where a source is named.

### Keeping moths out of wool and hemp

Penn State Extension describes the two common clothes moths. Both live
mainly on animal products such as wool, fur, feathers and hair, and
their larvae often feed out of sight under collars and cuffs. The
casemaking clothes moth goes further: it also eats some materials that
come from plants, hemp among them, along with stored dried herbs and
seeds, so hemp cloth and hemp cord need the same care as wool. The
webbing clothes moth leaves cotton and synthetics alone unless they are
stained with food or body oils, which is the reason to wash clothes
before you store them (our reading). Its advice:
launder or dry-clean infested clothing and store it in an airtight
container or bag; vacuum thoroughly, because larvae can live on shed pet
hair under baseboards and in air return vents; and never apply
pesticides to clothing or bedding.

### Mending

A small hole mended now saves a garment; a big one often does not.
Darn a hole in a sock or a sweater by weaving new yarn across it; patch
a tear in heavier cloth with a piece of similar fabric sewn on behind
it; and reinforce the places that wear first, knees, elbows and seat,
before they go through (general practice). [Keeping Things
Working](keeping_things_working.md) is about the same habit for
machines.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Anything that holds up a person.** Climbing rope, rescue rope,
  fall-protection lines, swings for children and tree-work rigging are
  made to standards and used by trained people. Do not make your own or
  improvise them (general practice).
- **Lifting loads over people or anything valuable.** Use rated slings
  and lifting gear, and keep everyone clear (OSHA's sling rule).
- **Felling or pulling trees with rope.** The anchor and the tree can
  both fail suddenly; see the rigging manual's warning about dead trees
  and stumps, and get help from someone trained (general practice).
- **Clothing that must resist fire.** Certified flame-resistant
  workwear is tested to a standard; home treatment is not the same
  thing ([Clothing for Fire
  Performance](../making/fire_performance_clothing.md)).

## Traps

- **"It held last time."** Rope weakens with use, weather and every
  knot. Inspect it, inside and out.
- **A square knot joining two different ropes.** It slips. Use a sheet
  bend.
- **A tight, flat line.** The flatter the rope, the harder it is pulled.
- **A knot where a splice should be.** On a lifting sling, OSHA forbids
  it.
- **Storing rope wet.** It mildews and rots from the inside, where you
  cannot see.
- **Dangling sleeves at the stove.** Roll them up or wear close-fitting
  ones.
- **Putting clothes away dirty.** Food and body oils draw moths even to
  cotton and synthetics, and wool and hemp are food to them already.

## How the game models it

The game has fibre, rope and cloth as materials and ingredients.

- **Where fibre comes from.** Cotton, flax, hemp and jute grow from seed
  in the garden (`data/plants.csv`); a stand of wild flax grows a short
  walk beyond the fields, and you collect from it by walking up and
  pressing E (`data/entities/wild_spawns.ron`); and the homestead's two
  sheep give wool the same way (`data/entities/livestock.ron`). Collecting
  from plants and animals counts toward the Farming skill.
- **From crop to fibre.** At a workbench, three Cotton, Flax, Hemp or
  Jute make two Fiber Bundles. You can also strip a Wood Log into three
  Fiber Bundles by hand (`data/recipes.csv`).
- **Rope and cloth.** Make Rope, by hand, turns three Fiber Bundles into
  a 10-metre braided hemp Rope and trains Woodworking. Weave Cloth, at a
  loom, turns four Fiber Bundles into a Cloth Bolt; Spin Thread, by hand,
  turns two Fiber Bundles straight into a Cloth Bolt. Both train
  Tailoring, as does sewing clothes and backpacks at a sewing machine.
- **What rope and cloth are for.** Rope goes into a hammock (three Cloth
  Bolts and two Ropes), bows, a sling and backpacks; cloth into clothes,
  beds, curtains and rugs.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** The game starts in the Normal play mode (Settings >
  Gameplay > Play mode), where a craft uses up its materials and needs
  its station and its tools, so rope and cloth take fibre you have
  collected. In Creative, or in Dev if you choose it, a craft uses up no
  materials and wears no tools while the Creative mode switch on the
  Inventory page is on, which it is unless you turn it off, though the
  Crafting page still wants the fibre, the station and the tools on hand
  before it lets you press Craft; in Dev, its "Dev: stock all materials"
  button supplies the fibre.
- **Starting from the default home.** Settings > Gameplay > "Start every
  session from the default home" is off by default, so your progress is
  kept between launches, your fibre, rope and cloth included. With the
  setting on, only your character carries between launches, and they
  start the next session as they were in the default home.

**Clothes and the cold.** The game's body heat model counts the warmth
of what you wear, garment by garment, and soaked clothes keep only
about 70 percent of their insulation in it (`src/systems/body_heat.rs`, with
each garment's insulation in `data/equipment.csv`). It treats every
fabric alike, though: wet wool and wet cotton lose the same share.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: a rope
has no strength in the game, so it never breaks, stretches, rots or
needs inspecting, and knots do not exist; clothes and rope never wear
out; retting appears as a growth stage of the flax crop before it is
mature, and in the description of the Bundle Flax Fiber recipe, where
in reality it
happens to the stalks after they are cut or pulled; spinning by hand
gives a whole Cloth Bolt with no weaving, from fewer fibres than the
loom uses; every Cloth Bolt is cotton, whatever fibre went into it; no
crafting recipe uses wool yet; and growing hemp needs no licence. In the
game, clothes cannot catch fire, whatever they are made of, and fibre
makes no dust; in real life those, with a breaking rope, are where the
harm comes from.

## You own this when

- You keep clear of loads being lifted and of ropes under heavy strain,
  and never stand in line with a rope pulling something heavy.
- You never trust homemade or unrated rope with anyone's weight.
- You keep sleeves and loose clothing away from the stove, and know to
  stop, drop and roll.
- You avoid cotton for cold, wet days.
- You can work out a fibre rope's safe load from its size, and you allow
  for knots and for angles.
- You can tie a stopper knot, a square knot and a sheet bend, a bowline,
  a clove hitch, and a round turn and two half hitches, and you know
  which to use when.
- You store rope dry and slack, inspect it inside, and retire it at the
  first sign of damage.
- You can test a plant fibre, and twist it into a two-ply cord.
- You can read a fibre content label and a care label.
- You store wool and hemp clean, in sealed containers, and never spray
  pesticide on clothes.
- You have permission before you gather plants, and you leave them be
  in a national park.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- That a rope under strain can fly back when it or its anchor fails, and
  standing out of line with it, are general practice; OSHA's rule is
  about keeping clear of lifted and hanging loads.
- Not keeping a failed rope back for light jobs beside good rope is
  general practice; the rigging manual says to destroy it or cut it up.
- That wet cotton in wind and rain can chill you dangerously is general
  knowledge beside the Park Service's advice; the detail is in Cold and
  Hypothermia.
- Never trusting homemade or unrated rope with a person's weight, and
  leaving climbing, rescue, fall protection, swings and tree rigging to
  rated gear and trained people, are general practice.
- That running fans burning clothes is general knowledge beside the
  CPSC's stop, drop and roll.
- That home spinning makes far less dust than a mill is our reading;
  working outdoors and a dust mask are general practice.
- Gloves against nettles, learning poison ivy before gathering, and
  washing off milkweed sap and keeping it from the eyes are general
  practice.
- Asking the landowner's permission, and asking the agency on other
  public lands, are general practice; treating fibre plants and bark in
  a national park as not yours to take is our reading of 36 CFR 2.1.
- That clean storage keeps moths off cotton and synthetics, and that
  hemp cloth and cord need the same care as wool, are our reading of
  Penn State Extension.
- That hemp and flax fibres grow in the stem around a woody core, and
  cotton as seed hairs, is general knowledge, as is the absence of a
  licence for flax and nettles.
- Keeping retting away from drinking water is our reading of the USDA's
  note about treating retting water.
- That twisted fibres hold by friction is general knowledge; folding one
  bundle unevenly, staggering joins, taking bark only from fallen or cut branches, and the uses of
  homemade cord are general practice.
- The metric conversion of the rule of thumb, and the hammock example,

- Using the maker's label for synthetic rope is general practice.
- Applying OSHA's sling rules to home lifting is our reading.

- The label example, the notes on cotton and synthetics, and the
  mending advice are general practice or our example.
- Leaving tree felling with ropes and certified flame-resistant
