# Heating Water

Hot water is one of the quiet necessities of a household: washing, cleaning,
cooking, a shower at the end of the day. It is also one of the biggest uses
of energy in a home, and the water heater is one of the few
machines in the house that can scald a child, fill a room with carbon
monoxide, or, if its safety valve is blocked, burst. This guide is about
deciding how you will get your hot water and running it safely:

- how much hot water a household uses, and what that costs in energy;
- the kinds of water heater, from the plain tank to the heat pump and the
  solar collector, and how their efficiencies compare;
- heating water on a stove or a fire when there is no power;
- what temperature to set, which is a real trade between scalding and the
  bacterium that causes Legionnaires' disease;
- the temperature and pressure relief valve, and why it must never be capped;
- carbon monoxide from heaters that burn gas, oil or propane;
- and boiling water to make it safe to drink.

Units: temperatures are in degrees Fahrenheit with Celsius in brackets,
because water heaters in the United States are labelled in Fahrenheit. Water
is in litres with US gallons alongside; one US gallon is 3.785 litres. Energy
is in kilowatt hours (kWh), the unit on an electricity bill.

## The energy in a litre of hot water

Everything else in this guide rests on one number. The US Geological Survey
puts it this way: a kilogram of water has to absorb 4,184 joules to warm by
1 C. A litre of water weighs about a kilogram, and 4,184 joules is 1.16 watt
hours, so:

**Warming one litre of water by one degree Celsius takes about 1.16 Wh.**
(For one US gallon warmed one degree Fahrenheit, it is about 2.4 Wh.)

That is the heat that has to end up in the water. Every real heater needs
more than that, because some heat leaks away from a tank, goes up a flue, or
never reaches the pot. How much more is what the efficiency ratings further
down measure.

A worked example, using the temperatures a Lawrence Berkeley National
Laboratory report for the Environmental Protection Agency adopted as a US
average: cold water arriving at 60.5 F (15.8 C) and a water heater set to
125 F (51.7 C), a rise of 64.5 F (35.8 C). Each litre heated through that
rise holds about 42 Wh (about 158 Wh per US gallon). A hundred litres (26
gallons) holds about 4.2 kWh.

## How much hot water a household uses

The LBNL report (Chen and others, June 2020) gathered the North American
studies of household hot water use, and they span a range worth knowing:

- Studies from the 1980s and 1990s found about 57 to 66 US gallons a day per
  household (about 215 to 250 litres).
- A Department of Energy model of 2010 put the national average at 49.3
  gallons a day (187 litres), and projected 43.0 gallons (163 litres) after
  2015, allowing for the federal efficiency standards for clothes washers
  and dishwashers.
- A survey of 1,019 households in Nova Scotia (Evarts and Swan, 2013, as
  reported by LBNL) found 36.7 gallons a day (139 litres) for one person and
  92.7 gallons (351 litres) for six. Use does not rise in step with the
  number of people, because washing dishes and clothes is shared.

**Where it goes.** The most recent measured study in LBNL's tables is the
Water Research Foundation's Residential End Uses of Water (DeOreo and
others, 2016, 94 homes in the United States and Canada). Of all the hot
water those homes used:

| Use | Share of all hot water |
|---|---|
| Showers | 39.1 percent |
| Faucets (taps) | 33.8 percent |
| Clothes washers | 9.7 percent |
| Baths | 5.7 percent |
| Dishwashers | 4.8 percent |
| Leaks | 4.6 percent |

And the same study measured how much of each fixture's water was hot: 66.2
percent of shower water, 59.1 percent of bath water, 57.0 percent of faucet
water, 20.0 percent of clothes washer water and all of the dishwasher's.

So the shower is the place to save. The EPA's WaterSense programme notes
that a standard showerhead uses 2.5 gallons (9.5 litres) a minute, and a
WaterSense labelled one no more than 2.0 gallons (7.6 litres). LBNL takes a
comfortable shower as 105 F (40.6 C). A worked example, not quoted from
anyone: a ten minute shower at 2.0 gallons a minute is 76 litres of water
warmed from 15.8 C to 40.6 C, which is about 2.2 kWh of heat.

## What it costs in energy

**How much.** The US Energy Information Administration's Residential Energy
Consumption Survey (RECS) measures what American homes actually use. In
2020, water heating took about 18 percent of all the energy used in US homes
(1,737 of 9,481 trillion Btu of site energy, RECS Table CE3.1; the share is
worked here from EIA's totals). For households that heat their water with
electricity, RECS Table CE5.3a gives the average electricity used for water
heating in a year, by the number of people in the home:

| People in the home | kWh a year | kWh a day | At 17.3 cents a kWh, a year |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1,427 | 3.9 | about $247 |
| 2 | 2,470 | 6.8 | about $427 |
| 3 | 3,482 | 9.5 | about $602 |
| 4 | 4,313 | 11.8 | about $746 |
| 5 | 4,552 | 12.5 | about $788 |
| 6 or more | 5,314 | 14.6 | about $919 |
| All homes | 2,706 | 7.4 | about $468 |

The price column is worked here from EIA's average US residential price for
2025, 17.30 cents a kWh (Electric Power Monthly, Table 5.3). Your own price
is on your bill; multiply by it instead.

These are EIA's estimates of what real households used, so they include
everything: the heat in the water, the heat the tank loses while it
stands, and the household's real habits. For comparison, the average household in RECS, counting all fuels,
used 14.1 million Btu for water heating, about 4,130 kWh of energy. Burning
gas, oil or propane loses some of the heat up the flue, which the efficiency
ratings below show plainly.

**Where the energy goes that is not in the water.** A storage tank is kept
hot around the clock, so it loses heat through its walls whether anyone uses
it or not. That standby loss is why DOE's Building Science Education pages
describe a tankless heater's advantage as heating water on demand, without
the standby losses of a tank.

## The kinds of water heater

**How efficiency is measured.** Water heaters sold in the United States
are rated by the Department of Energy's test method with a Uniform Energy
Factor (UEF): roughly, how much of the energy the heater uses ends up in
the hot water, over a standard pattern of daily draws. ENERGY STAR (an EPA
programme) puts it simply: the higher the UEF, the more efficient the
heater. A heater that burns fuel or runs a heating element can never beat
1.0, because it cannot put more heat into the water than the fuel or
electricity contains. A heat pump can, because it moves heat that is
already in the air rather than making it.

The minimums below are federal law (10 CFR 430.32(d), for heaters made
before 6 May 2029, medium draw pattern), worked out for common sizes. The
ENERGY STAR figures are the bar for its label.

| Kind | Federal minimum UEF | ENERGY STAR UEF |
|---|---|---|
| Electric storage tank, 50 gallons | 0.92 | (only heat pumps qualify) |
| Heat pump water heater, integrated | same class as above | 3.30 or more (2.20 for models on a 120 volt, 15 amp circuit) |
| Gas storage tank, 40 gallons | 0.58 | 0.81 or more |
| Gas tankless (instantaneous) | 0.81 | 0.95 or more |
| Electric tankless | 0.91 | (none) |
| Solar, with electric backup | (none) | solar UEF 3.00 or more |
| Solar, with gas backup | (none) | solar UEF 1.80 or more |

(For storage tanks the federal minimum is a formula of the tank's volume: an
electric tank of 20 to 55 gallons must reach 0.9307 minus 0.0002 times its
gallons, and a gas tank 0.6483 minus 0.0017 times its gallons.)

**What changes in 2029.** For heaters made on or after 6 May 2029, the same
regulation raises the minimum for an electric storage heater of 20 to 55
gallons (other than a separate class of small ones up to 35 gallons) to a
UEF of 2.30, and above 55 gallons to 2.50. A resistance element cannot reach
those numbers, so in practice a new full-size electric tank will be a heat
pump.

**A plain storage tank** (electric or gas) keeps a tankful of water hot and
ready, and can deliver a lot of it at once, but it pays the standby loss
all day. A tank is sized by its first-hour rating
(FHR): ENERGY STAR defines it as the most hot water, in gallons, the heater
can supply in an hour that starts with it fully heated. ENERGY STAR's labelled
heat pump heaters must reach at least 45 gallons (170 litres) in that hour,
and its gas tanks at least 51 (193 litres).

**A tankless (on-demand) heater** heats water only as it flows. DOE's
Building Science Education page gives a typical output of 2 to 5 gallons (8
to 19 litres) a minute, and ENERGY STAR requires its labelled gas models to
deliver at least 2.8 gallons a minute over a 67 F (37 C) rise. The catch is
the rate. A worked example, from the energy per litre above: warming 2.8
gallons a minute through 37 C takes about 27 kilowatts, continuously, for as
long as the tap runs. That is why a whole house electric tankless heater
needs a very large electrical supply, and why a gas one needs a large gas
line. Colder incoming water in winter (LBNL notes that inlet temperatures
vary by region and season) means less flow at the same temperature.

**A heat pump water heater** works on the same principle as a heat pump
that heats a house: DOE's Building Science Education page says it moves
heat from the surrounding space into the water tank, and that it can be two
to three times more efficient than a conventional electric resistance
heater. An integrated unit comes with its own tank and backup resistance
elements. On the standard test, a
labelled heat pump heater (UEF 3.30) uses about 28 percent of the
electricity a resistance tank (UEF 0.92) uses for the same hot water, a
saving of about 72 percent (worked here). ENERGY STAR's conditions: install
it in an indoor space that stays between 40 and 90 F (4 and 32 C) all year,
with about 1,000 cubic feet (28 cubic metres) of air around it. ENERGY STAR
adds that it cools and dehumidifies the space it sits in, which helps in
hot, humid weather, and that it runs less efficiently in a cold space.

A rough worked example of what the choice means, using the shower above
(2.2 kWh of heat) at 17.3 cents a kWh: about 41 cents from a resistance
tank at UEF 0.92, and about 11 cents from a heat pump heater at UEF 3.30.

**A solar water heater** uses a collector, usually on the roof, to heat
water or a fluid that carries heat to a storage tank. ENERGY STAR says a
labelled system can cut annual hot water costs in half, and DOE's Building
Science Education page says a standard home will see at least a 50 percent
reduction in water heating electricity, more where it is sunny. The points
ENERGY STAR makes before buying:

- **Sun.** The roof should get direct sun from 10 in the morning to 4 in the
  afternoon all year and face within 30 degrees of due south (true south,
  not magnetic south), or there should be unshaded ground nearby for a
  ground-mounted system.
- **Freezing.** Where the temperature has a good chance of falling below
  42 F (5.6 C), choose a closed loop with antifreeze as the fluid, or a
  system that drains back when it gets cold. Avoid batch systems where
  winters are distinct.
- **Batch collectors** (also called integrated collector-storage) are dark
  tanks or tubes in an insulated box. Water sitting in them on a sunny day
  with little use can get very hot, and ENERGY STAR names a tempering valve,
  which mixes in cold water before it reaches the tap, as your protection
  from scalding.
- **Size.** A flat-plate collector typically holds about 40 gallons (151
  litres), and ENERGY STAR says two of them provide roughly half the hot
  water a family of four needs.
- **Evacuated tube collectors** are the most efficient kind, work in
  overcast weather and down to minus 40 F, and cost about twice as much per
  square foot as flat plates.

Solar water heating gives the least in winter, when days are short, so it
lowers the bill rather than replacing the heater: ENERGY STAR says a system
is generally designed to work with an electric or gas backup heater.

## Heating water without power

When the power is out, or there is none, hot water comes from a stove or a
fire, and the arithmetic is the same. Bringing one litre from 15 C to the
boil at 100 C puts about 99 Wh, roughly 0.1 kWh, into the water (worked from
the USGS figure). A fire or a stove puts only part of its heat into the pot,
so it burns more fuel than that; heat only what you need, and heat it once.

Four rules come before convenience:

- **Burn fuel only where it is meant to burn.** The Centers for Disease
  Control and Prevention is plain: never use a portable gas camp stove
  indoors, never burn charcoal indoors, never heat your house with a gas
  oven, and run a generator only outside, more than 20 feet (6 metres) from
  windows, doors and vents. All of them make carbon monoxide.
  [Firewood](firewood.md) covers stoves and fires, and carbon monoxide, in
  full.
- **Hot pots burn.** Keep children well back from a stove or fire with water
  on it, and carry hot water in a vessel with a lid and a handle you trust.
  [Treating Burns](treating_burns.md) covers what to do if it goes wrong.
- **Sun-warmed water is a batch collector.** A dark container of water in
  the sun works on the same principle ENERGY STAR describes, and on a bright
  day it can get hot enough to scald. Test it before it touches skin.
- **Never heat water in anything closed.** A pipe, coil or tank run through a
  fire, with no way for steam to escape, can explode. The Consumer Product
  Safety Commission warned in 1982 about water heaters built into fireplaces:
  if the water stops flowing through them (a pump or power failure, or
  frozen pipes), it can boil into steam, and without an adequate, properly
  installed pressure relief device the pressure can cause an explosion. It
  added that nothing should restrict the pipe between the unit and its
  safety valve. Heat water in an open pot or a kettle with a spout.

## The setpoint: scalding against Legionella

Every storage water heater has a thermostat, and the number you set it to is
a real trade between two harms.

**Scalding.** The Consumer Product Safety Commission's figures (its
Publication 5098) for how long hot water takes to cause third degree burns
in most adults:

| Water temperature | Time to a third degree burn |
|---|---|
| 150 F (66 C) | 2 seconds |
| 140 F (60 C) | 6 seconds |
| 130 F (54 C) | 30 seconds |
| 120 F (49 C) | 5 minutes |

CPSC says most tap water scald injuries and deaths are to the elderly and to
children under five, urges everyone to set water heaters to 120 F (49 C),
and notes that a lower setting also saves energy. It also says: never take
hot water temperature for granted, hand test water before bathing infants
and young children, and never leave a child alone in the bathroom even for a
second.

**Legionella.** Legionnaires' disease is a serious pneumonia caused by
bacteria called Legionella. CDC's fact sheet (September 2025) says people
catch it by breathing in small droplets of water that carry the bacteria,
names water used for showering among the most likely sources, and says it
does not spread from person to person. Most healthy people exposed do not
get sick; being 50 or older, a current or former smoker, having a chronic
lung disease, or having an immune system weakened by disease or medicine
raises the risk, and about 1 in 10 people who get the disease die of it.
CDC gives the temperatures that favour the bacteria as 77 to 113 F (25 to
45 C), and notes it may grow as low as 68 F (20 C).

**What the sources recommend.**

- **CDC, for homes** (its guidance for vacation rental owners, reviewed
  January 2026): set the water heater at or above 120 F; a hotter 130 to 140
  F kills many harmful germs but raises the scalding risk; if you set it
  above 120 F, use thermostatic mixing valves at the faucet or shower; and
  follow state and local scalding laws.
- **CDC, for large building water systems** (its Legionella control
  toolkit): store hot water above 140 F (60 C), keep circulating hot water
  from falling below 120 F (49 C), and put thermostatic mixing valves as
  close to the fixtures as possible to prevent scalding.
- **OSHA** (for commercial, industrial and apartment building systems):
  store hot water at 140 F (60 C) or more and deliver it at 122 F (50 C) or
  more to all outlets, and drain tanks periodically to remove scale and
  sediment.
- **The New Jersey Department of Health**, in its guidance for draining and
  flushing a home water heater (September 2025): a setting of at least 120
  F; higher may be better for controlling bacteria, with extra care against
  scalding; consider a mixing valve where young children or others at
  higher risk of scalds live in the house. It also recommends hiring a
  plumber to drain and flush the tank, and says people at higher risk of
  Legionnaires' disease should keep clear of the job, because it throws
  water droplets into the air.

**Putting them together.** The sources meet at 120 F (49 C): it is CPSC's
recommended setting against scalds, and the lowest that CDC's home guidance
and New Jersey accept. Storing hotter, around 140 F (60 C), is what the
building guidance asks for against Legionella, but water at 140 F burns in
six seconds, so it is only safe with a thermostatic mixing valve (an
anti-scald valve) that blends it down before it reaches the tap. A
household with small children or elderly people has the most to lose from
scalds; a household with someone at high risk of Legionnaires' disease has
the most to lose from a cool tank. The valve serves both. Portland's Water
Bureau says that if you set the heater at the high end, have a plumber
install anti-scald devices.

Check your setting rather than trusting the dial. CPSC's method: first thing
in the morning, or at least two hours after hot water was last used, hold a
candy or meat thermometer under the running tap.

## The temperature and pressure relief valve

Every storage water heater carries a temperature and pressure relief valve
(T&P valve) near the top of the tank, with a pipe running down from it. If
the thermostat fails and keeps heating, or the pressure climbs, the valve
opens and lets hot water out before the tank fails. The Department of
Housing and Urban Development's inspection standard (NSPIRE, Water Heater,
version 2.1) explains the danger: if the valve is blocked or its discharge
pipe is capped, and the water in the heater goes above its boiling point,
the heater may rupture. It classes that as a severe hazard to be fixed
within 24 hours.

The rules, from HUD's standard and from an International Code Council
article explaining the plumbing codes' requirements (Clifton, 2019):

- **Never cap, plug or valve the discharge pipe.** The codes forbid any
  valve or tee in it, and require that its end have no thread, precisely so
  nobody can screw a cap onto it to stop a drip. The ICC article's point is
  that what looks like an easy repair could cost the building and the lives
  of the people in it.
- **It must run downhill and end low.** The pipe must drain by gravity with
  no upward slope or sag that can hold water, scale or ice. It ends no more
  than 6 inches (15 cm) above the floor, a floor drain or a waste receptor,
  so that a discharge of scalding water goes down rather than onto someone
  standing near. HUD's standard counts an end more than 6 inches or less than
  2 inches (5 cm) above the receptor's flood level as a deficiency.
- **It must not be smaller than the valve's own outlet,** and must serve that
  one valve only.
- **Never block the valve itself** so that it cannot open fully.

**A valve that drips or discharges is telling you something.** The ICC
article says a relief valve should not discharge at all when the heater, the
water pressure and the valve are all working properly, so any discharge
means a problem with one of the three. Portland's Water Bureau notes that the
fix may be a new valve or an expansion tank. Either way it is a job for a
plumber, and the drip is the warning, not the fault.

**A valve that has never opened may not open.** CPSC's 1982 warning about
fire-heated water systems advised checking the safety valve periodically by
triggering it, to be sure it will relieve pressure, and doing it with
caution, by a knowledgeable person, because the water that comes out is
scalding. Follow your own water heater's manual for testing its valve,
and if you are not confident, have a plumber or the technician at the
yearly service do it.

**In earthquake country, secure the tank.** FEMA's Earthquake Safety
Checklist (October 2023) lists water heaters that could be pulled away from
their pipes and rupture among the hazards to find and correct in a home,
along with appliances that could move enough to rupture gas or electrical
lines.

## Carbon monoxide from fuel-burning water heaters

A water heater that burns natural gas, propane or oil makes carbon monoxide
(CO) as well as heat, and relies on its vent or flue to carry the exhaust
outside. CDC calls CO an odourless, colourless gas that kills without
warning. Its most common symptoms are headache, dizziness, weakness, upset
stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, often described as flu-like,
and breathing in a lot of it can make you pass out or kill you. Everyone is at risk;
infants, the elderly and people with chronic heart disease, anaemia or
breathing problems are more likely to get sick from it.

What CDC asks of every home:

- Install battery-powered or battery-backup CO detectors near every sleeping
  area.
- Check their batteries each spring and fall when the clocks change, and
  replace the detector following the maker's instructions or every 5 years.
- Have the heating system, the water heater and any other gas, oil or coal
  burning appliance serviced by a qualified technician every year.
- Horizontal vent pipes should rise slightly as they run toward the outdoors.

**The flue is a life-safety part.** HUD's inspection standard classes a
fuel-fired water heater's chimney or flue pipe that is blocked, misaligned
or missing as life-threatening, because it can let carbon monoxide into the
home. The standard's inspectors look for holes, disconnected joints, tape
covering a gap in the pipe, and a horizontal run that slopes the wrong way.
If you see any of those, or smell exhaust near the heater, stop using it and
call the gas utility or a qualified technician. [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md) explains why a new exhaust fan can pull
exhaust back down a water heater's flue.

**Keep flammable liquids away.** CPSC reported in 2003 that gas water heaters
igniting flammable vapours were involved in nearly 800 home fires a year,
with an average of five deaths and 130 injuries, and that tank-type gas
water heaters made after 1 July 2003 must carry technology to stop the
burner from lighting vapours in the room. Its advice applies to every
heater, new or old: gasoline should never be stored or used indoors, in a
basement or a garage, where its vapours can ignite.

**If the CO alarm sounds, or you suspect carbon monoxide,** CPSC's
instruction is to get outside to fresh air immediately and then call 911.
Do not wait for symptoms: CDC warns that people who are asleep, drunk or
under the influence of other substances can die of it before they have any.
[Firewood](firewood.md) has the full treatment of carbon monoxide, and
[Insulation and Heat Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md) covers the same
danger after air sealing.

## Hot water and drinking water

**Drink and cook from the cold tap.** The EPA's advice on lead is to use
only cold water for drinking, cooking and making baby formula, and it
reminds readers that boiling water does not remove lead.

**Boiling makes water safe from germs.** When a boil water advisory is on,
or the water's source is in doubt:

- CDC: bring clear water to a rolling boil for 1 minute; at elevations above
  6,500 feet (1,980 metres), boil for 3 minutes. Let it cool, and store it
  in clean, sanitised containers with tight covers.
- EPA (page updated 24 February 2026): bring water to a rolling boil for at
  least one minute, and at altitudes above 5,000 feet boil for three
  minutes. (EPA writes the metric figure as 1,000 metres, which is lower than
  5,000 feet, about 1,520 metres; the safe reading is to boil for three
  minutes above either.)

Boiling kills germs, but CDC warns that neither boiling nor disinfecting can
make water safe if it holds fuel, toxic chemicals or radioactive material.
[Making Water Safe to Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md) covers every
method, and what each one does and does not remove.

## Warning signs

- **The CO alarm goes off, or people in the house have CDC's list of
  symptoms: headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest
  pain or confusion.** Get outside, then call 911 (CPSC). Do not go back in
  to find the cause.
- **Water or steam from the relief valve, or the discharge pipe is capped,
  kinked or plugged.** Turn the heater off (the power, or the gas to "off" or
  "pilot") and call a plumber. Do not cap it.
- **A smell of exhaust near the heater, or a flue pipe with a hole, a gap or
  a disconnected joint.** Stop using the heater and have it inspected.
- **A thermometer reading over 120 F at the tap without a mixing valve.**
  Turn the thermostat down and check again after two hours (CPSC).
- **Sediment in the hot water.** New Jersey's health department says
  sediment at the bottom of the tank makes the heater work harder and can
  help bacteria grow, and recommends a plumber for draining and flushing it.

## How the game models it

The homes in this project have real water heaters on their plumbing, and
their numbers come from the sources in this guide.

- **The one-person home** (`data/machines/home_solo.ron`) has a 150 litre
  electric water heater that supplies the kitchen sink, the bathroom sink,
  the shower and the washing machine with 36.8 litres of hot water a day,
  43 percent of that home's 85 litres. Each fixture's hot share is the
  measured share from the Residential End Uses of Water study, as LBNL
  tabled it: 66.2 percent of shower water, 57.0 percent of faucet water and
  20.0 percent of washer water.
- **The family home** (`data/machines/home.ron`) draws 110.3 litres of hot
  water a day for three people, by the same shares.
- **Their energy** is the RECS average for a household of that size that
  heats with electricity: 3.9 kWh a day for one person (1,427 kWh a year)
  and 9.5 kWh a day for three (3,482 kWh a year), and a test in
  `src/machines.rs` holds them to it. The Construction page's power meter
  charges it, and in the family home the water heater is the largest load on
  the home's own supply. The family home's heating element is 2 kW, on a 30
  amp circuit.
- **The gap between the heat and the bill is real.** Warming the family
  home's 110 litres through LBNL's 35.8 C rise is 4.6 kWh of heat, about
  half the RECS figure; the rest is the tank's standby loss and the fact
  that the average US household in RECS draws more hot water than these
  homes do. [Ship Life Support](../../design/ship-life-support.md)
  records the comparison.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: the heater
has no thermostat or setpoint, and draws its daily average steadily around
the clock; there is no scalding, no Legionella and no relief valve, and
nothing in the tank can overheat or burst; every water heater is electric,
so there is no gas, flue or carbon monoxide; there are no heat pump, solar
or tankless heaters to choose between (the Home page lists a heat pump water
heater as a luxury item not yet in the game); there is no heating water on
the stove, and no boiling to make water safe, because drinking water comes
from the water purifier.
