# Rats, Flies, Mosquitoes and Ticks

Some animals live beside people because people feed them by accident.
Mice find the grain bin, rats find the compost, flies find the rubbish,
mosquitoes find the rain barrel and ticks wait in the long grass at the
edge of the lawn. A few of them carry germs that make people ill. The
biologist's word for an animal that carries a germ from one host to
another is a vector, and this guide is about keeping vectors away from
your household.

The aim is not to kill every one. You cannot, and you do not need to. The
aim is to deny them the food, water and shelter they need near your home,
so they do not build up in numbers where you live and eat; then to catch
the few that get through, clean up after them safely, and protect yourself
where you cannot avoid them. That order, prevention first, is the advice
of every source below.

This guide's advice comes from the Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention (CDC), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the Health
Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), which funds the national
Poison Help line, and a Department of Defense technical guide on flies
from the Armed Forces Pest Management Board, cleared for open publication.
All are United States government works, in the public domain. Where
something is general practice or our own reading, the text says so. The
pests and their diseases differ from place to place, so check with your
local or state health department about the ones where you live.

Other guides cover the places where pests start. [What Not to Compost,
Burn or Pour Away](what_not_to_compost_burn_or_pour_away.md) and [Your
First Compost](your_first_compost.md) cover rubbish and the compost heap;
[Collecting Rainwater](collecting_rainwater.md) and [Storing Water
Safely](storing_water_safely.md) cover screening and covering water;
[Keeping Animals](keeping_animals.md) and [Chickens and
Eggs](chickens_and_eggs.md) cover feed and enclosures; and [Handwashing
and Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) is the last line of defence.
Several of the commonest pests are themselves invasive species; that
story is in [Invasive Species](invasive_species.md).

## How pests make people ill

There are three routes, and each one has its own defence.

- **Contact with their droppings, urine and saliva.** The CDC says rodents
  spread disease to people through handling, through contact with their
  droppings, urine or saliva, and through bites, and that their waste can
  spread by breathing in contaminated air or eating contaminated food. Many
  of those diseases make no visible illness in the rodent, so you cannot
  tell a carrier by looking at it. The CDC names infestation in and around
  the home as the main reason disease spreads from rodents to people.
- **Carried on their bodies.** The Defense Department's fly guide calls
  filth flies mechanical vectors: they walk and feed on contaminated
  material such as rubbish, faeces and carrion, then do the same on food.
  Their hairy bodies and sticky feet pick germs up and drop them off, and
  they are especially linked to the germs that cause diarrhoea.
- **Through a bite.** Mosquitoes, ticks and fleas bite and pass germs into
  the blood. The CDC adds that rodents can carry the ticks, mites and fleas
  that do this, so a rodent problem can also be a bite problem.

And one more harm, without any germ: the EPA says droppings and body parts
of cockroaches and other pests can trigger asthma.

The CDC's rule for rodents covers most of the rest of this guide: the best
way to prevent an infestation is to remove their food, their water and the
things that give them shelter.

## Rats and mice

The EPA notes that many rodents native to the United States matter to the
health of grasslands and forests, and feed hawks, foxes and bobcats. The
pests are mostly non-native: the Norway rat, the roof rat and the house
mouse. This section is about keeping those out of your buildings and
stores, not about clearing every rodent from your land.

### Signs that they are there

The CDC says the first signs of rodents usually appear before you see a
rat or a mouse, and names two:

- **Droppings**, pointed at one end and often containing hair, in
  cupboards, drawers and other places out of sight.
- **Gnaw marks**, often near the droppings. Rats and mice gnaw to make
  ways in and to keep their teeth worn down.

To tell whether they are still there, clean the area safely (below) and
look again; new droppings mean they are active.

### Seal up

The CDC says a mouse can fit through a hole the width of a pencil, a
quarter of an inch (6 millimetres). Look inside and out:

- **Inside:** under, inside and behind kitchen cupboards; closet floors,
  especially corners; around the fireplace, windows and doors; behind
  appliances; around pipes under sinks and washing machines and to the
  water heater and furnace; around floor and dryer vents and every
  electrical, water, gas and sewer line; in the attic, the basement or
  crawl space, around floor drains, and where the floor meets the wall.
- **Outside:** in the roof among the rafters, gables, eaves and soffits;
  around windows and doors, especially those without weather stripping;
  between the foundation and the ground; around attic and crawl space
  vents; and around the holes where cables and pipes come in.

Checking the roof line means a ladder, and a fall from one is a far bigger
danger than any mouse. Look from the ground first, set the ladder up as
[Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md) describes (its foot
out a quarter of its working length, on firm level ground), and have
someone with you while you climb (general practice).

Then close them, as the CDC describes: fill small holes with steel wool,
held in place with caulk or spray foam around it; fix larger ones with
lath screen or metal, cement, hardware cloth or metal sheeting, cut to
fit around pipes. Seal outbuildings and garages too.

### Take away the food, water and shelter

The CDC's list, grouped:

- **Indoors.** Keep food in thick plastic, metal or glass containers with
  tight lids. Clean up spilled food at once and wash dishes and cooking
  things soon after use. Put pet food away after use and do not leave
  food or water bowls out overnight. Keep indoor rubbish in thick plastic
  or metal containers with tight lids, wash the containers with soap and
  water often, and take the rubbish out often and regularly.
- **Outdoors.** Use a thick plastic or metal rubbish bin with no holes and
  a tight lid. Keep outdoor cooking areas and grills clean. Keep compost
  bins at least 100 feet from the house. Keep bird feeders away from the
  house, with squirrel guards. Keep grain and animal feed in thick
  plastic or metal containers with tight lids.
- **Shelter.** Move woodpiles 100 feet or more from the house and raise
  the wood at least a foot off the ground. Get rid of abandoned vehicles
  or move them more than 100 feet away. Clear brush and weeds, and keep
  grass and shrubs within 100 feet of the house trimmed.

On a small lot, 100 feet may be impossible. Do what you can; farther and
tidier is better than nearer and cluttered (our reading of the CDC's
list).

### Trap up

When you see signs, the CDC says to find and seal how they got in, then
trap the ones still inside:

- **Use snap traps.** The CDC recommends traditional snap traps, the right
  size for mice or for rats. **Do not use glue traps or live traps**: they
  frighten the rodent, which then urinates, raising your chance of getting
  sick.
- **Bait and place them well.** A small amount of chunky peanut butter on
  the bait pan works well. Put the trap on the floor against the wall with
  the baited end touching the wall, making a T, because rodents run along
  walls. Place traps where you have seen droppings, nesting material,
  nibbled food or gnaw marks: behind the stove and the refrigerator, at
  the back of cupboards and drawers, in attics, basements, crawl spaces
  and outbuildings.
- **Pre-bait for rats.** Rats are wary of anything new. Bait the traps
  without setting them until the bait is being taken, then set them.
- **Keep at it.** Check traps every day and dispose of any dead rodent at
  once (safely, below). Check the bait every week for at least 15 days.
  The CDC says that if nothing is caught for a week and there are no new
  signs, they are gone.
- **Keep traps and bait out of reach of children and pets.** Set a snap
  trap with your fingers clear of the bar (general practice).

### Poison is the last resort

The CDC says to use poison or bait stations only when an infestation
persists, and to use EPA-registered products. If you do:

- **Rat and mouse poison for households comes only as a bait station.**
  The EPA says the products sold in ordinary shops are ready-to-use bait
  stations that hold the bait, a block or a paste, or come packed with it.
  It also says the second-generation anticoagulants, which are more likely
  than the older kinds to kill after a single feeding and stay longer in
  the bodies of animals that eat poisoned rodents, are no longer registered
  for household products. That does not make household bait harmless: the
  stations sold to households hold bromethalin, chlorophacinone or
  diphacinone, and the EPA says the poisons that are not anticoagulants,
  bromethalin among them, are each "toxic in other ways".
- **Follow the label, and place stations only where children and pets
  cannot reach them.** The EPA also says to store pesticides out of reach
  of children and pets, in a locked cabinet or shed, never next to food,
  pet food included.
- **Remove dead rodents promptly**, so that pets and wildlife do not eat
  them, as the EPA advises.
- **If someone swallows poison**, the EPA's advice is to call 911 if they
  are unconscious, having trouble breathing or having convulsions, and
  otherwise to read the label's first aid and call Poison Help at
  1-800-222-1222. If a pet eats bait, call a vet at once, with the
  package in hand (general practice).

### Cleaning up after them, safely

This is the part most people do wrong, and the part that matters most for
your health. The CDC says diseases spread from rodents to people mainly
when people breathe in contaminated air, and that sweeping or vacuuming
droppings throws tiny droplets carrying viruses into the air. So:

**Never sweep or vacuum rodent droppings, urine or nests.**

The CDC's method:

1. **Air it first.** For a shed, cabin, barn or closed room, open the
   doors and windows for 30 minutes before you start, and stay out while
   it airs.
2. **Make a disinfectant.** Use a household product whose label says
   "disinfectant", or mix a bleach solution fresh: 1.5 cups of household
   bleach in 1 gallon of water (the CDC also gives it as 1 part bleach to
   9 parts water). **Never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner**:
   the CDC says that makes a poisonous gas, and HRSA's advice is that
   bleach should be mixed with nothing but water.
3. **Put on rubber or plastic gloves.**
4. **Soak it.** Spray the droppings and urine until very wet and leave for
   5 minutes, or as long as the disinfectant's label says.
5. **Wipe up with paper towels** and put them in a covered rubbish bin
   that is emptied regularly.
6. **Mop or sponge the area** with disinfectant, then clean the hard
   surfaces nearby: floors, counters, cupboards and drawers.
7. **Wash your gloved hands** with soap and water or disinfectant, take the
   gloves off, then wash your hands with soap and warm water.

**A dead rodent or a nest:** spray it and the area around it with
disinfectant and leave for 5 minutes; put it in a plastic bag with any
used trap you are throwing away; knot the bag, put it inside a second bag
and knot that; and put it in a covered bin that is emptied regularly.
Rodents often carry fleas, so the CDC suggests insect repellent, such as
DEET or another EPA-registered repellent, on clothes, shoes and hands
while you do this. Bedding, clothing and soft toys go through a hot wash
and a hot dryer or a line in the sun.

**A vehicle that has stood unused:** the CDC says to open the bonnet, the
doors and the boot and let it air for 20 minutes, to wear plastic gloves
and a long-sleeved shirt, to take the cables off the battery to avoid a
shock before inspecting the engine compartment, and to use no vacuum or
pressure washer on droppings until they have been disinfected. Take the
negative (black, minus) cable off first and put it back last, so a
spanner touching the metal body cannot make a spark (general practice);
the CDC says to reconnect the battery only once the area is dry. Check the
air intake and filter before starting the engine; a nest there means a
new filter. If you are not sure of any of this, the CDC's advice is to
consult a qualified mechanic.

### What they can give you

- **Hantavirus.** The CDC says hantaviruses spread mainly from rodents to
  people, usually by breathing air contaminated with their urine,
  droppings or nest material, and that the one causing most hantavirus
  lung disease in the United States is spread by the deer mouse. The first
  symptoms, 1 to 8 weeks after contact, are tiredness, fever and muscle
  aches, especially in the thighs, hips and back; 4 to 10 days later
  come coughing and shortness of breath. The CDC says 38 percent of people
  who develop the breathing symptoms may die. If you fall ill after
  cleaning up after rodents, see a health care provider and tell them
  about the rodents, as the CDC asks.
- **Leptospirosis.** The CDC says these bacteria spread in the urine of
  infected animals and can survive in water or soil for weeks to months,
  with the risk often rising after floods. People catch it from
  contaminated water or soil, from animals' body fluids, or from food or
  water contaminated with urine. Symptoms start 2 to 30 days later and
  include fever, aches, headache, vomiting, diarrhoea, red eyes and
  yellowed skin; see a health care provider right away if you think you
  have it.
- **Plague, where it occurs.** In the parts of the country where plague
  is found, the CDC says people usually catch it from the bite of an
  infected rodent flea or from handling an infected animal. Its advice:
  clear rodent habitat around the home, wear gloves to handle or skin
  animals that might be infected, use repellent against fleas outdoors,
  keep fleas off pets with flea control products, and do not let dogs or
  cats that roam free in those areas sleep on your bed.

## Flies

### Where they come from

The Defense Department's fly guide (Technical Guide 30) describes the
house fly, usually the commonest fly in homes. Females lay eggs in
manure, carrion, decaying fruit and vegetables, kitchen waste, the
drainage from rubbish piles and cesspool material. Each female lays 4 to 6
batches of 100 to 150 eggs in her life. Around 25 C (77 F), eggs hatch in
18 to 24 hours, and under the best conditions adults come out of their
pupal cases in 4 to 5 days; in poor conditions the cycle takes weeks.

Two details from the guide shape everything else:

- **Moisture is critical, and finished material is useless to them.** The
  guide says material that has completed decomposing is not suitable for
  the maggots. So a finished compost does not breed flies, while wet,
  fresh rubbish and manure do (our reading of the guide).
- **Flies travel.** The guide calls house flies very strong fliers, with
  flies recaptured up to 5 miles from where they were released within 24
  hours in one study and dispersal of up to 20 miles documented in
  another. Flies at your table may have bred somewhere else, which is why
  a neighbourhood's rubbish is everybody's business.

### Break the breeding

The guide's rules for field sanitation translate directly to a household:
store rubbish in fly-proof containers until it is collected, and empty or
service rubbish containers and toilets often enough to keep flies from
being drawn to them and breeding in them. It says sanitation is the key to
long-term control, and that pesticides alone are not sustainable.

At home that means (general practice, built on the guide): bins with
tight lids, emptied at least weekly in warm weather and rinsed out; food
scraps wrapped or sealed before they go in; pet and livestock manure
picked up or composted properly rather than left in wet heaps; and a
compost heap managed so it heats and finishes, with meat, fish and dairy
kept out ([Your First Compost](your_first_compost.md)).

### Keep them off food and out of the house

- **Screens on windows and doors**, kept in repair, are one of the CDC's
  defences against mosquitoes, and they keep flies out too (general
  practice for flies).
- **Cover food**, especially outdoors (general practice).
- **Traps and swatters.** The fly guide recommends light traps with glue
  boards for indoors, mounted out of drafts, less than 3 feet above the
  floor and out of sight of windows and doors, with the glue boards
  changed at least monthly and the bulbs at least twice a year. It says
  insect-zapping electric grids are not recommended near food, because the
  insects can burst and scatter fragments, possibly with germs, onto the
  food below. And it has a kind word for the fly swatter.
- **Insecticide baits** belong outdoors only, around bins, and only as the
  label directs; the guide says pesticides are a backup to sanitation, not
  a replacement.

## Mosquitoes

### Take away the water

Mosquitoes lay their eggs in or near water, and the CDC's routine is
weekly:

- **Once a week, empty and scrub, turn over, cover or throw out** anything
  that holds water: tyres, buckets, planters, toys, paddling pools,
  birdbaths, plant pot saucers and rubbish bins, indoors as well as out.
- **Tightly cover water storage**, such as buckets, cisterns and rain
  barrels, so mosquitoes cannot get in to lay eggs, and use wire mesh with
  holes smaller than an adult mosquito on containers without lids.
  [Collecting Rainwater](collecting_rainwater.md) covers screening a
  barrel.
- **Fill tree holes**, repair cracks in a septic tank, and cover open vent
  and plumbing pipes with fine mesh.
- **Larvicides** are for large bodies of water that will not be used for
  drinking and cannot be covered or emptied, and only as the label says.

The rhythm is the point: once a week, every week, through the warm
months, not once when you happen to notice wrigglers (our reading of the
CDC's routine).

### Keep them off you

The CDC's advice:

- **Use an EPA-registered insect repellent** with DEET, picaridin, IR3535,
  oil of lemon eucalyptus (OLE), para-menthane-diol (PMD) or 2-undecanone.
  Look for the EPA registration number on the label. Used as directed,
  the CDC says these are proven safe and effective, even for pregnant and
  breastfeeding women. It says the effectiveness of repellents that are
  not EPA-registered, including some "natural" ones, is unknown.
- **Follow the label, and reapply as it directs.** Put sunscreen on first
  and repellent second. Do not put repellent on skin under clothing.
- **Children:** do not use products with OLE or PMD on children under 3.
  Do not put repellent on a child's hands, eyes, mouth, cuts or irritated
  skin; spray it on your own hands and apply it to the child's face. Dress
  children in clothes that cover arms and legs and cover strollers and
  carriers with mosquito netting.
- **Clothing:** loose, long-sleeved shirts and long trousers. Clothing and
  gear treated with permethrin keeps working through several washes, but
  **never put permethrin on skin.**
- **The house:** screens on windows and doors, repaired; doors kept shut,
  including the garage door; air conditioning if you have it.

### Why it matters

The CDC calls West Nile virus the leading cause of mosquito-borne disease
in the contiguous United States, and every state in it has reported
cases. Most infected people have no symptoms; about 2,000 people a year
are diagnosed, which the CDC says is an undercount, and more than 1,300 a
year develop severe illness affecting the nervous system, of whom more
than 130 die. The season runs from summer into autumn, typically June to
October, peaking in late August and early September. There is no vaccine
and no medicine for it; the CDC says the best protection is preventing
bites.

## Ticks

### Where and when

The CDC says ticks live in grassy, brushy or wooded areas and on animals,
that many people pick them up in their own yard, and that while ticks can
bite year-round they are most active from April to September.

### Before and after going out

- **Treat clothes and gear** with products containing 0.5 percent
  permethrin, which lasts through several washes, or buy them treated.
- **Use an EPA-registered repellent** (the same list as for mosquitoes,
  with the same rule against OLE and PMD for children under 3).
- **Walk in the middle of trails** and avoid brush, high grass and leaf
  litter.
- **When you come in:** check your clothes; tumble dry clothes on high heat
  for 10 minutes to kill ticks on dry clothing (longer if damp; if they
  need washing first, use hot water, because the CDC says cold and medium
  water will not kill ticks). Check your pets, coats and daypacks.
- **Shower soon**, within two hours; the CDC says that has been shown to
  reduce the risk of Lyme disease, and it is a chance to look.
- **Check your whole body**, with a mirror, and your children's: under the
  arms, in and around the ears, inside the belly button, behind the knees,
  in and around the hair, between the legs and around the waist.

### Removing a tick

The CDC's instructions, and they are simple:

1. **Remove it as soon as possible.** Do not wait to see a health care
   provider to do it; waiting can raise the risk of disease.
2. **Grasp it as close to the skin as you can** with clean, fine-tipped
   tweezers (regular tweezers or fingers if that is all you have, without
   squeezing the tick's body).
3. **Pull it away with steady, even pressure.** Do not twist or jerk it,
   which can leave the mouthparts in the skin. If that happens, try to
   lift them out with the tweezers; if you cannot easily, leave them, and
   the skin will push them out as it heals.
4. **Clean the bite and your hands** with soap and water, rubbing alcohol
   or hand sanitiser.
5. **Dispose of the tick**: in a sealed container, wrapped tightly in tape,
   flushed down the toilet or put in alcohol. Never crush it with your
   fingers.
6. **Look for more.** One tick often means others.

**Do not** use petroleum jelly, heat, nail polish or anything else to make
a tick let go; the CDC says that can agitate it and push infected fluid
into the skin. And it does not recommend having the tick tested.

**If you get a rash or a fever within several weeks** of removing a tick,
see a doctor, and tell them about the bite, when it happened and where you
probably picked up the tick. Writing the date down on the day is how you
will remember (general practice; [Keeping Records](keeping_records.md)).

### Making the yard less friendly to ticks

The CDC passes on landscaping steps from the Connecticut Agricultural
Experiment Station for reducing blacklegged ticks around a home: remove
leaf litter; clear tall grass and brush around the house and at the edge
of the lawn; put a 3-foot-wide strip of wood chips or gravel between lawn
and woods; mow often; stack wood neatly in a dry place, which also
discourages rodents; keep play equipment, decks and patios away from the
yard edges and trees; fence out deer, raccoons and stray dogs; and remove
old furniture, mattresses and rubbish where ticks can hide. Pesticides can
cut tick numbers in a treated yard, but the CDC says you should not rely
on spraying to protect yourself, and to check with local health or
agricultural officials before using them.

## Cockroaches

The EPA says cockroaches are common in crowded cities and in the southern
United States, and that proteins in their droppings and saliva can cause
allergic reactions or trigger asthma. Its advice is the same food, water
and shelter rule: keep counters, sinks, tables and floors clean and
uncluttered; clean dishes, crumbs and spills right away; store food in
airtight containers; and seal cracks and openings around and inside
cupboards. It also warns that insecticides are toxic to people as well as
pests, and to prefer methods that carry less risk.

## Worked example: a walk round the homestead

Twice a year, and once a week in summer for the water, walk round with a
notebook (the schedule is our arrangement; each check comes from a source
above):

| Where | Check | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pantry and kitchen | Food in thick plastic, metal or glass with tight lids; no spills; pet bowls not left overnight | CDC |
| Bins | Thick plastic or metal, no holes, tight lids, washed; emptied often | CDC, fly guide |
| Outside walls | Gaps a pencil could pass sealed with steel wool and caulk, or metal | CDC |
| Feed store | Grain and feed in thick plastic or metal with tight lids | CDC |
| Woodpile | Well away from the house, a foot off the ground | CDC |
| Compost | Away from the house; no meat, fish or dairy | CDC, [Your First Compost](your_first_compost.md) |
| Anything that holds water | Emptied and scrubbed weekly; barrels covered and screened | CDC |
| Window and door screens | No holes | CDC |
| Lawn edge | Mown; leaf litter cleared; chip or gravel strip at the woods | CDC |
| Signs | Droppings, gnawing, fly maggots, wrigglers in water | CDC |

Write down what you found and what you did. A log tells you whether the
mice are coming back in the same corner each autumn, which is the corner
to seal properly ([Keeping Records](keeping_records.md)).

## Where your own work stops

Prevention, a few traps, safe small cleanups and protecting yourself are
yours. Hand over:

- **Heavy infestations, empty buildings full of rodents, and rodents in
  the heating or cooling ducts.** The CDC says special precautions apply
  to these: coveralls, boots, gloves, goggles and a respirator with HEPA
  filters, worn only after the fit testing local and state rules require.
  That is a trained worker's kit, not a household's. The CDC also says
  rodents in ventilation systems are a job for a professional
  exterminating service, and suggests a pest control specialist when
  rodents are in places you cannot reach to clean.
- **Choosing a professional.** The EPA's advice: choose carefully, ask to
  see the company's licence, and call your state pesticide regulatory
  agency if you have concerns.
- **Professional-grade poisons.** The EPA says the second-generation
  anticoagulants are registered only for commercial and structural pest
  control, and that baits made for professional and agricultural users
  are not to be sold in consumer stores.
- **Illness.** Anyone who falls ill after cleaning up rodent waste, or
  with a rash or fever after a tick bite, needs a health care provider,
  told about the exposure.
- **Plague country, dead animals and anything you are unsure of.** The CDC
  sends you to your local or state health department.

## Traps

- **Sweeping or vacuuming droppings.** It puts the germs in the air you
  breathe. Soak, wait, wipe.
- **Glue traps and live traps.** The CDC advises against both.
- **Mixing bleach with anything but water.**
- **Poison where a child or a dog can reach it**, or left in place of
  sealing the holes.
- **"I'll empty the bucket next week."** Weekly is the whole rhythm.
- **A bug zapper over the picnic table.**
- **Twisting a tick, or burning or painting it off.**
- **Leaving dead rodents** where a pet or a hawk will eat them.
- **Treating pests as one person's problem.** Flies travel miles, and a
  neighbour's woodpile is your rats' home too (our reading of the fly
  guide).

## How the game models it

The game has no rats, mice, flies, mosquitoes, ticks or cockroaches, and
nothing carries a disease to you.

- **No vermin.** None of these animals is in the game's creature list
  (`data/creatures.csv`), and nothing eats your stored food. Food spoils
  on its own clock instead, with no cold storage yet.
- **Diseases are listed but never given.** The game's effects data
  (`data/status_effects.csv`) has rows for plague, parasites, infection
  and flu, but nothing in the game applies them yet. The one illness you
  can catch is food poisoning, from spoiled or some raw foods.
- **Your waste is the one sanitation mechanic.** On the Inventory page,
  the Waste reading rises slowly while you live, filling in about three
  days of game time, and each solid meal adds to it. Above 75 you get the
  Unsanitary effect, which makes you 5 percent slower. The "Compost and
  draw off urine" button turns your waste into bags of fertiliser and
  empties it. The Vitals drain slider in Settings > Gameplay slows hunger,
  thirst and tiredness, but not the Waste reading.
- **Garden pests are a different thing.** Aphids, whiteflies, thrips,
  slugs and the rest in `data/garden/pests.ron` attack your crops, never
  you; [Pests and Disease](pests_and_disease.md) covers them.

**The default settings.** The Dev play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play
mode) does not change any of this; your waste rises in every mode. The
Waste reading is not saved, so it starts again from empty each launch, and
while "Start every session from the default home" is on, which it is by
default during development, only your character carries over, so the
fertiliser in your pack does not.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the
mouse in the grain bin, the maggots in the rubbish, the rain barrel full
of wrigglers, the tick on the back of the knee, and the slow work of
sealing, storing, emptying and checking that keeps them away. In a real
home, those are where most of the household's disease risk lives.

## You own this when

- You can say how rodents, flies and biting insects each make people ill,
  and which defence goes with each.
- Your food, feed and rubbish are in thick plastic, metal or glass with
  tight lids, and you know where every gap a pencil could pass through has
  been sealed.
- You use snap traps, not glue traps, set against the wall, checked daily.
- You never sweep or vacuum droppings: you air the space, soak with
  disinfectant for 5 minutes, wipe with paper towels, double-bag dead
  rodents, and wash your hands, and you never mix bleach with anything but
  water.
- Any poison you use is in a bait station, out of reach of children and
  pets, and you remove dead rodents promptly.
- Your bins are fly-proof and emptied often, and your compost heap
  finishes rather than festers.
- You empty, scrub, turn over or cover anything that holds water once a
  week, and your rain barrel is covered and screened.
- You use an EPA-registered repellent by its label, keep OLE and PMD off
  children under 3, and put permethrin on clothes, never skin.
- You check for ticks after being outside, shower within two hours, and
  can remove one with tweezers the CDC's way.
- You know when to call a professional, and to tell a provider about a
  tick bite or rodent cleanup if you fall ill.
