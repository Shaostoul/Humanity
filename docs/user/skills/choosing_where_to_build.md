# Choosing Where to Build

Almost everything about a building can be fixed later. A cold house can
be insulated. A leaking roof can be replaced. A bad layout can be
rearranged, a small house extended, an ugly one resurfaced, a damp one
dried out. All of that costs money, and money is the only thing it
costs.

The site is different. You cannot move a house off a failing bluff. You
cannot lift it out of a floodway. You cannot turn a north-facing slope
to the south, or drain a spring that runs under the foundation, or make
the county reclassify the ground. You can spend enormous sums making a
bad site tolerable, and people do, and at the end of it they still have
a bad site with an expensive house on it.

That is the whole reason this guide exists. Siting is the one decision
in building that is close to permanent, and it is usually made fastest,
on the least information, by the person with the least expertise, under
the most time pressure. It is made by whoever liked the view.

So this is about slowing that decision down and putting it in order.

**You may not have a choice.** Most people do not. You inherited the
land, you bought the only lot you could afford, you rent, you are
building on the corner of a family property, or the house is already
there and has been for sixty years. If that is you, none of the
following is wasted: it becomes the list of things to find out about
ground you are already standing on. Knowing that the till is at two feet
does not move your house, but it explains your wet crawlspace, tells you
why the drainfield struggles, and stops you spending real money on the
wrong fix. Read it as a diagnosis rather than a choice.

The worked example throughout is Silverdale, Washington, at the head of
Dyes Inlet on the Kitsap Peninsula, because there is real sourced data
behind every number. The method is the part that transfers.

## Ask the questions in order of how permanent the answer is

This ordering is a **rule of thumb**, not a standard anyone published.
But the logic is hard to argue with: each question below is harder to
undo than the one after it, so answering them out of order means you can
fall in love with a site for a reason that does not survive contact with
the reason above it.

| # | Question | If the answer is bad | Reversible? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Can the ground hold it? | The site is not a building site | No |
| 2 | Will water reach it? | The site is not a building site | Almost never |
| 3 | Can you get water, and get rid of waste? | Possibly unbuildable, certainly expensive | Sometimes, at real cost |
| 4 | Can you get to it, in February? | Expensive forever, and a safety problem | Sometimes, at real cost |
| 5 | Is it a pleasant place to live? | You will be a bit colder and a bit gloomier | Partly, with design |

People do this in reverse. They start at 5, because 5 is what you can
see from the road. The view, the trees, the quiet, the way the light
falls in June. Those are real goods and they are the last thing to
weigh, because a beautiful site that fails question 1 is not a
compromise, it is a different kind of object entirely.

Two of these questions have a specific character worth naming in
advance. Questions 1 and 2 are answered by the ground and cannot be
negotiated with. Questions 3, 4 and 5 are answered by money, and the
honest form of each is not "can I?" but "how much, forever?"

## 1. Can the ground hold it?

### Slope, and the hazard that is actually here

The famous geologic hazard on this coast is the Cascadia megathrust
earthquake. The everyday one is the coastal bluff landslide, and for a
siting decision the everyday one is the one that should move you. USGS
puts a magnitude 9 on Cascadia at 10 to 15 percent in the next 50 years.
Landslide-producing winters here recur on a scale of years.

The numbers are from a USGS study that mapped landslides across Kitsap
County using lidar. It found **231 landslides**, covering about **0.8
percent of the county's land area**, and the distribution is the part
that matters for siting: **67 percent of the mapped landslide area lies
within 500 metres of the present coastline**, and about **77 percent of
mapped landslides involve Vashon advance outwash deposits**. Two mapped
slides sit directly on the Dyes Inlet shoreline near Silverdale, one
pair on the east shore and northern end of the inlet totalling about
32,000 square metres with possible evidence of recent activity, and one
of about 9,000 square metres west of the inlet, north of Chico.

This is not a theoretical hazard. In the winter of 1996 to 1997, coastal
landslides in Kitsap County destroyed millions of dollars of public and
private property and **killed a family of four on Bainbridge Island**.
NOAA's storm record for the Kitsap Peninsula zones logs 10 debris-flow
events since 1996, including a 2007 fatality and a December 2010 episode
causing about a million dollars of damage.

**The mechanism explains the map.** The Kitsap Peninsula is mantled by
late Pleistocene glacial deposits. The critical arrangement is permeable
advance outwash sand sitting on the low-permeability Lawton Clay.
Rainwater sinks freely through the sand, reaches the clay, cannot get
through it, and perches. Pore pressure builds through a sustained wet
spell, and the bluff face lets go. Washington's Department of Ecology
describes the same pattern in plain terms for property owners: most
landslides happen after heavy rainfall or rapid snowmelt saturates the
soil, and loose sand and gravel lying over dense silt and clay is one of
the conditions that raises the risk.

Three things follow, and all three are siting decisions.

**The top of a bluff and the toe of a bluff are both hazardous, and for
different reasons.** On top, the ground you are standing on is what
leaves. Below, you are where it lands. Ecology's guidance for Puget
Sound bluff owners is to build a prudent distance from the top or the
bottom of a steep slope, and it declines to put a number on "prudent",
which is the correct thing for a general document to do.

**Nothing you do to the house changes the slope, but plenty of things
you do to the site change the water.** Ecology is specific that drainage
improvements are the most cost-effective way to reduce slide risk, that
septic drainfields and irrigation systems placed between a house and the
bluff edge make instability worse, that roof runoff should be piped away
from the slope rather than discharged near it, that clearing upland
vegetation creates excess runoff, and that native vegetation helps
anchor soil and absorb water. Every one of those is a decision you make
at siting time and live with afterward.

**And the bluff stays dangerous after the shaking stops.** The April
1949 deep earthquake triggered a large landslide on 90-metre-high bluffs
at the Tacoma Narrows **three days later**, and that slide generated its
own local wave. Surviving the shaking is not the same as surviving the
earthquake.

### What a map can and cannot tell you about a slope

Two federal products are worth knowing apart, because people use one
when they need the other.

USGS says a **landslide hazard map** indicates the possibility of
landslides occurring throughout a given area, and that a **landslide
susceptibility map** describes the relative likelihood of future
landsliding based solely on the intrinsic properties of a locale or
site. Note "relative" and "solely on the intrinsic properties". A
susceptibility map tells you that this kind of ground fails more often
than that kind of ground. It does not tell you what your ground will do.

USGS also notes that an ideal hazard map would show not only the chance
that a landslide starts at a place, but the chance that it travels
downslope a given distance. Most available maps do not show the second
thing, which is exactly the thing that matters if you are at the toe.

A **landslide inventory**, like the Kitsap study above, is different
again and more useful: it is a record of failures somebody actually
found and mapped. If your parcel is inside a mapped landslide polygon,
that is not a probability, it is a history.

### The honest limit of this section

A document cannot tell you whether a particular slope will hold a
particular house. Neither can a map, a neighbour, a contractor, or the
person selling you the land.

Ready.gov's instruction is blunt and correct: seek advice from a
geotechnical expert for evaluating landslide hazards or designing
corrective techniques to reduce landslide risk. Ecology says the same
for this coast specifically, and adds the qualifier that matters, which
is to find an engineering geologist or geotechnical engineer familiar
with **coastal slopes**. Bluff behaviour here is a specialism, not a
general civil engineering question.

A geotechnical investigation on a residential lot is not cheap. It is
also, by a wide margin, the cheapest thing on this page relative to what
it prevents, and it is the one expense in this guide that is
unambiguously worth paying for if there is any slope in the picture at
all. If the answer comes back ambiguous, that is an answer.

### The ground itself, apart from the slope

Slope is not the only way ground fails to hold a building. The soil
survey rates ground for exactly this, and
[Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) is the guide to
reading it. Three of its findings are siting findings rather than
gardening findings.

**Dense till at two feet is the character of this place.** About 64
percent of the mapped land around Silverdale is a thin gravelly mantle
over dense glacial till. Alderwood alone is 49.9 percent of it, with its
restriction at a representative 56 cm and a mapped range of 50 to 102
cm, and a seasonal perched water table as shallow as 65 cm. The survey
rates Alderwood **Very limited** for dwellings with basements, for
perched ground water at 75 to 180 cm plus slope on the steeper phases,
and Very limited for shallow excavations for wetness and the dense layer
itself. The binding constraint in Central Kitsap is not bedrock. Only
one series in the whole area, Schneider at 0.3 percent, has a lithic
contact at all.

**Wet ground announces itself in the ratings and nowhere else on a
sunny day.** Norma, 6.1 percent of the land, is a hydric soil with an
apparent water table at the surface and winter ponding, rated Very
limited for dwellings with and without basements, for local roads and
streets, for septic, for small commercial buildings and for shallow
excavations. It looks like the best ground on the property: dark, deep,
7.5 percent organic matter. In February it is a pond.

**Some ground moves or disappears under you.** Shalcar is muck and
Mukilteo is peat, and the survey rates both for more than 30 to 60 cm of
total subsidence: drain them and the ground surface drops by half a
metre as the organic material oxidises. Bellingham is a vertic silty
clay that shrinks and swells, rated Very limited for dwellings for
shrink-swell, wetness and ponding over four hours, and Somewhat limited
to Very limited for local roads for low strength. Tacoma is tidally
influenced low ground at the heads of the inlets that floods, ponds,
runs pH 4.5 to 5.5 and carries sulfidic material. The locale data's
grower note on Tacoma is three words long: leave it alone.

And one category that means the survey is declining to answer. **Urban
land** is not a soil. It is pavement, buildings and engineered fill, and
the survey carries no interpretations for it, which is honesty rather
than unhelpfulness. On any parcel that has been built on, graded or
filled, assume the top layer is not what the survey describes and that
the only way to find out is to dig.

## 2. Will water reach it?

Water arrives from three directions, and a site can be fine in two of
them and ruined by the third. Take them in order: what falls on it, what
flows across it, and what sits under it.
[Where Water Goes](where_water_goes.md) is the full treatment of the
mechanism; this is the siting subset.

### What falls on it

Silverdale gets about 1,446 mm of rain a year at the nearest station
with a full record, and the distribution is the design problem: December
brings 252 mm, July brings 21 mm, and just under 80 percent of the
year's water falls between October and March. County-wide the swing
between years is nearly twofold, from 818.6 mm in 2013 to 1,544.6 mm in
1999.

For siting, the useful consequence is that **every drainage question
here is a winter question and every water-supply question is a summer
question**, and a site visit in one season tells you nothing about the
other. A lot inspected in August is a lot you have not seen.

The other consequence is volume. A roof concentrates rain that used to
fall over the whole footprint into a few discharge points, and where
those points discharge is a siting decision made at the same moment as
the building's position. On a bluff lot, it is the siting decision.

### What flows across it

This is the one you can see, and only in the rain.

Water arriving from uphill does not care about your property line. A
site can be perfectly drained on its own and still receive the runoff of
five acres above it, especially if the ground above was recently cleared
or paved. Ecology names upland vegetation clearing as one of the
conditions that raises landslide risk here, for exactly this reason.

Look for: a swale or shallow channel crossing the lot, a culvert
discharging above you, a road ditch that ends nowhere in particular, a
neighbour's downspouts, bare scoured soil, silt fans at the bottom of a
grade, and the line where grass changes species. All of that is a record
of where water has been going, written in the ground.

### What sits under it

The perched water table is the local signature. Over half the land here
sits on dense till with water sitting on top of it through the wet
season, at 46 to 75 cm depending on the series. A crawlspace or footing
excavated to that depth in August is dry; the same hole in February is
in the water table.

There is also the water that arrives sideways underground and surfaces
as a **seep or spring**. This is the classic ruinous surprise, because
it is invisible for eight months of the year and it does not appear on
any map. A spring under or above a building is not a drainage
inconvenience, it is a permanent pressure source in the exact place you
least want one, and on a bluff lot it is the mechanism of failure.

Signs, in the wet season: rushes, sedges, skunk cabbage or willow in a
line or a patch; ground that stays green in a dry August while
everything around it browns off; a wet spot on a slope with no channel
feeding it; water in a test hole that rises after you stop digging.

**Rule of thumb:** if you can only visit a site once, visit it in the
wettest week you can arrange, in the rain, on foot. Everything in this
section is visible then and invisible in summer.

## The mapped flood zone, and the water that actually arrives

These are two different things and conflating them is the single most
common flood mistake.

### What the map says

FEMA's National Flood Hazard Layer is the starting point and it is free.
For Silverdale it returns a clear picture: the waterfront at the head of
Dyes Inlet is **Zone AE with a base flood elevation of 14 feet**, the
Chico Creek mouth is AE with a BFE of 13 feet, the Barker Creek mouth is
**AE FLOODWAY**, and the built-up upland, including the census-designated
place centroid, Clear Creek at Silverdale Way, and Bucklin Hill, is
**Zone X, minimal flood hazard**. The panels covering Silverdale are
DFIRM 53035C at 1:6000, effective 2010-11-04 and 2017-02-03.

The vocabulary is precise and worth learning. A **Special Flood Hazard
Area** is, in FEMA's words, the area that will be inundated by the flood
event having a 1-percent chance of being equaled or exceeded in any
given year. **Shaded Zone X** is the band between the limit of that
flood and the 0.2-percent-annual-chance flood. **Unshaded Zone X** is
outside the SFHA and above the 0.2-percent flood elevation. "100-year
flood" means the 1-percent zone and is a worse name for it, because it
sounds like a timetable and it is an annual probability.

**Floodway** is the sharpest word on the map. It is the part of the
channel and adjacent land that has to stay clear to carry the flood.
Barker Creek's mouth is in one. Building in a floodway is a different
regulatory conversation from building in an AE zone, and the answer is
usually no.

### What the map does not say

FEMA states it plainly: **there is no such thing as a "no-risk zone"**,
and heavy rains, poor drainage, and even nearby construction projects
can put you at risk for flood damage. Ready.gov puts the same point the
other way round: flooding does not follow lines on a map. Being outside
the zone is not the same as being safe, it is the same as being
unmapped.

Three specific reasons the map understates a particular lot:

**It maps the sources it studied.** Riverine and coastal flooding are
studied. Local ponding behind a plugged culvert, sheet flow off a newly
cleared hillside, a blocked roadside ditch and a failed detention pond
generally are not. FEMA's own summary of what it cannot map for you is
short and blunt: heavy rains, poor drainage and nearby construction
projects can put you at risk. That gap is worth more attention here than
in most places, because there is no large river on this peninsula, so
the flooding the locale data records is localised rather than
catastrophic. Concluding from that that the unmapped kind is the kind
most houses here actually meet is our inference, not FEMA's.

**It is a snapshot of a watershed that keeps changing.** FEMA says
flood risk is always changing, and names nearby construction as one of
the things that changes it. The Silverdale panels became effective in
2010 and 2017, and the study behind a panel predates the panel. A
watershed that has been built on since is not the watershed the
elevations were computed for, and the map does not update itself.

**The zone boundary is a line on a 1:6000 map, and your lot is a point.**
The same caution the soil survey prints about its own scale applies
here. At the edge of a zone, the line is an interpretation, not a
measurement of your ground.

So use the map as the first gate, not the last word. If it puts you in a
zone, that is dispositive and you should believe it. If it puts you out
of one, you have learned that the studied sources do not reach you, and
you still have to answer the local-drainage question yourself, in the
rain, with your own eyes.

## The tide, for anything near the shoreline

Two things make a shoreline site different from an ordinary low-lying
one, and both are about reference levels.

### Which zero is your number measured from?

An elevation is meaningless without its datum. NOAA distinguishes the
two families you will meet: geodetic vertical datums are generally used
to express land elevations, while water level datums are a slightly
different vertical datum, used as the reference to which bathymetric
soundings on nautical charts are referenced. A surveyor's elevation, a
base flood elevation and a tide prediction may all be quoted in feet and
none of them need share a zero. NOAA publishes a tool, VDatum, whose
whole purpose is converting elevation data from various sources into a
common reference system, and the fact that such a tool has to exist is
the point.

Practically: **before you compare any two elevations on a shoreline
site, make somebody tell you the datum of each.** Ask the surveyor in
writing. A half-metre error here is not a rounding problem, it is the
difference between a finished floor above the base flood elevation and
one below it.

[Reading the Tide](reading_the_tide.md) covers the datum ladder in full,
including why mean lower low water is the United States chart zero and
why predicted heights can legitimately be negative.

### The range here is large, and everything rides on top of it

At the reference station for this inlet the **great diurnal range**,
from mean higher high water to mean lower low water, is about **11.72
feet**, and the mean range of tide is **7.97 feet**. That is a large
swing, and it means the same shoreline elevation is well underwater or
well clear depending on the hour.

Two local details that matter for a site near Dyes Inlet.

**Your nearest tide station may not have published datums.** Dyes Inlet
has its own NOAA station, 9445901 at Tracyton, but it is a subordinate
station and NOAA publishes no datums for it. The datums above come from
its reference station at Bremerton, in a different inlet. That is
normal, and it means the numbers you are using for your shoreline have
been transferred from somewhere else.

**There is no seamless land-to-seabed elevation surface for this
inlet.** The National Map returns zero topobathymetric lidar products
here, and zero seamless 1-metre products. There is excellent 1-metre
bare-earth lidar on the land, from the 2017 Olympic Peninsula survey,
and it simply stops at the water.

### The tsunami rides on whatever the tide is doing

This is the part people get wrong, and it is arithmetic rather than
opinion.

The Washington Geological Survey's modelled inundation for a large
Seattle Fault earthquake reaches the head of Dyes Inlet, with a maximum
amplitude of **5 feet at the Silverdale point and a modelled arrival
time of 5 minutes**. Nearby modelled points run higher: Bremerton and
Sinclair Inlet 9 feet, Agate Passage 11, Point White 16. The Cascadia
megathrust case is smaller inside Puget Sound, about 2.5 feet at
Bremerton, arriving in roughly 2 hours 40 minutes.

Now put that 5-foot wave against an 11.72-foot tidal range. **The tide
stage dominates the outcome**, and the locale hazard data says so
outright. A 5-foot wave arriving at mean lower low water starts from
nearly twelve feet below mean higher high water. The same wave at a high
tide starts from the top of the range. The modelled amplitude is not the
water level you will see; it is what gets added to whatever the water is
already doing, and nobody gets to choose the hour.

For siting, the conclusion is simple and unpopular: on a low shoreline
parcel, the elevation that matters is not the one that keeps you dry on
an ordinary winter storm tide. Five minutes of warning also means there
is no evacuation plan that involves a vehicle.
[The Hazards Your Place Actually Has](the_hazards_where_you_live.md)
covers what to do about that; this guide's contribution is that the
decision is made when you pick the contour line.

## 3. Can you get water, and get rid of waste?

On a peninsula these two questions are the same question, because the
water you drink and the water your drainfield discharges into are the
same body of groundwater.

### Water in

The Kitsap Peninsula has no upstream river to divert from. USGS
describes its hydrologic setting as similar to that of an island, and
groundwater is the normal domestic water source and the only one. Every
litre of fresh water on this peninsula fell on this peninsula.

What that means for a specific parcel:

**Drilling depth is knowable in advance.** From the recorded depths of
646 USGS-inventoried wells within about 6 km of Silverdale, the median
is **98 feet, about 30 metres**, with the middle half falling between 41
and 255 feet and the deepest at 1,200 feet. That distribution is your
budget range, and the spread between the 25th and 75th percentile is a
factor of six.

**How much you may legally take is not the statewide answer.** The
Washington statewide groundwater permit exemption allows a domestic
withdrawal of up to 5,000 gallons per day without a water right. But
Silverdale is in WRIA 15, named in state law among the watersheds with a
streamflow restoration requirement, and there a new domestic
permit-exempt withdrawal is capped at an annual average of **950 gallons
per day per connection**, dropping to **350 gallons per day, indoor use
only**, under a drought emergency order. There is a $500 fee to the
permitting authority, and a condition that the applicant manage
stormwater on-site by maximising infiltration. Those are interim
statutory measures that apply until rules say otherwise, and Ecology
adopted the WRIA 15 watershed plan in December 2024 with rulemaking to
follow, so confirm the current number before relying on it.

**On a shoreline parcel, ask about chloride.** The most detailed federal
study found no widespread seawater contamination of Kitsap Peninsula
wells, and listed the local areas where chloride exceeds 25 mg/L:
Silverdale and Dyes Inlet are not on that list, Sinclair Inlet about 11
km south is. The asterisk is that the finding rests on 1980 field data.
Test rather than assume. [Where Water Goes](where_water_goes.md)
explains why this particular contamination does not undo.

**If a well is not the plan, connection is a question with an address.**
Whether a parcel can connect to a public water system, what the
connection costs, and how far the main is from the property line are all
answerable before you buy, by asking. They are frequently not asked.

### Waste out

The drainfield is the part of a house that is most tightly coupled to
the ground under it, which makes it the part most likely to decide
whether a lot is buildable at all.

[Where Water Goes](where_water_goes.md) explains the mechanism: the tank
settles solids, and the **soil** is the treatment plant. That is why the
soil survey's septic ratings are a siting document.

The survey's verdict for this area is unusual and worth knowing before
you look at a single listing: **septic drainfields rate Very limited on
essentially every major series here**, for opposite reasons depending on
which ground you are standing on. Too slow on the till and lake-sediment
soils, where the objection is wetness and restricted percolation. Too
fast on the outwash sands, where the objection is seepage through the
bottom layer and inadequate filtering in the 60 to 150 cm filter field
zone, which is a groundwater contamination finding rather than a
drainage failure. Indianola, the best-draining ground in the area, fails
for the second reason.

"Very limited" does not mean forbidden. In the survey's own vocabulary
it means the limitation generally cannot be overcome without major soil
reclamation, special design, or expensive installation, and that poor
performance and high maintenance can be expected. In siting terms: this
lot needs an engineered system, and you should find out what kind and
what it costs before you commit, not after.

Two regulatory facts turn all of that into numbers on a site plan.

**Separation distances eat small lots.** Washington's on-site sewage
rule sets minimum horizontal separations that include **100 feet from a
nonpublic drinking water well**, **100 feet from nonpublic surface
water** measured from the ordinary high water mark, **10 feet from a
building foundation** and **5 feet from a property line**, and the local
health officer may increase them where the contamination risk is higher.
Draw those circles on a plan of a one-acre sloping waterfront lot with a
well, a house, a driveway and a creek on it, and the buildable area is
often much smaller than the lot.

**Depth of usable soil sets the system type, and therefore the price.**
The same rules set the required treatment level from the **vertical
separation**, meaning the depth of original undisturbed soil below the
bottom of the treatment component. The table runs in bands from 12 to 18
inches at the shallow end up to 60 inches or more at the deep end, and
the shallower the separation the higher the treatment level demanded and
the more likely the system has to distribute effluent under pressure
with timed dosing rather than by gravity. Deep separation on a
medium-textured soil is the one combination that gets you a plain
gravity drainfield.

Read that against the local geology and the shape of the problem is
obvious. Alderwood's till sits at a representative 56 cm, which is about
22 inches from the surface, with a mapped range of roughly 20 to 40
inches. The trench bottom has to sit somewhere in that column, and
whatever is left below it is your vertical separation. On half the land
here, there is not much left. That is not a reason nothing can be built;
it is the reason the system costs what it costs, and it is knowable
before you buy.

**Who actually decides** is the local health jurisdiction, not the
state. In Kitsap County that is the Kitsap Public Health District, and
the sequence is worth knowing because people get it backwards: the
health district's building clearance, which checks the proposed project
against the septic system and water supply, comes **before** the county
issues a building permit.

## 4. Can you get to it, in February?

Access is the question people underrate most reliably, because on the
day you look at the property it is July and the road is fine.

### The road, and what it is made of

The soil survey rates ground for **local roads and streets** in the same
way it rates it for dwellings, and the ratings are instructive. Norma
and McKenna rate Very limited for ponding and wetness. Shalcar and
Mukilteo rate Very limited for ponding, wetness, subsidence and very low
strength. Kitsap silt loam rates Somewhat limited for low strength by
AASHTO group index, shrink-swell and wetness. Those are engineering
statements about a road surface in the wet season, and they are free.

A private road or long driveway on the wrong ground is a permanent
running cost. It is also a shared one: if three houses use it, the
question of who pays for rebuilding it is a social arrangement you are
buying into, not a line in a contract.

### Grade, and the vehicles that have to come

Every vehicle you will ever need has to arrive under its own power, in
the conditions of the day it is needed. That list is longer than people
expect: a fuel truck, a septic pump truck, a well drilling rig, a
concrete mixer, a lumber delivery, a moving van, a propane tanker, an
ambulance, a fire engine.

This is a **rule of thumb** rather than a code citation, and it is worth
more than most things in this guide: before you commit to a steep
driveway, ask the local propane supplier and the local septic pumper
whether they will come up it, and ask in writing. They know the answer
immediately, they answer for free, and their answer is a real constraint
on a real vehicle rather than a theoretical gradient. A driveway your car
climbs easily in August can be one a loaded truck will not attempt in
January.

The same rule of thumb applies to turning. A vehicle that gets up your
driveway and then cannot turn around has to reverse down it.

### The fire service question

Ask the fire district that covers the parcel, before you buy, what they
need in order to reach it: access width, surface, grade, turnaround,
overhead clearance, bridge capacity if there is a bridge, and where the
nearest water supply for firefighting is. In much of the United States
these requirements come from a model code the county has adopted, so the
authoritative answer is local and specific. There is also usually an
insurance consequence attached to distance from a fire station and
distance from a hydrant, which is a recurring cost you can find out in
advance.

Around Silverdale, the wildfire risk is genuinely low by Washington
standards, and it is also the hazard most likely to be underestimated
here, which is a combination worth holding in mind rather than
resolving. There is
no modern mapped fire perimeter within about 15 km; the only perimeters
intersecting that area are three very large pre-settlement events dated
1308, 1508 and 1701. But the westside Douglas-fir regime is infrequent
and stand-replacing, and the binding constraint in a fire here is not
fire behaviour, it is getting off a peninsula reached by two bridges and
ferries.

### The season that actually closes roads

Wind and snow, not fire.

In NOAA's record for the Kitsap Peninsula zones from 1996 to 2025, **24
of 30 years** had at least one high or strong wind event, 80 events in
total, peaking at 77 mph in December 2000. On 15 November 2006 about
8,000 people in Kitsap County lost power, three roads were closed or
partly blocked by downed trees and power lines, and the Hood Canal
Bridge closed for several hours. The same record holds 48 heavy snow
events, with heavy snow in 18 of the 30 years, and wet lowland snow near
freezing is what brings limbs and lines down.

So the siting question is not whether the road ever fails. It is how
long you are prepared to be on the wrong side of a fallen tree, and how
many houses are on your side of it to help clear it. A single-access
road serving one house is a different proposition from a loop serving
twenty.

## 5. Only now: is it a pleasant place to live?

Everything above is a veto. This section is the part that decides
whether the house is good, and in a marine climate at 47.6 degrees north
it is not a matter of taste. It is a comfort and energy decision with
numbers behind it.

### Aspect and solar access

The Department of Energy's building science guidance is simple at the
level that matters for siting: a home oriented north-south will optimise
exposure to solar energy while minimising overheating from the
low-angled east-west sun, and this requires proper placement of windows
and overhangs, and careful placement of plants and trees so that shading
falls where it does the most good. For anything intended to use passive
solar heating or photovoltaics, DOE's guidance is to carry out a solar
assessment and mark the proposed array location on the site plan, which
is to say: settle it at siting time, not later.

What makes that concrete here is the winter sun angle, and it is worse
than people imagine.

The following is **arithmetic, not a citation**. The sun's altitude at
solar noon is 90 degrees minus your latitude, plus or minus the 23.44
degrees of the Earth's axial tilt depending on the season. At Silverdale,
latitude 47.6445 north:

| Date | Sun's altitude at solar noon |
|---|---|
| Winter solstice, about 21 December | **18.9 degrees** |
| Equinoxes, about 20 March and 22 September | 42.4 degrees |
| Summer solstice, about 21 June | 65.8 degrees |

Nineteen degrees is very low. Three consequences follow, all of them
arithmetic from that one number.

**Shadows in midwinter are about 2.9 times the height of whatever casts
them.** The multiplier is 1 divided by the tangent of 18.9 degrees. A
30-metre stand of second-growth conifer on your southern boundary throws
a shadow roughly 87 metres long at noon on the shortest day. A two-storey
neighbour throws about 20 metres. A tree line that is comfortably out of
the way in June is across your whole building envelope in December.

**A north-facing slope steeper than about 19 degrees receives no direct
sun at all at midwinter noon.** Nineteen degrees is a 34 percent grade,
which is not a cliff. For scale, the soil survey maps Kitsap silt loam
on terrace escarpments around Dyes Inlet at slopes up to 70 percent,
including an Indianola-Kitsap complex mapped at 45 to 70 percent, and 45
to 70 percent is about 24 to 35 degrees. A north-facing parcel on one of
those escarpments is in its own shadow at noon in December, and that is
before any trees.

**The window is short even where the sun does reach you.** The US Naval
Observatory gives sunrise at 07:56 and sunset at 16:22 for 21 December
2026 at Silverdale, with solar noon at 12:09: eight hours and
twenty-six minutes of daylight, against sixteen hours at midsummer. Any
ridge or tree line to the south cuts a real bite out of that eight and a
half hours.

**How to check it, on the ground, for nothing:** stand on the proposed
building site at solar noon, face south, and look at the skyline. Hold
your arm out and use your fist at arm's length, which subtends roughly
ten degrees of sky. If the ridge, the trees or the neighbour's roof
stand higher than about two fists above the horizon to the south, the
midwinter sun does not clear them. The fist measure is a **rule of
thumb** from field navigation, not a surveying instrument, but it
answers the question well enough to change a decision. Do it in winter
if you can, when the low sun will simply show you the answer.

There is a second-order benefit worth naming. Every degree of comfort
you win from aspect is a degree you do not have to buy fuel for, every
year, forever. Western Washington is climate zone 4C, the marine zone,
and its recommended insulation levels are as demanding as genuinely cold
zones 5 and 6 because mild and long still adds up. See
[Insulation and Heat Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md). Siting is the
only insulation decision you make for free.

### Wind, and the shelter that is worth having

The prevailing damaging wind here comes with Pacific winter storms, and
those are synoptic systems that arrive from the south and southwest.
Exposure to that quarter is what costs you.

The trade-off to think about explicitly is that the same trees that
shelter a site from wind are the trees that shade it in winter and drop
limbs on it in a storm. That is a genuine tension, and the resolution is
usually geometric rather than binary: shelter to the south-west and
north, open to the south for light, and nothing tall enough to reach the
house standing where it would fall on it.

### Cold air drainage, and the frost pocket

The bottom of a hollow is colder than the ground above it, and the
mechanism is straightforward.

The National Weather Service describes it directly: cold air will settle
in the valleys since it is heavier than warm air, therefore frost
conditions are more prone in these regions, and valleys also shelter the
area from stronger winds, which enhances the potential for frost. The
drainage itself is a named process: over the course of a clear night the
denser, cooler air on higher ground sinks into the valley, which begins
to fill from the bottom with cold layers of air. NWS calls this cold air
drainage.

The magnitude is not small. On a clear, calm night with strong
radiational cooling, NWS notes that the super-cooled layer right at the
surface can be up to 10 degrees Fahrenheit cooler than the air at the
four to five feet where observations are normally taken. So the
temperature in the hollow at plant height is not the temperature on the
forecast, and it is not the temperature at the top of your own driveway.

For siting this has three practical consequences.

**A slight rise beats a hollow, even a small one.** A building site a few
metres up the slope from a depression sits above the pooled cold air on
exactly the nights when it matters.

**Anything that dams the flow creates a pond of cold air behind it.** A
solid fence, a dense hedge, a road embankment or a building across the
slope will hold cold air upslope of it. This is a real effect on a
garden and a mild one on a house.

**The frost dates in the climate record are the station's, not yours.**
[The Growing Calendar Where You Live](the_growing_calendar.md) covers
how to read them; the siting point is that a hollow runs colder than the
published dates and a slope runs warmer.

There is a local wrinkle that shows how fine-grained this gets. Silverdale
sits on a hardiness zone boundary: the ZIP codes covering Silverdale,
west Bremerton and Poulsbo are zone 8b, while two nearer the Bremerton
waterfront are 9a. Shoreline sites here run about half a zone warmer
than sites a few hundred metres inland or uphill, because the water
moderates them. Half a zone is a real difference for a fruit tree, and
it is decided by where you stand.

## Who to ask, before you commit

This is a checklist of bodies to contact, not legal advice. Nothing
here is a substitute for asking them, and the answers are specific to a
parcel number. The pattern holds in most of the United States even
though the names change; the Silverdale specifics are the worked
example.

**The county planning and permitting department.** In Kitsap County this
is the Department of Community Development, which issues building, fire,
stormwater and site development permits, and which administers the
zoning code, the Critical Areas Ordinance and the county's Shoreline
Master Program. Ask: what is this parcel zoned, is it a legal lot of
record, what are the setbacks, what critical areas are mapped on it, and
what studies will be required with an application.

**The critical areas question specifically.** Washington's Growth
Management Act requires counties to designate and protect critical
areas, and defines them as wetlands, areas with a critical recharging
effect on aquifers used for potable water, fish and wildlife habitat
conservation areas, frequently flooded areas, and geologically hazardous
areas. The statute defines geologically hazardous areas as areas that,
because of their susceptibility to erosion, sliding, earthquake or other
geological events, are not suited to the siting of commercial,
residential or industrial development consistent with public health or
safety concerns. Read that definition again: the state has written down,
as a matter of law, that some ground is not a building site. A bluff
parcel here is very likely to be mapped as one, and a mapped
geologically hazardous area normally triggers a required geotechnical
report and a buffer around the top and the toe. The size of that buffer
is set by your county's ordinance, so get the number from the county
rather than from any general document, this one included.

**Shoreline rules, if you are anywhere near saltwater.** Washington's
Shoreline Management Act reaches inland: shorelands extend landward 200
feet in all directions, measured on a horizontal plane from the ordinary
high water mark, and the definition also picks up floodways, floodplain
areas extending 200 feet from those floodways, and associated wetlands
and river deltas. Two hundred feet is further than people assume, and a
parcel can be entirely inside shoreline jurisdiction without touching
the beach.

**The local health jurisdiction, for septic and water supply.** Kitsap
Public Health District. Ask whether the parcel has an existing approved
system or a valid site evaluation, what soil type and vertical
separation the site carries, what system type that implies, and where
the reserve drainfield area is. Remember that its building clearance
precedes the county building permit.

**The state water agency, for what you may legally take.** Washington's
Department of Ecology administers water rights, and WRIA 15 overrides
the statewide permit-exempt allowance here. The locale's authorities map
in `data/locales/silverdale_wa/locale.json` records the current contact
for this and for the other state-level bodies below.

**The fire district**, for access and water supply, as above.

**The state geological survey**, for the mapped landslide, liquefaction
and tsunami layers. Washington's is the Washington Geological Survey and
its Geologic Information Portal. Every state has an equivalent and what
each has mapped varies enormously.

**Utilities**, for the boring and decisive question of what it costs to
bring power, and whether there is any. A long service run is a large
number and it is quotable in advance.

**And the seller's own disclosures and the title.** Easements,
access rights across someone else's land, shared well agreements, road
maintenance agreements, and any recorded geotechnical report or
condition. A shared well with no written agreement is a dispute waiting
for a dry August.

The locale file's `authorities` block holds the current bodies and URLs
for the state extension service, the state water agency, the state fish
and wildlife agency, the health department's biotoxin programme, the
noxious weed board, the soil testing lab and the regional air agency. It
is the place to look up who is who rather than trusting a name in a
document that may be years old by the time you read it.

## How to survey a candidate site yourself, in a day

None of this replaces a professional. All of it is free, takes one day,
and will either raise a question worth paying a professional to answer
or save you the fee entirely.

**Before you go, one hour at a desk.**

1. Look up the parcel on FEMA's Flood Map Service Center. Write down the
   zone, and the base flood elevation if there is one.
2. Look it up on your state's geologic hazard portal. Write down whether
   any landslide, liquefaction or tsunami layer touches it.
3. Look it up in the soil survey, following
   [Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md). Write down the
   map unit name and phase, the drainage class, the depth to any
   restrictive layer, and the ratings for dwellings, local roads and
   septic, **with the reason printed next to each rating**.
4. Find the 12-digit hydrologic unit it sits in and what is upstream of
   it, following [Where Water Goes](where_water_goes.md).
5. Look at whatever aerial imagery you can get, at several dates if
   possible. Old imagery shows what used to be there: a pond, a pit, a
   barn, a logging road, a slide scar.

You now have a hypothesis. The day out is for testing it.

**What to walk.**

Walk the whole parcel, the boundaries included, not just the pretty
part. Walk the slope above you and the slope below you, and, if you can
get permission, walk onto the neighbouring ground uphill, because that
is where your water comes from.

Walk the access from the public road to the building site, and back
down. Then imagine a concrete truck doing it.

**What to look at.**

- The shape of the ground. Hummocky, irregular, stepped ground on a
  slope, with trees leaning at odd angles or curving at the base, is
  what old movement looks like. So is a bench or a scarp that does not
  belong to the general grade.
- Ready.gov's list of slow landslide signs, which apply to any existing
  structure on the site: doors or windows that stick or jam; new cracks
  in plaster, tile, brick or foundations; slowly developing, widening
  cracks in the ground; and changes in the pattern of storm-water
  drainage on slopes.
- Standing water, and the vegetation that indicates it. Rushes, sedges,
  skunk cabbage and willow are telling you about the water table.
- The bluff edge, if there is one, from a safe distance, and the beach
  below it if you can reach it. Fresh raw soil, toppled trees, exposed
  roots and a fan of debris at the toe are a recent failure.
- The skyline to the south, with the fist measure above.
- The neighbours' houses. Where did people who have lived here a while
  choose to put their buildings, and what do their driveways, retaining
  walls and drainage look like? That is decades of accumulated local
  knowledge, sitting in plain view.

**What to dig.**

Dig at least three holes, well apart, in the parts of the site you
actually intend to use. Not one. The point is to find out whether the
ground is uniform, and one hole cannot answer that. Dig each one past
the depth the soil survey gives for its restrictive layer, and look for:
the horizon depths and colours against the published profile; where the
gravel starts; where roots stop and turn sideways; whether the spade
hits something it cannot get through, and at what depth; and whether
water seeps into the hole and rises after you stop.

A steel probe rod or a digging bar maps a hardpan across a site far
faster than a spade, on a rough grid of points. That is a **rule of
thumb**, not a published method, and it works.

Watch for signs that the ground is fill rather than natural soil: an
abrupt knife-sharp boundary between layers, mixed colours with no
pattern down the profile, fragments of brick, concrete, asphalt, glass
or plastic, a hard compacted layer at exactly the depth machinery would
have tracked, and horizons out of order.

**When to go.**

Twice, if you possibly can: once in the wettest week of winter and once
in late summer. If you only get one visit, make it the wet one. Almost
everything that makes a site bad is visible in February and invisible in
August. That timing advice is a **rule of thumb**; the underlying
seasonality is in the climate data.

**Who to talk to.**

The neighbours, and this is not a courtesy, it is data collection.
Nobody has published what they know and no map contains it. Ask, in
these words or near enough:

- How deep is your well, and has it ever run low in August?
- Does your drainfield give you trouble, and what kind of system is it?
- How often does the power go out, and for how long?
- Has the road ever been impassable, and what closed it?
- Has anything ever slid, anywhere along here?
- Where does the water come out in a big storm?
- Who owns the road, and who maintains it?

People answer these questions generously and at length, because nobody
usually asks. Half an hour on a doorstep routinely turns up something no
agency knows.

## If you already own the site

Then this stops being a choice and becomes a list of things to find out,
which is still worth doing. Several of them cost money and are worth it.

**Free, this week.** The flood zone, the state geologic hazard layers,
the soil map unit and its ratings, the hydrologic unit and what is
upstream, the parcel's zoning and mapped critical areas, and the walk in
the rain with three test holes. That is most of the picture.

**Worth paying for, roughly in order of what it prevents.**

1. **A geotechnical investigation**, if there is any slope, any bluff,
   any sign of past movement, or any intention to build or add. This is
   the one that prevents the outcome nothing else prevents.
2. **A current survey with the datum stated in writing**, if you are
   near a shoreline, a floodplain, or a boundary anyone could argue
   about. A surveyor's elevation whose datum nobody recorded is a number
   you cannot use.
3. **A septic inspection and a locate of the existing system and its
   reserve area**, if you are on septic and do not have the as-built.
4. **A water test**, including chloride if you are near saltwater, and
   the basic bacteriological and nitrate panel regardless.
5. **A lead test on the soil**, if the house is old or close to a road.
   [Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) covers why and
   against what number to read the result.

**Free and permanent, whatever the above says.** Get the roof water away
from the building and away from any slope, in pipes, discharging
somewhere that can take it. Keep drainfields and irrigation off the
ground between a house and a bluff edge. Keep native vegetation on a
slope. Ecology's summary is that drainage improvements are the most
cost-effective way to reduce slide risk, and drainage is the one part of
this whole subject where a homeowner with no budget can genuinely change
the outcome.

## What can go wrong

- **You judged the site in summer.** The perched water table, the seep,
  the boggy corner, the road that fails and the way water crosses the
  lot are all February phenomena. A July inspection is a partial
  inspection and it is the one everybody does.
- **You treated a flood map as a safety certificate.** Outside the zone
  means the studied sources do not reach you. FEMA says there is no such
  thing as a no-risk zone.
- **You compared two elevations with different datums.** A survey
  elevation, a base flood elevation and a tide height need not share a
  zero. Ask for the datum in writing.
- **You looked at the bluff top and forgot the toe.** Both are the
  hazard. So is the ground three days after an earthquake.
- **You bought the view.** A view lot on this coast is very frequently a
  bluff lot, and the two facts are the same fact.
- **You assumed "Very limited" meant "a bit awkward".** In the survey's
  own words it means major reclamation, special design or expensive
  installation, with poor performance and high maintenance expected.
- **You checked whether a septic system was possible but not what type.**
  The type follows from the vertical separation, and the price follows
  from the type.
- **You never drew the separation circles on the plan.** A hundred feet
  from the well, a hundred feet from surface water, plus setbacks, can
  leave a large lot with a small buildable area.
- **You planned the driveway for your own car.** The pump truck, the fuel
  truck and the fire engine are the ones that decide.
- **You did not ask the neighbours.** They know about the slide, the
  flood, the well that ran dry and the road that closes, and none of it
  is written down anywhere.
- **You cleared the trees uphill.** That is named by Ecology as one of
  the conditions that raises landslide risk here, and it is a thing
  people do to improve a view.
- **You assumed the fine-grained public data exists.** There is superb
  1-metre lidar on this land and no topobathymetric coverage of the
  inlet at all. Check before relying on a dataset.

## How you know it worked

- You can name, for your site, the soil map unit and phase, the depth to
  its restrictive layer, and what the survey rates it for dwellings,
  roads and septic, with the reason next to each rating.
- You know its flood zone, and if it has a base flood elevation you know
  the number and the datum it is measured from.
- You know whether any landslide, liquefaction or tsunami layer touches
  it, and if it is on or below a slope, a geotechnical engineer has
  looked at it rather than a document.
- You have walked the site in heavy rain and can say where water enters
  it, crosses it and leaves it.
- You have dug at least three holes and know what stops a spade, and at
  what depth.
- You know what a well costs here and what you are legally allowed to
  draw, or you know what a connection costs and how far away the main
  is.
- You know what septic system type the ground implies, and roughly what
  that costs, before you have committed to anything.
- You have asked the fire district and the septic pumper whether they
  can reach the building site.
- You have stood on the site at midwinter noon, or done the fist
  measure, and you know whether the sun reaches it in December.
- You have talked to at least two neighbours about water, power and the
  road.

When those are true, you are no longer choosing a site by how it looks.
And if the answer is that this site is a poor one and you are going to
build on it anyway, because it is the site you have, then you at least
know exactly which problems you have bought, which is the difference
between a budget and a surprise.

### Arithmetic rather than citation

Three figures in this guide are geometry, not measurements, and are
labelled as such in the text.

The sun's altitude at solar noon is 90 degrees minus the latitude, plus
or minus the Earth's axial tilt of 23.44 degrees. At 47.6445 degrees
north that gives 18.9 degrees at the winter solstice, 42.4 at the
equinoxes and 65.8 at the summer solstice. The USNO sunrise and sunset
times above are the sourced cross-check that the December day really is
that short.

The midwinter shadow multiplier of about 2.9 is 1 divided by the tangent
of 18.9 degrees, so a 30-metre tree casts roughly an 87-metre shadow at
solar noon on the shortest day.

A directly north-facing slope is in its own shadow at solar noon on the
winter solstice once its angle exceeds the sun's altitude, so once it is
steeper than about 18.9 degrees, which is a 34 percent grade. The 45 to
70 percent slope phases the soil survey maps on the Dyes Inlet terrace
escarpments correspond to about 24 to 35 degrees.

### Labelled in the text as rules of thumb, not sourced

- The ordering of the five siting questions by how permanent each answer
  is. Each individual constraint is sourced above; the ranking is ours.
- Visiting a candidate site in the wettest week of winter, in the rain,
  and treating a summer-only inspection as a partial one. The
  seasonality is in the climate data; the advice to plan around it is
  ours.
- Digging at least three test holes rather than one, and using a probe
  rod or digging bar on a grid to map a hardpan quickly. Standard field
  practice, published as a method by nobody.
- Asking the propane supplier and the septic pumper, in writing, whether
  they will drive your driveway. No agency says this; it is simply the
  cheapest way to convert a gradient into an answer.
- The fist-at-arm's-length measure of about ten degrees of sky, used to
  check whether a southern skyline blocks the midwinter sun. A field
  navigation convention, not an instrument.
- The list of signs that a layer is fill rather than natural soil, and
  the list of vegetation and landform signs of old movement and of
  seasonal water. Each sign is ordinary field practice; the lists as
  given are not quoted from any single publication.
- Interviewing the neighbours, and the specific questions to ask them.
