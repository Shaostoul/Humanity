# Greywater

Stand in the shower and count while the water warms up. However long that
takes in your house, every second of it is water that was treated to
drinking standard, that you paid for, and that went down the drain without
touching you.

Put a bucket there and you catch it. That part is easy. The bucket costs
nothing and the water is clean.

Now scale it up. The Washington State Department of Health puts the
greywater a household produces at up to forty gallons per person each day,
and calls it the largest single portion of a home's wastewater. For two
people that is eighty gallons a day leaving the building, most of it
barely dirty, at exactly the time of year the garden is thirsty.

That is the appeal, and it is a real one. But the bucket is the honest
beginning of this subject and it is worth noticing how far it is from the
end of it. Everything difficult about greywater happens after you have
caught it: where you are allowed to put it, how long you may keep it, what
was dissolved in it before you caught it, and which of those things
quietly ruins your soil two years from now while you are congratulating
yourself on the water you saved.

## What this guide will and will not do

It will tell you what greywater legally is in Washington, which is not
what most writing on the subject says it is. It will give you the real
numbers from the real rule, with the section they come from, so you can
check every one. It will explain the failure modes and where each one
bites.

It will not tell you whether your system is legal. It cannot. In
Washington the decision is made by a named official in your county, the
local health officer, and that official is allowed to be stricter than
the state and is allowed to decide your site is unsuitable. There is no
way to read your way to that answer. The most useful thing this guide can
do is make the phone call you eventually make a short one, because you
will already know what you are asking about.

It also will not help you build a system. Plumbing a house for greywater
is work for a licensed designer in most of the cases the rule
contemplates, and the rule says so explicitly. What this guide gives you
is the ability to understand what that designer is doing and to notice
when something is wrong.

One more limit, stated here because it is the single most common error.
**Greywater reuse never replaces a sewer connection or a septic system.**
The Washington rule says this outright: using a greywater irrigation
system does not serve as an alternative to an approved on-site sewage
system or a connection to an approved public sewer, and that holds even
for buildings with waterless toilets (WAC 246-274-005(3)). A greywater
system in Washington is a seasonal side branch off a permanent wastewater
system, with a valve so you can send the water back down the normal path
whenever you need to. If you are imagining greywater as the way to avoid
installing a septic system, stop here. That is not what this is.

## The definition, which is genuinely different depending on where you stand

Here is a claim you will find stated confidently and without qualification
all over the internet: kitchen sink water is not greywater.

In Washington that is false. It is false in the statute and false in the
rule, and the difference matters enough to change what you are allowed to
build.

**Washington.** The state defines greywater in statute at RCW 90.46.010(8)
and repeats it in the rule at WAC 246-274-009(4). Both give the same list:
domestic type flows from bathtubs, showers, bathroom sinks, washing
machines, dishwashers, and kitchen or utility sinks. Greywater does not
include flow from a toilet or urinal. That is the entire boundary. Kitchen
sinks are in. Dishwashers are in.

What Washington does instead of excluding the kitchen is split greywater
into two grades, in the same definition:

- **Light greywater** is flows from bathtubs, showers, bathroom sinks,
  washing machines, and laundry-utility sinks.
- **Dark greywater** is flows from dishwashers, kitchen and nonlaundry
  utility sinks, either alone or mixed with light greywater.

Read that second definition slowly, because it contains the trap. Dark
greywater is the kitchen flows **alone or in combination with light
greywater**. One kitchen sink plumbed into your shower line does not
produce mostly-light greywater. It produces dark greywater, all of it, and
dark greywater requires a treatment component and the most demanding tier
of system (WAC 246-274-300(2)(a)). Mixing is a one-way door.

**California.** The California definition, at Health and Safety Code
section 17922.12, lists bathtubs, showers, bathroom washbasins, clothes
washing machines and laundry tubs, and then says specifically that
kitchen sinks and dishwashers are not included. The opposite of
Washington, in the same country, on the same coast.

**Oregon.** Oregon's Department of Environmental Quality includes showers
and bathtubs, bathroom sinks, kitchen sinks and laundry, and excludes
toilet discharge, garbage wastes from kitchen sinks fitted with garbage
disposal units, and wastewater contaminated by soiled diapers. A third
answer: the kitchen sink is in, but the grinder under it puts you out.

Three neighbouring states, three different boundaries. So when you read a
confident sentence online about what greywater "is", the useful question
is not whether the writer is careless but which state they live in. Most
of them are in California, which is why the kitchen exclusion is repeated
so often as though it were physics.

**The practical consequence for you.** If you are in Washington and you
want the simplest legal system, you want light greywater only, and you
want the kitchen nowhere near it. Not because kitchen water is not
greywater here, but because it is the expensive kind.

## Blackwater is a word the Washington rule does not use

You will notice something if you read the whole of chapter 246-274 WAC:
the word blackwater never appears in it. Not once.

That is not an oversight. Washington's statute defines domestic wastewater
as wastewater from greywater, toilet, or urinal sources (RCW 90.46.010(7)),
and defines greywater by listing fixtures and excluding toilets and
urinals. The line is drawn by naming what is on each side, not by defining
a second category. So the honest answer to "what is the regulatory
definition of blackwater in Washington" is that there is not one in this
chapter, and anybody who gives you a crisp Washington blackwater
definition is importing it from somewhere else.

This matters more than a terminology quibble, because the Department of
Health's own literature review reached a conclusion that ought to be
uncomfortable for anyone who thinks of greywater as obviously the clean
half. Reviewing the published studies, the department concluded that the
pollution level in a greywater stream that includes kitchen sinks,
dishwashers and laundry machines used to wash dirty diapers can be equal
to or greater than blackwater, and needs regulating consistently with
on-site sewage rules. The same review puts it more bluntly elsewhere: a
sink with a garbage grinder used for washing raw meat or dishes, and a
washing machine used for soiled diapers, can carry more pollution than a
toilet.

The numbers behind that are worth carrying around. Biochemical oxygen
demand, BOD5, is the standard measure of how much organic material is in
water and therefore how hard it will be for an environment to digest it.
The department's review reports these figures from the literature:

| Water | BOD5 |
|---|---|
| Bathroom-only greywater, Danish apartment study | 26 to 130 mg/L |
| Greywater including kitchen wastewater | 26 to 295 mg/L |
| Raw sewage | about 300 mg/L |
| Treated wastewater | under 10 mg/L |

The top of the kitchen-inclusive greywater range, 295 mg/L, is
indistinguishable from the raw sewage benchmark of about 300 mg/L. Not
typical greywater, the top of its range. But a category whose range
reaches raw sewage is not a category you can treat as harmless because of
its name.

### The four things that stop being greywater

Washington's general requirements (WAC 246-274-011(1)) put four kinds of
water outside the system regardless of which fixture they came from. These
are the lines that turn permitted reuse into an illegal discharge:

**Diaper wash water.** The greywater may not contain water used to wash
diapers or similarly soiled or infectious materials. This is the classic
line and all three states draw it: Oregon excludes wastewater contaminated
by soiled diapers, and California's definition excludes water affected by
infectious, contaminated or unhealthy bodily wastes. The reason is direct.
Diaper laundry is faecal material, and faecal material is the exact thing
the whole regulatory structure exists to keep out of soil that people
touch. If there is a baby in the house and the nappies go through the
washing machine, that washing machine is not a greywater source while that
is true. The department's review makes the same point from the other
direction when it identifies the lowest-risk sources as bathroom fixtures
and washing machines **in homes without children living in them**.

**Toxic substances, cleaning chemicals and hazardous household products.**
The rule names examples: waste from a water softener, cleaning car parts,
washing greasy or oily rags or clothing, rinsing paint brushes, waste
solutions from home photo labs or similar hobby or home-occupation
activity, and home maintenance activities. Note the first one on that
list. **Water softener regeneration waste is prohibited by name**, and
that is the single most sodium-loaded thing a house produces. Come back to
this when you reach the soil section; it is not an arbitrary exclusion.

**Biomedical waste**, as that term is defined in chapter 70A.228 RCW.

**Anything that is not domestic type flow.** The greywater has to have the
consistency and strength typical of a household. A home business that
generates something else does not get to route it through the garden
because it happens to leave via a sink.

## Storage: the twenty four hour line, and what is happening on the other side of it

There is a widely published short storage limit for untreated greywater,
and in Washington it is real, it is written down, and it is twenty four
hours.

The exact form it takes depends on the tier:

- **Tier 1 systems may not store greywater at all.** Not for a day, not at
  all. The rule says the greywater may not be stored and that it must be
  delivered by gravity, with no pumps (WAC 246-274-100(1)(f) and (g)). A
  Tier 1 system is a pipe, a slope and a garden.
- **Tier 2 systems may store it for no more than twenty four hours**
  (WAC 246-274-200(1)(g)).
- **Beyond twenty four hours you need a treatment component**, which by
  definition makes it a Tier 3 system (WAC 246-274-300(2)(b)).

So the limit is not a rule of thumb somebody repeated. It is the boundary
between two regulatory tiers, and crossing it changes what you are legally
required to build.

**Why the number exists.** The Department of Health's literature review
gives the mechanism, and it is worth understanding rather than memorising.
Greywater has a high BOD5, which means it contains a lot of organic
material that bacteria want to eat. Bacteria eating it consume oxygen.
In a closed tank the oxygen is a fixed and small supply, so it runs out
quickly. What grows next is anaerobic bacteria, which do not need oxygen,
and a characteristic product of anaerobic bacteria is hydrogen sulfide,
which the review describes as an extremely odorous gas. That is the rotten
egg smell, and it is the smell of a tank that has gone septic. The review
concludes that storing untreated greywater longer than twenty four hours
is not recommended.

**The second reason, which is worse.** The same review reports that the
warm wet conditions inside plumbing and storage tanks actually increase
indicator organisms when greywater is held past twenty four hours. Stored
greywater is not a container of a fixed number of germs slowly dying. It
is a nutrient broth at room temperature. The review, citing the Water
Environment Research Foundation, reports that indicator organisms can
persist and even multiply in stored greywater because of the available
nutrients and biofilm formation, and that pathogens seeded into greywater
are capable of reproducing during storage. Specific findings it cites:
Salmonella typhimurium and Shigella dysenteriae survived several days
when seeded into greywater at pH 6.5 and 25 degrees C. A virus, poliovirus
type 1, behaved differently and fell by 90 percent or more after six days
under the same conditions.

So the direction of travel depends on the organism. Some bacteria grow.
Some viruses die off. A tank is not a disinfection step for either.

**A number for pathogen load.** The review reports faecal coliform results
in greywater samples ranging from about 1.8 x 10^4 to 7.9 x 10^6 per 100
mL. For scale, drinking water standards are written around zero. Greywater
is not lightly contaminated water; it is contaminated water whose
contamination is invisible because it is dissolved in something that looks
like weak tea.

**The honest caveat, because it is in the source.** The department's review
also states plainly that despite the presence of faecal indicator organisms
and pathogens, a consistent relationship between greywater reuse and
illness rates is unknown at this time. That is a real and important
qualification and it should not be sanded off. The hazard is established;
the population-level illness rate attributable to domestic greywater reuse
is not. Design as though the pathogens matter, because they are measurably
there, but do not tell people greywater irrigation is a known cause of
outbreaks, because the literature the state relied on does not say that.

**What this means for a bucket.** If you are hand-carrying shower warm-up
water to a shrub the same afternoon, the storage problem is not really
yours; you have not stored it. If you are filling a drum in the basement
to water the garden at the weekend, you have built the exact thing the
twenty four hour rule exists to prevent, and by Monday it will smell like
it. A drum of four day old greywater is a disposal problem, not a
resource.

One more thing about storage, because you will meet it. Some published
greywater guidance from drier states suggests adding chlorine bleach to
extend how long greywater can be held. A New Mexico State University
extension publication says exactly that. Do not carry that advice into
Washington. Bleach is a cleaning chemical, the Washington rule prohibits
cleaning chemicals in greywater, bleach damages the plants you are
irrigating, and the tier structure here answers the same problem with a
treatment component instead. Advice written for one regulatory regime does
not travel.

## Where it may go, and where it may not

### Subsurface, always, with no exceptions in this chapter

The entire Washington chapter is named for this: greywater reuse for
subsurface irrigation. The general requirements say the greywater must be
used only for subsurface irrigation, and separately that it may not
surface in any way, including by ponding or runoff, and must stay below
the ground surface so that people and animals do not come into contact
with it (WAC 246-274-011(1)(a) and (g)).

Two separate requirements, which is deliberate. The first governs how you
design it. The second governs what happens afterwards. A system that was
designed subsurface but is now ponding in a low corner of the garden has
violated the second even though it satisfied the first, and the rule
treats surfacing greywater as a definition of failure (WAC
246-274-009(2)(a)).

This rules out, in Washington, a set of things people do elsewhere and
write about enthusiastically: sprinklers, surface drip lines lying on
mulch, watering cans, hoses, spraying anything, and washing the car with
it. The Department of Health's own review reaches the same conclusion from
the risk side, finding that untreated greywater from any source is not
suitable for uses involving direct exposure such as washing cars, toilet
flushing and spray irrigation, but that subsurface irrigation can be an
acceptable use.

### How deep

For Tier 2 and Tier 3 systems the rule is specific (WAC 246-274-415(5)):
the subsurface irrigation components go a minimum of four inches deep and
no deeper than twelve inches below finished grade, and the four inch cover
must be two inches of suitable soil plus two inches of mulch. For Tier 1
the requirement is at least four inches of appropriate cover, which may be
suitable soil or mulch, humus or compost, topped up through the season as
it settles (WAC 246-274-100(1)(k)).

There is a reason for the upper bound as well as the lower one. Too
shallow and the water surfaces, which is the exposure failure. Too deep
and you are past the root zone, which means you are no longer irrigating
plants, you are disposing of wastewater into the ground, which is a
different activity with a different permit.

### How much soil has to be underneath

This is the requirement that decides whether a site works, and it is the
one most likely to stop you. For Tier 2 and Tier 3 systems there must be a
minimum of **twenty four inches of suitable soil** between the irrigation
components and any restrictive layer, or the highest water table during
the growing season (WAC 246-274-415(6)).

Be precise about which tier that binds. Twenty four inches is a Tier 2 and
Tier 3 design requirement; the Tier 1 section does not state a separation
distance. What Tier 1 has instead is the general requirement that applies
to every tier: the system must be located in suitable soil (WAC
246-274-011(1)(j)). Since suitable soil is *defined* as unsaturated soil
above the seasonally high water table and above any restrictive layer,
shallow ground is still a problem for a Tier 1 system. It is just a
problem with no published number attached, which means it is resolved by
the local health officer's judgement rather than by a measurement you can
take yourself and check.

Suitable soil has a definition: unsaturated soil above the seasonally high
water table and above any restrictive layer, in which water, air and root
growth are sustained (WAC 246-274-009(22)). A restrictive layer is a
stratum that impedes vertical movement of water, air and roots, and the
rule names hardpan, claypan, fragipan, caliche, some compacted soils,
bedrock and unstructured clay soils (WAC 246-274-009(19)).

Do the stacking. If your irrigation line is at the shallowest allowed
depth of four inches, you still need twenty four inches of suitable soil
below it, so you need a soil column at least twenty eight inches deep
before you hit anything restrictive. Twenty eight inches is about 71 cm.
If you place the line at the deepest allowed twelve inches, you need
thirty six inches, about 91 cm. Hold onto the 71 cm figure; it is about to
matter locally.

### The setbacks, which are Washington's and only Washington's

WAC 246-274-405, Table I, sets minimum horizontal setbacks. Two columns:
one measured from the edge of the subsurface irrigation components, one
from tanks and other system components.

| From | Irrigation edge | Tanks and components |
|---|---|---|
| Building foundation, down-gradient | 10 ft | not applicable |
| Building foundation, up-gradient | 2 ft | not applicable |
| Property or easement line | 2 ft | 2 ft |
| Pressurised water supply line or public water main | 10 ft | 10 ft |
| Interceptor, curtain drain or drainage ditch, down-gradient | 30 ft | not applicable |
| Interceptor, curtain drain or drainage ditch, up-gradient | 10 ft | not applicable |
| In-ground swimming pool | 10 ft | 5 ft |
| Spring or surface water, from the ordinary high-water mark | 100 ft | 50 ft |
| Well or suction line | 100 ft | 50 ft |
| Public drinking water well | 100 ft | 100 ft |
| Public drinking water spring, from the ordinary high-water mark | 200 ft | 200 ft |
| Decommissioned well | 10 ft | not applicable |
| Down-gradient cut or bank, 5 ft or more undisturbed soil above a restrictive layer | 25 ft | not applicable |
| Down-gradient cut or bank, under 5 ft undisturbed soil above a restrictive layer | 50 ft | not applicable |
| On-site sewage system, primary and reserve areas | 10 ft | not applicable |

**These are Washington's numbers. Do not carry them anywhere else.** Every
state writes its own and they are not close to each other. If you are
reading this outside Washington, the correct use of this table is as a
list of the *kinds* of thing that get a setback, so you know what to ask
your own regulator about. The distances are not transferable and using
them elsewhere would be inventing a regulation.

Two definitions the table depends on. Down-gradient means liquid will flow
toward the thing when it hits a water table or restrictive layer;
up-gradient means it will flow away. That is why the same feature gets two
different numbers: a foundation uphill of your irrigation field is a much
smaller problem than one downhill of it. And if surface water in the area
is used as a public drinking water supply, the system has to sit outside
that supply's source water protection area entirely, which is a different
and larger constraint than any distance in the table.

**The well setback deserves its own paragraph.** One hundred feet from a
well, measured from the edge of the irrigated area. If you are on a
private well, as much of rural Kitsap is, this is frequently the binding
constraint on a small lot, and it interacts badly with the two foot
property line setback: you must keep a hundred feet from *a* well, and
your neighbour's well counts. The point of the setback is travel time
through soil. Soil is the treatment step in a subsurface greywater system;
there is no other one in Tier 1 or Tier 2. Distance is how you buy enough
of it. The related guide [Testing Water](testing_water.md) covers what you
would actually test a well for and what coliform in a well result means,
which is the other end of this same concern.

### The other siting rules

Beyond the table, the general requirements add several conditions that
each rule out real sites (WAC 246-274-011(1)):

- The greywater must be used and contained within the property boundary of
  the building it came from, or on nearby property where you are legally
  allowed to use it. It may not run onto a neighbour.
- The system may only be used during the growing season.
- It must be in suitable soil, on land that is stable.
- It may not be in an environmentally sensitive area, as determined by the
  local health officer. That determination is theirs, not yours.
- Irrigation rates may not exceed the evapotranspiration rate of the field.
  You may not apply more water than the plants and evaporation can remove.
- There must be a readily accessible, visibly labelled diversion valve that
  sends the greywater back to the sewer or septic system when needed: when
  soils are saturated or frozen, when something blocks or backs up, when
  you have hit the daily limit, or simply when you would rather not use it.
- Pipes and above-ground tanks must be labelled: caution, nonpotable water,
  do not drink.
- Mulch, if used, must be permeable enough to let greywater infiltrate
  quickly.

For Tier 2 and Tier 3 there is also a slope limit: the irrigation field
may only be on slopes of less than thirty percent, which the rule notes is
about seventeen degrees (WAC 246-274-415(10)).

### Never letting it touch the drinking water

This is the failure with the worst consequences and the least warning, and
it deserves separating out from the rest. Everything above is about
greywater reaching people slowly, through soil and plants. Cross-connection
is greywater reaching people immediately, through a tap.

A cross-connection is any point where a non-potable system can reach a
potable one. The ways it happens in practice are mundane: a greywater line
teed into a hose bib so the garden can be watered from either, a greywater
tank topped up from the mains with a hose left dangling below the water
line in the tank, a pump plumbed so it can draw from either source, or
simply two similar-looking pipes in a crawlspace and a future owner who
guesses wrong. The pressure in a household supply is not constant, and when
it drops, water in a connected non-potable line can move the wrong way.

The Washington greywater chapter carries several requirements that exist
for this reason, and it is worth seeing them as one group rather than as
scattered details:

- **Labelling.** Pipes and above-ground tanks must carry the words:
  caution, nonpotable water, do not drink (WAC 246-274-011(1)(p)). This is
  not decoration. It is the control that protects the person who opens up
  your plumbing in fifteen years and has never heard of you.
- **Separation from supply pipes.** Ten feet between the irrigation
  components and any pressurised water supply line or public water main,
  and ten feet from tanks and other system components (WAC 246-274-405,
  Table I).
- **One hundred feet from a well or suction line**, and a hundred feet from
  a public drinking water well, two hundred from a public drinking water
  spring (same table). If area surface water is a public drinking supply,
  the system must sit outside its source water protection area entirely.
- **Backflow from the sewer side.** Tanks must have an overflow pipe, at
  least as large as the inlet, flowing by gravity to the sewer or septic
  system, fitted with a check valve or backwater valve so that sewage
  cannot come back up it (WAC 246-274-410(2)(a)(vi)). Note the direction
  this one guards: it is protecting your greywater tank from your septic
  tank.
- **Tamper-proof tanks.** Storage and pump tanks must be water-tight,
  anchored against overturning, and not susceptible to intrusion by humans
  or vectors (WAC 246-274-410(2)(a)). A tank a child can open is a tank a
  child can drink from.

And the umbrella requirement: greywater reuse must comply with all
applicable local codes and state rules including the Uniform Plumbing Code
as adopted in chapters 51-56 and 51-57 WAC (WAC 246-274-005(1)).

**What I could not check, said plainly.** Backflow prevention proper, which
is to say the assemblies and air gaps that stop non-potable water entering
a potable system, is governed by the plumbing code rather than by the
greywater chapter, and I was not able to open the text of that plumbing
code chapter. So this guide can tell you that the greywater rule points at
the plumbing code and that cross-connection is the reason, and it cannot
tell you what the plumbing code requires. That is a question for the
person doing the plumbing, and it is a good test of whether they know this
subject: a plumber who does not immediately talk about cross-connection
when you say the word greywater is the wrong plumber.

The one rule you can apply yourself without any of that: **never create a
path, of any kind, by which greywater and drinking water can occupy the
same pipe.** Not a temporary one, not a valved one, not one you promise to
be careful with. Separate pipes, separate fittings that do not mate,
everything labelled.

[Storing Water Safely](storing_water_safely.md) is the other half of this
habit, and the contrast between the two guides is the lesson. Clean stored
drinking water is rotated on something like a six month cycle. Untreated
greywater is not storable for twenty four hours. Six months against one
day: two liquids, both of them water, about a hundred and eighty times
apart in how long they keep. If those two ever share a container or a
pipe, the shorter number wins.

And for Tier 2 and Tier 3 the designer must consider site sensitivity, for
which the rule gives examples: shellfish growing areas, designated
swimming areas, wellhead protection areas for Group A public water
systems, and aquifer recharge areas critically important under the Growth
Management Act (WAC 246-274-410(2)(d)(i)). If you live on Puget Sound,
read that list again. The first item is the water you can see from the
road.

## What this means on the ground in Silverdale

The canonical place for these guides is Silverdale, at the head of Dyes
Inlet in Kitsap County, at about 47.65 degrees north. Several of the
requirements above land here in specific and checkable ways.

### The soil is probably the thing that stops you

The soil survey for the Silverdale area
(`data/locales/silverdale_wa/soil.json`, from the USDA NRCS SSURGO survey
of Kitsap County) says the dominant series is **Alderwood**, at 49.9
percent of mapped land. Nearly half the ground here is one soil.

Alderwood is a gravelly sandy loam over very gravelly sandy loam, sitting
on dense glacial till. The record gives the representative depth to that
restriction as **56 cm**, with a full mapped range of 50 to 102 cm. It
also notes a seasonal perched water table as shallow as 65 cm, and rates
the series very limited for septic tank absorption fields.

Now put that against the rule. For a Tier 2 or Tier 3 system the
shallowest legal placement of an irrigation line is four inches down, and
it needs twenty four inches of suitable soil beneath it, so the minimum
workable soil column is about 71 cm. The representative Alderwood profile
gives 56 cm. That is about 15 cm short, and it is short at the most
favourable possible placement of the line.

So on the single most common soil in Silverdale, at its representative
depth, **a Tier 2 or Tier 3 irrigation field does not have room to meet
the state separation requirement.** The top of the mapped range, 102 cm,
clearly does. Somewhere in between is a threshold, and which side of it
your particular garden sits on is not something a soil survey can tell
you.

A Tier 1 system is not measured against that twenty four inch figure, as
explained above, but it is not thereby in the clear: it still has to sit
in suitable soil, and a densic layer at 56 cm with perched water above it
is the definition of the thing suitable soil is defined against. The
difference is that for Tier 1 you are asking the health officer a
judgement question rather than failing an arithmetic test.

Which is exactly why the rule does not let you decide this from a map. A
Tier 2 or Tier 3 system requires a soil and site evaluation performed by a
qualified professional or the local health officer, and only a soil
scientist or those people may do it (WAC 246-274-420(1)). The survey
number is a reason to expect a problem, not a finding. If you take one
thing from this section, take this: **on Alderwood soil, have the
separation checked before you spend money on anything else**, because it
is the requirement most likely to end the project and the cheapest one to
test first.

The second most common series, Indianola at 10.3 percent, is loamy sand to
sand with no recorded restriction and very high conductivity. That solves
the separation problem and introduces the opposite one: the survey rates
it very limited for septic absorption fields because of seepage and
inadequate filtering. Soil is the treatment step. Soil that water races
through is not doing the step.

[Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) goes into what these
series are and how to tell which one you are standing on.

### The rain is on your side here, which is unusual

The Department of Health's guidance on irrigating plants with greywater
recommends periodically flushing the soil where it is high in clay, or in
areas receiving less than twenty inches of annual precipitation. The idea
is that rain washes accumulated salts down past the root zone, and where
there is not enough rain you have to irrigate deliberately with clean
water to do the same job.

Silverdale receives 1446 mm of precipitation a year
(`data/locales/silverdale_wa/climate.json`). That is about 57 inches,
nearly three times the twenty inch threshold, and it arrives mostly in
winter, when the greywater system is required to be switched off anyway.
December alone is 252 mm and January 236 mm.

This is a genuine local advantage and it is worth saying plainly: the
winter here does the salt flushing that a gardener in New Mexico has to do
on purpose. It does not make sodium a non-problem, for reasons in the next
section, but it does mean the accumulation story that dominates greywater
writing from the southwest is weaker on the Kitsap Peninsula than the
source material implies.

### The season you are allowed to use it is not the season you want it

Greywater systems may only run during the growing season, defined as
between the last frost of spring and the first frost of autumn, extendable
by greenhouse if the plants keep growing (WAC 246-274-009(6) and
246-274-011(1)(i)).

Locally the average last spring frost is March 29 and the average first
autumn frost is November 14, with a growing season of 227 days in the
locale climate record. So the legal window is roughly the start of April
to the middle of November.

But look at where the water shortage actually is. July receives 21 mm of
rain and August 28 mm: 49 mm across the two months, under two inches. That
is the dry season, and it is squarely inside the legal window, which is
good news. The trouble is the shoulders. In April, May, October and
November the ground here is already wet, evapotranspiration is low, and
the rule caps your application at the evapotranspiration rate. Applying
greywater to saturated Puget Sound soil in late October is how you get
surfacing, and surfacing is a regulatory failure as well as an exposure
one.

The practical shape of that: in this climate the diversion valve is not an
emergency device, it is the normal control you will use for most of the
year. A Silverdale greywater system is a July and August appliance that
happens to be legal for longer.

### Two local details that are easy to miss

First, three separate provisions of the rule allow a resident owner of a
single-family residence to do work that would otherwise need a
professional, and all three carry the same qualifier: **not adjacent to a
marine shoreline**. The owner may design the system (WAC
246-274-410(1)(a)), perform the soil and site evaluation (WAC
246-274-420(2)) and install it (WAC 246-274-430(2)), in each case only if
the residence is not on a marine shoreline and the system reuses no more
than 300 gallons per day. Silverdale sits at the head of Dyes Inlet. A
waterfront property here loses the do-it-yourself route in all three
places at once.

Second, shellfish growing areas are the first example the rule gives of a
sensitive site. Dyes Inlet drains through Port Washington Narrows into
Sinclair Inlet and then the main basin of Puget Sound
(`data/locales/silverdale_wa/water.json`). [Where Water Goes](where_water_goes.md)
follows that path in detail and explains why shellfish function as the
receipt for what a watershed's households did. Greywater applied here is
applied upstream of a shellfish resource, and the local health officer is
entitled to weigh that.

### The comparison nobody makes, which in this climate they should

The following is my reasoning from the numbers above rather than a
position any regulator has published, and it is offered as such.

Greywater is not the only non-potable water available for a garden. The
other one is rain, and Silverdale gets about 57 inches of it a year.

Set the two side by side honestly. Greywater's apparent advantage is that
it arrives all year, including in summer when no rain falls. But
Washington confines greywater irrigation to the growing season anyway, so
the winter supply is unusable, and the twenty four hour limit means you
cannot bank any of it for later. Greywater cannot be saved. Rainwater can:
that is the entire point of a cistern, and it is legal, ordinary and
uncontroversial.

So in this specific climate the comparison comes out lopsided. To irrigate
in July with greywater you need plumbing, a soil and site evaluation, in
most cases a permit and a qualified designer, a diversion valve, ongoing
records, an acceptance that you are putting detergent sodium and faecal
indicator organisms into your soil, and a set of restrictions on what you
may grow with it. To irrigate in July with rainwater you need a tank big
enough to carry water from a winter that delivers 252 mm in December
alone, and you need to have set it up before the rain stopped.

Greywater earns its complexity where water is genuinely scarce across the
whole year, which is why the best greywater literature comes from the
southwest, and why the department's own list of suitable plants reads like
a desert planting scheme. On the Kitsap Peninsula the scarcity is seasonal
and the rain is abundant, which is a different problem with an easier
answer.

None of which makes greywater reuse wrong here. It makes it the second
thing to try. [Collecting Rainwater](collecting_rainwater.md) covers the
first, including the same warning this guide has been making in a
different key: that water is not for drinking either.

## Food crops: the distinction is the edible part, not the plant

Washington's answer is one sentence and it is precise. Greywater may be
used for subsurface irrigation of plants that produce food, but it must
not come into contact with the edible portions of any plant (WAC
246-274-011(1)(b)).

So the rule is not "no food crops". It is "no contact with the part you
eat". That single distinction does most of the work, and it sorts real
plants into groups that are not intuitive.

**Generally compatible with the requirement.** Fruit and nut trees, cane
fruit, grapes, and anything where the edible part is held well above the
soil on a woody frame. The water goes into the root zone, the food is a
metre or more away in the air, and the contact the rule prohibits does not
occur in normal operation. This is where greywater irrigation makes the
most sense anyway, because established trees and canes want deep infrequent
water, which is what a subsurface line delivers.

**The problem group: root crops.** Carrots, beetroot, potatoes, radishes,
turnips, parsnips, onions. The edible portion of a root crop is, by
definition, in the ground, in the wetted zone, in direct contact with the
irrigation water. There is no way to subsurface-irrigate a carrot without
the greywater touching the carrot. A New Mexico State University extension
publication states the restriction in its sharpest form: root crops eaten
uncooked should not be irrigated with greywater. Under the Washington
requirement the concern is broader than eaten-uncooked, because the rule
prohibits the contact itself rather than prohibiting the eating.

**The other problem group: anything eaten raw and close to the ground.**
Lettuce, spinach, salad leaves, strawberries, low herbs. The edible part is
inches from the soil, the plants are usually harvested without cooking, and
soil splash onto leaves is normal. A properly buried line does not splash,
but a properly buried line that has begun to surface does, and surfacing is
a failure mode you may not notice for weeks under a mulch layer.

**The reasoning, which is worth having rather than just the rule.** Cooking
kills the pathogens that make greywater a health question. Salmonella and
Shigella do not survive a proper cook. So the hazard concentrates exactly
where food goes from soil to mouth without a heat step. That is why the
raw-eaten crops and the root crops are the ones that matter, and it is also
why the tidy version of this rule that people remember, "no greywater on
vegetables", is both too strict and too vague. An apple tree is fine. A
radish is not.

**Where the risk actually sits, and the honest ranking.** If you want one
rule that survives contact with a real garden: put greywater on the
perennials. Trees, shrubs, canes and ornamentals. Water the annual
vegetable beds with clean water. You lose almost nothing, because the
perennials are the thirsty deep-rooted things that benefit most from
subsurface water anyway, and you remove the entire category of failure at
once instead of managing it plant by plant.

[Growing Food You Can Live On](growing_food_you_can_live_on.md) covers what
is worth growing here; [Handwashing and Personal Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md)
covers the step that actually breaks the transmission chain after you have
been in the garden, which is the one people skip.

## What goes down the drain matters more than the plumbing

This is the part of the subject that ruins gardens, and it is the part
almost nobody thinks about when they are planning a system, because it
feels like a detail about shopping rather than a detail about engineering.

The Department of Health's review states the principle exactly: the amount
of pollutants carried by greywater varies depending on what goes down the
drain, and when people manage what goes down the drain, the amount of
chemicals and pathogens can be reduced significantly.

Your greywater system is a device that takes everything you pour into your
sinks and puts it in your soil. That is the whole machine.

### Sodium, and why it is the one that actually gets you

Laundry detergents contain salts, used as fillers and to improve cleaning.
How much varies a great deal by brand, and the department notes that top
loading machines designed to save water tend to produce higher
concentrations of pollutants, because the same salt load arrives in less
water.

The measure that matters is the **sodium adsorption ratio**, SAR, which
compares sodium against calcium and magnesium. The department's review,
drawing on an Australian study of thirty wash samples from fifteen
households, reports that irrigation waters with SAR above 6.0 are cause for
concern, and that approximately **two-thirds of all the laundry water
samples tested exceeded 6.0**. The review's conclusion from that study is
that long-term application of such water to a single location, without
adding calcium such as gypsum, could be expected to cause significant soil
structural damage.

Read that again: two thirds of ordinary household laundry water was above
the threshold of concern.

**Why sodium does this.** [What Soil Is](what_soil_is.md) explains that
soil is not just particles but particles arranged into aggregates, which
USDA calls peds when they formed through soil development, and that the
gaps between and inside those crumbs are what hold air and water and let
roots through. It also explains that clay particles are small and
plate-shaped. Both facts are the setup for this one.

What holds clay plates together into a crumb is the cations sitting on
their surfaces. Calcium and magnesium carry two positive charges each, so a
single ion can bridge between two clay surfaces and pull them together.
Sodium carries one. It cannot bridge, and it holds a larger shell of water
around itself, so where sodium takes the place of calcium on those surfaces
the plates stop being pulled together and start pushing apart. The crumbs
come apart into individual plates. That is dispersion.

The consequences follow from the geometry. The department's review
describes them: soils low in electrical conductivity but high in sodium
relative to other cations become unstable in water, hard surfaces form that
make it difficult for plants to germinate or grow, infiltration rates are
reduced, and waterlogging can result. Dispersed plates travel into the pores
and block them, so the structure that made the soil drain is gone and the
same soil now sheds water it used to absorb. The review quotes a researcher
it cites, Amoozegar, to the effect that the adverse impact of sodium on soil
hydraulic properties is well known, and reports that greywater including
kitchen wastewater was found detrimental to plant growth and damaging to
soil in testing.

This is the failure mode that makes greywater worth taking seriously even
if nobody ever gets sick from your system. It is slow, it is cumulative, it
is invisible for a long time, and it is difficult to reverse. Reversing it
means adding calcium, usually as gypsum, to displace the sodium, and then
leaching the displaced sodium away, which takes water and time.

And it connects back to a rule you have already read. **Water softener
waste is prohibited in greywater by name** (WAC 246-274-011(1)(d)). A
softener works by swapping calcium and magnesium out of your water and
sodium in, then flushing the captured calcium away in a brine. Its waste
stream is the highest-sodium, lowest-calcium water in the house: precisely
the worst possible SAR. The rule is not being fussy. It is excluding the
one appliance whose entire purpose produces the exact chemistry that
destroys soil structure.

**What to do about it.** The department's specific guidance is that liquid
detergents generally contain less sodium than powdered detergents and are
recommended when irrigating with greywater. Beyond that, the useful habits
are: rotate which area receives greywater rather than always hitting one
bed, and let the winter rain do the leaching, which in this climate it will.

### Boron, which is a narrower problem and a harder one

Boron is a plant nutrient with an unusually narrow window between too
little and too much. USDA Agricultural Research Service publishes crop
tolerance categories based on boron concentration in soil water:

| Category | Boron |
|---|---|
| Very sensitive | under 0.5 mg/L |
| Sensitive | 0.5 to 1.0 mg/L |
| Moderately sensitive | 1.0 to 2.0 mg/L |
| Moderately tolerant | 2.0 to 4.0 mg/L |
| Tolerant | 4.0 to 6.0 mg/L |
| Very tolerant | 6.0 to 15.0 mg/L |

ARS places citrus and stone fruits, along with onion and snap beans, in the
sensitive range, and alfalfa, sugar beet, tomato, cotton and asparagus
among the more tolerant. It also cautions that tolerances vary with
climate, soil and variety.

The household connection is borax and perborate bleaches, which are boron
compounds and appear in laundry products. The New Mexico State University
extension publication names sodium, bleach and borax together as things in
greywater that can damage plants.

**What I could not source, said plainly.** I could not find a measured
boron concentration for household laundry greywater from any regulator
whose page I was able to open. The Washington Department of Health's own
literature review, which covers salts, pH, nitrogen, phosphorus, fats and
oils and pathogens in detail, does not discuss boron at all. So the
sourced position is: boron has a narrow tolerance band, published crop
thresholds start below 0.5 mg/L for sensitive species, borax-containing
laundry products put boron into greywater, and nobody in the sources I
opened has published what concentration that produces in a typical
household's wash water. Avoid borax-based products if you are irrigating
with the result. Treat any specific number you see quoted for boron in
greywater as unverified until you find its source.

### Bleach, and the rest of the cleaning cupboard

The rule prohibits cleaning chemicals and hazardous household products
generally. The department's review reinforces it from the other side,
concluding that the lower-pollutant subset of greywater is lower-pollutant
especially when people avoid cleaners containing harsh chemicals, bleaches,
disinfectants or phosphates.

There is a real conflict to be aware of here, and it is not a small one.
Bleach and disinfectants exist to kill microorganisms. Soil function
depends on microorganisms. A greywater system delivers your cleaning
products directly to the living part of your soil, bypassing every
treatment step that would otherwise degrade them. The same is true of a
septic system, which is why EPA's septic guidance warns that pouring toxins
down the drain can kill the organisms that treat your waste. A greywater
system has even less buffer than a septic tank, because it has no tank.

### The rest of the chemistry, briefly

From the department's review:

- **pH** of greywater runs from 6.5 to 8.7. It is typically alkaline
  because of soaps and detergents. This is the mechanism behind the
  department's plant guidance discussed below.
- **Nitrogen** is not a significant concern in greywater, because most of
  the nitrogen in household wastewater comes from urine and urine is not
  in greywater.
- **Phosphorus** does matter, and its main source in greywater is
  dishwashing detergent. Phosphorus drives algal growth in fresh water.
- **Fats, oils and grease** are highest in kitchen greywater; the review
  cites a study finding roughly a tenfold increase in oils and greases
  from kitchen waste over all other greywater sources. Grease is also what
  clogs a subsurface irrigation system, which is a maintenance failure
  before it is an environmental one.

### The plants that will not tolerate it, which is awkward here

The Department of Health publishes a list of plants poorly suited to
greywater irrigation, and the pattern is that shade-loving and acid-loving
plants do badly, because they evolved in the acid forest soils and
greywater is alkaline.

The unsuitable list includes azaleas, begonias, bleeding hearts, crepe
myrtle, deodar cedar, dogwood, ferns, foxgloves, gardenias, holly,
hydrangeas, impatiens, magnolias, oxalis, primroses, redwoods,
rhododendrons and violets.

Read that list as a Pacific Northwest gardener and wince. Rhododendrons,
ferns, dogwood, holly, hydrangeas, azaleas, primroses: that is not a list
of exotics, that is a description of a Kitsap garden and a good deal of the
surrounding forest understorey. The rhododendron is Washington's state
flower.

The better-suited list is drier-country planting: Austrian pine, bearded
iris, big basin sage, burning bush, cottonwood, fringed sage, honeysuckle,
Italian stone pine, junipers, mugo pine, many native desert plants, oaks,
rabbit brush, roses, rosemary, Russian olive, sedum and sumac.

So the uncomfortable local conclusion is that the plants greywater suits
best are substantially not the plants that grow here by default, and the
plants that define the local landscape are on the avoid list. That does not
make greywater useless in western Washington. It does mean the fantasy
version, where you quietly route the shower onto the existing shrubbery and
everything is better, is the wrong picture. You would be irrigating
rhododendrons with alkaline water.

The department adds two warnings worth repeating exactly. Some harmful
effects are not visible immediately and may take one or two years to
appear. And if you see signs of stress in irrigated plants, stop using
greywater on them.

That first one is the reason this section is long. A system that is
damaging your soil looks exactly like a system that is working, for about
two growing seasons.

## Permits, tiers and who actually decides

### The three tiers, with the numbers

| | Tier 1 | Tier 2 | Tier 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Greywater grade | Light only | Light only | Light or dark |
| Flow limit | 60 gal/day | under 3,500 gal/day | under 3,500 gal/day |
| Storage | None permitted | Up to 24 hours | Any, with treatment |
| Distribution | Gravity, no pumps | Even, typically pressure | Even, typically pressure |
| Treatment | None | None | Required |
| Approval | Registration may be required | Permit required | Permit required |

A treatment component becomes mandatory, moving you to Tier 3, in five
situations (WAC 246-274-300(2)): dark greywater, storage beyond twenty four
hours, irrigating a green roof, serving a high public exposure area such as
a playground or sports field, or whenever the local health officer decides
treatment is needed to protect public health or water quality. Where
treatment is required for light greywater the product must meet NSF/ANSI
Standard 350-1; for dark greywater, NSF/ANSI Standard 40 (WAC
246-274-400).

### Tier 1 in practice, and the arithmetic that decides whether it fits

Tier 1 is the accessible option: light greywater, sixty gallons a day or
less, from a single-family residence, through a single diversion point, by
gravity, no storage, at least four inches of cover. You may install up to
two separate Tier 1 systems from two diversion points, with combined flows
of 120 gallons per day or less, and no single field may exceed sixty (WAC
246-274-100(2)).

You must keep a record of the system showing where it is, which fixtures
feed it, how the design meets the requirements, its maintenance needs and
the irrigation area calculation, recorded on the department's Greywater
System Checklist and Irrigation Area Estimation Tool. The local health
officer may require you to register it by filing that record, and may
require extra review if you install two systems or if your property is on
an on-site sewage system with design flows under 3,500 gallons per day
(WAC 246-274-100(1)(m), (n) and (3)).

Does sixty gallons a day fit a real household? The rule itself gives you
the numbers to check, in the fixture design flow table it provides for when
actual flows are unknown (WAC 246-274-410(2)(b)(ii)): water-conserving
washing machine 8 gallons per person per day, traditional 11; laundry sink
3; water-conserving bathroom sink 5.4, traditional 6; water-conserving
shower 10, traditional 17; bathtub 24 gallons per bath; kitchen sink 6;
dishwasher 1.

Take a two person household and total the light greywater fixtures:

- With water-conserving fixtures: 10 plus 5.4 plus 8 equals 23.4 gallons
  per person per day, so 46.8 gallons for two people. Inside the sixty
  gallon limit.
- With traditional fixtures: 17 plus 6 plus 11 equals 34 gallons per person
  per day, so 68 gallons for two people. Over the limit.

So Tier 1 fits a small, water-conserving household and does not fit a
conventional one, and whether it fits yours turns on your shower head. Note
also that these are the *totals available*; you do not have to route all of
them. Diverting only the washing machine, which is the single most common
approach, gives 16 gallons a day for two people on a conserving machine,
comfortably inside Tier 1.

For scale on the other side, the Department of Health puts household
greywater at up to forty gallons per person each day, which is the largest
single portion of a home's wastewater. Two people at that rate produce
eighty gallons, more than one Tier 1 system can take. Greywater reuse at
this tier captures a slice of your greywater, not all of it.

### Tier 2 and Tier 3, and the septic catch

Both require a permit from the local health officer before installation
(WAC 246-274-200(2) and 246-274-300(4)), a soil and site evaluation (WAC
246-274-420), design by a qualified professional in most cases (WAC
246-274-410(1)), installation only by someone the health officer has
approved (WAC 246-274-430(1)), and inspection before the system is covered
(WAC 246-274-435).

Here is the catch that affects most rural Kitsap properties. Tier 2 and
Tier 3 systems are written for buildings served by a public sewer or a
*large* on-site sewage system, meaning one with design flows between 3,500
and 100,000 gallons per day. An ordinary house on an ordinary septic system
is well under that. The route back in is a carve-out: if the building is
served by an on-site sewage system with design flows under 3,500 gallons
per day, the greywater must originate from a single-family residence and
must not exceed **300 gallons per day** (WAC 246-274-200(1)(e) and
246-274-300(3)(e)).

That 300 gallon figure is the one to remember, because it recurs. It is
also the ceiling under which a resident owner may design, evaluate and
install their own system, provided the residence is not adjacent to a
marine shoreline, and the threshold under which the health officer may
reduce permitting to registration when a qualified professional designed a
Tier 2 system for a single-family residence (WAC 246-274-425(2)(d)).

The rule also requires that diverting greywater must not adversely affect
the functioning of the septic system it is diverted from (WAC
246-274-410(2)(c)), and separately that using a greywater system changes
none of the design, capacity or reserve area requirements for that septic
system (WAC 246-274-005(2)). You do not get a smaller septic system for
having a greywater system.

### The question you have to ask before any of this

Here is the part that is easy to miss and would waste the most of your
time. **Chapter 246-274 WAC may not be in force in your county.**

The rule took effect on July 31, 2011, and required local boards of health
to implement it within three years. But it also provides that if a local
board of health cannot adjust its resources to implement and enforce the
chapter, the older on-site sewage rules in chapter 246-272A WAC continue to
apply to greywater reuse for subsurface irrigation in that jurisdiction
(WAC 246-274-007(1) and (2)). The Department of Health's own page tells
readers to check with their local health jurisdiction to learn whether
greywater reuse under chapter 246-274 WAC is allowed there, or to ask how
to support that effort if it is not allowed yet.

And local boards may adopt requirements more stringent than the state's
(WAC 246-274-007(4)), with Tier 1 owners expressly required to comply with
any more stringent local regulations including design and permitting (WAC
246-274-100(4)).

So there are three possible answers to "can I do this here": yes under the
tier system, yes but only as an on-site sewage system under the older
chapter, or yes with additional local requirements on top. You cannot tell
which from reading state law.

The local health jurisdiction here is the **Kitsap Public Health District**.
Their Drinking Water and Onsite Sewage program is at 345 6th Street, Suite
300, Bremerton, WA 98337, telephone 360-728-2235, open weekdays 9 a.m. to
4 p.m.

### Ongoing obligations, once it exists

For Tier 2 and Tier 3 (WAC 246-274-440), the owner must get approval before
altering or expanding the system, protect it from surface drainage
including footing and roof drains, ensure the greywater keeps coming from
the correct fixtures, and either repair problems promptly or divert to the
sewer or septic until repaired. At property transfer the owner must give
the buyer the system record and any maintenance records, in addition to
the seller disclosure statement Washington requires. If the system is
abandoned or removed, the owner must notify the local health officer in
writing.

And if any system of any tier fails or is suspected of failing, the owner
must immediately divert the greywater to the sewer or septic system, and
nobody may use the greywater system until the failure is corrected (WAC
246-274-445). Note that standard: *suspected* of failing. You do not get to
wait for confirmation.

## What can go wrong

**Greywater surfacing.** The most common and most serious failure. Causes:
application faster than the soil accepts, saturated ground, a blocked line
forcing water back, settling that left the line too shallow, or a field
sized for plants that have since died. Consequence: direct human and animal
contact with water carrying faecal indicator organisms at the levels
described earlier. Response is not optional, it is the rule: divert
immediately, stop using the system, fix it before restarting.

**A storage tank that has gone septic.** You will smell it, because
hydrogen sulfide is unmistakable. It means the water went anaerobic, which
means it was held too long. The fix is not deodoriser, it is not holding it
that long.

**Kitchen water sneaking in.** A plumber ties the kitchen sink into the
greywater line because it was convenient, and your Tier 1 or Tier 2 system
is now handling dark greywater, which is illegal without treatment and
brings tenfold grease, the phosphorus load, and the sodium that was proven
detrimental to plants in testing. This is the change most likely to happen
without anyone thinking of it as a change.

**A prohibited input entering routinely.** Somebody starts washing nappies,
a water softener gets installed, a hobby moves into the utility sink, a car
part gets degreased in the laundry tub. The plumbing did not change but the
water did, and what is coming out of it is no longer legally greywater.

**Slow sodium damage.** No symptom for a season or two, then poor
infiltration, crusting, water sitting where it used to soak away, and
plants doing worse each year on more water. Two thirds of laundry samples
in the study the state relied on were above the SAR threshold of concern,
so this is the default trajectory rather than an unlucky one. Rotating the
irrigated area and letting winter rain leach it are the practical defences
here; gypsum is the remedial one.

**Irrigating the wrong plants.** Alkaline water onto rhododendrons, ferns,
azaleas and the rest of the acid-loving list. Symptoms may take one to two
years to appear.

**Running it out of season.** October here is wet, evapotranspiration is
low and the soil has no capacity. The rule confines use to the growing
season, and the season ends before the calendar suggests.

**Assuming greywater reduces your septic requirement.** It does not. The
rule says so twice.

**Building it before checking how much soil is actually under the field.**
On Alderwood soil, which is about half of Silverdale, the representative
profile does not have room for the twenty four inch separation a Tier 2 or
Tier 3 system needs, and is marginal for the suitable-soil requirement
every tier needs. This is the cheapest thing to check and the most likely
to end the project. Check it first.

## How you know it worked

- You can state, without looking it up, which fixtures feed the system, and
  the kitchen is not among them unless you have a treatment component.
- The diversion valve is reachable without moving anything, is visibly
  labelled, and you have actually operated it.
- Pipes and any above-ground tank carry the nonpotable water warning.
- Nothing is wet on the surface anywhere over the field, in any weather,
  checked by walking it rather than by looking out of a window.
- You know the separation distance between your irrigation line and the
  first restrictive layer beneath it, because somebody dug and looked.
- The plants over the field are the same or better after two full seasons,
  not just after one. Water still soaks in at the same rate it did.
- You have the system record, and it is current with what is actually
  buried out there rather than what was drawn.
- You have spoken to the Kitsap Public Health District and know which rule
  applies to you here.
- You have a season, each year, when the valve is turned to the sewer and
  stays there.
