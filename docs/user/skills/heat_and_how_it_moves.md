# Heat and How It Moves

Heat is always on the move, from hot things to cold ones. A pot handle
warms because heat creeps along the metal. A room warms because hot air
rises off the stove and spreads across the ceiling. Your face warms in
front of a fire because heat crosses the gap as invisible light. And
your skin cools in a breeze because water leaves it and takes heat with
it. Those four ways are nearly the whole subject, and once you can tell
them apart you can use each one on purpose: to cook with less fuel, to
keep food cold without power, to keep a person warm or cool, and to
know why a parked car in the sun can kill a child.

The physics in this guide comes from the Department of Energy's
*Fundamentals Handbook: Thermodynamics, Heat Transfer, and Fluid Flow*
(1992), written to teach plant operators with no background, and from
the US Geological Survey's Water Science School. The safety advice comes
from the Consumer Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the US Fire
Administration, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the
Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the National
Weather Service and the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). All are
United States government publications, in the public domain. Where
something is general practice, arithmetic or our own reasoning rather
than a published rule, the text says so.

This guide is about heat everywhere. Some neighbours go deeper on one
part of it: [Insulation and Heat Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md) is
heat leaving a building, [Heating Water](heating_water.md) is the water
heater and its setting, [Firewood](firewood.md) is burning wood and
chimneys, [Cold and Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) is the body
losing heat, and [Treating Burns](treating_burns.md) is what to do when
heat has done its damage.

## First: where heat hurts people

- **Hot water and steam.** The CPSC says most tap water scald injuries
  and deaths are to the elderly and to children under five. Its figures:
  "Most adults will suffer third-degree burns if exposed to 150 degree
  water for two seconds" (150 F is 66 C). Burns also occur in six
  seconds at 140 F (60 C) and in thirty seconds at 130 F (54 C), and
  "Even if the temperature is 120 degrees" (49 C), "a five minute
  exposure could result in third-degree burns." It says to hand-test
  water before bathing infants and young children, and never to leave a
  child alone in the bathroom. Setting the water heater is in [Heating
  Water](heating_water.md). Steam is worse than boiling water, for a
  reason shown in the worked examples below; lift a pot lid so the steam
  comes out on the side away from you (general practice).
- **Water heated in a microwave.** The FDA warns that water heated by
  itself in a clean cup can pass its boiling point without looking as if
  it is boiling, and then "a slight disturbance or movement such as
  picking up the cup, or pouring in a spoon full of instant coffee, may
  result in a violent eruption with the boiling water exploding out of
  the cup." Adding instant coffee or sugar before heating "greatly
  reduces this risk," and the oven's own heating times should be
  followed. The FDA adds that most injuries from microwave ovens are
  burns "from hot containers, overheated foods, or exploding liquids,"
  not radiation.
- **Cooking fires.** The US Fire Administration says cooking is "by far,
  the leading cause of home fires and home fire injuries." Its rules:
  stand by your pan, and turn the burner off if you leave the kitchen; if
  you see smoke or the grease starts to boil, turn the burner off; turn
  pot handles towards the back of the stove so nobody can bump them or
  pull them over; and keep a pan lid or baking sheet nearby to cover a
  pan that catches fire. The CPSC: "If a grease fire starts, smother the
  flames by sliding a lid over the pan and turning off the burner,"
  "Never use water on a grease fire," keep children and pets at least
  three feet from the cooking area, and "Have a fire extinguisher nearby
  and know how to use it." Leave a burning pan where it is, lid on and
  heat off; carrying it spills burning oil (general practice). For a
  fire inside the oven, the fire administration says to turn the oven
  off and let it cool, and if the flames get out of the oven, to leave
  the home and call 911. (Why
  water is wrong: it sinks under the hot oil, boils at once and throws
  the burning oil out of the pan. That explanation is general knowledge.)
- **Heaters.** The US Fire Administration says to keep anything that can
  burn at least 3 feet (about 1 m) from every heat source, including
  fireplaces, stoves, radiators, space heaters and candles; to turn
  space heaters off when leaving the room or going to bed; and "Never
  use an oven to heat your home." It reports that portable heaters were
  3 percent of home heating fires in 2017 to 2019 but 41 percent of the
  fatal ones. The EPA says the same about the gas stove: "Never use a gas
  stove to heat your home." Anything that burns fuel indoors can make
  carbon monoxide; that hazard is in [Ventilation, Damp and
  Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md).
- **Too much heat in the body.** OSHA lists the signs of heat exhaustion
  as fatigue, irritability, thirst, nausea or vomiting, dizziness or
  lightheadedness, heavy sweating, and a raised body temperature or fast
  heart rate. The signs of heat stroke are confusion, slurred speech,
  unconsciousness, seizures, heavy sweating or hot dry skin, a very high
  body temperature and a rapid heart rate. Its rule for any of them:
  "When any of these symptoms is present, promptly provide first aid. Do
  not try to diagnose which illness is occurring," because the illnesses
  overlap and can worsen quickly; someone with symptoms should stop
  working. Its first aid: move the person somewhere cooler, such as shade
  or air conditioning, and cool them at once. Putting them in cold water
  or an ice bath is "the best method to cool workers rapidly in an
  emergency"; the other ways it lists are taking off outer clothing,
  ice or cold wet towels on the head, neck, trunk, armpits and groin,
  and fans moving air over them. "Never leave a worker with heat-related
  illness alone." And: "When in doubt, call 911!" Confusion, slurred
  speech or unconsciousness are heat stroke, and mean calling 911
  immediately and cooling the person until help arrives. OSHA writes for
  workers; the body is the same at home. Someone who is fully awake can
  sip cool water, but never try to give a drink to someone who is
  confused or not fully awake; they can choke (general first aid
  practice).
- **Cars in the sun.** The National Weather Service: "It is NEVER safe to
  leave a child, disabled person or pet locked in a car, even in the
  winter." It says children can die in 10 minutes, and that if you have
  a toddler at home, you should lock your cars even in your own driveway,
  because children get into them to play.
- **Burns.** The first aid is in [Treating Burns](treating_burns.md):
  stop the burning, take off rings and anything tight, and cool the burn
  under cool running water for twenty minutes, never ice.

## Heat is not the same as temperature

The Department of Energy's handbook starts here, because people mix
them up. **Temperature** "is a measure of the amount of energy possessed
by the molecules of a substance." It tells you how hot something is, and
which way heat will flow. **Heat** "is energy in transit": energy moving
because one thing is hotter than another.

Heat always flows from the hotter thing to the colder one, never back
the other way on its own; that is the Second Law of Thermodynamics. The
handbook's example: two blocks of metal at different temperatures,
insulated from everything else and put in contact, exchange heat until
they reach the same temperature, and then heat transfer stops. Nothing
is lost; energy has moved from one block to the other.

### How much heat it takes

The handbook's unit is the British thermal unit (Btu): the heat that
raises one pound of water by one degree Fahrenheit. The US Geological
Survey gives the metric figure: a kilogram of water "has to absorb 4,184
Joules of heat" to warm by 1 C, while "it only takes 385 Joules of heat
to raise 1 kilogram of copper" by the same degree. So, kilogram for
kilogram, water takes nearly eleven times as much heat as copper to warm
by the same amount (arithmetic).

That property, which the handbook calls **specific heat**, explains a lot.
Water is slow to heat and slow to cool, which is why a pot of soup stays
hot so long and why a hot-water bottle works (our examples). The
Geological Survey uses it to explain climate: oceans and lakes take
longer to heat and to cool than land, so coastal places have milder
temperatures. Its example is Nebraska, with colder winters and hotter
summers than Oregon, though Oregon is farther north, because Oregon has
the Pacific beside it.

The handbook also separates two kinds of heat. **Sensible heat** changes
an object's temperature. **Latent heat** changes its state, ice to water or
water to steam, and "When latent heat is added, no temperature change
occurs." That one sentence explains boiling, sweating and steam burns,
below.

## Conduction: through solid things

The handbook describes three ways heat moves. The first: "Conduction
involves the transfer of heat by the interactions of atoms or molecules
of a material through which the heat is being transferred." It is how
heat moves through a solid. Its rate depends on the "temperature
difference and the resistance to heat transfer", and that resistance
"is dependent upon the nature and dimensions" of what the heat passes
through. In plain terms: a bigger temperature difference pushes heat
faster, a thicker layer slows it, and some materials pass it far more
easily than others.

How easily is measured as **thermal conductivity**, which the handbook
defines as "a measure of a substance's ability to transfer heat through a
solid by conduction." The spread between materials is enormous. The
Forest Products Laboratory's figures, tabled in [Insulation and Heat
Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md), put steel at about 310 units against
0.7 to 1.0 for softwood lumber: steel passes heat several hundred times
faster than wood (arithmetic from that table).

What you can do with that:

- **Why metal feels colder than wood in the same room.** Both are at the
  same temperature. The metal draws heat out of your hand far faster, so
  your skin cools, and that is what you feel (our explanation from the
  handbook's definitions).
- **Handles.** Wooden and plastic handles stay cool because they conduct
  slowly; a metal handle, or a cast iron pan's handle, does not. Use a
  dry pot holder. A wet one passes heat quickly and can turn to steam
  against your hand (general practice).
- **Thickness.** Doubling an insulating layer roughly halves the heat
  through it, which is the whole idea of insulation (our reading of the
  handbook's "dimensions"; the building version is in [Insulation and
  Heat Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md)).

## Convection: carried by moving air and water

"Convection involves the transfer of heat by the mixing and motion of
macroscopic portions of a fluid," the handbook's second definition. Air
and water carry heat with them as they move.

It describes two kinds. **Natural convection** happens when the moving is
"caused by density variations resulting from temperature differences
within the fluid": warm air or water is lighter and rises, cool sinks.
Its example is a hot water radiator warming a room. **Forced convection**
happens when an outside force moves the fluid, "such as a pump"; at home,
a fan, a breeze or a spoon stirring a pot (our examples).

The handbook also explains why moving air takes heat faster than still
air. Next to any surface is a thin layer of fluid that hardly moves, and
heat has to get across it. Among the things that set how thick that film
is, it lists fluid velocity: faster flow, thinner film, more heat moved
(our summary of its "stagnant film" passage). That is why a breeze cools
you, why wind makes cold weather more dangerous, as [Cold and
Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) explains, and why OSHA's first aid
for heat illness includes fans.

What you can do with that:

- **Warm air rises.** Upstairs and near the ceiling are warmer than the
  floor (our example of natural convection). The draughts this drives
  through a whole house are in [Insulation and Heat
  Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md).
- **Stir to share heat.** Stirring a pot moves hot liquid from the bottom
  and cool liquid to it, so it heats evenly and does not catch on the
  bottom (general practice).
- **Still air insulates.** Clothing, blankets and bedding work by
  trapping air so it cannot carry heat away (general knowledge; [Cold
  and Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) has the details).

## Radiation: across open space

"Radiation, or radiant heat transfer, involves the transfer of heat by
electromagnetic radiation that arises due to the temperature of a body."
The handbook says most of it is in the infrared, invisible to the eye,
and gives the example everyone knows: "The transfer of heat from a
fireplace across a room in the line of sight is an example of radiant
heat transfer." It needs nothing to travel through: radiant heat
transfer "does not need a medium, such as air or metal, to take place,"
and "Any material that has a temperature above absolute zero gives off
some radiant energy." Its everyday proof: "When a cloud covers the sun,
both its heat and light diminish."

Real surfaces radiate less than the ideal the handbook calls a black
body; how much less is the surface's **emissivity**, a number between 0
and 1. Shiny metal surfaces radiate and absorb little, which is how a
foil-faced product works when it faces an air space (the details, from
the FTC's insulation rule, are in [Insulation and Heat
Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md)).

What you can do with that:

- **Line of sight.** Radiant heat warms only what it can see. The side of
  you facing a fire is hot and your back is cold, and anything in a
  heater's line of sight can be heated until it catches, which is what
  the 3-foot rule above protects against (our reading).
- **Shade.** Shade stops the sun's radiation, which is why it can feel so
  much cooler than the sunny spot beside it even though the air is the
  same (our example).
- **Clear nights.** On a clear, calm night, the ground and everything on
  it radiate heat to the open sky and cool below the air above them,
  which is how frost forms on a night the thermometer said was above
  freezing. [The Growing Calendar](the_growing_calendar.md) explains
  this with NOAA's figures, and [Extending the
  Season](extending_the_season.md) covers what a cover does about it.

## Evaporation, and the heat you cannot see

The handbook counts three ways heat moves, but in daily life a fourth
matters as much: water carrying heat away as it turns to vapour. The
Geological Survey: "evaporation removes heat from the environment,
leading to a net cooling." Its example: an alcohol wipe on your arm
feels cold because the alcohol evaporates and takes heat from your skin
with it.

The amount is large. The Geological Survey gives the heat of
vaporization of water as "540 calories/gram, or 2,260
kilojoules/kilogram," which it says is "about five times the energy
needed for heating water from the freezing point to the boiling point."
Melting ice takes 80 calories a gram. Those are latent heats: the water
changes state without changing temperature.

That is how sweat cools you: each gram that evaporates from your skin
takes its share of that heat. When the air is already humid, sweat
evaporates more slowly and cools you less, which is why a humid heat is
harder on the body than a dry one (general physics). And it is why wet
clothes chill a person in the cold, as [Cold and
Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) explains.

## Boiling, pressure and height

The handbook calls the temperature at which water starts to boil its
saturation temperature, and ties it to pressure: for water at 14.7
pounds per square inch, ordinary sea-level air pressure, it is 212 F
(100 C), and "The higher the pressure, the higher the saturation
temperature."

Three practical things follow:

- **A boiling pot does not get hotter.** Once water is boiling, more heat
  goes into making steam, latent heat with "no temperature change." A
  pot at a hard boil is no hotter than one at a gentle boil; it just
  boils away faster and uses more fuel (our application of the handbook).
  A simmer, with only a few small bubbles, is usually a little below
  boiling, so it is cooler than either (general cooking knowledge).
- **Water boils cooler up a mountain,** where the air pressure is lower
  (our application). That is why cooking takes longer at altitude, and
  why tested canning times change with altitude, as [Why Canning Has
  Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md) explains from the USDA.
- **A pressure canner works by raising the pressure,** which raises the
  boiling point above what an open pot can reach (our application;
  the canning guide has the temperatures and the rules).

## Worked examples

**A pot of water for cooking** (arithmetic from the Geological Survey's
figures). Heating 2 litres of water (2 kg) from 15 C to boiling takes 2 x
4,184 x 85, about 711,000 joules, or 0.2 kilowatt hours. Boiling those 2
litres away completely would take 2 x 2,260,000, about 4,520,000 joules,
or 1.26 kilowatt hours: more than six times as much. So once a pot
boils, nearly all the extra fuel goes into steam. A lid keeps much of
that steam, and its heat, in the pot, and turning a hard boil down to a
gentle one cooks at the same temperature for less fuel (general
practice, from the physics).

**Why steam burns worse than boiling water** (arithmetic from the same
figures). A gram of boiling water that lands on skin and cools to about
40 C gives up about 60 calories. A gram of steam first condenses, giving
up 540 calories, and then cools the same way: about 600 calories, ten
times as much. That is why a jet of steam from a kettle spout or a pot
lid scalds so badly. Keep hands and faces clear of steam vents, and
open lids away from you (general practice).

**Ice in a cooler** (arithmetic from the Geological Survey's 80 calories
a gram). Melting a kilogram of ice absorbs about 335 kilojoules, as much
heat as it takes to warm that same kilogram of water from 0 C to 80 C.
So ice keeps a cooler cold far longer than the same weight of cold
water. Then deal with each way heat gets in: keep the lid shut, so warm
air cannot replace the cold (convection); keep it in the shade
(radiation); and stand it on something that insulates rather than hot
ground (conduction). That last list is our application of the physics.

**A drink that stays hot** (our application of the handbook's
definitions). A vacuum flask has a vacuum between two walls. Conduction
needs a material and convection needs a fluid, so the vacuum stops both
across the gap; radiation can still cross it, but silvered walls radiate
very little. Some heat still leaks out through the neck, where the two
walls join, and through the stopper. All three ways in this guide are
cut at once, which is why the drink cools slowly rather than not at
all.

## Using heat on purpose at home

A short list of habits that follow from the four ways, all general
practice from the physics above:

- **Cook with lids, and keep a gentle boil rather than a hard one.** It
  saves fuel without cooking any cooler.
- **Dry things with air and sun.** Laundry and firewood dry fastest where
  air moves over them and the sun reaches them; the details for wood
  are in [Firewood](firewood.md).
- **Keep cool with shade, moving air and water.** Block the sun before it
  gets in, move air across your skin, and let water evaporate from it.
  In serious heat, follow OSHA's signs and first aid above.
- **Keep warm by trapping still air and staying dry.** Layers, a hat, out
  of the wind, and dry clothes. More in [Cold and
  Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md).
- **Stop the heat where it leaves.** In a house, seal the draughts first
  and then insulate; the order is explained in [Insulation and Heat
  Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Gas appliances, burners, heat exchangers and flues.** Annual
  professional inspection, as [Keeping Things
  Working](keeping_things_working.md) sets out from the US Fire
  Administration and ENERGY STAR.
- **Chimneys,** cleaned and inspected each year by a professional, says
  the US Fire Administration.
- **Anyone confused, slurring, collapsed or fitting in the heat:** call
  911 and start cooling them (OSHA).
- **A burn on the list that needs professional care**, in [Treating
  Burns](treating_burns.md).
- **A carbon monoxide alarm going off:** everyone out, then call for help;
  see [Ventilation, Damp and Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md).
- **A fire that is more than a pan you can cover,** or an oven fire that
  has escaped the oven: leave and call 911 (US Fire Administration).

## How the game models it

The game models one kind of heat in real detail, the heat of your own
body, and one more simply: a heater's heat in the air of a room aboard.

- **Your body's heat balance.** The body heat system is based on a
  published model of human temperature regulation, the two-node model
  that the ASHRAE comfort standards use. Your body makes heat, more when
  you walk or sprint than when you stand or sleep, and loses it in the
  ways this guide describes: to moving air by convection, to your
  surroundings by radiation, and by sweat evaporating, which costs you
  water. Your body answers the way a real one does: blood flow to the
  skin narrows in the cold and widens in the heat, you sweat, and you
  shiver, and shivering tires after hours.
- **Clothes add insulation.** Everyday clothes count as 0.61 clo (the
  standard measure of clothing insulation), and worn gear adds its own
  figure from `data/equipment.csv`: 0.36 for a windbreaker, 0.55 for an
  insulated winter jacket, 0.70 for a heavy overcoat.
- **Wind, rain and shelter.** Out in the weather, the wind takes heat
  faster, and rain soaks your clothes, which then insulate less and chill
  you as they dry. Under a roof you are out of the rain, and out of the
  sun and the cold sky: the roof shades you. Walls around you cut the
  wind. The HUD's shelter line tells you which: "Sheltered", "Out of the
  rain and the wind", or how much of the wind still gets in.
- **The sun and the sky.** In the open, the game counts radiation from
  the sun by day and to a clear sky by night. When that makes the air
  feel at least 2 C warmer or colder than the thermometer says, the
  weather line on the HUD adds what it feels like, such as "feels 35C"
  in the noon sun.
- **Indoors aboard.** Inside your home aboard the station the air is
  sealed and still, at the home's own temperature, and the weather line
  says so: "Indoors", with the temperature and "still air". In a grow
  room it is that room's own air, warmer or damper than the rest.
- **A heater warms the room it stands in.** A space heater you build and
  place aboard puts its 1,500 W into the air of the room it stands in, and
  that air settles where the heater's watts and the heat the room loses
  balance: by conduction through its walls and ceiling, and by convection
  with the air it trades with the rest of the home
  (`src/systems/farming/heat.rs`). In a small room that is a few degrees,
  held at 24 C by its thermostat; in a big glass greenhouse a fraction of
  one. Your body feels it when you stand there. [Heating a Home
  Safely](heating_a_home_safely.md) has the numbers.
- **Two modes.** Settings > Gameplay > Body heat. Forgiving, the default,
  runs the same physics but your temperature swings half as far from
  normal, so harm waits until the real balance would have you below about
  27 C or above about 43 C, and then comes at half the rate for each
  degree you read past 32 C or 40 C (the setting's own hint explains it
  the same way). Realistic is the real balance. In either, the Inventory page always shows your core
  temperature, the HUD shows it once it leaves the normal range, and the
  game gives you the conditions Shivering, Hypothermia (below 35 C,
  which slows you), Heat Exhaustion (above 39 C, which also slows you)
  and Heatstroke (above 40 C, which slows you more and does lasting
  harm). Body heat runs in every play mode, the default Normal among
  them; only the developer flying mode, a tool of the Dev play mode,
  switches it off.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: apart
from a heated room losing heat through its walls, nothing in the game
conducts heat through a solid, so there are no hot handles and no metal
that feels colder than wood; there are no scalds, no steam,
no grease fires and no superheated water; heaters set nothing alight;
and a parked vehicle never heats up in the sun. The body heat model is
the honest part. The rest of the heat in this guide is yours to learn
outside.

## You own this when

- You can explain the difference between heat and temperature, and why
  heat flows only from hot to cold.
- You can name the four ways heat moves (conduction, convection,
  radiation and evaporation) and give an example of each from your own
  home.
- You know how long hot water takes to scald, and you hand-test a child's
  bath.
- You stand by your pan, keep a lid within reach, turn handles inwards,
  and would never put water on a grease fire.
- You keep everything that burns 3 feet from heaters, turn space heaters
  off when you leave or sleep, and never heat a home with an oven or a
  gas stove.
- You know the signs of heat exhaustion and heat stroke, give first aid
  at the first of them without waiting to tell which it is, call 911
  for confusion, slurred speech or collapse, and start cooling at once.
- You never leave a child, a disabled person or a pet in a car.
- You can explain why steam burns worse than boiling water, and why a
  boiling pot does not get hotter.
- You can work out the heat to warm a pot of water from the Geological
  Survey's 4,184 joules per kilogram per degree.
- You can keep a cooler cold and a drink hot by blocking each way heat
  moves.
