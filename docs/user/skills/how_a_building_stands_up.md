# How a Building Stands Up

A building is a path. The weight of the roof, the snow on it, the people
and furniture on the floors, the push of the wind and the shove of an
earthquake all have to travel through the building, piece by piece and
joint by joint, until they reach the ground. Every part of the structure
is either carrying some of that load on its way down, or it is not, and
the difference matters the moment you want to cut a hole, take out a
wall, add a heavy stove or wonder whether a sagging floor is a problem.

Once you can see the path, a lot of building stops being mysterious. You
can tell why a floor joist stands on its edge rather than lying flat, why
a span table says what it says, why most collapses begin at a joint
rather than in the middle of a beam, and which parts of a house you can
change yourself and which you must not touch without someone qualified.

This guide takes its building practice from the US Army's carpentry
manual (FM 5-426), its explanation of how wood carries load from the
Forest Products Laboratory's *Wood Handbook*, its account of the load
path from the Department of Energy's Building America Solution Center,
its snow and storm safety from the Federal Emergency Management Agency
(FEMA), its signs of trouble in an existing house from a guide prepared
for the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD), its safety
rules from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), and
one famous collapse from the investigation by the National Bureau of
Standards. All are United States government publications. Where
something is arithmetic, general practice or our own reading, the text
says so.

Two guides sit beside this one. [Foundations and
Ground](foundations_and_ground.md) is the bottom of the path, where the
load meets the soil. [Framing a Building](framing_a_building.md) is how
the path is built in wood. [Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](force_levers_and_mechanical_advantage.md) covers force and
weight from the beginning, if those words are new.

## First: where structures hurt people

HUD's guide to inspecting older houses says that small houses mostly have
no structural problems built into them (restated). The dangerous moments
are the ones when something is removed, cut, overloaded or damaged, or
when the building is only half built. Six situations deserve care before
anything else in this guide.

### Cutting or removing something that holds the building up

The guide HUD commissioned for inspecting older houses (the *Residential
Rehabilitation Inspection Guide*, February 2000, which we restate rather
than quote) lists the usual causes of sagging, sloping or springy floors.
Besides settlement of the foundation and spans that are too long, they
include structural members that were cut or drilled, overloading, and
supporting walls or columns removed during an earlier alteration. Its
list for roofs is similar: a roof can spread outward when collar beams
are missing or the rafters and ceiling joists are not properly tied
together at the eaves, and trusses that have been altered, with members
cut, removed or disconnected, can cause the same problem.

So, before you take out a wall, a post or a beam, or cut a notch or a
hole in a joist, a rafter or any part of a truss, find out what it
carries. If you cannot tell, treat it as carrying load and ask someone
qualified (general practice). Holding a load up temporarily while a
structural member is replaced, called shoring, is work for people who do
it professionally (general practice).

The Army's carpentry manual gives the habit in one line, written about
cutting a hole for a stovepipe: "The opening should be cut in an area
selected to avoid cutting studs, braces, plates, or other structural
members." That is good advice for any hole in any wall.

### A building that is warning you

Wood and steel often bend visibly before they break. FEMA's *Snow Load
Safety Guide* (FEMA P-957, January 2013) says so directly: "Wood and
steel structures may show noticeable signs of excessive deflection
before failure." Its list of warning signs during a snow event, for
wood, metal and steel buildings, is a good list for any overloaded roof
or floor:

- sagging ceiling tiles or boards, or ceiling boards falling out of
  their grid;
- popping, cracking and creaking noises;
- sagging roof members, including the roof sheathing;
- bowing truss bottom chords or web members;
- doors or windows that can no longer be opened or closed;
- cracked or split wood members;
- cracks in walls or masonry;
- severe roof leaks, and water collecting where it should drain on a
  low-slope roof.

If you see any of them, FEMA's instruction is that "the building should
be promptly evacuated and a qualified design professional should be
contacted to perform a detailed structural inspection." Get everyone
out first and phone from outside. (Applying the snow list to other loads
is our reading; the signs are the same because a beam does not know what
is bending it.)

After an earthquake, Ready.gov, FEMA's preparedness site, is as blunt:
"If you are in a damaged building, go outside and quickly move away from
the building. Do not enter damaged buildings."

### Snow on the roof

FEMA's snow guide opens with a sentence worth remembering: "Most
buildings are not at risk of snow induced failure. More often than not,
attempting to remove snow from a roof is more hazardous than beneficial,
posing a risk to both personnel and the roofing structure." Where
removal is needed, it says, "Snow removal should be performed by a
licensed, insured professional roofing contractor who has experience in
removing snow from roofs." Its other rules:

- Work on a roof follows OSHA's rules for rooftop work, with fall arrest
  harnesses where they apply.
- Someone stays below to keep people away from where snow and ice will
  fall, and icicles are dangerous even from a short height.
- With a snow rake from the ground, roof snow can slide at any moment:
  "Keep a safe distance away from the eave to remain outside of the
  sliding range."
- Leave at least 2 inches (5 cm) of snow on the roof, use plastic rather
  than metal shovels, no sharp tools and no machines, and remove drifted
  snow first.
- "If there is any concern that snow loads may cause a collapse of the
  roof structure, cease all removal activity and evacuate the building."

### Walls that are standing but not finished

A wall that stands on its own, before the floor or roof above ties it
in, can be pushed over by wind or a knock. OSHA's rule for masonry walls
being built (29 CFR 1926.706) shows how seriously that is taken on a
building site: a limited access zone is set up on the unscaffolded side,
and "The limited access zone shall be equal to the height of the wall to
be constructed plus four feet, and shall run the entire length of the
wall." Only the people building the wall may enter it, and "All masonry
walls over eight feet in height shall be adequately braced to prevent
overturning and to prevent collapse", with the bracing left in place
until the permanent structure supports the wall. For a wood wall frame
the same idea applies: brace it the moment it stands, and keep people
out from where it would fall (general practice). The bracing is covered
in [Framing a Building](framing_a_building.md).

### Falls

Most structural work is done above the ground. OSHA's construction rule
(29 CFR 1926.501(b)(13)) requires each worker in residential
construction "6 feet (1.8 m) or more above lower levels" to be protected
by a guardrail, a safety net or a personal fall arrest system. That is
written for employers, but the height is a fair warning for anyone: a
fall from an open floor edge, a roof or the top of a wall at that height
can kill. The ladder rules are in [Keeping Things
Working](keeping_things_working.md).

### Adding a heavy load

A floor that has carried a bedroom for fifty years has not been tested
with a cast-iron bath, a stone worktop or a wall of bookshelves. HUD's
inspection guide says that when a building's load is to be increased a
great deal during a renovation, with examples such as a water bed, a
stone kitchen countertop, tile flooring or a heavy stove and oven, the
structural members involved should be checked by calculation, using span
tables for simple wood spans and a structural engineer for anything more
complex (restated). Ask before you add, not after the floor sags
(general practice).

## Loads: what a building carries

The Army's carpentry manual divides the weight on a floor into two
kinds, and the two words are used everywhere in building.

- **Dead load** is the weight of the building itself. The manual lists
  the floor joists, the flooring, bearing partitions, attic joists and
  the ceilings among its parts.
- **Live load** is "the weight of furniture, persons, and other movable
  loads, not actually a part of the building but still carried by the
  girder." The manual adds: "Snow on the roof is also a part of the live
  load."

How many pounds per square foot to allow for each is not something to
guess. The manual says: "The allowance per square foot for live loads is
usually governed by local building specifications and regulations." The
numbers that apply to your building are set where you live, and your
building department will tell you which (general practice).

### Snow and ice

Snow shows how much a load can vary. FEMA's snow guide says that a foot
of fresh snow weighs from 3 pounds per square foot (14.6 kg per square
metre) for light, dry snow to 21 pounds per square foot (102.5 kg per
square metre) for wet, heavy snow, and that "One inch of ice weighs a
little less than 5 pounds per square foot, and 1 foot of ice weighs
approximately 57 pounds per square foot." Ice weighs much more than wet
snow per inch of depth, which is one reason to keep ice from building up.

It also warns that the snow on a roof is not the snow on the ground:
wind piles drifts on a lower roof in the shelter of a higher one and
against parapets, chimneys and dormers, snow sliding off a higher roof
lands on the porch or lean-to below, and
"attempting to measure ground snow weight and assuming that it is the
same as the roof snow load is incorrect."

**Worked example: a garden shed under snow.** A shed roof 10 feet by 12
feet has 120 square feet of area (our arithmetic throughout this
example). A foot of light, dry snow on it weighs about 3 x 120 = 360
pounds. A foot of wet, heavy snow weighs about 21 x 120 = 2,520 pounds,
seven times as much for the same depth. Two feet of wet snow is over
5,000 pounds, and a foot of ice would be about 57 x 120 = 6,840 pounds,
more than three tonnes. Same roof, same depth on a ruler, very different
loads. That is why a roof is designed for the snow of a particular
place, and why a shed built for a dry climate can fail in a wet one.

### Wind

Wind does not only push on a wall. The Building America Solution
Center's guide to the load path, from the Department of Energy, sorts
the loads on a building into vertical loads, which "include downward
gravity forces (weight of the building) and uplift forces from wind",
and horizontal loads, which include wind pressing on a wall face and
wind acting along the length of a wall. Flood and earthquake can bring
both. Uplift is the surprise for most people: wind over a roof can lift
it, so the roof has to be held down as well as held up.

### Earthquakes

An earthquake shakes the ground sideways under a building. HUD's
inspection guide names the weak points it asks a structural engineer to
check in earthquake country: wood-frame houses that are not anchored to
their foundations, which can shift or slide; houses standing on short
stud walls (cripple walls) or on piers, which can tilt or fall over;
unreinforced masonry; and irregular shapes, open ground storeys such as
a garage under living space, and hillside sites (restated). [The Hazards
Where You Live](the_hazards_where_you_live.md) covers how to find out
whether earthquakes are a hazard where you are.

## The load path

The Building America guide defines the idea this whole guide rests on:
the term continuous load path "describes the structural condition
required to resist all loads acting on a building." It goes on: a
continuous load path must carry every vertical and horizontal load to
the ground; the connections between the major parts, roof, walls, floors
and foundation, "tend to be the weakest link within a load path"; and
"If a connection fails, an alternative load path will form. If the
members and connections in the new load path are not strong enough,
progressive failure can occur."

Follow the snow on a pitched roof down through an ordinary wood-frame
house (the sequence is our summary of the framing in the Army manual):

1. The snow presses on the roof covering and the **sheathing** under it.
2. The sheathing spans between **rafters** or **trusses**, and hands the
   load to them.
3. The rafters rest on the **top plate** of the walls.
4. The top plate spreads the load into the **studs**, the upright pieces
   of the wall.
5. The studs stand on the **bottom plate**, which sits on the **floor
   framing**: joists, and girders where the span is long.
6. The floor framing rests on the **sill**, which is anchored to the
   **foundation**.
7. The foundation spreads the load over enough **soil** to hold it,
   which is the subject of [Foundations and
   Ground](foundations_and_ground.md).

Wind uplift runs the same path backwards, and that is where the
connections earn their keep. A rafter that only rests on a wall can carry
snow well and still be lifted by a strong wind (our reading). The Building
America guide's examples of the parts that hold the path together under
uplift: "Hurricane straps, hold-down connectors, and bolts help to
transfer loads from the building's walls to its foundation."

Openings interrupt the path. In the guide's words, "Loads must be routed
around openings such as windows and doors", and "Accumulated loads on
headers are transferred to the studs on the sides of the opening." The
header over a door is a short beam that collects everything the missing
studs would have carried and hands it to the studs beside the opening.

### Who carries what

The Army's manual explains how load divides with a picture anyone can
try. A 10 foot plank weighing 5 pounds a foot weighs 50 pounds; two men,
one at each end, carry 25 pounds each. Put a second plank end to end
with the first, with a third man in the middle holding the ends of both:
the two men on the outside still carry 25 pounds each, but the man in
the middle carries 50, half of each plank. The outside men are the
foundation walls, and the man in the middle is a girder, the beam that
holds up the middle of a floor. The rule that follows: "a girder will
carry the weight of the floor on each side to the midpoint of the joists
that rest upon it."

**Worked example (our arithmetic).** A floor 12 feet wide and 20 feet
long has a girder running down its middle, so the joists span 6 feet
from each outside wall to the girder. Each outside wall carries the
floor out to the middle of its joists, a strip 3 feet wide and 20 feet
long, 60 square feet. The girder carries 3 feet on each side, a strip 6
feet wide, 120 square feet. The total is 60 + 60 + 120 = 240 square feet,
the whole floor, and the girder carries half of it, as the planks
predicted. Whatever load per square foot the floor is designed for,
multiply it by those areas and you know what each support must carry.
The girder's load then goes down into the posts under it, and every post
needs a footing that the soil can hold.

## Four ways a piece is loaded

Every member in a building is being squeezed, stretched, bent or sheared,
and often more than one at once.

- **Compression** squeezes a piece along its length: a stud, a post, a
  column. A short, thick piece fails by crushing.
- **Tension** stretches it: a ceiling joist holding the bottoms of two
  rafters together, a strap holding a roof down.
- **Bending** is what a beam, joist or rafter does under load. The
  *Wood Handbook*'s chapter on structural analysis (FPL-GTR-282, 2021,
  restated) explains that in a bent beam the inside of the curve, the
  top of an ordinary floor joist, is squeezed, and the outside, the
  bottom edge, is stretched, with the bending stress greatest at the top
  and bottom edges and zero along the middle of the depth.
- **Shear** is one part of a piece sliding past the next. The handbook
  notes that wood is relatively weak in shear, that the shear stress in
  a beam is greatest at mid-depth, where it can open a crack running
  along the length, and that shear rather than bending limits the load a
  short beam can carry (restated).

Two consequences follow, and both are practical.

**The edges of a joist matter most.** The *Wood Handbook*'s chapter on
grading lumber says a knot weakens wood more in tension than in
compression, and that in a beam its effect depends on whether it sits on
the tension or the compression side, with knots near the middle of the
depth having little effect (restated). The same reasoning explains why a
notch or a hole cut into the top or bottom edge of a joist does far more
harm than a small hole bored near the middle of its depth (our reading;
the exact limits are set by the building code, and [Framing a
Building](framing_a_building.md) covers them).

**Slender pieces fail sideways.** A long, thin piece in compression does
not usually crush. The handbook explains that for slender members a
sudden loss of stability, buckling, is the main way they fail: the piece
bows out sideways and its strength falls away, even to collapse. The
standard formula for buckling, Euler's, which the handbook gives, puts
the length that is free to bow in the bottom of a fraction, squared
(restated). Halve the free length and the piece resists four times the
buckling stress (our arithmetic from that formula). That is one
reason a wall's studs are held by sheathing and blocking, and why a tall
post gets braces: the bracing does not make the wood stronger, it
shortens the length that can bow (our reading).

Beams buckle too. A deep, narrow joist that is not held at its top edge
can roll sideways and twist under load; the handbook notes that a floor
or roof deck fastened to the top edge often holds it, and that
blocking or bracing does the same where the deck cannot (restated).

## Why depth beats width

The Army's manual states the rule a carpenter learns first: "For greater
carrying capacity, it is better to increase a girder's depth (within
limits) than its width." It says that doubling the depth makes the safe
load four times as great.

The *Wood Handbook* gives the reason in its formulas: a rectangular
beam's resistance to bending grows with its width times its depth
squared, and its stiffness, its resistance to sagging, with its width
times its depth cubed (restated). So adding depth pays twice over.

Here is what that means for ordinary lumber, using the actual dressed
sizes of dry dimension lumber from the *Wood Handbook*'s table of
standard sizes (a 2 x 6 is really 1 1/2 by 5 1/2 inches, a 2 x 8 is 1
1/2 by 7 1/4, a 2 x 10 is 1 1/2 by 9 1/4 and a 2 x 12 is 1 1/2 by 11
1/4). The comparison is our arithmetic, for the same wood, the same span
and the same supports:

| Joist | Depth (in) | Strength compared with a 2 x 6 | Stiffness compared with a 2 x 6 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 x 6 | 5 1/2 | 1 | 1 |
| 2 x 8 | 7 1/4 | 1.7 | 2.3 |
| 2 x 10 | 9 1/4 | 2.8 | 4.8 |
| 2 x 12 | 11 1/4 | 4.2 | 8.6 |

And a board laid flat is nearly useless as a beam. A 2 x 6 standing on
its edge is about 3.7 times as strong as the same 2 x 6 lying flat, and
about 13 times as stiff (our arithmetic). That is why joists and rafters
stand on edge.

### Span matters even more

Length works against you faster than depth works for you. Using the
handbook's formula for a simply supported beam carrying an even load,
the bending stress grows with the square of the span, and the sag with
the fourth power (our arithmetic from the handbook's equation). Make a
joist span twice as long, with the same load per foot, and it is
stressed four times as hard and sags sixteen times as far. Even going
from a 10 foot span to a 12 foot span raises the stress by 44 percent
and roughly doubles the sag.

That is why span tables, which list the longest span allowed for each
size, species, grade and spacing of joist, look the way they do, and why
the Army manual warns that joists "as small as 2 x 6 are sometimes used
in light frame buildings. These are too small for floors with spans over
10 feet". Use the span table your building department accepts; never
stretch a span by eye (general practice).

## Why things sag over time

Wood under a steady load keeps bending slowly for years. The *Wood
Handbook* calls this creep and says wood beams usually sag over time
because of it, by an amount that depends on how heavy the load is, how
long it stays, and conditions such as heat and humidity; it adds that
green timber left to dry while it carries a load will sag (restated).
That is part of why old houses have gentle dips in their floors and
ridges.

HUD's inspection guide takes a practical view of that sag (restated):
some deflection is common in older buildings and can usually be
tolerated, unless it causes a beam or joist to lose its bearing on its
support, weakens connections, or opens watertight joints in a roof. A
member that has taken a permanent set cannot be straightened, but the
sag can be stopped by adding support or strengthening members. And
sagging, sloping or springy floors can mean anything from an annoyance
to a serious problem, so each case needs its own diagnosis.

**Water on a flat roof is a special trap.** The *Wood Handbook*
describes ponding: a flat roof whose beams are not stiff enough, or are
too far apart, sags a little under rain; the sag collects more water,
which makes it sag more, and it can fail by this progressive deflection
under the weight of water (restated). HUD's guide calls a sagging flat
roof a common problem that matters mainly when it leaks or holds ponds
of water (restated). Keep flat roofs draining and their drains clear
(general practice), and treat a pond that grows after each rain as one
of FEMA's warning signs.

## Joints are where buildings fail

The Army's manual is direct: "Weak points in a structure usually occur
at the connections (joints and splices) between pieces of lumber.
However, these connections can be structurally sound if done correctly.
Such weak points are usually a sign of poor workmanship." It explains
why some joints are weaker than others. "The end grain is the weakest
part of a piece of wood when used in joints", so a butt joint, where the
end of one board meets the face of another, holds only as well as that
end grain: "A butt joint is, therefore, the weakest type of joint." By
contrast, "The lap joint is the strongest joint." And a joint made for
one kind of load can be useless for another: "splices for resisting
compression are usually worthless for resisting tension or bending."
Why nails pull out of end grain is in [Nails, Screws and
Bolts](nails_screws_and_bolts.md).

### The walkways in Kansas City

One of the worst structural collapses in the history of the United States
was a failure of one connection, and it is a clear lesson in how a small
change to a joint changes the whole load path.

On July 17, 1981, two suspended walkways in the atrium of the Hyatt
Regency Hotel in Kansas City, Missouri, fell to the floor below. The
National Bureau of Standards (now NIST) investigated, and its report
(Building Science Series 143, May 1982) counted 113 people dead and 186
injured and said that in those terms it was "the most devastating
structural collapse ever to take place in the United States." It
concluded that "the most probable cause of failure was insufficient load
capacity of the box beam-hanger rod connections."

The design had the fourth floor walkway and the second floor walkway
below it both hung from the roof by the same continuous rods, each
walkway's beam held where it crossed the rod. As built, the rods were
split in two: one set ran from the roof to the fourth floor beams, and a
second set hung the second floor walkway from the fourth floor beams.
The report found that this change "essentially doubled the load on the
box beam-hanger rod connections at the fourth floor walkway", because
those connections now carried the second floor walkway as well as their
own. It also found that the original connection was itself inadequate,
and that as built the walkways "had only minimal capacity to resist their
own weight and had virtually no capacity to resist additional loads
imposed by people."

Nothing in the beams, the rods or the materials was unusual. One detail
of how two pieces met was changed during construction, and nobody
followed the load through the change. The habit to take away is the one
this guide is built on: whenever you change how a piece is held, trace
where its load goes now (our reading).

## Reading a building you already have

You can learn a great deal about an existing house's structure by
looking, as long as you know where to look. HUD's inspection guide
(restated throughout this section) offers these starting points.

- **Where the structure shows.** Wood structural members are usually
  hidden behind finishes, and are directly visible mainly in attics,
  crawl spaces and basements. Start there.
- **Common signs of trouble.** Sloping or springy floors, cracks in
  walls and ceilings, bulges in walls, and doors and windows that stick.
  Many of these may come from the foundation moving rather than the
  framing;
  [Foundations and Ground](foundations_and_ground.md) covers that side.
- **A simple test for slope.** Roll a marble or other heavy round object
  across the floor to see which way, and how much, it slopes, or use a
  carpenter's level.
- **Which walls are load-bearing.** Usually found by looking at the
  beams and joists in the basement, crawl space or attic. The Army
  manual's definition helps: a bearing partition "supports ceiling
  joists; the nonbearing type supports only itself". As general
  practice, a wall is likely to be bearing if joists or rafters rest on
  it or are lapped over it, if there is a wall, beam or post directly
  above or below it, or if it runs down the middle of the house; and a
  wall you are not sure about is bearing until someone qualified says
  otherwise.
- **Damage from earlier work.** Holes and notches cut through joists,
  rafters and beams for pipes, wires and ducts, especially near their
  top or bottom edges; joist hangers missing or half-nailed; cut or
  disconnected truss members; supporting walls or posts removed.
- **Rot and insects.** Probe suspect wood with a sharp tool. Sound wood
  lifts in long, fibrous splinters; decayed wood breaks up in short,
  irregular pieces. Wood that reads above 20 to 25 percent on a moisture
  meter should be examined closely for decay or insects. [Rust, Rot and
  Decay](rust_rot_and_decay.md) is the guide to what decay is and how to
  stop it.

None of that is a structural assessment. It tells you what to ask about
and when to call someone.

## Worked example: can this wall come out?

You want to open up a kitchen by removing the wall between it and the
dining room. This is a procedure for finding out how big a job that is,
not for doing it.

1. **Look up.** Go into the attic, or look at the ceiling from the
   room above. Do ceiling joists or rafters rest on the top of this
   wall, or overlap on it? If they do, treat the wall as bearing.
2. **Look down.** In the basement or crawl space, is there a beam, a
   girder, a wall or a row of posts directly under it? A wall with
   support lined up under it is very likely carrying load to it.
3. **Look at its middle.** Does it run along the middle of the house,
   parallel to the ridge? That is a common place for a bearing wall
   (general practice).
4. **Look inside it.** If the house was built before 1978, stop first:
   the EPA says such a home may have lead-based paint, that renovation
   in a pre-1978 home with lead-based paint "can easily create dangerous
   lead dust", and it recommends hiring a lead-safe certified
   contractor; the lead paint advice in [Rust, Rot and
   Decay](rust_rot_and_decay.md) applies before you cut. Switch off the
   circuits (the procedure is in [Working Out Why Something
   Broke](working_out_why_something_broke.md)), then look for pipes,
   wires, ducts and gas lines that will have to be moved.
5. **Decide.** If the wall is not bearing, removing it is still a
   permit question where you live (general practice). If it is bearing,
   removing it means replacing it with a beam sized for the load, posts
   under the beam's ends, and footings under the posts, all following the
   load path down to the soil. That is a design for an engineer or the
   building department, and temporary support while the wall comes out
   is professional work (general practice).

## Know where your own work stops

You can learn to read loads, judge a sag and spot damage from this
guide. These are past it:

- **Anything that changes the load path**: removing or cutting a
  bearing wall, post, beam, joist, rafter or truss member; adding a
  storey, a heavy bath, a stone floor or a deck; changing what holds the
  roof down. HUD's guide puts anything beyond simple span tables in the
  hands of a structural engineer (restated).
- **A building showing FEMA's warning signs.** Get out, then call a
  qualified design professional.
- **A building damaged by an earthquake, storm, flood or fire.** Do not
  go in until it has been inspected. (Ready.gov says this for
  earthquakes; applying it to other damage is our reading.)
- **Removing snow from a roof.** FEMA recommends a licensed, insured
  professional roofing contractor.
- **Anything in earthquake country that HUD's guide lists for an
  engineer**: unanchored houses, cripple walls, piers, unreinforced
  masonry.
- **Building or altering a structure at all, without asking your
  building department what needs a permit** (general practice). Rules
  differ by place, and they are where the local loads, spans and
  connections are written down.

## Traps

- **"It has stood for fifty years."** It stood under the loads it has
  had. A new load, a removed wall or one heavy snow is a new test.
- **Cutting a notch "just a little".** The edges of a joist do the work.
- **Laying a beam flat to save height.** On its edge it is several
  times stronger and stiffer.
- **Treating a roof as only held up.** Wind lifts roofs. A rafter that
  only rests on a wall is not held down.
- **Shovelling a roof yourself because it feels responsible.** FEMA says
  it is often more dangerous than the snow.
- **Changing a joint without tracing the load.** That is how the
  Kansas City walkways fell.

## How the game models it

The game builds shelters, but it does not yet model loads, so it cannot
teach the subject of this guide. Here is what it does and does not do.

**What a building is for, it models.** A roof keeps the rain off you,
and walls keep the wind off, but only on the side the wind comes from.
Standing under a finished roof, the game checks which sides have a wall:
with the open side turned away from the wind, nothing gets in, and with
a roof over at least three walls the heads-up display says "Sheltered".
Turn the open side into the wind and the display tells you what share of
the wind gets in. The body heat model uses that, so a
shelter built with its back to the wind keeps you warmer than one built
the other way round (`src/systems/construction/uses.rs`,
`src/engine/survival_env.rs`).

**What holds a building up, it does not model.** Every built piece has a
health figure in its data (200 for a Wood Foundation, 150 for a Wood
Wall, 180 for the roof, in `data/blueprints/basic.ron`), but nothing in
the game ever reduces it: snow, wind and weather do not load or damage
what you build. A roof rests at the height of the walls under it when you
place it, but if you then take those walls down, the roof stays where it
was, in the air. A wall needs no foundation under it and no roof on top
to stand. In the home construction editor, the wall materials list real
figures for density and tensile strength (concrete is shown as strong in
compression and weak in tension), but those figures are information for
you: a wall stands the same whatever material or thickness you give it,
down to the 1 mm the editor allows (`src/gui/pages/construction.rs`,
`data/blueprints/wall_materials.ron`).

**What exists in the code but is not yet in the game.** The code holds a
first prototype of a structural check: it carries load down through a
frame's members to its foundation, breaks a member that is loaded beyond
its capacity, lets that load move to the neighbours so failures can
cascade, and finds any part that has lost every path to the ground
(`src/systems/construction/solver.rs`). It is tested on its own and is
not connected to anything you build.

**Building trains you.** Finishing a piece from the Crafting page gives
experience in the Shelter Building skill (`src/systems/construction/mod.rs`).

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, crafting
  takes no materials, so you can make planks without having any logs, but
  building a piece from the Crafting page still takes its planks or
  bricks from your pack or the home's storage, and on a planet from what
  you carry.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches, so
  what you built and the experience it earned start over at the next
  launch. Turn the setting off to keep them.

What to practise outside the game, then: everything about loads, spans,
sag, joints and the load path. The game will tell you whether you are
out of the wind; it will not tell you whether your roof would hold a
foot of wet snow.

## You own this when

- You can trace any load, snow, people or wind, from where it lands down
  to the soil, naming each member and connection it passes through.
- You know dead load from live load, and you know that the numbers for
  your building come from the rules where you live.
- You can say why a joist stands on edge, and why doubling its depth
  does more than doubling its width.
- You know that span hurts faster than depth helps, and you use a span
  table instead of your eye.
- You can recognise FEMA's warning signs, and you get people out before
  you investigate.
- You never cut or remove a wall, post, beam, joist, rafter or truss
  member without knowing what it carries.
- You leave roof snow to professionals, stay out of damaged buildings,
  and brace walls the moment they stand.
- You can look at an existing house from the attic and the crawl space
  and say which walls are probably bearing, and what you would ask an
  engineer.
- You can tell the story of the Kansas City walkways, and what it says
  about changing a joint.

### Labelled in the text as arithmetic, general practice or our reading, not sourced

- The shed snow loads, the floor load-sharing areas, the depth and
  flat-versus-edge comparisons, the effect of span on stress and sag,
  the effect of halving a buckling length, and the metric conversions
  are arithmetic from the sources' figures and formulas.
- Treating any member you cannot identify as carrying load, shoring as
  professional work, keeping people out from where a standing wall
  would fall, keeping flat roof drains clear, asking before adding a
  heavy load, using an accepted span table rather than the eye (and the
  description of what a span table lists), getting everyone out before
  phoning, and asking the building department about permits are general
  practice.
- The signs of a bearing wall (joists resting on it, support lined up
  under it, a wall down the middle of the house) beyond the Army
  manual's definition and HUD's advice to look in the basement and attic
  are general practice.
- Applying FEMA's snow warning signs to other overloads, applying
  Ready.gov's advice about damaged buildings beyond earthquakes, the step
  by step route of the load path through a house, the header as a short
  beam, a rafter that only rests on a wall being lifted by wind, bracing
  as a way of shortening the length that can bow, why notches and holes
  at the edges of a joist do the most harm, and the lesson drawn from the
  Kansas City walkways are our reading of the sources.
