# First Aid Until Help Arrives

Someone has fallen off a ladder. Someone at the table has stopped talking
and is holding their throat. Someone in the garden has gone down and is not
getting up. For the next few minutes, the most useful person in the world
is whoever is standing nearest, and that may be you.

The Federal Emergency Management Agency (FEMA) built a short course around
exactly that moment and called it Until Help Arrives; its web tutorial is
published on Ready.gov. Its reasoning fits in two
sentences: "The typical EMS response time is only about 6 to 9 minutes.
However, life-threatening injuries can kill within minutes, before medical
responders arrive!" The minutes before the
ambulance belong to the bystander. What happens in them is first aid.

First aid is not medicine. It is a short list of things done in the right
order and done well: keep yourself safe, call for help, keep the person
breathing, stop bleeding, keep them warm and still, and stay with them.
FEMA's course names five steps: call 911, stay safe, stop the bleeding,
position the injured, and provide comfort. Nearly everything in this guide
hangs from those five.

The procedures here come from FEMA's Until Help Arrives course and its
Community Emergency Response Team (CERT) training, the Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention (CDC), the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute
(NHLBI) and the diabetes institute (NIDDK) of the National Institutes of
Health, the National 911 Program, the Health Resources and Services
Administration (HRSA) and the Occupational Safety and Health Administration
(OSHA), all United States government works in the public domain. Several
step-by-step techniques come from MedlinePlus, the National Library of
Medicine's consumer site, whose first aid articles are copyrighted, so they
are restated here in our own words and never quoted. A few points come from
the American Heart Association's 2025 resuscitation guidelines, which are
cited and restated, never quoted. The American Red Cross is cited only for
what its classes offer, and three Washington State laws are restated. Where
something is general practice or our own reading, the text says so.

This guide is the hub of the shelf it sits on, and it hands off to the
others rather than repeating them. [How Your Body Works](how_your_body_works.md)
covers the emergencies where minutes count and how to recognise them: heart
attack, stroke, a severe allergic reaction, sepsis, carbon monoxide and
poisoning. [Bleeding and Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md), [Treating
Burns](treating_burns.md), [Cold and Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md)
and [When Heat Becomes Dangerous](when_heat_becomes_dangerous.md) each take
one injury further. Someone being shocked is in [Where Your Electrical Work
Stops](where_your_electrical_work_stops.md), someone in the water is in
[Floating and Boats](floating_and_boats.md), more on calling 911 from a
mobile phone is in [What to Do When You Are Lost](what_to_do_when_you_are_lost.md),
and first aid when many people are hurt and no ambulance is coming soon is
in [Helping Your Neighbours After a Disaster](helping_your_neighbours_after_a_disaster.md).

## What this guide is, and what it is not

This guide will help you recognise the emergencies a bystander can do
something about, call for help well, and do the handful of things that keep
a person alive until trained people take over. It does not teach you to
diagnose anything, it recommends no medicine, and it is not a substitute
for a class.

That last point matters more here than anywhere else in the Library.
MedlinePlus is plain that reading about CPR is not the same as hands-on
training, and that taking an accredited course is the best way to be
prepared; it says to refresh that training every two years, and names
community organisations, hospitals, the American Heart Association and the
American Red Cross among those that teach it. The Red Cross offers its
first aid, CPR and AED (defibrillator) courses online, in person, or
blended, which is online study followed by a skills check with an
instructor; its certificate lasts two years, and it notes that a
certificate from the online-only course may not meet workplace
requirements.

Hands forget. OSHA's guide to workplace first aid programmes, published in
2006, reported studies finding that CPR and AED skills are retained for
about 6 to 12 months, and that the American Heart Association's emergency
care committee encouraged review and practice at least every six months.
If you take only one thing from this guide, take a class, and then practise.

**The law is on the side of a bystander who helps.** FEMA's course says that
state Good Samaritan laws generally protect people who give unpaid care in
a life-threatening situation, that the laws vary from state to state, and
that their shared aim is to protect care given in a prudent and reasonable
way. Washington's version, where the simulation is set, is RCW 4.24.300.
In our own words: a person who gives emergency care at the scene of an
emergency without pay or the expectation of pay, or who helps carry an
injured person to treatment, is not liable for civil damages for what they
do or fail to do in giving that care, unless it amounts to gross negligence
or willful or wanton misconduct; the protection does not cover someone who
gives that care as part of a paid job. Doing what this guide describes,
carefully and in good faith, and stopping where it says to stop, is the
kind of help that law has in mind (our reading, and not legal advice).
Washington also protects a person who uses a defibrillator at the scene of
an emergency: they are not liable for injury from its use unless their
acts amount to gross negligence or willful or wanton misconduct, and the
training and upkeep duties the law places on a defibrillator's owner do not
apply to a Good Samaritan using it (RCW 70.54.310, restated).

## The five steps, and the order they really go in

FEMA's course lists them as call 911, stay safe, stop the bleeding,
position the injured and provide comfort. The same course sets the real
order with one exception: if an injured person is in immediate danger that
could hurt them further, get them out of harm's way first and then call,
or have someone else call while you help.

### Stay safe

"You can't help anyone if you get hurt yourself." The course asks you to
stop for a moment and use every sense before you go in: what you see, what
you hear (voices, screams, noises that worry you), and what you smell
(fumes or chemicals). Then decide whether you are safer where you are or
somewhere else.

The usual ways a helper becomes a second casualty, and where each is
covered:

- **Electricity.** Never touch someone who may still be in contact with a
  live wire. Cut the power, or push them clear with something dry that
  does not conduct, and never go near a fallen power line at all: the
  detail is in [Where Your Electrical Work
  Stops](where_your_electrical_work_stops.md).
- **Gas and fumes.** A smell of gas, an engine or a burner in an enclosed
  space, or several people ill in one room at once: get everyone out and
  call from outside. Carbon monoxide is in [How Your Body
  Works](how_your_body_works.md); the steps for a gas leak are in [Helping
  Your Neighbours After a Disaster](helping_your_neighbours_after_a_disaster.md).
- **Water.** Throw something that floats; do not go in after someone
  ([Floating and Boats](floating_and_boats.md)).
- **Fire, smoke and damaged buildings.** Covered in [Helping Your
  Neighbours After a Disaster](helping_your_neighbours_after_a_disaster.md).
- **Traffic.** Park well clear, switch on your hazard lights, and keep
  yourself out of the lane (general practice).
- **Blood.** Put something between your hands and it. FEMA's course
  suggests gloves, a piece of cloth or even a plastic bag; the trade-off
  when there is no time to find gloves is in [Bleeding and
  Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md).

**Moving someone.** Leave an injured person where they are unless staying
there is the danger (general practice, and the reason the advice on neck
injuries below reads as it does). When it is the danger, the course is
blunt: a person lying in a busy street has to be got out of the traffic
however you can, including dragging them if that is the best way, and then
you call. It asks you to "move smartly", using what is around you (other
people, a cart or a vehicle, chairs, blankets) and as little of your own
energy as possible. If a hip, the pelvis or a thigh may be broken,
MedlinePlus says that if you must move the person, you pull them to safety
by their clothing, such as the shoulders of a shirt, a belt or the
trouser legs.

### Call 911

FEMA's course is emphatic: "No matter what, you should ALWAYS call 911!"
It adds that you should make the call even if you think someone else
already has. If other people are there, point at one of them, tell them to
call 911 and to come back and tell you it is done (general practice:
"someone call" often means nobody does).

The National 911 Program lists what the call-taker is likely to ask: the
location, including the street address and the room or apartment number in
a large building; the number you are calling from; what the emergency is;
and details, such as the injuries or symptoms. FEMA's course says the
first question to be ready for is where your emergency is, and that it is
fine not to know every answer: give what you can, such as the floor you
are on, or a sign or landmark nearby.

Then let the call-taker help you. The National 911 Program: "Many 911
centers can tell you exactly what to do until help arrives, such as
providing step-by-step instructions to aid someone who is choking or needs
first aid or CPR." And: "Do not hang up until the call-taker instructs you
to do so." Put the phone on speaker and set it down so both your hands are
free (general practice).

One other number belongs beside 911. For a possible poisoning in someone
who is awake and breathing normally, Poison Help is 1-800-222-1222. HRSA,
which funds the line, says it connects you to your local poison center and
is available around the clock in more than 100 languages. For someone
unconscious, struggling to breathe or having a seizure after a poisoning,
call 911 ([How Your Body Works](how_your_body_works.md) has the rule and
its source). Calling from a
mobile phone with no service, and texting 911, are covered in [What to Do
When You Are Lost](what_to_do_when_you_are_lost.md).

### Is the person awake, and are they breathing normally?

This one check decides almost everything that follows (our summary of the
sources below).

- **Ask and touch.** Tap their shoulder and shout to them (MedlinePlus), by
  name if you know it (general practice).
- **Look and listen.** Watch the chest and listen at the mouth, for at
  least 5 seconds and no more than 10 (the American Heart Association,
  restated). MedlinePlus warns that gasping or gurgling is not normal
  breathing. The CDC lists the signs that someone may be in cardiac
  arrest: they collapse suddenly and lose consciousness, are not breathing
  or are only gasping for air, do not respond to shouting or shaking, and
  have no pulse.

Then one of three things is true:

- **Not awake, and not breathing normally.** Their heart has probably
  stopped. Start CPR now, and get an AED: the next section.
- **Not awake, but breathing normally.** Put them in the recovery position
  and keep watching their breathing: the section after.
- **Awake.** FEMA's course says to always start by asking whether you can
  help: give your name and ask what happened or what they need. The CERT
  manual adds consent: ask permission before you treat someone who is
  awake, and ask a parent or guardian before you treat a child, if you
  can. Then let them settle into whatever position they find easiest, and
  look for bleeding.

Do not spend time hunting for a pulse in someone who has collapsed and is
not breathing normally. Feeling for one under pressure is hard, and the
call-taker can talk you through what to do (general practice, as in [How
Your Body Works](how_your_body_works.md)). MedlinePlus's article on CPR for
children goes further and tells anyone who is not a health professional
not to check for a pulse at all.

## When the heart has stopped: CPR and the defibrillator

The NHLBI, part of the National Institutes of Health: "A cardiac arrest is
fatal unless treatment begins immediately."
It adds that most cardiac arrests happen outside hospitals, so whether the
person lives depends on family, friends or people in the community.
MedlinePlus explains the hurry: without oxygen, permanent brain damage can
begin after about four minutes, and death can follow within another four to
six.

The steps, from MedlinePlus's overview of CPR, restated:

1. **Call 911, and send for an AED.** Send one person to call and another
   to fetch a defibrillator if there is one nearby. If you are alone, the
   NHLBI says a family member or caregiver can call 911 while performing
   CPR; put the phone on speaker beside you (general practice).
2. **Put them on their back on something firm,** such as the floor, if you
   can get them there in a moment (a bed or a sofa gives way under your
   hands), then start at once. Do not lose time hunting for a better
   surface. If a neck or back injury is possible, MedlinePlus says two
   people should turn them, so the head and neck do not twist.
3. **Place your hands.** The heel of one hand in the centre of the chest,
   the other hand on top. Shoulders over your hands, elbows locked.
4. **Push hard and fast.** Straight down, at least 2 inches (5 cm) deep
   (the American Heart Association's 2025 figure for an adult; MedlinePlus
   says about 2 inches), at about 100 to 120 pushes a minute, letting the
   chest rise all the way back up between pushes.
5. **Breaths only if you are trained.** If you have had no training,
   MedlinePlus says you can give hands-only CPR, compressions with no
   breaths, to a teen or adult whose heart has stopped. If you are trained,
   give 2 breaths after every 30 compressions.
6. **Keep going** until help arrives and takes over, or the person starts
   breathing normally. If two of you can do it, MedlinePlus says to switch
   every two minutes, before the one pushing tires.

**Children are different.** MedlinePlus says children need breaths as well
as compressions and that hands-only CPR alone is not recommended for them;
it says to push down about a third of the depth of the child's chest, and
to use child pads on an AED if it has them (adult pads if not). That is
exactly the part of CPR to learn with a manikin in a class (general
practice). Its article on CPR for children adds that if you are alone, you
shout for help and give about 2 minutes of CPR before you leave the child
to call 911; with a phone at hand, the American Heart Association's 2025
guidance is to call 911 on speaker straight away instead, so that you never
have to leave (restated). If a child's heart stops before you have learned
it, do not do nothing: call 911 on a phone you can use beside the child,
with the speaker on, or have someone else call, and do what the call-taker
tells you. If you cannot give breaths, push on the chest anyway: the
American Heart Association's 2025 guidelines count chest compressions alone
as a reasonable course for a rescuer who cannot give a child or a baby
breaths (restated). The National 911 Program says many call-takers can
give step-by-step CPR instructions.

**The AED.** The NHLBI says these defibrillators are made for untrained
bystanders to use, are found in places such as airports, office buildings,
gyms and shopping centres, and talk the user through each step. Turn it on
and do what it says: MedlinePlus notes that it will tell you when to stop
compressions and when to deliver a shock, and to start CPR again straight
after. If the person is an adult and you know an AED is nearby, MedlinePlus
says to fetch it even if that means leaving them for a moment, but not to
leave them to search for one that may not be there; alone with a child or a
baby, give about 2 minutes of CPR before you leave (MedlinePlus's articles
on children and infants). The NHLBI suggests phone apps that show
where nearby AEDs are; find out where the ones near your home and work are
before you need them (general practice).

**Do not start compressions on someone who is breathing normally, coughing
or moving** (MedlinePlus).

**When it might be an opioid overdose.** Opioids include some prescription
medicines, heroin and fentanyl, and naloxone, sold for example as Narcan,
is a medicine that can help someone who is overdosing on one (the CDC).
The CDC lists the signs: the person cannot be woken, breathing is slow or
shallow or makes choking, gurgling or snoring sounds, the skin is
discoloured (especially the lips and nails), and the pupils are small
pinpoints that do not react to light. Its steps: give naloxone if you have
it and call 911, try to keep the person awake and breathing, lay them on
their side to prevent choking, and stay with them until help arrives. It says naloxone is sold over the
counter in all 50 states, and that "Naloxone will not harm someone if you
give it to them and they are not overdosing on an opioid." It also says to
wait 2 to 3 minutes after the first dose to see whether normal breathing
returns before giving a second. If they are not breathing normally, give
CPR as well; MedlinePlus says to give naloxone in a way that does not
interrupt it. The CDC notes that most states have laws that may protect
the person overdosing, or the person who calls for help, from criminal
penalties. Washington's is RCW 69.50.315: someone who seeks medical help in
good faith for a person having a drug overdose, and the person overdosing,
are not to be charged or prosecuted for drug possession on evidence found
because help was sought; it covers possession and nothing else (restated,
not legal advice).

**When the person is cold.** Someone found cold and apparently lifeless may
still be revived, and is handled gently: [Cold and
Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) explains why, and why you do not
stop.

## Unconscious and breathing: the recovery position

FEMA's course: "The recovery position is a way to place the body on its
side to keep the airway open and prevent choking." A person lying on their
back, unconscious, can choke on their own tongue or on vomit; on their
side, both drain away (our explanation of why the position works).

How to do it, restated from MedlinePlus:

1. Kneel beside them while they lie on their back.
2. Lay the arm nearer you out at a right angle to their body, palm up.
3. Bring the other arm across their chest and put that hand under their
   cheek on your side. Hold it there.
4. With your free hand, bend their far knee up to a right angle.
5. Pull on that bent knee to roll them towards you onto their side. Their
   hand under the cheek supports the head, and the outstretched arm stops
   them rolling too far. Leave the top leg bent at a right angle to steady
   them.
6. Keep checking their breathing until help arrives. If it stops, roll
   them onto their back and start CPR (general practice, from the CPR
   steps above).

**If the neck or back may be hurt, do not do this unless you have to.**
MedlinePlus says not to roll a person with a possible spinal injury unless
they are vomiting or choking on blood, or you need to check their
breathing; then roll them with a helper, one person at the head, keeping
the head, neck and back in line so they turn as one piece.

**Someone awake and short of breath** should be let alone to find their own
position. FEMA's course says that if someone is conscious, "let them sit or
lie however they feel best", and not to force them to lie down or sit up.
The CERT manual describes the position many breathless people choose on
their own: sitting or standing, leaning forward with hands or elbows on the
knees, which lets the chest expand as fully as it can.

## Choking

A choking person often grabs their throat with a hand. If they do not,
MedlinePlus lists the danger signs: they cannot speak, they have trouble
breathing, they make a noisy or high-pitched sound breathing in, their
cough is weak and useless, their skin turns bluish, and if the blockage is
not cleared, they lose consciousness.

What to do, restated from MedlinePlus, whose article cites the American
Heart Association's 2025 resuscitation guidelines:

- **Ask whether they are choking and whether they can speak.** If they are
  coughing hard and can talk, do not interfere: a strong cough is the best
  way to clear the object. Encourage them to keep coughing, and be ready.
- **If they cannot speak, or are struggling to breathe,** act fast. Give 5
  back blows, then 5 abdominal thrusts, and keep repeating the pair until
  the object comes out or they lose consciousness.
- **Back blows.** Stand behind them (kneel for a child), support their
  chest with one arm and lean them forward until the chest is about level
  with the ground, then give a firm blow between the shoulder blades with
  the heel of your other hand. Check after each one whether the object has
  come out.
- **Abdominal thrusts.** Stand behind them and wrap your arms around their
  waist. Make a fist and put its thumb side just above the navel, well below
  the breastbone. Grasp the fist with your other hand and give a quick
  thrust inward and upward. Check after each one.
- **Pregnant or obese people** get chest thrusts instead: arms around
  the chest, fist in the middle of the breastbone, firm thrusts straight
  back.
- **If they lose consciousness,** shout for help and have someone call 911,
  lower them onto a firm flat surface, and start CPR; the compressions
  themselves may push the object out. Before giving a breath, look in the
  mouth. Take an object out only if you can see it and it is loose: do not
  reach blindly into the throat, which can push it further in.
- **Afterwards, everyone who has choked needs to be seen by a doctor,**
  even if they seem fine, because both the choking and the thrusts can do
  damage. MedlinePlus says to contact a provider right away if a cough
  that will not go away, a fever, trouble swallowing or speaking,
  shortness of breath or wheezing follows in the next few days.

**A baby under a year old is different.** MedlinePlus says not to do
anything while the baby is coughing hard or crying strongly. Otherwise:
lay the baby face down along your forearm, supported on your thigh, head
lower than the body and the jaw held in your fingers, and give up to 5
firm blows between the shoulder blades with the palm of your hand; then
turn the baby face up, supporting the head, and give up to 5 quick chest
thrusts with the heel of one hand on the middle of the breastbone, just
below the nipple line. Repeat
until the object comes out or the baby stops responding. If the baby stops
responding, stops breathing or turns blue, shout for help, have someone
call 911, and give infant CPR on a firm flat surface. If you are alone and
have a phone, call 911 on speaker beside the baby and start at once (the
American Heart Association, restated); if you have no phone, MedlinePlus
says to give about 2 minutes of CPR before you leave the baby, or carry it
with you, to call. Only try to take an object out of the mouth if you can
see it.

**Infant CPR,** restated from MedlinePlus: lay the baby on its back on
something firm; put both thumbs side by side on the centre of the chest,
just below the nipple line, with your hands wrapped round the baby (or use
the heel of one hand if your thumbs cannot press deep enough); press about
1 1/2 inches (4 cm), 100 to 120 times a minute, letting the chest rise all
the way after each press; after every 30 presses, cover the baby's mouth
and nose with your mouth and give 2 breaths, each lasting about a second
and making the chest rise. The American Heart Association's 2025
guidelines dropped the old two-finger method because it did not press deep
enough (restated). This is a skill to practise on a manikin; a parents'
first aid class teaches it (general practice).

## Bleeding

Bleeding is the one place this guide gives the steps and sends you
straight on for the rest. FEMA's course reduces it to four: find where the
blood is coming from; put something between your hands and the blood if
you have it; press firmly and steadily, right on the source, hard, even if
it hurts the person; and keep pressing until the ambulance crew takes over,
handing over to someone else if you tire. The one exception to pressing
hard is a head wound over a skull that may be broken (MedlinePlus; see
Head, neck and back injuries below). When the wound is on an arm or a
leg and the bleeding cannot be controlled any other way, a tourniquet is
another way of applying that pressure.

Everything else, from how hard to press and why you must not lift the
dressing to look, to packing a wound and where a tourniquet goes, is in
[Bleeding and Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md). Read it before you need it.

## Shock

Shock is what happens when the body cannot keep enough blood moving to its
organs, after heavy bleeding, a severe burn, a serious infection or an
allergic reaction (our summary; the causes are named across the guides
linked above). MedlinePlus lists its signs: anxiety or restlessness, bluish
lips and fingernails, chest pain, confusion, dizziness or faintness, pale,
cool and clammy skin, little or no urine, heavy sweating, a rapid but weak
pulse, shallow breathing, and unconsciousness. The CERT manual names three
main signs a volunteer can check: breathing that is rapid and shallow; a
capillary refill of more than two seconds, meaning skin that is slow to
turn pink again after you press on it and let go (pressing a fingernail is
the usual way to test it: general practice); and failure to follow a
simple instruction, such as being asked to squeeze your hand. It warns
that the body hides shock at first, and that a person in shock can be
fully coherent and not complaining of pain.

**If it may be the heat, cool them instead.** Pale, clammy skin, heavy
sweating, a fast weak pulse and dizziness are also the signs of heat
exhaustion. Someone who has collapsed in the heat, and is breathing
normally, needs cooling, not blankets (if they are not breathing normally,
CPR comes first), and someone with heat exhaustion who is fully awake can sip
water: [When Heat Becomes Dangerous](when_heat_becomes_dangerous.md) has
the steps (our reading of the two sets of signs together).

What to do:

- **Call 911** for anyone with signs of shock, and do not wait for mild
  signs to get worse (MedlinePlus).
- **Treat the cause you can treat,** above all bleeding.
- **Lay them down flat** and keep them still, as [Bleeding and
  Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md) also says. MedlinePlus adds that if the
  person is awake and has no injury to the head, neck, back or legs, you can
  raise the legs about 30 cm (12 inches), keeping the head flat, and that
  you leave them flat if raising the legs causes pain or harm. Handle them
  gently: the CERT manual warns against rough or excessive handling.
- **Keep them warm.** The CERT manual: put a blanket or anything else
  under and over them to protect them from the ground, take off wet
  clothing, wrap them in dry layers, and shield them from the wind. FEMA's
  course gives the reason: "Keeping an injured person warm improves blood
  clotting, reduces stress and lowers the risk of shock."
- **Nothing to eat or drink** (MedlinePlus).
- **Watch their breathing,** at least every five minutes, until help comes
  (MedlinePlus). If they vomit, turn them onto their side, or roll them as
  one piece if the neck or back may be hurt.

## Head, neck and back injuries

**When to suspect one.** After a fall, a vehicle crash, a diving accident,
a sports injury, an electric shock, or any hard blow to the head, face,
neck or back (MedlinePlus); and, according to the CERT manual, whenever
someone is unconscious, or was found under collapsed building material or
heavy debris. The CERT manual's signs include a change in consciousness,
being unable to move a part of the body,
severe pain or pressure in the head, neck or back, tingling or numbness in
the hands or feet, blood or fluid from the ears or nose, bruising behind
the ear or around both eyes, uneven pupils, seizures, and nausea or
vomiting.

**What to do,** from MedlinePlus, restated:

- Call 911.
- Do not move them unless you must, to get them out of danger or to help
  them breathe.
- Hold the head and neck in the position you found them, with a hand on
  each side. Do not try to straighten the neck, and do not let it bend or
  twist.
- Do not let them get up and walk.
- Do not take off a helmet if a serious head or neck injury is possible.
- If the skull may be broken, do not press directly on the wound and do
  not pull anything out of it; cover it with sterile gauze if you have it,
  or a clean dressing.
- If they stop breathing normally, give chest compressions only, without
  moving the neck.

**After a blow to the head, watch for hours.** The CDC says the symptoms of
a concussion may not appear for hours or days, and that a person may not
recognise or admit that they are having problems. Its danger signs in an
adult, which mean the people checking on them should call 911 or take them
to an emergency department right away, are: a headache that gets worse and
does not go away; weakness, numbness, decreased coordination, convulsions
or seizures; repeated vomiting; slurred speech or unusual behaviour; one
pupil larger than the other; not recognising people or places, confusion,
restlessness or agitation; and losing consciousness, looking very drowsy
or being impossible to wake. For a child, add crying that will not stop
and refusing to nurse or eat. Someone should stay with them and check on
them through the first night (general practice, consistent with the CDC
addressing those danger signs to the people checking on the injured
person).

## Broken bones

From MedlinePlus, restated:

- **Keep the person still and calm,** and look them over closely for other
  injuries.
- **Do not straighten the limb or test whether it moves.** If help is coming
  quickly, leave the rest to the crew.
- **Splint it where it lies** if it must be held still: anything rigid, such
  as a rolled-up newspaper or a strip of wood, holding the area both above
  and below the break still (padding it and tying it on gently is general
  practice).
- **Check the circulation beyond the injury.** Press on the skin past the
  break (on the foot, for a broken leg): it should blanch and then turn pink
  again within about two seconds. Pale or blue skin, numbness or tingling,
  or a lost pulse below the injury is a reason to call 911.
- **A bone through the skin:** cover the wound with a clean or sterile
  dressing, do not push the bone back, and call 911.
- **Hip, pelvis or upper leg:** do not move the person unless you have to;
  if you must, pull them by their clothing.
- **Ice packs** help pain and swelling. Wrap the ice in a towel so it does
  not touch the skin, as MedlinePlus says in its head injury article.

MedlinePlus says to call 911 for a possible broken bone in the head, neck,
back, hip, pelvis or upper leg; for a person who is not responding; for
severe bleeding; for a bone through the skin; for skin below the injury
that is pale, cold, clammy or blue; and whenever you cannot hold the injury
still yourself. It adds that a young child who will not put weight on a
limb or move it after an accident should be treated as having a broken
bone.

MedlinePlus also describes straightening a limb whose circulation is
failing when no trained help can come quickly. That is a wilderness first
aid skill, taught hands-on in a course, and is not one to try from a page
(our reading).

## Seizures

The CDC says about 1 in 10 people in the United States may have a seizure
in their lifetime, so someday you may need to help. Some seizures look
like nothing more than confusion or staring; others make a person fall,
lose awareness and shake. Its steps:

- Stay calm, stay with the person, and move away anything nearby that could
  hurt them.
- If they are falling, ease them to the ground. Turn them gently onto one
  side with the mouth pointing down to keep the airway clear. Put something
  soft and flat under the head, take off their glasses, and loosen anything
  tight around the neck.
- Time it. The CDC: "Time the seizure to track if it lasts more than 5
  minutes. If it does, seek immediate medical attention or call 911."
- Check for a medical bracelet.
- When it is over, help them sit somewhere safe, explain what happened once
  they are alert, and offer to call someone to see them home.

What not to do, also from the CDC: do not hold them down or try to stop
their movements; "Don't put anything in their mouth. This can hurt their
teeth or jaw."; do not give mouth-to-mouth breaths during the seizure,
because people usually start breathing again on their own afterwards; and
offer no food or water until they are fully alert.

The CDC says to call 911 if the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, if
another follows soon after, if they have trouble breathing or waking
afterwards, if they were hurt, or if it happened in water; and also if it
is their first seizure ever, if they have diabetes and lose consciousness,
or if they are pregnant.

## Fainting

From MedlinePlus, restated: check that they are breathing (and if not,
call 911 and start CPR); loosen anything tight at the neck; raise their
feet about 30 cm (12 inches) above the level of the heart; turn them on
their side if they vomit; and keep them lying down for at least 10 to 15
minutes, somewhere cool and quiet. If they cannot lie down, sit them
leaning forward with the head between the knees.

Call 911 if the person who fainted fell from a height or was hurt, does
not come round within a couple of minutes, is pregnant, is over 50, has
diabetes, has chest pain or pressure, has a pounding or irregular
heartbeat, has lost the power of speech, has trouble seeing or cannot move
a limb, or had convulsions, a bitten tongue or lost bladder or bowel
control. Anyone who
faints for the first time should see a doctor (MedlinePlus). Do not raise
the legs of someone who may have hurt their neck, back or legs in the fall
(our reading of the MedlinePlus advice on shock and spinal injury above).

## Low blood sugar in someone with diabetes

The CDC says people with diabetes can have low blood sugar as often as once
or twice a week, and that as it gets worse a person may feel weak, have
trouble walking or seeing, act strangely or seem confused, or have a
seizure. Common early signs include a fast heartbeat, shaking, sweating,
nervousness, irritability or confusion, dizziness and hunger.

If the person is awake and able to swallow, the CDC's treatment is fast
sugar: 4 ounces (half a cup) of juice or ordinary, not diet, soda, a
tablespoon of sugar, honey or syrup, or glucose tablets or gel. Then give
it time. The CDC calls it the 15-15 rule: 15 grams of carbohydrate, a
15-minute wait, then another check of the blood sugar, and the same again
if it is still below 70 mg/dL, with a snack once they recover. With no
meter to check, give more sugar if they are no better after 15 minutes
(our reading). Someone with severe low blood sugar may need glucagon, an
injection or a nasal spray prescribed in advance, and the CDC says to get
emergency medical treatment immediately after giving it. If they cannot
swallow safely, give nothing by mouth (general practice, the same rule as
for shock and seizures) and call 911. The National Institute of Diabetes
and Digestive and Kidney Diseases (NIDDK), part of the
National Institutes of Health, asks people with diabetes to teach their
family and friends to give glucagon, and adds: "Tell them to call 911
right away after giving you glucagon or if you don't have a glucagon
emergency kit with you." The CDC's seizure advice says to call 911 for
someone with diabetes who has a seizure and loses consciousness, and
MedlinePlus says to call 911 for anyone with diabetes who faints.

## What to have ready

**A kit you can find in the dark.** The CERT manual says to assemble a
first aid kit for the home and one for each car, and its list includes
two-inch and four-inch sterile gauze pads, roller bandages, triangular
bandages, adhesive tape, sterile adhesive bandages in assorted sizes,
non-latex exam gloves, scissors, tweezers, safety pins, a thermometer,
moist towelettes, soap, a hot and cold compress and a first aid manual. Add the
medicines your household needs, chosen with a pharmacist or doctor rather
than from a list (general practice). The case for a commercial tourniquet,
and for learning to use it, is in [Bleeding and
Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md).

**The things that are not in the box** (general practice): the street
address written where a visitor or a child can read it out to 911; the
Poison Help number, 1-800-222-1222, saved in every phone; knowing where the
nearest AED is; and someone in the household who has taken a class.

## Handing over

When the ambulance crew arrives, tell them what happened, what you saw and
what you have done, and when. Times matter: when the person collapsed,
when CPR started, when a seizure began, when a tourniquet went on. The
CDC's stroke guidance, in [How Your Body Works](how_your_body_works.md),
asks for the time symptoms began, and [Bleeding and
Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md) says to note when a tourniquet goes on.
The CERT manual's advice for anyone
responding: "Write it down!" A note on your phone or the back of your hand
will do (general practice). Then do what the crew asks, and let them take
over.

Afterwards, wash your hands ([Handwashing and
Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md)). And expect to feel it. FEMA's course
says that in a life-threatening emergency your sense of time may be
distorted, you may get tunnel vision or hear sounds as if muffled, and you
may feel shaky or sick, and that these are normal reactions to stress
which most people experience. If the feelings stay with you, talk to
someone; [How Your Body Works](how_your_body_works.md) has the 988 line.

## Worked examples

Made-up situations, to show the order of the steps.

**A fall from a stepladder.** A neighbour clearing a gutter falls about two
metres onto a patio. He does not answer for a few seconds, then says he is
fine and starts to get up.

1. **Safe?** The ladder has fallen clear; there is nothing live or burning.
2. **Call.** You tell his wife to call 911 and come back. He fell from a
   height and was unresponsive, even briefly, which is enough (MedlinePlus
   and the CDC's danger signs).
3. **Keep him still.** You kneel at his head and ask him not to move; you
   hold his head steady with a hand on each side, in the position you found
   it.
4. **Check and comfort.** He is breathing normally and talking. You tell him
   help is on the way. You watch for vomiting, worsening headache,
   drowsiness and unequal pupils, and keep him warm with a coat laid over
   him, not pushed under him.
5. **Hand over.** You tell the crew the height, that he was out for a few
   seconds, and the time it happened.

**Choking at dinner.** A guest stands up holding her throat and cannot
answer when you ask whether she is choking. You stand behind her, lean her
forward and give five back blows, checking after each, then five
abdominal thrusts. On the third thrust the food comes out. She coughs and
breathes. She still goes to be checked, because both the choking and the
thrusts can do harm (MedlinePlus).

**A collapse in the garden.** An older neighbour is lying face down by the
compost heap. He does not respond when you tap his shoulder and shout. You
roll him onto his back: he is not breathing, only an occasional gasp. You
call 911 on speaker, put the phone down, and start pushing hard and fast in
the centre of his chest while the call-taker counts with you. Your teenager
runs for the defibrillator at the community hall two streets away, which
you had checked on a walk the month before. When it arrives, you switch it
on and do what it says.

## Where your own help stops

- **Diagnosis and medicine** belong to trained people. Beyond helping
  someone use their own prescribed device (an epinephrine injector, a
  glucagon kit) and giving naloxone, this guide recommends no medicine.
- **Moving someone with a possible neck or back injury,** except out of
  real danger or to keep them breathing.
- **Straightening a broken limb, pushing a bone back in,** or pulling out
  an object stuck in a wound ([Bleeding and Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md)
  says why not).
- **Breaths for a child or a baby,** and CPR beyond hands-only, until you
  have been taught, unless a 911 call-taker is talking you through them.
- **Anything the 911 call-taker tells you not to do.** The National 911
  Program asks callers to be prepared to follow any instructions the
  call-taker gives.
- **Going in where you would become the second casualty:** live wires,
  water, fire, smoke, gas, a damaged building.

When in doubt, call. A call that turns out not to be needed costs little;
one made too late can cost a life (our reading of the sources above).

## Traps

- **"Someone has probably called."** FEMA's course says to make the call
  anyway.
- **"She is talking, so she is fine."** A person in shock, or with a head
  injury, can be coherent at first.
- **"Give him some water."** Not to anyone who is in shock (MedlinePlus),
  not yet fully alert after a seizure (the CDC), drowsy or confused, or
  unable to swallow safely.
- **"Put something in her mouth so she doesn't swallow her tongue."** The
  CDC says not to put anything in the mouth of someone having a seizure.
- **"Help him up."** Not after a fall from a height or a blow to the head.
- **"Lift the dressing and see if it has stopped."** Every look tears the
  clot ([Bleeding and Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md)).
- **"I'll wait for the ambulance to start CPR."** Without it, brain damage
  begins within minutes.
- **"I don't know CPR."** Hands-only CPR on an adult needs no breaths, a
  child or a baby still gets chest compressions from someone who cannot
  give breaths, and many 911 call-takers can coach you through it.

## How the game models it

The game has a health bar, a First Aid ability that heals you on the
spot, and little else of what this guide is about.

- **Health, and coming back.** Your character has Health out of 100, and
  the ways it falls are listed in [How Your Body
  Works](how_your_body_works.md). When it runs out, the screen names the
  cause and offers a Respawn button (`src/gui/pages/hud.rs`). Under
  Settings > Gameplay > Death, Simplified, the default, its message says
  "Nothing was lost"; Realistic leaves what was in your backpack where you
  fell, in a pack you can walk back to (`data/world/death.ron`). Real life
  has no such button, which is why this guide exists.
- **First Aid, the ability, heals you at once.** The game's list of
  abilities includes First Aid, marked as a real ability rather than a
  fantasy one: for 15 energy it puts 35 health back immediately, and it
  can be used again 10 seconds later (`data/abilities.csv`,
  `src/systems/abilities.rs`). You use it with its Cast button on the
  Profile page, or with the number keys 1 to 9 on the HUD, which cast the
  first nine abilities you are able to use (`src/gui/pages/profile.rs`,
  `src/lib.rs`). The same list holds fantasy healing spells, such as Heal
  and Holy Light. Both are open from the start too, and put back more than
  First Aid: 50 and 55 health for 30 energy. Real first aid heals nobody.
  It keeps a person breathing, stops them losing blood, keeps them warm and
  gets them to people who can treat them, which is why this guide spends
  its time on calling, pressing and staying (our reading).
- **No injuries to treat.** Losing health in the game never leaves a wound,
  a burn, a fracture or a concussion. The game's data includes a catalogue
  of injuries, illnesses and treatments (`data/medical.ron`), but the
  system that would apply it (`src/systems/medical.rs`) is not registered
  with the game, so nothing in it ever happens to you. The Bleeding, Broken
  Bone, Concussion and Infected Wound conditions in
  `data/status_effects.csv` are not applied by anything either.
- **Medical items put health back, or say why not.** The item list
  includes a Bandage, a Medkit, a Splint, Antiseptic, Painkillers and a
  Defibrillator (`data/items.csv`), and the Crafting page has recipes
  that make some of them (`data/recipes.csv`). In the Inventory each one
  has a Use button. A Bandage puts back 5 health, a Medkit 30 and an
  Advanced Medkit 50, up to full, and each would also stop the Bleeding
  condition. The rest do nothing yet, because nothing in the game causes
  what they treat: pain, an infection, poisoning, venom, a broken bone or
  a stopped heart. Pressing Use on one, or on a medkit when you are not
  hurt, keeps it in your pack and tells you why in plain words. An item
  is used up only when it helps
  (`data/medical/treatments.ron`, `src/systems/treatment.rs`,
  `src/gui/pages/inventory.rs`). Like First Aid, a medkit restores
  health at once, which real first aid does not.
- **The Medicine skill.** Crafting a recipe trains the skill that recipe
  names (`src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`), and the medical recipes name
  Medicine. So in the game, Medicine rises by making supplies, not by
  helping anyone, and a high Medicine level says nothing about whether you
  can do CPR (our reading). The Profile page's "Dev: max skills" button,
  which sets every skill, Medicine included, to its maximum in one click,
  shows only in the Dev play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), not
  in Normal, where the game starts (`src/gui/pages/profile.rs`,
  `src/systems/skills/mod.rs`).
- **Your body carries over.** Your health, your vitals and the conditions
  on you are saved with your game, so quitting heals nothing
  (`src/save_load.rs`), and with Settings > Gameplay > "Start every
  session from the default home" off, as it is by default, the next
  launch brings them back as you left them. With that setting on, every
  session starts with a new body, and only your character's name, look
  and clothes carry over.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no CPR and no defibrillator to use, nobody chokes, faints, has a seizure or
falls off a ladder, there is no 911 to call, and there is no other person
for you to help. Everything in this guide is practice you have to find
outside the game: a class, a manikin, and the neighbours you would be
helping.

## You own this when

- Your first move is to look, listen and smell before you go in, and you
  know the ways a helper becomes the second casualty.
- You call 911 even when you think someone else has, you can give your
  address under stress, and you keep the line open until told to hang up.
- You can tell, in a few seconds, whether a person is awake and whether
  they are breathing normally, and you know that gasping is not breathing.
- You would start hands-only CPR on an adult who has collapsed and is not
  breathing normally, push hard and fast in the centre of the chest at 100
  to 120 a minute, and fetch or send for an AED and do what it says.
- You know where the nearest AED is.
- You can put someone in the recovery position, and you know when not to.
- You can tell a choking person who can still cough from one who cannot,
  and you know the back blows and thrusts for an adult and for a baby.
- You press hard on bleeding and do not lift to look, and you have read
  [Bleeding and Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md).
- You keep a person in shock flat, warm, still and fed nothing.
- You keep the head still after a fall or a blow to it, and you know the
  CDC's danger signs for the hours after.
- You time a seizure, put nothing in the mouth, and know when to call 911.
- You have Poison Help, 1-800-222-1222, saved in your phone.
- You have taken a first aid and CPR class, or booked one, and you know
  that a Red Cross certificate lasts two years and the skills fade sooner.

### Washington State (cited as the authority, restated in our own words)

- Revised Code of Washington 4.24.300, as last amended in 2014 (no civil
  liability for emergency care given at the scene of an emergency without
  compensation or the expectation of it, other than for gross negligence
  or willful or wanton misconduct; excluding care given in the course of
  paid employment).
  https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=4.24.300
- Revised Code of Washington 70.54.310, 1998 (no civil liability for injury
  from using a defibrillator at the scene of an emergency, other than for

  using it).
  https://app.leg.wa.gov/RCW/default.aspx?cite=70.54.310

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- Calling a person by name; naming one person to call 911 and report back;
  the phone on speaker; hazard lights in traffic; leaving a person where
  they are unless that place is the danger.
- Not hunting for a pulse in someone who has collapsed and is not
  breathing normally (as in How Your Body Works).
- Finding the AEDs near home and work in advance.
- Why the recovery position works; why shock happens, as a summary;
  pressing a fingernail as the usual capillary refill test.
- Nothing by mouth for someone who cannot swallow safely; not raising the
  legs of someone who may be hurt in the neck, back or legs; padding a
  splint and tying it on gently.
- Someone staying with a person through the night after a head injury.
- Practising infant choking first aid on a manikin in a class; treating
  limb straightening as a course-taught wilderness skill.
- The household medicines chosen with a pharmacist; the address where a
  child can read it; saving the Poison Help number.
- A note of times on your phone; that a call made too late costs more than
  one not needed; that the game's Medicine level says nothing about CPR,
  and that real first aid, unlike the game's ability, heals nobody; that
  the law protects careful help in good faith (not legal advice).
- Treating a collapse in the heat as heat illness rather than shock, read
  from the two sets of signs together.
- That a bed or a sofa gives way under CPR; giving more sugar after 15
  minutes when there is no meter to check the blood sugar.
