# Testing and Correcting Soil

You cannot see pH, and you cannot see phosphorus. Plants short of one
nutrient, poisoned by too much of another, or growing in soil too acid to
release what it holds can all look much the same: pale, slow and
disappointing. So people guess. They buy lime for soil that did not need
it, or a bag of balanced fertilizer for ground already overloaded with
phosphorus, and nothing gets better.

A laboratory soil test ends the guessing, costs little, and comes with a
recommendation written for your ground and your crop. This guide covers
getting one, reading the report, and making the two corrections most
gardens need: changing the pH, and adding the nutrients the test says are
short. It ends with the one test that is about your health rather than
your plants: lead, which is common in the soil around old houses and
along roads, and which you should check before growing food there.

It assumes you know what soil is made of. If terms like texture, organic
matter or pH are new, read [What Soil Is](what_soil_is.md) first, and for
looking up the mapped soil under your own ground, see
[Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md).

The rule that runs through everything below: **test first, change slowly,
test again.** Lime and sulfur take months to work, and an overcorrection
takes a year or more to undo.

## Why a laboratory, not a kit or a guess

Garden centres sell cheap colour-change test kits. They have a use, but
not the one people buy them for.

- South Dakota State University Extension compared home kits with
  laboratory results. The kits generally got pH to within one unit of the
  lab. They were inconsistent on phosphorus and potassium, and one kit
  recommended adding phosphorus to soils the lab found already high.
- One pH unit sounds small. It is not: the scale is logarithmic, so a
  reading one unit off is a tenfold error in acidity.
- Oregon State University Extension says colour kits can give an estimate
  of pH but are often not accurate enough to guide acidifying a soil, that
  their solutions degrade with heat and age, and that matching the colour
  to the chart is hard. NC State Extension puts it more bluntly: cheap kits
  are unreliable.
- Symptoms are no better. Oregon State warns that yellowing leaves have
  several possible causes and that you should not start acidifying your
  soil on symptoms alone.

A kit is fine for curiosity or a quick check between lab tests. Make
decisions from a laboratory report.

## Getting a laboratory test

**Where.** Start with your county or state cooperative extension office.
Many land-grant universities run a soil testing lab; where they do not,
extension keeps a list of labs that serve the area. The standard test is
inexpensive, and extension staff can help you read the result.

**What the standard test covers.** At the University of Minnesota's lab,
the regular test reports estimated texture, pH, percent organic matter,
phosphorus and potassium, with lead, soluble salts, other nutrients and
nitrate available as extra tests. Penn State's lab measures water pH, a
buffer test for lime, and phosphorus, potassium, magnesium and calcium.
Tell the lab what you intend to grow: vegetables, a lawn and blueberries
get different recommendations from the same soil.

**Taking the sample.** Guidance differs a little between labs, so follow
the instructions your lab sends. The common pattern:

1. Use a clean trowel or spade and a clean bucket.
2. Take slices or cores from many spots across the area: 8 to 10 random
   spots (Penn State) or five to eight (NC State).
3. Go as deep as the soil is, or will be, dug: 6 to 8 inches for a
   vegetable garden (Penn State). Lawns are sampled shallower.
4. Mix all the cores together in the bucket. That one mixed sample stands
   for the whole area.
5. If it is wet, let it air-dry, then bag about 1 to 2 cups of it
   (University of Minnesota).
6. Sample each area you manage differently on its own: the vegetable
   garden, the lawn, the blueberry bed. For raised beds, sample the beds
   themselves, not the paths between them (University of Minnesota).

**When.** Clemson Extension advises sampling in the fall for the next
year's garden, which leaves time for lime to work before planting. As for
how often: every two to three years for sandy soils and every three to
four for clays (Penn State), or every three to five years and whenever you
change what an area is used for (University of Minnesota).

## Reading the report

A report is a list of numbers, a rating for each, and a recommendation.
Here is what each line is telling you.

### Soil pH

This is the acidity the roots actually experience (Clemson). It tells you
whether the soil needs correcting at all. What counts as right depends on
the crop. Most vegetables do well in slightly acid soil: UC Cooperative
Extension says most nutrients are at their most available between 6.0
and 6.5, Oregon State says vegetable gardens produce well at 6.5, and
NC State says 6.5 to 7.0 is often considered ideal for most plants,
while adding that it is worth looking up the plants you actually grow.
Blueberries, azaleas and rhododendrons need it below 6, and preferably
below 5.5 (Oregon State). Wisconsin's extension guidelines list a target
pH for each crop; for tomatoes on mineral soil it is 6.0.

### Buffer pH

This is the line people confuse with the first one, and it is the one
that decides how much lime you need. Iowa State Extension says it plainly:
buffer pH measures the soil's resistance to changing its pH, and is not
the same thing as its pH.

Here is why it exists. Acidity in soil is not only in the water around the
particles. A reserve of it is held by the clay and organic matter, and it
comes out to replace whatever acidity you neutralise, so adding lime moves
the pH far less than the acidity in the soil water alone would suggest
(the University of Delaware's methods chapter explains the chemistry).
So two soils at the same pH can need very different amounts of lime.
The lab mixes your soil with a buffer solution that starts at a known pH
(pH 8 at Clemson's lab; the buffers other labs use start lower, as the
Delaware chapter shows) and measures how far the soil's reserve acidity
pulls it down. Cornell's lime guide puts the roles in one line:
soil pH tells you whether lime is needed, and buffer pH tells you how
much. The lower the buffer pH, the more lime. In Cornell's table for
reaching pH 6.5 (for the buffer its lab uses), a buffer pH of 5.5 calls
for 4.5 tons of lime an acre, and a buffer pH of 5.0 calls for 8.5.

NC State names the things that decide how much lime a soil needs,
among them its texture, organic matter, cation exchange capacity and type
of clay, and notes that sandy soils and soils low in organic matter need
less lime for the same change than clays and soils rich in organic
matter.

Not every report shows a buffer pH. Labs estimate lime need in more than
one way; the University of Delaware's review of Northeast lab methods
describes buffer tests, direct measures of soil acidity, and estimates
from organic matter or texture. Whichever your lab uses, the lime
recommendation on your report already accounts for the soil's buffering.

### Phosphorus and potassium

These come as a number, usually in parts per million, and a rating such as
below optimum, optimum and above optimum (Penn State), or low, medium,
high and very high. Read the rating, not the number, and do not compare
numbers from different labs. Labs use different chemical extractions, and
they give different numbers for the same soil: Penn State uses the
Mehlich 3 test, while Iowa State uses the Bray test on soils below pH 7.4
and the Olsen test above it. The rating is the lab's interpretation of its
own method.

Above optimum is a real result, not a good score. Penn State notes that
phosphorus beyond what crops can use is a water pollution risk; NC State
explains that phosphorus clings to soil particles rather than washing
through, so it travels with eroded soil and runoff into streams and lakes.
Colorado State adds that too much phosphorus fertilizer can worsen iron
and zinc shortages in plants. If phosphorus or potassium reads above
optimum, add none of it, however the bag is labelled.

### Organic matter

A percentage of the soil's weight. What Soil Is explains why it matters so
much. For a sense of scale, Iowa State says unamended Iowa soils usually
hold 2 to 4 percent, and treats 3 to 6 percent as ideal; soils elsewhere
differ.

### Cation exchange capacity (CEC)

This is how many positively charged nutrients (calcium, magnesium,
potassium and others) the soil can hold on its particles, reported in
milliequivalents per 100 grams of soil (Clemson). It is set mostly by clay
and organic matter. NC State gives the range: sandy soils run 1 to 5, and
soils rich in clay, silt or organic matter run 10 or more. Iowa State
treats anything below 6 as needing attention.

A low CEC means a soil that holds few nutrients and gives them up
quickly, and also one that needs little lime to change its pH. The
practical way to raise it is to build organic matter (NC State). Some
reports also give base saturation, the share of those holding sites
occupied by calcium, magnesium, potassium and sodium rather than acidity
(Clemson).

### Nitrogen

Usually absent, and that is not an oversight. Iowa State says soil tests
do not measure nitrogen. The University of Vermont explains why: soil
nitrogen keeps changing from one form to another and its availability
shifts quickly with weather, moisture and temperature. Penn State says it
is hard to measure meaningfully and recommends it from what the crop
needs instead.

### The recommendation

The last part of the report tells you what to add, usually in pounds per
100 or per 1,000 square feet. To scale it to your area, NC State's method:
work out the area in square feet, divide by 1,000, and multiply the rate
by that number. A bed 10 by 20 feet is 200 square feet, 0.2 of a
thousand, so a rate of 50 pounds per 1,000 square feet means 10 pounds.

## Raising pH: lime

**What to use.** Finely ground limestone, which is calcium carbonate, is
the usual recommendation (UC Cooperative Extension). Dolomitic limestone
also supplies magnesium, which helps where the soil test shows magnesium
is low and is not needed where it is already high (Penn State; UC).
Buy it finely ground: Penn State notes that even finely ground lime takes
several months to react, and it sets a minimum fineness for agricultural
lime.

**How much.** Use the number on your report. The table below is only to
give a sense of scale: it is UC Cooperative Extension's approximate
limestone requirement for mixing into the top 7 inches of soil, converted
from tons per acre to pounds per 100 square feet.

| Soil texture | pH 4.5 to 5.5 | pH 5.5 to 6.5 |
|---|---|---|
| Sand and loamy sand | 2.3 lb | 2.8 lb |
| Sandy loam | 3.7 lb | 6.0 lb |
| Loam | 5.5 lb | 7.8 lb |
| Silt loam | 6.9 lb | 9.2 lb |
| Clay loam | 8.7 lb | 10.6 lb |

Two things to see in it. A clay loam takes nearly four times the lime a
sand does for the same change, which is buffering in action. And moving a
loam one full pH unit takes nearly 8 pounds of limestone per 100 square
feet.

**How to apply it.** Spread it evenly and mix it into the root zone. UC
says to till it into the top 7 inches, and notes that limestone moves
downward very slowly, taking years to travel a few inches. It follows that lime
spread on the surface of an established bed or lawn acts mainly near the
surface.

**When.** Clemson advises applying lime two to three months before
planting to give it time to react. Penn State says even finely ground
lime needs several months, and that agricultural lime should generally
react completely within three years. Lime in the fall, plant in the
spring, retest in two or three years.

**Do not overshoot.** Raising pH above what your crop wants is easy with
a heavy hand, and lowering it again is the slow direction: UC says it
takes one to two years to see a reaction.

**Nitrogen fertilizer pushes the other way.** Ammonium-based fertilizers
acidify soil as soil bacteria convert them. Penn State puts the lime
needed to cancel that at about 3 pounds of calcium carbonate per pound of
nitrogen from urea or ammonium nitrate, and 7 from ammonium sulfate. A
bed fed that way for years drifts acid, which is one more reason to
retest on a schedule.

## Wood ash: a weak lime with potash

Ash from a wood stove or fire raises pH and adds potassium. Iowa State
Extension's guidance:

- It is weaker than lime. Its calcium carbonate equivalent varies from 25
  to 59 percent (when you do not know, assume 50), against 90 to 95
  percent for lime, so you may need 2 to 4 times as much ash to match
  lime's effect.
- Its fertilizer value is roughly 0-1-3: no nitrogen, a little
  phosphorus, some potash.
- Put on no more than 20 pounds per 1,000 square feet a year, about one
  five-gallon bucket, sprinkled over the ground in winter or early spring.
- Do not use it if your soil test shows pH above 7.0, around acid-loving
  plants such as blueberries, azaleas and rhododendrons, or heavily around
  vegetables, most of which prefer slightly acid soil; potato scab in
  particular is much worse in higher pH soil.
- Never use ash from treated or painted wood.
- It is caustic. Wear gloves, long sleeves, eye protection and a mask when
  spreading it.

Clemson adds that it can raise pH considerably in sandy soils, and to
spread it thinly and keep it off seedlings and roots.

Here is why ash is maintenance rather than a cure, worked from the numbers
above: 20 pounds per 1,000 square feet is 2 pounds per 100, and at 50
percent strength that equals about 1 pound of limestone. A loam needs
nearly 8 pounds of limestone per 100 square feet to rise one pH unit, so a
full year's ash moves it by about an eighth of a unit.

## Lowering pH: sulfur

Most vegetables never need this. Oregon State points out that west of the
Cascades, winter rain leaches the soil acid and gardeners usually need to
raise pH instead. Lowering it is mainly for
acid-loving plants like blueberries.

**What to use.** Elemental sulfur. Soil bacteria turn it into sulfuric
acid, and they need the soil warm, moist and airy to do it; mixing it in
works faster than leaving it on the surface (Purdue Extension).

**How slow.** Slower than lime. Clemson says several months, Purdue up to
a year for the full effect, Oregon State a year or more to finish, and UC
one to two years. Oregon State's practical schedule: apply in the fall,
test in the spring, and repeat if the pH is not there yet.

**How much.** Again, follow the lab. For scale, Purdue's table for a silt
loam mixed 6 inches deep, per 100 square feet:

| From pH | To 6.5 | To 6.0 | To 5.5 | To 5.0 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 7.5 | 2.0 lb | 3.5 lb | 4.5 lb | 6.0 lb |
| 7.0 | 1.0 lb | 2.0 lb | 3.5 lb | 5.0 lb |
| 6.5 | | 1.0 lb | 2.5 lb | 4.0 lb |
| 6.0 | | | 1.0 lb | 2.5 lb |

Use one third less on sandy soil and half as much again on clay (Purdue).

**Go in small steps.** Oregon State's amounts per application are 10 to
20 pounds per 1,000 square feet on sandy soil, rising to 40 to 50 on
clayey soil. It recommends two small applications a year apart over one
large one, checking pH every year at the same time of year, and says
plainly not to hurry the process by adding more than recommended: too
much sulfur can drive the pH so low that some elements in the soil become
toxic to plants. Purdue also warns against adding too much, which can
push the pH below the range your plants need. As an example of real
pace, Oregon State describes a loam at pH 5.7 given one application of
45 pounds of sulfur per 1,000 square feet, which came down to pH 5.1 and
settled there after two years.

**Avoid aluminum sulfate.** It acts faster, but Oregon State does not
recommend it because the aluminum can poison roots, Purdue warns of
putting on too much aluminum or iron above about 5 pounds per 100 square
feet, and Clemson warns that heavy use injures roots.

Keep records of what you added, how much, when, and how deeply you mixed
it (Oregon State). A year later you will not remember.

## Adding nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium

**Read the bag.** The three numbers on a fertilizer label are percentages
by weight of nitrogen (N), phosphate (P2O5) and potash (K2O), in that
order (Colorado State). A 10 pound bag of 10-10-10 holds 1 pound of
nitrogen, 1 of phosphate and 1 of potash; the other 7 pounds are the rest
of the compounds those nutrients come in, and filler. Phosphorus and
potassium are counted as those oxides by convention, so a soil test
recommendation given as P2O5 matches the middle number directly.

**Turn a recommendation into an amount.** Divide the pounds of nutrient
you need by the percentage on the bag. Colorado State's example: for 0.2
pound of nitrogen per 100 square feet from ammonium sulfate, 21-0-0,
spread about 1 pound of product per 100 square feet, since 1 pound times
21 percent is 0.21 pound of nitrogen.

**Nitrogen** is recommended by crop, not by test (Penn State). Organic
sources such as manure and blood meal release theirs slowly, over weeks
to months (Colorado State). Compost is mainly a way to build organic
matter; [Your First Compost](your_first_compost.md) covers making your
own.

**Phosphorus and potassium**: add them only where the test rates them
below optimum, and only as much as the report recommends. See the
phosphorus warning above.

**What a harvest takes away.** Nutrients leave your garden in the food you
carry out of it. A kilogram of tomatoes removes about 1.5 grams of
nitrogen (California's fertilization guidelines give 3 pounds per ton of
processing tomatoes), about 0.9 grams of phosphate and about 4 grams of
potash (Wisconsin's guidelines give 1.8 and 8 pounds per ton). So a 20
kilogram
tomato harvest carries off about 30 grams of nitrogen, 18 of phosphate
and 80 of potash. That is what a soil test is ultimately tracking: what
the crops took, what you gave back, and what is left.

## Lead: test before you grow food on old ground

Everything above is about growing well. This is about not harming
yourself or a child, and it is the reason this guide carries a caution.

**Where lead is.** The US Environmental Protection Agency lists where soil
lead tends to be higher: near roads, from decades of leaded gasoline
exhaust; around the outside of older buildings, especially along the
dripline, from lead-based paint and lead gutters and flashing; and near
hazardous waste sites, lead smelters, battery processing sites and other
industrial areas. Lead also stays where it landed: the University of
Minnesota notes that it does not move far in soil and stays concentrated
near the surface unless the soil is mixed.

**It is not in the standard test.** Ask for it. The University of
Minnesota offers lead as an add-on to its garden test, and EPA says the
best way to test soil for lead is to send samples to a laboratory
qualified to measure it. Some places have other metals of concern as well.
In the Puget Sound region of Washington, emissions from the former Asarco
smelter in Tacoma left arsenic and lead in the surface soil over more than
1,000 square miles; the Washington Department of Ecology studied King,
Kitsap, Pierce and Thurston counties and keeps a Dirt Alert map where you
can look up an address. There, ask the lab for arsenic too.

**Sampling for lead** is shallower than for nutrients. For a garden, the
University of Minnesota says to take 6 to 12 subsamples from the top 3 to
4 inches, and where children play, only the top half inch to inch, the
layer they actually touch.

**Reading the number.** There is no single national garden standard, so
read your result against several reference points:

- Background: surface agricultural soils in the United States average
  about 10 parts per million (ppm) of lead, ranging from 7 to 20. Much
  above that is contamination (University of Minnesota).
- The University of Minnesota's guidance: produce from soil below about
  300 ppm has generally been considered safe to eat, but because children
  swallow soil through hand-to-mouth play, soil above 100 ppm should not be
  used for gardening where children are exposed to it. Minnesota's own
  bare-soil standard is 100 ppm. If a result is over 100 ppm and children
  under six live there, it recommends asking a doctor or the local health
  department about a blood lead test.
- EPA's 2011 guide to gardening in urban soils states that EPA has no soil
  screening levels for gardening. The number EPA does use for homes
  belongs to its cleanup programmes: since a directive of 16 October 2025,
  a single screening level of 200 ppm for lead in residential soil, which
  replaced its January 2024 guidance (that guidance had added a lower 100
  ppm level where there were other sources of lead; the directive dropped
  it). That is the point at which EPA looks further at a property on a
  contaminated site, not a line below which a garden is declared safe.
- Your state may set its own numbers. Ask your extension office or state
  environmental agency what they use.

**Where the risk actually comes from.** EPA's urban gardening guide is
clear that the biggest exposure while gardening is soil getting into your
mouth, or contaminated dust being breathed in, especially by small
children. The same guide says research shows minimal risk from eating
plants grown in contaminated soil, though root vegetables accumulate
more. EPA's lead page adds that root vegetables such as carrots, and leafy
greens, take up more lead than fruiting crops like tomatoes, peppers and
cucumbers. The University of Minnesota agrees that lead on the outside of
unwashed produce is a bigger concern than lead taken up inside the plant.

**What to do where lead is raised**, from EPA's guidance unless noted:

- Grow food in raised beds or containers of clean soil. Lay landscape
  fabric on the ground first, so roots stay out of the soil below, and ask
  the supplier for proof that the soil you buy was tested.
- Keep food beds at least 10 feet from buildings, roads and driveways.
- Cover paths and bare ground with mulch, grass or other ground cover to
  cut down dust and splash onto crops.
- Keep the soil pH above 6.5 and add organic matter; both make lead less
  available to plants (University of Minnesota). EPA notes phosphate
  and compost have also been shown to reduce how much soil lead the body
  takes up.
- Wear gloves, wash your hands right after gardening and before eating,
  and watch small children so they do not eat soil.
- Wash produce under running water, peel root crops, and throw away the
  outer leaves of leafy greens, where soil particles collect.
- Clean tools, gloves and shoes before bringing them indoors, and wash
  heavily soiled clothes separately.
- Do not count on plants to clean the soil. EPA says growing plants to
  extract lead is ineffective, because lead is generally not available for
  plants to take up.

If the numbers are high enough to worry you, your state environmental
agency and EPA's brownfields technical assistance programme can point you
to specialists who advise on removing, replacing or treating the soil.

## A year of it, worked

- **Fall**: take your samples, including lead if the ground is near an old
  house or a road. Send them.
- **Late fall**: the report comes back. Spread and dig in the lime it
  recommends, or the sulfur for a blueberry bed. Do not add phosphorus or
  potassium the report rates above optimum.
- **Spring**: plant. Apply nitrogen as the recommendation and the crop
  require.
- **Every year**: if you are lowering pH for blueberries, test at the same
  time of year until it settles.
- **Every two to four years**: test again, and compare.

## How the game models it

The garden in this project follows the same chemistry, and its numbers
come from the same sources (`data/garden/soil_ph.ron`,
`data/garden/nutrients.ron`).

- Every soil bed remembers its own pH between crops. A new bed starts at
  6.5, a bed prepared the way the guides describe.
- Lime, sulfur and wood ash are counted in grams of calcium carbonate
  equivalent. Their effect on the pH is set by the UC table above: a loam
  takes about 381 grams of limestone per square metre to move one pH unit.
- None of them acts at once. Each reacts over time, sulfur slowest and
  wood ash fastest. The exact speeds are game estimates anchored on the
  months-long timings in this guide, because no source gives a simple rate.
- Sulfur and wood ash are capped per application at 20 pounds per 1,000
  square feet (97.6 grams per square metre). For sulfur that is the top
  of Oregon State's rate for sandy soil; for wood ash it is Iowa State's
  yearly limit, but the game caps each application and leaves the
  once-a-year part to you. Wood ash also adds potash to the bed.
- Nitrogen fertilizer slowly acidifies a bed. Stored urine is counted like
  urea, at the lower of the published rates for a crop that takes up the
  nitrogen it is fed.
- A crop in soil outside its pH range loses harvest, about 30 percent per
  pH unit outside, taken from USDA's relative yield table for field crops.
- Each bed also holds a store of nitrogen, phosphate and potash that every
  harvest draws down by that crop's measured removal per kilogram, the
  tomato figures above among them.
- Settings has a Soil pH switch: Off freezes all of it.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no soil test to send away, because the garden panel shows each bed's pH
directly; every bed is the same loam, where real gardens differ; and
nothing in the game's soil carries lead.
