# Keeping Animals

A goat in the yard is not a tool you put away at night. It needs water
every day of its life, feed that suits its gut, shelter from wind, wet
and heat, a fence it respects, company of its own kind, and a person
who looks at it closely at least twice a day. In return it turns grass
and scrub that people cannot eat into milk, meat, fibre, manure and
sometimes work. Kept well, a few animals are one of the oldest ways a
household feeds itself. Kept badly, they suffer, and they can hurt the
people around them.

This guide is about keeping a few grazing and farmyard animals on a
homestead: goats, sheep, a pig or two, perhaps a cow or a horse. Hens
have their own guide, [Chickens and Eggs](chickens_and_eggs.md). Most of
the numbers here come from the US Department of Agriculture: its
Natural Resources Conservation Service (NRCS), whose National Range and
Pasture Handbook (June 2022) has a chapter on livestock nutrition,
husbandry and behaviour, its Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service
(APHIS) and its Agricultural Research Service (ARS). Others come from
the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), the Food and Drug
Administration (FDA), the Occupational Safety and Health Administration
(OSHA), the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health
(NIOSH), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the Federal
Emergency Management Agency's Ready.gov and federal regulations. Those
are United States government works, in the public domain. A few facts
come from the extension services of Oklahoma State, North Dakota State,
Nebraska and Minnesota; those are copyrighted, so their facts are
restated in our own words, never quoted. Where something is general
practice or our own reading, the text says so.

Rules about keeping animals differ from place to place, and many of the
rules below are United States federal rules. Wherever you live, your
own town, county and state or country have the final word.

Three other guides sit beside this one. [How Animals Work](how_animals_work.md)
explains the bodies and behaviour behind every rule here. [Animal Health
and Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md) covers spotting illness, the
diseases that pass from animals to people, and medicines.
[Handwashing and Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) covers the habit
that does most to keep animals from making you ill. Killing and
butchering animals for meat is its own topic, not yet written, and this
guide does not teach it.

## First: decide whether you can, and whether you may

**Find out what is allowed.** Towns and counties limit which animals may
be kept, how many, and how close their pens may be to a neighbour's
house or a well, and some forbid livestock altogether. Ask your city or
county planning and zoning office before you buy (general practice).

**Find your vet before you need one.** Not every vet treats farm animals.
The FDA's leaflet on antibiotics for sheep and goats puts it plainly:
illness never comes at a convenient time, and the best time to talk to
your vet is before your animals are sick. Since June 2023 the
antibiotics that also matter in human medicine need a vet's
prescription when they are used for animals (see [Animal Health and
Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md)), so a keeper with no vet cannot
buy them.

**Count the hours.** Animals need someone every morning and every evening,
in every kind of weather, including the days you are ill or away. Know
who will do it when you cannot (general practice).

**Never keep one alone.** Goats, sheep, cattle and horses are herd
animals. The Oklahoma State University extension service notes that a
lone animal left in a pen may become agitated and try to jump the fence
to rejoin its herd. Keep at least two of a kind, or a compatible
companion (general practice, following [How Animals
Work](how_animals_work.md)).

### Choosing your animals

How much an animal eats and drinks is the first thing to match to your
land and your water. The NRCS handbook measures grazing animals in
animal units: one animal unit is a mature cow of about 1,000 pounds
(450 kg) with a calf up to 6 months old, and NRCS takes its standard
forage demand as 26 pounds (11.8 kg) of oven-dried forage a day, or 30
pounds (13.6 kg) as fed, air-dry. Its guide to other animals, which can
be adjusted for actual weights, includes:

| Animal | Animal units | Oven-dry forage a day |
|---|---|---|
| Cow with calf | 1.00 | 26 lb (11.8 kg) |
| Horse, mature | 1.25 | 32.5 lb (14.7 kg) |
| Sheep, mature | 0.20 | 5.2 lb (2.4 kg) |
| Goat, mature | 0.15 | 3.9 lb (1.8 kg) |

The metric figures are our conversions. The handbook stresses that real
intake varies with the forage, the animal and the weather: beef cows
have been measured eating from 1.5 to 3.5 percent of their body weight
a day.

Pigs and poultry are different. The handbook says they can get only
about 5 to 20 percent of their needs from forage, and the rest must
come from grain or other feeds, so they cost feed rather than turning
your grass into food (our reading).

## Water, every day

The handbook says animals are more sensitive to a lack of water than of
food: the first sign of a shortage is that they eat less, the loss of
10 percent of the body's water is severe, and 20 percent is fatal. Its
expected daily drinking for adult livestock in a temperate climate is:

| Animal | Water a day |
|---|---|
| Sheep and goats | 1 to 4 gallons (4 to 15 litres) |
| Pigs (adult and market) | 1.5 to 3 gallons (6 to 11 litres) |
| Beef cattle | 6 to 18 gallons (23 to 68 litres) |
| Horses | 8 to 12 gallons (30 to 45 litres) |
| Dairy cattle | 10 to 30 gallons (38 to 114 litres) |
| Poultry | about twice the weight of the feed they eat |

The litres are our conversions. The handbook's note on that table warns
that in heat stress these upper limits can rise dramatically, and for
poultry it says: "During periods of extreme heat stress, water
requirements may easily quadruple." Being in milk raises the need too.
The handbook's section on dairy cattle says a lactating dairy cow needs
30 to 50 gallons (114 to 189 litres) a day, well above the table's range
for dairy cattle, and its section on sheep says their needs increase
greatly in late pregnancy and while they are in milk.

**A worked example.** Two goats and two sheep need about 4 to 16 gallons
(15 to 60 litres) of water a day in mild weather (our arithmetic), and
far more on a hot day or while the does are in milk. Plan your water for
the top of that range, not the bottom, and check troughs morning and
evening, because an animal that knocks over a bucket at 8 in the morning
has no water until you notice (general practice). Where water comes from
and how to store it are in [Where Water Comes
From](where_water_comes_from.md) and [Storing Water
Safely](storing_water_safely.md).

**Winter.** The handbook advises warm drinking water in cold areas, so
animals do not have to spend body energy warming it, and notes that they
then drink more. Ice on a trough has to be broken or prevented every
day (general practice).

**Water quality.** The handbook says new water sources should be tested,
naming nitrites, sulfates, total dissolved solids, salinity, bacteria,
pH and pesticide residues, because poor water cuts drinking and feed
intake and can make animals ill. Nitrites, it explains, stop the blood
carrying oxygen and can kill.

## Feed: forage first, and nothing that does not belong

### How much

Grazing animals live on forage: pasture, browse and hay. Using the
handbook's figures, two mature goats need about 7.8 pounds (3.5 kg) of
oven-dry forage a day between them, which is about 9 pounds (4.1 kg) of
air-dry hay as fed, or about 3,300 pounds (1,500 kg) of hay to carry them
through a year with no grazing at all (our arithmetic from the handbook's
26 to 30 ratio). Animals waste some hay, so buy more than the arithmetic
says (general practice).

Quality matters as much as quantity. The handbook says intake is
influenced most by the quality of the forage: as quality falls, animals
eat drastically less, and as plants mature they become less digestible.
It calls a lack of protein probably the most common of all nutrient
deficiencies, and for ruminants protein matters twice over, because the
microbes in the rumen need it to digest forage ([How Animals
Work](how_animals_work.md) explains the rumen). An animal on old, dry,
weathered hay can lose condition with a full belly (our reading).

**Change feed slowly.** The handbook describes the rumen's microbes
adjusting to the feed they are given, and the Nebraska extension service
recommends a consistent, steady diet to prevent bloat. So change from
one feed to another over days, not overnight (our reading). Keep grain
and feed sacks where animals cannot break in and gorge; Nebraska links
grain-heavy diets in cattle to bloat and to acidosis, an acid upset of
the rumen (general practice for the lock).

### Salt and minerals

The handbook gives the general salt needs of grazing animals as 1.5 to
3 pounds a month for a cow, 2 to 3.5 for a horse and 0.25 to 0.5 for a
sheep or goat, and says salt and minerals should not be placed beside
the water. Minerals are where species differ most dangerously. The
handbook says copper should not be part of a mineral mix for sheep,
because it is toxic to them, even though other livestock may need it.
Buy a mineral mix labelled for the species you are feeding, and never
give sheep a mix made for goats or cattle (general practice from that
warning).

### Feeds that kill the wrong animal

Some feeds are safe for one species and deadly to another.

- **Medicated feeds.** Some feeds carry a drug, and the label says which
  animals may eat them. The NRCS handbook names monensin and tells
  keepers to refer to its label. Federal rules (21 CFR 558.355) approve
  monensin in some feeds for cattle and goats, and in some for broiler
  (meat) chickens, growing turkeys and quail, and every feed containing
  it must carry this warning: "Do not allow horses or other equines
  access to feed containing monensin. Ingestion of monensin by horses has
  been fatal." The poultry feeds add mature turkeys and guinea fowl to
  the animals to keep away, and are not for laying hens. The cattle and
  goat feeds must say they "are safe for use in cattle and goats only.
  Consumption by unapproved species may result in toxic reactions", and
  "Do not feed to lactating goats." The handbook adds that monensin is
  fatal in sheep, and the rules record that mixing errors which left too
  much of it in feed have killed cattle. So read every feed label, store
  each species' feed apart, and never let a horse, a sheep, a laying hen
  or a milking goat reach a medicated feed made for another animal
  (general practice built on those warnings).
- **Meat and bone in ruminant feed.** Under 21 CFR 589.2000, feed for
  cattle, sheep, goats, deer and other ruminants that contains protein
  from mammal tissue is adulterated, with listed exceptions such as milk
  products. Feeds made with such protein must carry the label "Do not
  feed to cattle or other ruminants". The rule is built around the
  agents of transmissible spongiform encephalopathies (its exemptions
  are for processes that destroy or detect them), the family of brain
  diseases that, APHIS says, includes scrapie in sheep and goats. Retail
  pet food is exempt from that label, so a bag of dog or cat food may
  not warn you.
  Anyone who feeds ruminants must keep the invoices and labels of feeds
  containing animal protein for at least a year. At home: no meat, meat
  scraps, bone meal, pet food or pig and poultry feed for your goats,
  sheep or cattle (our rule, built on the regulation).
- **Kitchen scraps for pigs.** Meat can carry serious pig diseases. APHIS
  says diseases that do not exist in the United States, foot-and-mouth
  disease, African swine fever and classical swine fever among them,
  could be brought in through contaminated meat, and
  that the African swine fever virus can survive for months in pork and
  pork products. Federal rules (9 CFR 166) say food waste containing meat
  or other animal material may be fed to pigs only after it has been
  heated through at boiling for 30 minutes at a licensed facility, with
  some listed exceptions such as bakery waste, eggs and dairy products.
  Waste from a household fed directly to pigs on the same premises is
  outside that federal definition, but APHIS's advice to small farm and
  pet pig owners is simply not to feed pigs garbage or let them eat
  trash, and its 2024 fact sheet lists 25 states that forbid feeding food
  waste to pigs altogether. At home: never give a pig meat, or any food
  that contains meat or has touched meat while it was being prepared,
  which is how APHIS describes the waste the law covers (our rule, built
  on APHIS's advice). Ask your state animal health official before you
  feed a pig any scraps at all.

### Plants that poison

The ARS runs a laboratory that studies plants poisonous to livestock.
Its research leader explains that poisonous plants are very common on
rangeland in the United States, that some taste good to livestock while
others are usually eaten only by a hungry animal, that the same plant
can affect species differently (larkspur is highly toxic to cattle but
generally not to sheep), that
some ornamental plants used in landscaping are toxic to livestock, and
that there are very few antidotes once an animal is poisoned. So:

- **Never turn hungry animals onto unfamiliar ground.** Fill them with
  hay first (general practice, consistent with the hunger warning).
- **Never feed garden or hedge clippings** unless you know every plant in
  them is safe for that species (general practice, following the
  ornamental warning).
- **Learn your local poisonous plants** from your extension office before
  the animals arrive (general practice). [Identifying Wild
  Plants](identifying_wild_plants.md) teaches the skill of telling one
  plant from another.

The handbook adds two plant hazards that depend on the weather. Some
plants release prussic acid (hydrogen cyanide) after freezing, wilting
or crushing, and grass tetany, a deficiency of magnesium and calcium,
comes from rapidly growing plants in cold, cloudy weather.

## Pasture: rest it, and do not graze it bare

The handbook's first rule of grazing is balance: the number of animals
matched to the forage the land actually grows, adjusted every year,
because no two years have the same weather. It says avoiding overgrazing
is paramount, especially when plants are growing fast.

Grazing also helps control worms, but how long a pasture must rest is
not settled, and rest on its own will not clear them. The handbook says
to rest pastures for at least 20 days and graze no closer than 4 inches
(10 cm) from the ground to break stomach-worm life cycles, with longer
rests for goats and sheep; for the barber pole worm of goats it gives a
rest of 30 to 45 days and a stubble height of 4 inches, because the
worms lie near the soil surface. The University of Minnesota Extension's
livestock vet and educator set the bar higher. They explain that the
barber pole worm's infective larvae can live on grass for up to 2 months
in warm weather and 6 months or more in cool weather, so they advise
resting a pasture grazed in a Minnesota summer for at least 2 months,
and moving sheep and goats off before the grass is shorter than 6
inches (15 cm). Grazing adult cattle or horses after the sheep and
goats, or cutting the pasture for hay, also clears larvae, they say.
They add that worms have become resistant to every class of chemical
dewormer, so getting rid of them entirely is no longer realistic, and
pasture management, breeding from animals that resist worms and
treating only the animals that need it have to work together.

So the right rest depends on your climate and season (our reading).
Dividing a pasture into several paddocks and moving the animals round
them is how keepers do it (general practice), and the plan for worms is
one to make with your vet (general practice). [Animal Health and
Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md) covers the signs of worms.

**Lush legumes and bloat.** The Nebraska extension service names
alfalfa and many clovers among the plants that cause bloat in cattle: a
build-up of gas in the rumen that can kill if it is not treated
promptly (the signs are in [Animal Health and
Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md)). Its prevention advice is:

- never turn hungry animals into a pasture with a lot of bloat-causing
  plants;
- fill them with dry hay or grass first;
- avoid putting them onto such a pasture while it is wet with dew, rain
  or irrigation;
- watch them closely for the first hours and days;
- wait three to five days after frost damage before grazing high-risk
  plants; and
- plan with your vet, before the grazing season, what you will do in a
  bloat emergency.

Its guide is written about cattle; the same rumen is at work in sheep
and goats (our reading).

## Shelter, bedding and feed storage

**Livestock live outside the house.** The CDC says farm animals are not
meant to live inside the home or be kept indoors, because that raises
the risk of illness in people.

**Shut them in at night.** The CDC advises keeping farm animals in an
appropriately sized enclosed area at night, to cut their contact with
wild animals that could carry rabies or other diseases.

**Shelter from weather.** The handbook says livestock shelter should be
considered where weather stress is a factor. Wind and wet strip the
insulation from an animal's coat ([How Animals
Work](how_animals_work.md)), so a windbreak, a dry place to lie down and
shade in summer do the most good (our reading). Space allowances for
your species and climate come from your extension office (general
practice).

**Air.** OSHA notes that the air in barns can carry organic dust,
microorganisms and gases from the breakdown of grain and animal waste,
and that confinement increases the exposure. A shed needs fresh air
moving through it above the animals, without a draught across them
where they lie (general practice).

**Clean, and keep the rats out.** The CDC advises cleaning enclosures and
stalls frequently while wearing boots, gloves and a mask; disposing
properly of soiled bedding and spoiled, expired or uneaten feed; and
using raised feeders and storing feed off the ground, to keep mice and
rats away.

**Heat lamps for newborns.** A heat lamp over a lamb, kid or piglet is a
fire risk. North Dakota State University's extension service advises
hanging it from a chain with a locking connector, never twine or rope;
plugging it straight into an outlet protected against ground faults and
arc faults, never an extension cord; keeping a 10-pound ABC extinguisher
at each entrance; and checking it several times a day. The full list is
in [Chickens and Eggs](chickens_and_eggs.md). New wiring for a barn is a
job for an electrician (general practice).

### Hay can set itself on fire

Wet hay heats up as it cures, and it can catch fire on its own. North
Dakota State University's extension service explains:

- **The cause is moisture.** Small bales stacked at 20 percent moisture
  or more, and big bales at more than 16 percent, are a fire danger, and
  hay fires usually happen within six weeks of baling. Buy or make hay
  that was baled dry.
- **The warning signs** are a slight caramel smell or a distinctly musty
  one, which probably mean the hay is heating.
- **Check the temperature** with a probe: a pipe driven into the stack,
  with a thermometer lowered inside it and left for 10 minutes, in
  several places.
- **Know the normal warmth.** All hay baled above 15 percent moisture
  heats as it cures. The release says it usually peaks at 125 to 130 F
  (about 52 to 54 C) within three to seven days, with little risk, and
  then cools to a safe temperature over the next 15 to 60 days.
- **Above 130 F (55 C), keep watching.** At that heat, the release
  explains, a chemical reaction starts making a flammable gas, which can
  catch fire if the hay gets hotter. Once a stack is heating, it says,
  keep monitoring its temperature: at least daily, and more often as it
  climbs (general practice for how often).
- **Above 175 F (79 C) a fire is imminent.** At that point, or at any
  smell or sight of smoke, call the fire department at once, and do not
  move the hay: opening it lets air reach the hot core.
- **Tell the firefighters about preservatives.** The release warns that
  hay treated with preservatives containing ethoxyquin and butylated
  hydroxytoluene gives off hydrogen cyanide, a deadly gas, at about 240 F
  (115 C). If your hay was treated, say so when you call (our reading).
- **Never walk on a heating stack.** If you must go on top, lay planks,
  wear a safety line, and have someone holding it from a safe place,
  because the surface can collapse into a burning pocket.

The release also advises spreading bales away from other feed and from
buildings when you suspect a fire could develop. Once the hay is above
175 F or smoking, leave it where it is for the fire department; and if
you are unsure which stage you are at, call the fire department before
you move anything (general practice). The release also suggests not
piling the whole harvest in one place, so that one fire cannot take it
all. Store hay away from the house (general practice).

## Fences

Animals that get out get hit by cars, eat poison, ruin a neighbour's
garden and get lost. Fences are a daily job, not a one-off.

Electric fencing is common for goats, sheep and cattle. The NRCS
specification for electric fences in Illinois (May 2019) sets out how
it should be done:

- **Train the animals to it.** Put them in a well-fenced area with an
  electric wire across it; they need at least 12 hours of exposure, and
  most are trained in 48. Animals that approach the fence cautiously are
  trained.
- **Shear first.** Sheep and goats must be clean shorn before they meet
  an electric fence, because a fleece keeps the shock from reaching them
  (that reason is our reading).
- **Never electrify barbed wire.** The specification forbids it.
- **Use a proper energiser,** installed to the maker's instructions,
  with its own ground rods. Keep ground rods at least 25 feet (7.6 m)
  from water lines, well casings and other grounding systems, and never
  connect the energiser into an existing ground system.
- **Protect it from lightning.** The specification says every energiser
  needs an external lightning arrestor attached to the fence wires, with
  more ground rods of its own than the energiser has, placed at least 50
  feet (15 m) from the energiser's ground rods. An energiser that runs on
  mains power also needs a surge protector fitted between it and the
  power supply.
- **Height.** Interior fences must be at least two thirds of the shoulder
  height of the animals they hold.

Never make your own energiser or connect a fence wire to mains power
directly; buy a commercial fence energiser and follow its instructions,
and tell children and visitors which wires are live (general practice).

## Handling animals without getting hurt

Livestock are strong, and the CDC warns that being kicked by an animal
can cause serious injury. Its rules for being around farm animals:

- **Know where the animals are, and where your way out is,** at all times.
- **Avoid standing directly behind an animal or approaching from the
  rear,** even when it is in stocks or restrained.
- **Supervise children around farm animals,** teach them to keep fingers
  away from mouths and never to approach from behind, and never let them
  grab tails.
- **If you are kicked, get away from the animal at once and seek medical
  attention, especially for a kick to the head.**

The Oklahoma State extension service studied 150 cattle handling
injuries on 100 Oklahoma farms in 1997 and found more than half came
from human error, most often when people were tired, hurried, upset,
preoccupied or careless. Its advice, restated:

- Move animals calmly, working at the edge of their flight zone (see
  [How Animals Work](how_animals_work.md)).
- Handle cattle in small groups, up to about ten.
- Do not climb into a crowding pen where the animals are bunched up.
- Do not stand on fences or reach your arms between or over the rails.
- If an animal is not properly caught in a crush or chute, let it out
  and bring it through again rather than working on it loose.

And from general practice: male animals (bulls, rams, billy goats, boars
and stallions) can be dangerous at any age and should never be treated
as pets; never wrap a lead rope round your hand; and never go into a pen
with a large animal when you are alone and nobody knows where you are.

## Records and identification

- **Sheep and goats.** Federal scrapie rules (9 CFR 79.2) require sheep
  and goats that move in interstate commerce, or pass through a market
  or show that handles animals from other states, to carry official
  identification traceable to the flock they came from, applied before
  they are first mixed with animals from another flock, when they change
  owners, or before they cross a state line. Some animals are exempt,
  and states add their own rules. Ask your state animal health official or APHIS how to get
  official tags before you buy or sell.
- **Crossing a state line.** Under 9 CFR 86.5, cattle and bison moved
  between states generally need an interstate certificate of veterinary
  inspection, issued by an accredited vet or an animal health official,
  and the same section sets the papers for horses, poultry, sheep,
  goats, pigs and farmed deer. States add their own. Call the state animal health
  official before you buy an animal from another state.
- **Your own records.** Write down every animal's birth, purchase,
  breeding, treatments and the feed you buy (the ruminant feed rule above
  requires the feed records), as [Keeping Records](keeping_records.md)
  describes.

New animals bring new disease. A 2007 APHIS fact sheet's rule of thumb
is to keep new animals, and your own animals coming back from a show or
sale, apart from the rest for 30 days. The reasons, and the rest of
keeping disease out, are in [Animal Health and
Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md).

## Manure and your water

Manure is fertiliser and it is also a carrier of the germs in [Animal
Health and Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md). The EPA's advice for
private wells is to slope the ground so that surface runoff drains away
from the well, and to check with your local health department or
environmental agency that activities near your property are a safe
distance from the well. Put pens, manure heaps and compost downhill and
well away from your well and any stream (general practice from that
advice). How to make a hot compost heap, and how long manure must sit
before it touches food crops, are in [Your First
Compost](your_first_compost.md) and [Chickens and
Eggs](chickens_and_eggs.md).

**Never go into a manure pit or tank, even to save someone.** OSHA lists
manure pits among the respiratory hazards of farms, from gases released
as animal waste breaks down. A 1990 NIOSH alert names four dangerous
gases found in them, methane, hydrogen sulfide, carbon dioxide and ammonia,
and says the air in a pit can be short of oxygen, poisonous or
explosive. It describes two incidents that killed seven people: a dairy
farmer who went down into a pit to clear a blocked pipe and collapsed,
and his brother, who went in to rescue him and died too; and five farm
workers who died one after another going into a pit on their farm.
NIOSH's earlier alert on confined spaces says: "More than 60% of
confined space fatalities occur among would-be rescuers".

If someone collapses in a pit or tank, do not go in after them. Call
911 (or your local emergency number), keep everyone else out, and wait
for rescuers with breathing equipment (general practice built on those
alerts). A pit, a covered
tank or a deep slurry store is a job for professionals with gas monitors
and breathing equipment (general practice).

## When disaster comes

Ready.gov's advice for owners of large animals, such as horses, goats or
pigs, adds to its general pet plan:

- make sure every animal has some form of identification;
- evacuate animals early, whenever possible, with primary and secondary
  routes mapped in advance;
- have the vehicles and trailers each kind of animal needs, and
  experienced handlers and drivers;
- make sure the destination has food, water, veterinary care and
  handling equipment; and
- if you cannot evacuate, decide in advance whether to move large
  animals into a barn or turn them loose outside.

A trailer that has never been hitched, and animals that have never been
loaded into it, will not leave in a hurry (general practice). Practise
before you need to.

## A day and a year with livestock

These rhythms are general practice, built on the sources above.

- **Every morning and evening.** Water topped up and clean; feed given;
  every animal looked at, counted and seen to move, eat and chew its cud
  (the APHIS fact sheet advises assessing animals' health daily); fences
  and gates checked; any milk, eggs or fleece collected.
- **Spring.** Births, and with them heat lamps and the birthing hygiene
  in [Animal Health and Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md). Lush
  grass and clover bring bloat risk; turn animals out with full bellies.
  Worms build up as the weather warms; start the pasture rotation.
- **Summer.** Water use climbs; shade and moving air matter; flies and
  heat stress are the daily watch ([How Animals
  Work](how_animals_work.md) explains heat).
- **Autumn.** Hay in, dry and checked for heating. Breeding for spring
  births. Body condition scored before winter.
- **Winter.** Water kept liquid, warm if you can; wind and wet kept off;
  more feed, because cold costs energy.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Illness and injury:** your vet, with whom you have planned in advance.
  Reportable diseases go to the state animal health official, as [Animal
  Health and Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md) explains.
- **Hay that is heating past 175 F, or smoking:** the fire department.
- **Barn wiring, lights and heat lamp circuits:** an electrician.
- **Manure pits and tanks:** professionals with gas monitors. Someone
  collapsed inside one: 911 and trained rescuers, never you.
- **Hoof trimming, shearing and assisting a difficult birth:** learn from
  an experienced keeper or your vet before you try alone (general
  practice).
- **Killing an animal for meat:** your local and state rules first, and the
  butchery topic, not yet written. A sick or injured animal that must be
  put down is a job for a vet (general practice).
- **A dead animal:** state and local rules set how it is disposed of; ask
  your state animal health official or extension office (general
  practice). APHIS offers free scrapie testing of adult sheep and goats
  that die, as [Animal Health and Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md)
  explains.

## Traps

- **Buying first and asking the town later.** Ask first.
- **No vet lined up.** Find one before the animals arrive.
- **One animal on its own.** Herd animals need company.
- **A bucket for water.** It gets knocked over. Use a fixed trough, and
  check it twice a day.
- **Wrong-species feed or minerals.** Monensin kills horses; copper
  poisons sheep; meat has no place in ruminant feed.
- **Hungry animals onto lush clover, or onto unfamiliar ground.** Fill
  them with hay first.
- **Overgrazing.** Bare pasture means poor animals and wormy ones.
- **Damp hay stacked in the barn.** It can set the barn on fire weeks
  later.
- **Going in after someone who has collapsed in a pit or tank.** Most
  people who die in confined spaces were trying to rescue someone.
- **Standing behind an animal, or reaching through a fence.** That is where
  the kicks and crushes happen.
- **A disaster plan that has never been practised.**

## How the game models it

The game keeps a few animals at the home, in the simplest way that still
lets you collect from them.

- **The home's animals.** Three hens wander by the grain field, two goats
  by the tuber field and two sheep by the legume field
  (`data/entities/livestock.ron`). Look at one up close and the prompt
  reads, for example, "[E] collect Milk (Goat)" when it has something,
  or how long until the next. Press E to take it; collecting earns
  farming experience.
- **What they give.** A goat gives a litre of milk, then needs 400 seconds
  on the game clock before the next; a sheep gives 2 wool every 600
  seconds; a hen gives an egg every 300 seconds (`renewable_product` in
  `data/creatures.csv`; the litre is in `data/items.csv`). At the default
  Realistic time speed a game second is a real second, so a goat gives a
  litre every 6 minutes 40 seconds, far faster than any real animal. Each
  animal holds one yield until you collect it. Your pack takes as much of
  it as fits and the rest stays on the animal, which starts its next
  yield only once you have taken it all.
- **The milk.** Milk spoils after 168 game hours (7 days) at room
  temperature, and twenty times as slowly in the home's Freezer. Because
  the goats' milk is not pasteurised, drinking it carries a 5 percent
  chance of food poisoning; spoiled milk always poisons you
  (`data/food_system.ron`). Cooked dishes made with it, such as
  porridge, carry no such risk, and the game treats its cheese and butter
  as safe too. Real cheese and butter made from raw milk can carry the
  raw milk's germs, as [Animal Health and
  Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md) explains.
- **The wool** is unprocessed fleece; no recipe uses it yet.
- **Animals can be killed.** Swinging at one (F by default, with bare hands
  or whatever is in your hands) does damage, and an animal you kill puts
  what its loot table lists into your pack: meat, and by chance hide,
  bone, wool or feathers. The herd is placed again from the game data
  each time you enter the world: an animal you killed comes back with
  its yield ready, and the others keep the time left on theirs
  (`src/engine/world_load.rs`).

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, materials are
  free while the Creative mode switch on the Inventory page is on, which
  it is unless you turn it off, so cooking needs no milk. Collecting,
  spoiling and the risk of raw milk work the same in every mode.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches. What
  you collected is gone at the next launch, and every animal starts it
  with its milk, wool or egg ready.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: its
animals need no water, no feed, no salt, no shelter, no fence and no
company; they never fall ill, get worms, bloat, breed or age, and they
die only when something attacks them; they make no manure; they never
kick; and hay never heats. Real animals are the other way round, and
nearly all of this guide is the work the game leaves out.

## You own this when

- You checked your local rules and found a farm-animal vet before buying.
- You keep at least two of every herd animal.
- You know how much water and forage your animals need, planned for the
  top of the range, and check water morning and evening.
- You change feed slowly, keep grain locked away, and feed each species
  only its own feed and minerals.
- You never feed meat, pet food or pig and poultry feed to ruminants, and
  you know your state's rule on scraps for pigs.
- You know your local poisonous plants and never turn hungry animals onto
  unfamiliar or lush legume pasture.
- You rest pastures for as long as your climate needs, never graze them
  bare, and plan worm control with your vet.
- You shut animals in at night, keep their shelter dry and airy, and keep
  feed off the ground.
- You buy dry hay, smell and probe a stack that might be heating, keep
  checking it once it passes 130 F, and call the fire department at
  175 F or any smoke.
- You can build and run an electric fence the way the NRCS specification
  describes.
- You handle animals calmly, never stand behind them, keep your arms out
  of fences, and keep an escape route.
- You keep official identification and records for your sheep and goats,
  and quarantine new animals for 30 days.
- You keep manure away from your well and never enter a manure pit or
  tank, not even to rescue someone.
- You have a practised disaster plan for every animal.
