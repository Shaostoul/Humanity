# The Germs That Matter

"Germs" is a useful word and a limiting one. It lumps together a virus
that hand sanitiser does not work well against, a bacterium that a
proper cook kills, a parasite that shrugs off the chlorine in tap water,
and a worm whose eggs lie in garden soil until they can infect someone.
Each of those is stopped by a different habit. Knowing which is which is what
lets you work out what to do in a situation no guide anticipated.

This guide names the short list of germs that sanitation exists to stop:
most of them live in the gut and leave the body in faeces, and the rest
arrive with food or water. For each one it says where it comes from, how
it reaches the next person, and what stops it. Then it takes the routes
one at a time, hands, food, water, waste, surfaces, animals and the
beach, with the habit that closes each. Last comes what to do when one
of these germs gets into the house, and when it is time for a doctor.
The worked example is a winter household in Silverdale, Kitsap County,
Washington, where the simulation is set.

It is not a medical text and it does not diagnose anyone. It leans on
other guides and does not repeat them. [Handwashing and
Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) covers the hands, and why sanitiser
is not soap. [Toilets and Human Waste](toilets_and_human_waste.md)
covers keeping waste contained when the plumbing fails. [Making Water
Safe to Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md) and [Testing
Water](testing_water.md) cover water. [Telling Spoiled From
Safe](telling_spoiled_from_safe.md) explains why food can look and smell
fine and still make you ill, and [Why Canning Has
Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md) covers botulism. [Rats, Flies,
Mosquitoes and Ticks](rats_flies_mosquitoes_and_ticks.md) covers the
animals that carry germs about, and [Chickens and
Eggs](chickens_and_eggs.md) the germs a backyard flock carries. [How
Your Body Works](how_your_body_works.md) has the signs of dehydration,
[When Food or Water Makes You
Sick](when_food_or_water_makes_you_sick.md) covers looking after
someone with a stomach bug, the fluids, the medicines to avoid and the
danger signs, and [Preventing and Spotting
Infection](preventing_and_spotting_infection.md) covers infections of
wounds and the signs that an illness is getting worse.

Its main source is the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC),
with the National Institutes of Health's digestive diseases institute
(NIDDK) on when diarrhoea needs a doctor, both US federal and in the
public domain. Restated in our own words, it also draws on the World
Health Organization (WHO), the Washington State Department of Health
and the Kitsap Public Health District. Where something is general
practice or our own reading, the text says so.

## The short version

1. **Most of these germs travel from a gut, human or animal, to a
   mouth,** by way of hands, food, water or surfaces. Break the route and
   the germ goes nowhere.
2. **Wash hands with soap and water at the key moments.** Sanitiser does
   not work well on several of the germs below.
3. **Clean, separate, cook and chill** your food, the CDC's four steps.
4. **Treat any water that sewage or animal waste may have reached.**
   Boiling kills the bacteria, viruses and parasites that travel in it.
5. **Keep human waste contained,** in a toilet or a lidded bucket, never
   on the ground or in water.
6. **Someone sick with vomiting or diarrhoea does not cook for anyone
   else** until at least 2 days after the symptoms stop.
7. **Clean up vomit and diarrhoea at once,** with gloves and a bleach
   solution, and wash the laundry hot.
8. **Around Puget Sound, cook shellfish,** and check for closures before
   you dig and for advisories before you swim.

## How these germs travel

The CDC's description of how norovirus spreads is the pattern for most
of this guide: "You can get norovirus by accidentally getting tiny
particles of feces (poop) or vomit in your mouth from a person infected
with norovirus." Faeces in, by mouth. The route in between is the part
you can do something about.

The WHO's 2018 guidelines on sanitation map that route as a chain (all
restated). Germs leave a person in faeces and urine. If the toilet is
missing or unsafe, or the waste leaks while it is stored, carried or
treated, the germs reach fields and crops, rivers, drains and
groundwater, and the household itself, where flies and animals move
them onto food, fingers and surfaces. From there they reach a new person
through drinking water, food, hands, feet and skin, and objects, floors
and surfaces. The WHO notes that the older and still widely used
F-diagram (a teaching diagram of the same routes), adapted from Wagner
and Lanoix's work of 1958, shows several of the same steps. Either way
the lesson is the same, and it gives two kinds of defence (our
reading):

- **Keep the waste contained in the first place,** so the germs never
  start out. That is the whole job of a toilet, and of [Toilets and
  Human Waste](toilets_and_human_waste.md).
- **Break each route that is left:** wash the hands, cook the food,
  treat the water, clean the surfaces, and keep the flies off.

Two numbers explain why the second line of defence has to be good. The
CDC says a single gram of human faeces, about the weight of a paper clip,
can contain a trillion germs, and of norovirus: "It only takes a few
norovirus particles to make you and other people sick." A smear too
small to see is plenty (our reading).

## Four kinds of germ, and why the difference matters

- **Viruses,** such as norovirus and hepatitis A. Antibiotics do nothing
  to them: the CDC says plainly that antibiotics do not work on viruses.
  Norovirus is hard to kill: the CDC says hand sanitiser does not work
  well against it, and that noroviruses can survive temperatures as high
  as 145 F.
- **Bacteria,** such as Salmonella, Campylobacter, E. coli, Shigella,
  Vibrio and Listeria. Cooking to the right temperature kills them, and
  some of them multiply in food left in the CDC's danger zone, between
  40 F and 140 F. Cooking does not rescue food that was left out too
  long, because some bacteria leave behind poisons that heat does not
  destroy (general practice; [Telling Spoiled From
  Safe](telling_spoiled_from_safe.md) has the detail).
- **Single-celled parasites,** such as Giardia and Cryptosporidium. They
  can survive outside the body for a long time (the CDC says weeks to
  months for Giardia), and Cryptosporidium shrugs off the chlorine that
  kills most other germs in water ([Making Water Safe to
  Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md)).
- **Worms,** such as roundworm, whipworm and pinworm. People swallow
  their eggs, from soil in the case of roundworm and whipworm, and from
  hands and bedding in the case of pinworm.

## The short list

These are the germs this guide picks out from the CDC's pages on
diarrhoea, food poisoning, water and worms, with the one that lives in
Puget Sound itself (our selection, not the CDC's). Symptoms and when they
start are given so that you can recognise a pattern, not so that you can
diagnose it; a doctor does that.

### Norovirus

- **What it is:** a virus. The CDC calls it the leading cause of
  vomiting and diarrhoea, and of foodborne illness, in the United States.
- **How it travels:** in the vomit and faeces of a sick person, even
  before they feel ill, and for 2 weeks or more after they feel better.
  The CDC lists food handled by a sick person, raw or undercooked
  shellfish, water contaminated at its source, such as "when a septic
  tank leaks into a well", tiny drops of vomit landing on food and
  surfaces, and caring for or eating with someone who has it.
- **When it shows:** usually 12 to 48 hours after exposure; most people
  are better in 1 to 3 days.
- **What stops it:** washing hands with soap and water, sick people
  staying out of the kitchen for 2 days after symptoms stop, a strong
  bleach solution on surfaces, hot laundry, and cooking shellfish
  thoroughly (all the CDC, below).

### Hepatitis A

- **What it is:** a virus that attacks the liver.
- **How it travels:** the CDC says it spreads when someone swallows the
  virus, even in very small amounts, usually through close contact with
  a person who has it or through contaminated food or drink. A person can
  spread it for up to 2 weeks before they have any symptoms, and young
  children often have none at all.
- **When it shows:** usually 2 to 7 weeks after exposure, with symptoms
  that can include yellow skin or eyes, dark urine and pale stools.
- **What stops it:** the CDC: "Vaccination is the best way to prevent
  infection." Ask a doctor or pharmacist whether you are protected. If
  someone in the house has hepatitis A, everyone else should call a
  doctor at once: the CDC says a single shot of hepatitis A vaccine
  within 2 weeks of exposure can help prevent illness, and that,
  depending on age and health, a doctor may also recommend a treatment
  called immune globulin. Wash hands after the toilet and nappy changes
  and before food.

### Salmonella

- **What it is:** a bacterium.
- **How it travels:** the CDC's list of common sources is raw or
  undercooked chicken, turkey and other meats; eggs; unpasteurised milk
  and juice; raw fruit and vegetables; and many animals, including
  backyard poultry, reptiles and amphibians, and small mammals.
- **When it shows:** 6 hours to 6 days, with diarrhoea that can be
  bloody, fever, stomach cramps and vomiting.
- **What stops it:** cooking to a safe temperature, keeping raw meat and
  eggs apart from food that will not be cooked, and washing hands after
  handling animals ([Chickens and Eggs](chickens_and_eggs.md)).

### Campylobacter

- **What it is:** a bacterium.
- **How it travels:** the CDC lists raw or undercooked poultry, raw
  milk, contaminated water, and pets, including cats and dogs.
- **When it shows:** 2 to 5 days, with diarrhoea that is often bloody,
  fever and cramps.
- **What stops it:** cooking poultry to 165 F, drinking pasteurised milk
  and treated water, and washing hands after pets.

### E. coli that makes toxins

- **What it is:** a bacterium. The CDC says most kinds of E. coli are
  harmless and live in a healthy gut, but some make people sick. The
  kinds that make Shiga toxin can lead to
  hemolytic uremic syndrome, which can cause kidney failure, and the
  CDC's food poisoning table says around 5 to 10 percent of people
  diagnosed with E. coli develop it. The CDC adds that children under 5,
  adults 65 and older and people with weakened immune systems are at
  increased risk of E. coli infection.
- **How it travels:** the CDC lists raw or undercooked ground beef, raw
  milk and juice, raw vegetables such as lettuce, raw sprouts, and
  contaminated water.
- **When it shows:** 3 to 4 days, with severe stomach cramps, diarrhoea
  that is often bloody, and vomiting.
- **Watch for:** the signs of hemolytic uremic syndrome, which the CDC
  calls a medical emergency: little or no peeing, losing the pink colour
  in the cheeks and inside the lower eyelids, unexplained bruising or a
  rash of tiny red spots, blood in the pee, being very tired or
  irritable, and being less alert. Any of them during or after an
  E. coli illness means emergency care now. The CDC's E. coli page also
  sets an earlier line for calling the doctor than its general food
  poisoning page: diarrhoea or vomiting lasting more than 2 days.
- **What stops it:** cooking ground beef to 160 F, choosing pasteurised
  milk and juice, rinsing produce, and treating doubtful water.
  [Testing Water](testing_water.md) explains why E. coli in a well test
  means faeces have reached the water.

### Shigella

- **What it is:** a bacterium.
- **How it travels:** the CDC says Shigella spreads easily, and that
  "swallowing just a small amount of Shigella germs can make you sick."
  It is in faeces, so anything faeces touch can carry it: other people,
  water, food, surfaces and objects. Children under 5 get it most, and
  many outbreaks are in childcare settings and schools.
- **When it shows:** usually 1 to 2 days, with diarrhoea that can be
  bloody or last more than 3 days, fever and stomach pain.
- **What stops it:** handwashing at the key times, care when changing
  nappies, and not swallowing water while swimming.

### Vibrio

- **What it is:** a bacterium that lives naturally in coastal salt and
  brackish water. The Washington State Department of Health names Vibrio
  parahaemolyticus as the kind found most often in the northwest, and the
  Kitsap Public Health District says pollution does not cause it, and
  that it is most plentiful in summer, when the water warms (both
  restated, read 5 October 2026).
- **How it travels:** the CDC says most people get it by eating raw or
  undercooked shellfish, especially oysters, and some through an open
  wound that meets coastal water, raw seafood, or its drippings or
  juices. The Washington State Department of Health adds that even fully
  cooked shellfish can be contaminated again if it is rinsed in seawater
  (restated).
- **What stops it:** cooking shellfish (below), keeping shellfish cold
  from the moment it is harvested, and keeping open wounds out of
  seawater ([Preventing and Spotting
  Infection](preventing_and_spotting_infection.md)). The CDC: "Do not eat
  raw or undercooked seafood."

### Listeria

- **What it is:** a bacterium. The CDC says infection is rare but
  serious, and especially harmful for pregnant women, newborns, adults 65
  or older and people with weakened immune systems.
- **How it travels:** the CDC's list of common sources is queso fresco
  and other soft cheeses, raw sprouts, melons, hot dogs, pates, deli
  meats, smoked fish and raw milk.
- **When it shows:** about 2 weeks. In pregnancy it can cause
  miscarriage, stillbirth, premature delivery or a life-threatening
  infection of the newborn, and the CDC's advice to a pregnant woman is
  to call the doctor right away if she has a fever and feels more tired
  and achy than usual.
- **What stops it:** for people at risk, knowing those foods and choosing
  safer options, as the CDC puts it; for everyone, the four steps below.

### Giardia

- **What it is:** a single-celled parasite. The CDC says more people in
  the United States get sick from Giardia than from any other gut
  parasite, and that it can survive for weeks to months outside the
  body, in soil for example.
- **How it travels:** in faeces, from people and animals: person to
  person, especially in childcare, and through untreated water from
  springs, lakes, rivers or shallow wells, swallowed swimming water,
  food, surfaces and animals.
- **When it shows:** usually 1 to 2 weeks after infection, lasting 2 to
  6 weeks.
- **What stops it:** boiling or filtering water from lakes, springs and
  rivers, handwashing, and not swallowing swimming water.

### Cryptosporidium ("Crypto")

- **What it is:** a single-celled parasite, which the CDC calls the
  leading cause of waterborne disease in the United States.
- **How it travels:** in the faeces of people and animals, through
  water, food, soil and surfaces.
- **When it shows:** 2 to 10 days after infection, usually as watery
  diarrhoea lasting 1 to 2 weeks. The CDC says people typically stop
  shedding it in their faeces within 2 weeks after the symptoms
  completely stop.
- **What stops it:** boiling, or a filter certified for it (the CDC
  names labels reading NSF 53 or NSF 58, or an absolute pore size of 1
  micron or smaller), and washing hands with soap and water: the CDC says
  alcohol-based sanitisers are not effective against it. Chlorine does
  not reliably kill it, and on surfaces the CDC says no disinfectant is
  guaranteed to work, though hydrogen peroxide is more effective than
  standard bleach solutions (method below, under Surfaces and laundry).

### Roundworm and whipworm

- **What they are:** worms that live in the human gut.
- **How they travel:** an infected person passes the eggs in their
  faeces, and the CDC says the eggs reach the soil when people go to the
  toilet outside, use unimproved toilets, or use infected human faeces as
  fertiliser. The eggs become infective as they mature in the soil, and
  people swallow them on unwashed fruit and vegetables, in drinking
  water, or from their hands. The CDC says these worms are found where
  the climate is warm and moist and sanitation is poor, and may occur in
  temperate climates in the warmer months.
- **What stops them:** toilets, no human waste on food gardens ([Toilets
  and Human Waste](toilets_and_human_waste.md) has Washington's rule),
  handwashing before food, and, in the CDC's words, washing, peeling and
  cooking raw fruit and vegetables.

### Pinworm

- **What it is:** a small worm, which the CDC calls the most common worm
  infection in the United States.
- **How it travels:** the eggs are laid at night on the skin around the
  anus, get under fingernails and onto bedding, clothes and food, and
  reach the next person's mouth. The CDC says the eggs can survive 2 to 3
  weeks on objects that are not cleaned, and that it often affects more
  than one person in a household.
- **What stops it:** washing hands with soap and warm water after the
  toilet and nappy changes and before food, short nails ([Handwashing
  and Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md)), and, once someone has it,
  treatment: the CDC says to talk with a healthcare provider, and that
  everyone in the household and the people who care for them are treated
  at the same time, with a second dose two weeks after the first,
  because the medicine kills the worms but not the eggs.

## The routes, and the habits that close them

### Hands

[Handwashing and Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) is the whole
subject; this is the germ's-eye summary. Soap and water lift germs off
the skin, and the rinse carries them away, which is why soap works on
germs that sanitiser does not kill. The CDC says hand sanitiser does not
work well against norovirus, and that alcohol-based sanitisers are not
effective against Crypto. So in a house with a vomiting or diarrhoea
illness, it is soap and water, every time, for everyone (our reading of
those two warnings).

### Food

The CDC's four steps to food safety are clean, separate, cook and chill.

- **Clean.** Wash hands before, during and after preparing food, and
  before eating. Wash utensils, cutting boards and counters with hot,
  soapy water after each food. Rinse fresh fruit and vegetables under
  running water.
- **Separate.** Keep raw meat, poultry, seafood and eggs, and their
  juices, away from food that will not be cooked: in the shopping, in
  the refrigerator, and on a separate cutting board. Do not wash raw
  chicken; the CDC says washing it can spread germs to other food, the
  sink and the counter.
- **Cook.** The only way to tell that food is cooked safely is a food
  thermometer. The CDC's safe internal temperatures: 165 F for all
  poultry, and for leftovers and casseroles; 160 F for ground meats; 145
  F for whole cuts of beef, pork, veal and lamb, then a 3-minute rest;
  and 145 F for fish with fins, or until the flesh is opaque and
  separates easily with a fork.
- **Chill.** Bacteria multiply fast between 40 F and 140 F. Refrigerate
  perishable food within 2 hours, or 1 hour above 90 F, keep the
  refrigerator at 40 F or below, and never thaw food on the counter.

Two more food rules come from the germs above. **Someone with vomiting
or diarrhoea does not prepare food for anyone else,** and the CDC says
to wait at least 2 days (48 hours) after the symptoms stop, because a
person still sheds norovirus after they feel better. And **cooked food
can be contaminated again**, by hands, by a raw-meat board or by a
rinse in seawater (our reading of the CDC and the Department of Health
above).

The CDC's list of riskier foods: raw or undercooked poultry, meat,
seafood or eggs; raw or undercooked sprouts; unwashed fruit and
vegetables; cut melon; unpasteurised milk, juice, cider or soft cheese;
and raw dough or batter made with uncooked flour. Botulism, which the
CDC links to improperly canned or fermented foods, is covered in [Why
Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md).

### Water

Norovirus, Shigella, E. coli, Giardia and Crypto all travel in water
that faeces have reached. [Making Water Safe to
Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md) covers treating it: boiling works
on the bacteria, viruses and parasites that travel in water, while
bleach does not reliably kill Crypto and is less reliable against
Giardia.
[Wells and Groundwater](wells_and_groundwater.md) covers keeping a well
away from the septic system that could reach it, and [Testing
Water](testing_water.md) what a coliform or E. coli result means.

Swimming water counts too. The CDC says diarrhoea is the most common
cause of swimming-related outbreaks, that swallowing even a small amount
of water with diarrhoea germs in it can make you sick, and that Crypto
can survive for more than 7 days in properly chlorinated water. Its
rules: "Stay out if you are sick with diarrhea." If you have been
diagnosed with Crypto, stay out until two weeks after the diarrhoea has
completely stopped. Do not swallow the water, take children on toilet
breaks every hour, check nappies, and change them away from the water.
At the sea, lakes and rivers, look for swim warnings and closures, and
stay out where you see pipes draining into the water.

### Waste

Every faecal germ above leaves a body by way of a toilet, a nappy or the
ground. [Toilets and Human Waste](toilets_and_human_waste.md) covers keeping it
contained when the plumbing fails, and why human waste does not go on a
food garden. Nappies are faeces too: change them away from where food is
prepared, and wash your hands afterwards (general practice; the CDC's
handwashing moments include after changing nappies).

### Surfaces and laundry

After someone vomits or has diarrhoea, the CDC's instructions are:

1. Put on rubber or disposable gloves.
2. Wipe up the whole area with paper towels and put them in a plastic
   rubbish bag.
3. Disinfect: either a chlorine bleach solution of 5 to 25 tablespoons
   of household bleach (5 to 8 percent) per gallon of water, left on for
   at least 5 minutes, or a product registered with the EPA against
   norovirus, used as its label directs.
4. Clean the whole area again with soap and hot water.
5. Wash the laundry, take out the rubbish, and wash your hands.

While you use bleach, open a window and keep it off your skin and out of
your eyes (our reading of the CDC's advice on cleaning with bleach,
which calls for fresh air, gloves and goggles). Mix bleach with water
and nothing else; the CDC's warning: "Never mix bleach with ammonia or
other cleaners." The Washington State Department of Health explains
why: bleach with ammonia, which is in urine, gives off toxic chloramine
gas, and bleach with an acid, such as a toilet-bowl cleaner, gives off
chlorine gas (restated).

For the laundry, wearing rubber or disposable gloves: take off clothes
and linens with vomit or faeces on them at once, handle them without
shaking, wash them with detergent and hot water on the longest cycle,
machine-dry them on the highest heat, and then wash your hands (the
CDC).

Crypto is the exception to the bleach rule: the CDC says no disinfectant
is guaranteed to kill it, and hydrogen peroxide works better than
standard bleach solutions. Its method, written for childcare centres:
3 percent hydrogen peroxide (check the strength on the label), covering
surfaces and soaking objects for 20 minutes, then rinsing thoroughly.
Never mix hydrogen peroxide with bleach; the CDC warns that "The two
chemicals may react violently." If bleach has already been used, rinse
it off thoroughly with water before the peroxide goes on, the order the
CDC gives for when both are needed.

### Flies, rats and animals

Flies walk on faeces and then on food, and rats and mice leave germs
where they go. [Rats, Flies, Mosquitoes and
Ticks](rats_flies_mosquitoes_and_ticks.md) covers keeping them away and
cleaning up after them. Animals carry gut germs of their own: the CDC
lists backyard poultry, reptiles and amphibians, and small mammals among
the sources of Salmonella, and pets among the sources of Campylobacter.
Wash your hands after touching any animal, its food, its bedding or its
droppings ([Chickens and Eggs](chickens_and_eggs.md), [Animal Health
and Disease](animal_health_and_disease.md)).

### Shellfish and the beach

Puget Sound adds a route an inland home does not have: shellfish, which
the Kitsap Public Health District says absorb what is in the water,
bacteria and viruses included, which can build up in them (restated).

- **Two seasons, two germs.** The Kitsap Public Health District says
  Vibrio is most plentiful in summer, when the water is warm, and that
  norovirus from shellfish is mostly a problem of the colder months,
  November to March (restated, page read 5 October 2026).
- **Cook them through.** The Washington State Department of Health and
  the Kitsap Public Health District both say that Vibrio is destroyed
  once the inside of the shellfish has reached 145 F for 15 seconds, that
  shellfish are not fully cooked just because their shells have opened,
  and the district says to check with a thin-probed thermometer
  (restated). The CDC adds that norovirus can survive temperatures as
  high as 145 F, and its norovirus advice is: "Avoid steaming and instead
  cook oysters and other shellfish well."
- **How long.** The CDC's methods, from its Vibrio advice. For shellfish
  in the shell: "Discard any shellfish with open shells before cooking."
  Then boil them until the shells open, and keep boiling for another 3 to
  5 minutes. It also gives steaming for another 4 to 9 minutes once the
  steamer is steaming, but with norovirus in mind, boiling is the better
  choice, above all from November to March (our reading of its two
  pages). "Only eat shellfish that open during cooking. Throw out any
  that don't open fully during cooking." For shucked oysters: boil for at
  least 3 minutes, fry in oil at 375 F for at least 3 minutes, broil 3
  inches from the heat for 3 minutes, or bake at 450 F for 10 minutes.
- **Keep them cold** from the moment they are harvested (the Department
  of Health, restated).
- **Check before you dig.** The Department of Health asks harvesters,
  just before they set out, to look up any closure or advisory, whether
  for Vibrio, biotoxins or pollution, on its Shellfish Safety Map or its
  biotoxin hotline, 1-800-562-5632 (restated). Biotoxins are a
  separate danger that cooking does not destroy; [Where Water
  Goes](where_water_goes.md) explains the closures, and just under two
  thirds of Dyes Inlet is classified Prohibited for shellfish harvest
  (classification as of 4 September 2026).
- **Check before you swim.** The Kitsap Public Health District samples
  streams and swimming beaches for faecal bacteria and posts swimming
  beach and sewage spill advisories, which you can subscribe to by email
  or text. In 2026 its list included a sewage spill advisory for part of
  Liberty Bay and Keyport, from 22 to 25 June (restated, page read 5
  October 2026).
- **Some people should not eat them raw at all.** The Kitsap Public
  Health District says raw or undercooked fish and shellfish are never
  advised for people at high risk: infants, young children, older
  adults, anyone pregnant and anyone with a weakened immune system
  (restated). The CDC says its Vibrio precautions matter especially for
  people with an underlying medical condition, above all liver disease.

## When someone in the house is sick

When vomiting or diarrhoea starts in a household, norovirus, which the
CDC calls the leading cause of both, is the likeliest germ, and the
CDC's advice for it is the safe default for any stomach bug (our
reading):

- **The sick person stays home** and does not prepare food or care for
  others until at least 2 days after the symptoms stop. Remember that
  they can still spread norovirus for 2 weeks or more after they feel
  better, so their handwashing stays strict (the CDC).
- **Give them their own toilet, or their own bucket, if you can,** and
  their own towel (general practice).
- **Clean up at once,** as above, and wash their laundry hot.
- **Everyone washes with soap and water,** especially after the toilet,
  after cleaning up and before eating.
- **Drink.** The CDC says to drink plenty of liquids to replace what
  vomiting and diarrhoea take out, and that oral rehydration fluids from
  the pharmacy are most helpful for mild dehydration; sports drinks and
  other drinks without caffeine or alcohol can help with mild
  dehydration but may not replace important minerals. [When Food or
  Water Makes You Sick](when_food_or_water_makes_you_sick.md) has what
  to give and how, for babies and for older adults too.
- **No antibiotics for it.** The CDC says antibiotics will not help a
  norovirus infection, because they fight bacteria, not viruses, and
  there is no specific medicine for it.
- **Ask before any anti-diarrhoea medicine.** The CDC says to talk to a
  healthcare provider first, and: "Do not use anti-diarrheal medication
  with high fever or bloody diarrhea." It also says not to give a child
  under 12 one containing bismuth subsalicylate, and that with the
  E. coli that makes Shiga toxin, anti-diarrhoea medicine and
  antibiotics both raise the chance of hemolytic uremic syndrome. NIDDK
  says doctors usually advise against these medicines for babies and
  children.
- **Watch for dehydration,** above all in young children and older
  people. The CDC's signs are urinating less, a dry mouth and throat, and
  feeling dizzy when standing up, and in children, crying with few or no
  tears and being unusually sleepy or fussy. [How Your Body
  Works](how_your_body_works.md) has the full list and when it becomes
  urgent.

**See a doctor,** the CDC says, for any severe symptom, including:

- diarrhoea with blood in it;
- diarrhoea lasting more than 3 days;
- a fever over 102 F;
- vomiting so often that liquids will not stay down;
- signs of dehydration.

**For an adult, the day count is shorter elsewhere.** That list is the
CDC's general one for food poisoning. NIDDK, and the CDC's own page on
E. coli, say to call a doctor about an adult whose diarrhoea lasts more
than 2 days, and the E. coli page counts vomiting that lasts that long
too. Act on the 2 days, as [When Food or Water Makes You
Sick](when_food_or_water_makes_you_sick.md) does.

**For a baby or a child the line comes much sooner.** NIDDK says to
talk with a doctor right away if a baby or child has diarrhoea lasting
more than a day, frequent loose stools, any fever in a baby or a high
fever in an older child, or, in a baby, refuses all food and drink for
longer than a few hours. It says to get a doctor's help right away for
any child with diarrhoea who is younger than 12 months, was born early
or has other medical problems, or cannot drink enough to keep from
drying out. Its reason: "Diarrhea is especially dangerous in newborns
and infants and can lead to severe dehydration in just a day or two. A
child with symptoms of dehydration can die within a day if left
untreated." A baby under 3 months with any fever of 100.4 F (38 C) or
more needs the doctor straight away ([Preventing and Spotting
Infection](preventing_and_spotting_infection.md)).

And see a doctor if you are pregnant and have a fever with other
flu-like symptoms (the CDC). The Department of Health asks you to
contact a health care provider about an illness after eating shellfish
if the illness is severe or the diarrhoea goes on for more than 3 days,
and to report shellfish-related illness on its foodborne illness
complaint form (restated). After shellfish too, the 2-day line for an
adult and the 1-day line for a child still apply (our reading).

Some of the germs above show themselves in other ways, and these need a
doctor too:

- **Yellow skin or eyes, dark urine or pale stools** can be hepatitis A,
  and the CDC says a doctor can give you a blood test if you think you
  have been exposed.
- **Fever with muscle aches and tiredness, a headache, a stiff neck,
  confusion, loss of balance or seizures** are the CDC's signs of
  Listeria that has spread beyond the gut, for the people at risk from
  it. A stiff neck, confusion or a seizure is an emergency (our reading).

**Who gets hit hardest.** The CDC says children under 5, older adults
and people with weakened immune systems are more likely to develop
severe norovirus infections, and names pregnant women among those at
special risk from Listeria. For these people, call the doctor sooner
rather than later (our reading).

## Worked example: oysters in January

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on.

A family in Silverdale has friends over in January. Someone brings
oysters, and four people eat them raw. Late the next evening two of them
are vomiting.

1. **What it probably is.** Raw oysters, in the cold months, then
   vomiting within 12 to 48 hours: that is the norovirus pattern the CDC
   and the Kitsap Public Health District describe. Nobody needs to be
   certain to do the right things, because the steps for norovirus are
   the safe steps for any stomach bug (our reading).
2. **The first night.** One adult who is well does the cleaning, in
   gloves, with the window open: paper towels first, then a bleach
   solution mixed at 1/3 cup of household bleach to a gallon of water
   (about 5 tablespoons, the low end of the CDC's range for norovirus),
   left on for at least 5 minutes, then soap and hot water. The sheets go
   in the washer on hot, without being shaken. The sick pair get the
   downstairs bathroom to themselves.
3. **Fluids.** They sip water and an oral rehydration drink from the
   pharmacy, a little at a time. By the second day the vomiting has
   stopped.
4. **The kitchen.** The person who usually cooks is one of the sick
   ones. They stay out of the kitchen until at least two full days after
   their last symptom, and keep washing with soap and water for two weeks
   after that.
5. **Watching.** On the third day the host's daughter-in-law, who is
   pregnant and ate no oysters, starts vomiting too and has a fever:
   norovirus spreads from person to person as well as in food. She rings
   her doctor that morning rather than waiting.
6. **Next time.** Oysters at that house are now cooked: boiled until the
   shells open and then for another 3 to 5 minutes, any that stay shut
   thrown out, and never eaten raw by anyone pregnant, very young,
   elderly or with a weakened immune system (the CDC and the Kitsap
   Public Health District, restated).

## Know where your own effort stops

- **The see-a-doctor lists above:** blood in the diarrhoea, diarrhoea
  for more than 2 days in an adult, a fever over 102 F, vomiting that
  keeps liquids from staying down, signs of dehydration, and fever in
  pregnancy; for a baby or child, diarrhoea lasting more than a day, or
  any of NIDDK's other signs above; and for a baby under 12 months with
  diarrhoea, a doctor right away.
- **During or after an E. coli illness, the signs of hemolytic uremic
  syndrome** (little or no peeing, losing the pink colour in the cheeks
  and inside the lower eyelids, unexplained bruising or tiny red spots,
  blood in the pee, unusual tiredness or irritability, being less
  alert): emergency care now.
- **Dehydration that comes with confusion, fainting, no urine, or a
  racing heart or breathing:** medical help right away ([How Your Body
  Works](how_your_body_works.md)).
- **Difficulty swallowing, muscle weakness, double or blurred vision,
  drooping eyelids, slurred speech or difficulty moving the eyes,
  starting in the head and moving down, after home-canned or fermented
  food:** that is the CDC's description of botulism, and an emergency
  ([Why Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md)).
- **Tingling lips and tongue that spread to the hands and feet, within
  minutes or hours of eating shellfish:** the Kitsap Public Health
  District gives these as the first signs of paralytic shellfish
  poisoning, from the biotoxins cooking does not destroy, which can go on
  to losing control of the arms and legs, trouble breathing and death.
  The Washington State Department of Health says death has come in under
  30 minutes; for mild symptoms it says to call a health care provider
  and the local health department, and for severe ones to call 911 or be
  taken to an emergency room at once (both restated). Because it can
  kill that fast, treat it as severe once the tingling spreads beyond
  the mouth, or with any weakness or loss of control in the arms or legs
  or any difficulty breathing: call 911 (our reading).
- **Yellow skin or eyes, or the Listeria signs above in someone at
  risk:** a doctor, and for a stiff neck, confusion or a seizure,
  emergency care.
- **Pinworm, Giardia, Crypto or a roundworm that will not go away:**
  diagnosis and treatment are a doctor's.
- **A well that faeces may have reached:** a laboratory test, not a
  sniff ([Testing Water](testing_water.md)).
- **Illness after shellfish, or several households ill after the same
  meal:** tell the doctor where the food came from, and report it to the
  health department, so that others can be warned (our reading of the
  Department of Health's request).

Yours to do: the handwashing, the four steps, keeping the sick out of
the kitchen, the cleaning, the fluids, and watching.

## Traps

- **"Hand sanitiser is enough."** Not against norovirus or Crypto. Soap
  and water.
- **"I feel better, so I can cook tonight."** Wait at least 2 days after
  the symptoms stop, and keep washing for 2 weeks.
- **"It's cooked, so it's safe."** Not if it went back on the
  raw-chicken board, or was handled by someone who is ill.
- **"The shells opened, so they're done."** Not to the 145 F that
  destroys Vibrio, and norovirus can survive that.
- **"Raw oysters are a summer risk."** Vibrio is. Norovirus in shellfish
  is a winter one.
- **"The water looks clear."** Giardia and Crypto are invisible, and
  chlorine does not reliably kill Crypto.
- **"Rinse the chicken first."** The CDC says raw chicken does not need
  washing, and that washing it can spread germs to other food, the sink
  and the counter.
- **"An antibiotic will clear it up."** Not a virus, and not without a
  doctor deciding.
- **"Our own hens are clean."** Backyard poultry is on the CDC's list
  for Salmonella.
- **"It's only a smear."** A trillion germs can fit in a gram.

## How the game models it

The game has one illness you can catch, and no germs.

- **Food Poisoning, from food alone.** Spoiled food always gives you
  Food Poisoning, whether it was raw, cooked or preserved. Fresh food eaten
  raw can too, by chance: in the game's food data, raw meat 40 percent,
  raw fish 25, eggs 15 and fresh milk 5, and 1 to 5 percent for other raw
  foods such as grains and flour, vegetables, fruit, herbs, nuts and fruit
  juice. The two highest, dried beans and lentils at 50 percent and
  cassava at 30, stand in the data for natural poisons in the plants that
  soaking and cooking deal with, not for germs. The data file calls all of
  these game-scale judgements, not measured probabilities. Fresh cooked
  and preserved food carries no chance. Food Poisoning takes water, not
  health: 1.5 litres a day from your Hydration, on top of the usual 2.5,
  for two days of game time in the Realistic Illness mode, or half as
  much for one day in Forgiving, the default, and then it passes. Drinking
  keeps you from harm, oral rehydration solution best, and no medicine
  ends it ([When Food or Water Makes You
  Sick](when_food_or_water_makes_you_sick.md) has the detail;
  `data/food_system.ron`, `data/food/item_profiles.ron`,
  `src/systems/food.rs`, `data/status_effects.csv`,
  `data/medical/illnesses.ron`).
- **All water is safe.** Every water you can drink in the game is safe.
  The abilities list includes Purify Water, but it is shown locked, with
  a note that it does nothing yet: so far only abilities that heal you or
  hurt a creature you face do anything (`data/food_system.ron`,
  `data/abilities.csv`, `src/lib.rs`).
- **Diseases are listed, not caught.** The game's effects data has rows
  for the common cold, flu, infection, parasites and plague, but nothing
  in the game gives them to you (`data/status_effects.csv`).
- **No handwashing.** The Soap Bar item has no Use button, since only
  the medical items have one, and nothing in the game is spread by hands,
  surfaces, water, flies or other people (`data/items.csv`,
  `src/gui/pages/inventory.rs`, `data/medical/treatments.ron`).

**The default settings.** The play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play
mode; Normal, unless you choose another) does not change any of this.
The conditions on you are saved with your game, and "Start every session
from the default home" is off by default, so Food Poisoning is still
there at the next launch if it had not worn off (`src/save_load.rs`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: that
cooked food can be contaminated again; that a sick cook makes everyone
ill; that the same germ travels by hands, water, food and surfaces; that
one person's illness becomes the household's; that water can carry
illness at all; and Puget Sound's shellfish seasons. In the game, a meal
either poisons you or it does not. In a real house, what happens next
depends on what everyone does with their hands.

## You own this when

- You can name the four kinds of germ, and say why antibiotics, cooking,
  chlorine and sanitiser each work on some of them and not others.
- You can say where norovirus, Salmonella, E. coli, Shigella, Vibrio,
  Giardia, Crypto and roundworm come from, and how each reaches people.
- You wash with soap and water, not sanitiser, when someone in the house
  has vomiting or diarrhoea.
- You follow clean, separate, cook and chill, own a food thermometer,
  and know 165, 160 and 145 F.
- You keep anyone who is ill out of the kitchen until at least 2 days
  after their symptoms stop.
- You can clean up after vomiting or diarrhoea: gloves, an open window,
  paper towels, the CDC's bleach solution for at least 5 minutes, soap
  and hot water, hot laundry, and never bleach mixed with another
  cleaner.
- You know the signs of dehydration and the reasons to see a doctor,
  and that a baby or child needs one much sooner than an adult.
- You know the signs of hemolytic uremic syndrome after an E. coli
  illness, and that they mean emergency care.
- You would ask a healthcare provider before giving anyone an
  anti-diarrhoea medicine, and you would never mix hydrogen peroxide
  with bleach.
- You stay out of the water while you have diarrhoea, and for two weeks
  after it stops if it was Crypto.
- You boil shellfish until the shells open and for 3 to 5 minutes more,
  throw out any that stay shut, keep them cold, and check for closures
  before you dig and advisories before you swim.
