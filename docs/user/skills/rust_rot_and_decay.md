# Rust, Rot and Decay

Everything a home is made of is slowly coming apart. Iron rusts, wood
rots, paint chalks and peels, rope mildews, and boards left in the sun
turn grey and rough. None of it is random. Each kind of decay needs
particular conditions, and take one of them away and it slows or stops.
The Department of Energy's chemistry handbook defines corrosion as "the
deterioration of a material due to interaction with its environment,"
and adds the sentence that sets the tone for this guide: "Even though
corrosion cannot be eliminated, it can be controlled." The Forest
Products Laboratory says the same of wood: "Under proper conditions,
wood will give centuries of service."

The short version of everything below: **keep things dry, and when they
get wet, let them dry out.** Water is the common ingredient in rust, in
rot, in mildew and in most of the rest.

This guide takes its account of rust from the Department of Energy's
*Fundamentals Handbook: Chemistry* (1993), its account of rot, insects
and weathering from the Forest Products Laboratory's *Wood Handbook*
(2021), and its care of tools, rope and chain from the armed services'
hand tool manual (TM 9-243) and the Army's rigging manual (FM 5-125).
The safety rules come from the Occupational Safety and Health
Administration (OSHA), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the National
Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH). All are United
States government publications, in the public domain. Where something is
general practice or our own reading rather than a published rule, the
text says so.

How fast things decay depends on where you live: how wet, how warm and
how salty. The section "Where you live changes the answer" works that
through for Silverdale, Washington, the Library's example town.

Some neighbouring guides go deeper: [Metals and
Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md) on galvanic corrosion, stainless
steel and pitting; [Working With Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) on
moisture in wood and which species resist decay; [Nails, Screws and
Bolts](nails_screws_and_bolts.md) on fasteners in treated wood and near
the sea; [Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md) on regular
maintenance; and [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md) on damp inside a house.

## First: where decay hurts people

- **Rotten wood breaks without warning.** The Forest Products Laboratory
  says decay first takes away wood's toughness, its ability to stand a
  sudden load, and that strength losses come early: "By the time weight
  losses resulting from decay have reached 10%, most strength losses may
  be expected to exceed 50%," at a stage when the decay "is detectable
  only microscopically." Its rule of thumb: "It may be assumed that wood
  with visually discernible decay has been greatly reduced in all
  strength values." So a deck board, stair, railing, ladder rung or post
  with any visible rot is not to be trusted with a person's weight. The
  Army's rigging manual says the same of trees: dead or rotten trees and
  stumps "are likely to snap suddenly when a strain is placed on the
  line," so never tie a load, a swing or a rope to one.
- **Corroded ladders and lifting gear.** OSHA's ladder rule says a
  portable ladder with structural defects, "corroded components" among
  them, must be marked as defective or tagged "Do Not Use" and taken out
  of service until repaired (29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(16)). Its rule for
  slings takes a wire rope sling out of service for "Corrosion of the
  rope or end attachments", and a fibre rope sling for "Discoloration or
  rotting" (29 CFR 1910.184). The ladder rule also says "Wood ladders
  shall not be coated with any opaque covering," except for labels on one
  face (29 CFR 1926.1053(a)(12)); paint can hide the cracks and rot you
  are looking for (our reading of why).
- **Old paint may contain lead.** The EPA says a home built before 1978
  "may have lead-based paint; the older your home, the more likely it has
  lead-based paint," and if you cannot find out the age, "it's safest to
  assume the area you are renovating has lead-based paint." Scraping and
  sanding it makes dust that poisons, children above all. The EPA
  recommends hiring a lead-safe certified contractor for this work in a
  pre-1978 home. The Forest Products Laboratory, writing about stripping
  paint from wood, says not to sand paint that contains lead, that power
  washing and sandblasting are not suitable for it, and that heated pads,
  hot air guns and blowtorches are not suitable either, because at their
  working temperatures they turn lead into fumes, which it says are
  released at about 370 C (700 F). The EPA's own list of tools for
  do-it-yourself lead-safe work includes a low-temperature heat gun
  under 1,100 F; given the laboratory's warning,
  the cautious reading is no heat at all on paint that may contain lead
  (our reading of the two together). The Forest Products Laboratory also
  warns that blowtorches can ignite hidden material behind siding that
  smoulders "for hours before bursting into flame".
- **Treated wood is not firewood.** The Forest Products Laboratory:
  "Treated wood must not be burned in open fires or in stoves,
  fireplaces, or residential boilers, because the smoke and ashes may
  contain toxic chemicals." The EPA says the same for wood treated with
  chromated arsenicals (CCA), which was used in decks and playsets before
  2004, and adds: "Wear goggles and a dust mask when sawing wood treated
  with chromated arsenicals and wash your hands after handling." It
  should not be reused as mulch either.
- **Rust removers are acids.** The common ones are phosphoric acid and
  oxalic acid. NIOSH lists both as causing eye and skin burns; for either
  in the eyes, "Irrigate immediately"; and for oxalic acid swallowed,
  "Medical attention immediately." Wear eye protection and gloves, and
  keep them where children cannot reach (general practice). Never mix
  cleaning chemicals: the CDC warns "Never mix bleach with ammonia or
  other cleaners. This will produce a poisonous gas."
- **A rusty nail.** The danger is the puncture, not the rust. The CDC's
  page on tetanus does not mention rust at all; it says to see a
  healthcare provider about a puncture wound or a deep one, giving
  "stepping on a nail" as its example, and talks of keeping up a 10-year
  tetanus booster. The full rules are in [Bleeding and
  Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md).
- **Mould on damp wood.** Cleaning it safely is in [Ventilation, Damp and
  Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md).

## What rust is

Rust is iron combining with oxygen, with water as the go-between. The
Department of Energy's handbook gives the two conditions for what it
calls general corrosion: "1) metal and water in the same environment,
and 2) a chemical reaction between the metal and water that forms an
oxide." On iron the oxide builds up in layers, the outermost being
ferric oxide, the red-brown rust everyone knows.

The handbook explains why rust keeps going. An oxide layer can protect
the metal under it, slowing further corrosion, but "If the film is
loosely attached, develops defects, or is removed, the metal surface is
again exposed to the environment and corrosion occurs more readily."
Rust on iron flakes and crumbles, so it keeps exposing fresh metal (our
reading). Metals whose oxide film stays tight, like stainless steel and
aluminium, behave very differently, which is the subject of [Metals and
Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md).

What speeds it up, from the same handbook:

- **Oxygen.** "The presence of oxygen in water to which iron is exposed
  increases the corrosion rate."
- **Warmth.** "Like most other chemical reactions, corrosion rates
  increase as temperature increases." Its rule of thumb, written for
  water in plant systems, is that the reaction rate doubles with each
  rise of 20 to 50 degrees Fahrenheit.
- **Salt.** Pure water conducts electricity poorly, and adding "acids,
  bases, or salts that dissociate into ions increases the
  current-carrying capability of the water," which drives corrosion. It
  also says chloride ions, the chloride in salt, "cause pitting of iron
  and steel", and that in some tests "deep pits have been observed within
  a few hours." Sea spray and the salt spread on winter roads are why
  metal near the coast or on a car rusts so fast (our application).
- **Places where water sits and air cannot reach.** In a crevice, or under
  a crust of rust, the water runs short of oxygen, and the metal there
  becomes the part that corrodes. The handbook describes "a pit in the
  metal surface" forming "under the mound of surface oxide," and calls
  pitting and crevice corrosion a major hazard in the plants it was
  written for "because of the rapid penetration of the metal with little
  overall loss of mass." A rust
  blister can hide a hole (our reading). [Metals and
  Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md) lists the crevices in a house.

Two different metals touching in the wet make a battery, and one of them
corrodes faster; that, galvanised zinc protecting steel, and which metal
should be the bigger part, are all in [Metals and
Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md) and [Nails, Screws and
Bolts](nails_screws_and_bolts.md).

## Keeping metal from rusting

Take away the water, or keep it off the metal.

- **Store it dry.** The rigging manual says to store chains "in a dry and
  well-ventilated place," and wire rope where it "is protected from the
  weather and from chemicals and fumes." The hand tool manual says to
  store tools in a dry place.
- **A film of oil on tools.** The hand tool manual's standing instruction:
  "Apply a thin film of oil on tools to prevent rust," and for long-term
  storage, a rust-preventive compound. One exception it makes: "Do not
  oil files," because oil makes a file slide instead of cut.
- **Lubricate moving metal.** Wire rope is cleaned and then lubricated
  with an oil or grease "free of acids and alkalis", says the rigging
  manual, and stored clean and lubricated, so that "corrosion will be
  virtually eliminated." For chains, it says: "Do not paint chains to
  prevent rusting because the paint will interfere with the action of
  the links. Instead, apply a light coat of lubricant."
- **Paint and coatings keep water and oxygen off.** Touch up chips and
  scratches before rust gets under the paint (general practice, from
  the handbook's account of protective films).
- **Let it drain.** Do not leave water standing on metal or trapped in a
  joint; slope surfaces and leave drain holes (general practice; the
  design rules are in [Metals and Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md)).

## Removing rust

- **Light rust on tools.** The hand tool manual: "Remove rust with a fine
  aluminum-oxide abrasive cloth," then oil the tool. The rigging manual
  uses a wire brush on wire rope. Wear eye protection when you
  wire-brush or sand rust; the flakes fly. A wire wheel on a drill or
  grinder also throws broken wires, so wear a face shield over safety
  glasses with one (both general practice).
- **Old paint on metal.** The Forest Products Laboratory notes that
  lead-based paint "is still manufactured for special applications, such
  as paint for metal products, particularly those made of steel." Do not
  sand, grind, burn or blast old paint off railings, machinery or tools
  until you know it is lead free. For a home, the EPA recommends "hiring
  a certified lead inspector or lead risk assessor" to test the surfaces
  you plan to work on, and until then its lead-safe practices above
  apply.
- **A rusted nut.** The hand tool manual: "Apply penetrating oil to
  rusted nuts and/or bolts that resist turning. Allow time for oil to
  penetrate before attempting to turn." Do not reach for a longer
  wrench; [Force, Levers and Mechanical
  Advantage](force_levers_and_mechanical_advantage.md) explains why.
- **Chemical rust removers and converters.** Products based on phosphoric
  or oxalic acid are common. Follow the label, and see the NIOSH warnings
  above. Soaking small rusty parts in vinegar is a common home method
  (general practice). The Forest Products Laboratory notes one use for
  oxalic acid on wood: it removes the dark iron stain where iron has
  reacted with the tannins in wood, but the stain "will return unless the
  source of iron is eliminated."
- **When rust has eaten through.** Anything that carries a load or a
  person, such as a ladder, a chain, a hook, a trailer hitch or a stair
  stringer, is replaced, not painted over (our reading of OSHA's ladder
  and sling rules).

## What rot is

Rot is fungi eating wood. The Forest Products Laboratory: "Molds, most
sapwood stains, and decay are caused by fungi," and "The growth of fungi
depends on suitably mild temperatures, moisture, and air (oxygen)." The
wood itself is the food.

Its numbers for what decay needs, which [Working With
Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) covers in more depth:

- **Wet wood.** "Serious decay occurs only when the moisture content of
  the wood is above the fiber saturation point (average 30%)." That only
  happens when wood is wetted by rain or condensation, or sits on wet
  ground. Humid air alone "will not wet wood sufficiently to support
  significant decay, but it will permit development of some mold
  fungi." Fully air-dried wood is usually at no more than 20 percent,
  "a reasonable margin of safety."
- **The key sentence:** "wood will not decay if it is kept air dry, and
  decay already present from prior infection will not progress."
- **Not too wet.** Wood soaked through has too little air for decay
  fungi, which is why foundation piles below the water table do not rot.
- **Mild temperatures.** Most decay fungi grow best around 25 C (77 F),
  slow down below 10 C (50 F) and above 30 C (95 F), and essentially stop
  at 2 C (35 F) or 38 C (100 F).

What it looks like, from the same chapter:

- **Brown rot** leaves the wood browner, and it "can crack across the
  grain forming cube-like structures, shrink, collapse, and be crushed
  into powder."
- **White rot** bleaches the wood, which keeps its shape for a long time
  but "often feels spongy."
- **Soft rot** is shallow and affects the surface; the heavily fissured
  grey surface of old weathered wood has generally been degraded by it.
- **"Dry rot" is a misnomer.** The laboratory says the term "is
  incorrect because wood must have available moisture for decay." Some
  fungi grow strands that carry water from the soil into wood that
  would otherwise be dry, and are better called water-conducting fungi.
- **Mould and stain** mostly spoil appearance and only slightly affect
  strength, but they make wood soak up water more easily, which helps
  real decay get started.

### Insects that eat or hollow out wood

- **Subterranean termites** cause most termite damage to buildings in the
  United States, and are more common in the southern states. They live
  in the soil and need moisture, so they build "mud or shelter tubes"
  up to the wood; the tubes, and swarms of winged termites or their shed
  wings in spring or autumn, are the signs. Because their galleries are
  hidden inside a shell of sound wood, the laboratory advises "probing
  with a pick or knife" where you suspect them.
- **Carpenter ants** do not eat wood; they nest in it, preferring wood
  softened by decay. The laboratory: "the presence of carpenter ants can
  be indicative of a moisture issue somewhere in the structure." It
  names porch columns, porch roofs, window sills and hollow-core doors as
  their usual places.
- **Carpenter bees** bore half-inch holes into bare or thinly finished
  softwood; a good paint film protects against them.
- **Marine borers** attack wood in salt water. The laboratory says that
  along the Pacific, Gulf and South Atlantic coasts, "untreated pilings
  may be completely destroyed in a year or less."

## Keeping wood from rotting

The Forest Products Laboratory says "Serious decay problems are almost
always a sign of faulty design or construction, lack of reasonable care
in the handling the wood, or improper maintenance of the structure." Its
building rules, all from the same chapter:

- **Build with dry lumber,** and do not enclose wet or infected wood
  until it has dried.
- **Design so wood stays dry, and dries when it gets wet.** A roof
  overhang of about 2 feet (0.6 m), with gutters and downspouts kept free
  of debris; ground graded so water runs away from the building.
- **Keep wood off the soil.** At least 8 inches (200 mm) between soil and
  framing and 6 inches (150 mm) between soil and siding; 12 to 18 inches
  (300 to 460 mm) of foundation above grade where hard rains are
  frequent. Untreated wood should never touch the soil.
- **Crawl spaces:** cover the soil with a moisture barrier such as
  polyethylene, and leave at least 18 inches (460 mm) under joists for
  inspection.
- **Fix plumbing leaks quickly**; the laboratory says leaks "can result
  in serious decay problems within buildings, particularly if they are
  undetected for long periods."
- **Clear away stumps, offcuts and old stakes** near the building; they
  feed decay and termites.
- **Use the right wood where it cannot be kept dry:** pressure-treated
  wood, or the heartwood of a decay-resistant species (see [Working With
  Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md)), for porches, steps and decks;
  pressure-treated wood for anything in contact with the ground.

### Reading treated wood

The laboratory's chapter on preservatives describes a system of **use
categories** that says what a piece of treated wood is made to survive:
UC1 for interior wood kept dry, UC2 for interior wood occasionally damp,
UC3 for exterior wood above ground, UC4 for ground contact outdoors, and
UC5 for seawater and marine borers. Treated wood should carry a tag
showing the inspection agency, the preservative, its retention and the
use category. Buy for the category the job needs: wood rated for above
ground will rot in the ground (our reading of the categories).

Cutting treated wood exposes untreated wood inside. The laboratory says
the cut surfaces, the end grain in joints and the area around fasteners
and drill holes "will require supplemental on-site treatment", and
names copper naphthenate (1 to 2 percent copper) brushed onto the cut.
Treated wood also needs the right fasteners, which are in [Nails, Screws
and Bolts](nails_screws_and_bolts.md).

### Paint, sun and weathering

The laboratory's chapter on finishing: "Weathering is the general term
describing outdoor degradation of materials." Ultraviolet light in
sunlight breaks down lignin, the glue between wood cells, at the surface,
and the loosened fibres slowly wash and wear away: "Approximately 6 mm
(1/4 in.) of wood is lost in a century." That is slow, but the damage to
a surface about to be painted is fast. In one test, cedar boards left
facing south for 16 weeks before painting needed repainting within 3
years, boards left 1 week started peeling after 13 years, and boards not
exposed at all were in good condition after 20. So prime or paint new
exterior wood as soon as you can, or before it goes up.

Seal the end grain too. The laboratory says liquid water enters wood "10
to 100 times faster through the end grain than the other faces," and
calls sealing all end grain "probably the single most valuable
improvement that can be made in standard construction practice" for
wood exposed to water. Its example: upright pieces of cedar heartwood
taken from a porch railing after 25 years, in perfect condition where
the end grain had been primed before assembly and rotten where the
bottom ends had not.

The laboratory adds that mildew "does not decay wood directly" but
punctures the paint film and lets water in.

### Repairing rot

The laboratory's rule for repairs is to fix the water first: "every
effort should be made to correct the moisture condition that led to the
damage." If the moisture cannot be stopped, replace the rotted parts
with treated wood or durable heartwood; if it can be stopped entirely,
ordinary dry lumber will do.

## Rope, fabric and other things that rot

Natural fibre rope rots like wood does, and so do canvas and other
natural fabrics left damp (general knowledge). The Army's rigging manual
on fibre rope:

- Make sure it "is dry and then stored in a cool, dry place. This reduces
  the possibility of mildew and rotting," coiled or hung so air can
  circulate.
- Look inside, not just at the outside: untwist the strands a little.
  "Mildewed rope has a musty odor and the inner fibers of the strands
  have a dark, stained appearance."
- When a rope fails inspection, destroy it or cut it into short pieces
  "to prevent its being used in hoisting."
- Nylon rope, by contrast, "resists abrasion, rot, decay, and fungus
  growth."

Synthetic ropes, tarps, nets and plastic left in the sun weaken and go
brittle over time, so check them before trusting them with a load
(general practice). OSHA takes a synthetic web sling out of service for
"Acid or caustic burns" or "Melting or charring" of any part of it.

## A yearly look round

A walk round the house and yard once a year, and after big storms,
catches most decay early. The list is general practice, built from the
sources above:

- **Probe wood near the ground and where water collects** with a knife or
  an awl: deck posts and the board fixing a deck to the house, stair
  stringers, porch columns, window sills, door frames, fence posts at
  ground level. Sound wood resists the point; rotten wood lets it sink
  in or comes away in cubes or fibres.
- **Look for termite mud tubes** on foundations and piers, and for shed
  wings in spring.
- **Check gutters, downspouts and the grading,** so water leaves the
  building.
- **Look under sinks and around the water heater and toilets** for leaks.
- **Check rust on what carries load:** ladders, chains, hooks, hitches,
  railings, the bolts in a deck. Tag anything doubtful out of use.
- **Look at the paint.** Peeling, cracking and grey bare wood need
  attention before the wood beneath gets wet. In a house built before
  1978, treat old paint as lead paint until tested.

## Where you live changes the answer

Decay is a local problem. The Forest Products Laboratory says the growth
of mould, stain and decay "depend heavily on temperature and moisture
conditions," and publishes a climate index map of decay hazard for the
United States. It singles out humid regions such as the Gulf States,
where poles often "do not air-dry fast enough to avoid losses from
fungi," and calls Mississippi a severe decay hazard climate. Termites
are local too: subterranean termites are commoner in the south, and the
laboratory says dry-wood termites are found only in a narrow strip from
central California round the southern edge of the country to Virginia,
plus Hawaii. Salt matters near any coast. Your state's extension
service can tell you which insects and fungi are active where you live
(general practice).

**Silverdale, Washington.** The town sits at the head of Dyes Inlet on
Puget Sound. The nearest complete weather record, NOAA's 1991 to 2020
climate normals for Bremerton, gives 1,446 mm (57 inches) of precipitation
a year, just under 80 percent of it from October through March, with
January averaging highs of about 8 C and lows of about 2 C. July
averages only 21 mm of rain. Putting that beside the decay figures above
(our reading):

- **Winter keeps wood wet for months.** Outdoor wood that is not under a
  roof or well drained can stay above the 30 percent at which decay
  runs. Winter is cool enough to slow decay but not stop it, and wood
  that is still wet when spring warms up is the wood that rots. Roof
  overhangs, gutters, ground clearance and treated wood earn their keep
  here.
- **Summers are dry.** Wood that is protected from standing water dries
  out in July and August, which is when to paint and to treat cut ends.
- **Salt water is close.** Metal within reach of spray from the inlet
  meets chloride, the ion that pits steel and defeats ordinary stainless.
  [Metals and Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md) works through
  Silverdale's metal in detail. Untreated wood in the water itself faces
  the marine borers the laboratory warns about on the Pacific coast.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Rot or rust in anything that holds people up:** decks, stairs,
  balconies, railings, joists, beams, posts and sill plates. Visible
  decay means greatly reduced strength (Forest Products Laboratory), so
  stop using it and have a builder or engineer look at it (general
  practice).
- **Lead paint in a home built before 1978:** the EPA recommends a
  lead-safe certified contractor.
- **Termite treatment and fumigation.** The laboratory says fumigation
  "is very dangerous and should be conducted only by licensed
  professional fumigators," and points to pest control associations for
  current treatments.
- **Corroded chimneys and flues,** inspected each year by a professional
  (US Fire Administration, in [Keeping Things
  Working](keeping_things_working.md)).
- **Corroded gas lines and fittings, a water heater tank weeping rust,**
  or **corrosion inside an electrical panel:** a licensed plumber, gas
  fitter or electrician (general practice).

## How the game models it

The game models almost none of this yet.

- **Nothing rusts, rots or weathers.** Metal tools do not rust, wood does
  not rot, and the walls, floors and machines of your home never decay.
  A tool loses its uses only by being used, and in the default Dev play
  mode it does not wear at all; the details are in [Keeping Things
  Working](keeping_things_working.md).
- **A few garden things wear away.** A row cover left over a bed wears
  out after 365 garden days, the low end of the 1 to 3 years its
  extension source gives for the lightweight grade. A mulch of sawdust
  lasts 150 garden days and one of bark 1,095, and both take nitrogen
  from the soil as they rot, as the extension sources say woody mulch
  does. The mulch works while garden weeds are switched on (Settings >
  Gameplay > Garden pests, diseases and weeds, set to Gentle by
  default). In the default Dev play mode, laying a cover or a
  mulch takes nothing from your pack.
- **Food spoils.** Food in your pack spoils on a timer set by the kind of
  food, and spoiled food gives a quarter of its nourishment and food
  poisoning. Telling spoiled food from safe in real life is in [Telling
  Spoiled From Safe](telling_spoiled_from_safe.md).
- **The data knows about corrosion, but nothing uses it.** The alloy table
  (`data/chemistry/alloys.csv`) rates each metal's corrosion resistance
  from poor to excellent, and the compounds table
  (`data/chemistry/compounds.csv`) lists iron oxide as common rust,
  phosphoric acid as a rust converter and oxalic acid as a rust remover.
  These ship with the game as reference data, and nothing in the game
  reads them yet. [Metals and Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md)
  explains what the alloy ratings can and cannot tell you.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: in the
game a deck never rots under you, a ladder never rusts through and a
rope never mildews. In a real home, decay is the slow, steady work that
never stops, and keeping ahead of it is a large part of looking after a
place.

## You own this when

- You know that decay needs water, and you keep things dry and let wet
  things dry out.
- You treat any wood with visible rot as having lost most of its strength,
  and you never stand on it, tie to it or hang from it.
- You take rusted ladders, chains, hooks and slings out of use, and you
  never paint a wooden ladder.
- You assume old paint in a pre-1978 home contains lead, and you do not
  sand it, burn it or heat it.
- You never burn treated wood, and you wear goggles and a mask to saw it.
- You handle rust removers with eye protection and gloves, and never mix
  cleaning chemicals.
- You keep a film of oil on your tools, store rope and chain dry, and
  lubricate rather than paint a chain.
- You can explain why salt, warmth and trapped water make rust worse.
- You can tell brown rot from white rot, and know why "dry rot" needs
  water.
- You can read a treated-wood tag and choose the right use category, and
  you treat the cut ends.
- You know the signs of termites and carpenter ants, and what carpenter
  ants say about moisture.
- You walk round your home once a year, probe the wood near the ground,
  and fix the water before you fix the wood.
