# Your First Compost

Composting is letting food scraps and yard waste rot on purpose, in a
controlled spot, until they turn back into dark, crumbly soil. You are not
really doing the work; billions of bacteria and fungi are. Your whole job
is to keep them fed, damp, and supplied with air. Do that even roughly
right and you get free fertilizer while keeping waste out of the trash.

Your first pile may go wrong. It may smell one week, sit cold the next,
or take twice as long as you hoped. That is normal and useful: every
problem has a smell or a look that tells you exactly what to fix, and this
guide covers them all. Nobody composts perfectly the first time.

## What you need

Cheap or free options first:

- A spot of bare ground about 3 feet by 3 feet (roughly one big stride
  each way), or a container. Options, simplest first:
  - An open pile. Free. Works fine. Looks messy, and animals can reach it.
  - A homemade bin: a circle of wire fencing, a ring of pallets, or a
    plastic storage bin with air holes drilled in the sides. Nearly free.
  - A bought compost bin or a tumbler (a barrel on a stand that you spin
    to mix the contents). Costs money, but keeps animals out and mixing
    easy. Good for small yards.
  - No yard at all? A worm bin works indoors: red wiggler worms in a
    plastic tub about 21 by 15 inches, kept at 55 to 75 degrees F, eat
    your scraps and leave behind rich compost. It deserves its own guide;
    search "vermicomposting" plus your state university extension.
- A garden fork, shovel, or sturdy stick for mixing.
- A steady supply of two kinds of material, described next.

## Greens and browns

Everything you compost is one of two kinds:

- Greens are fresh and moist, and rich in nitrogen (the nutrient that
  feeds fast bacteria). Everyday examples: fruit and vegetable scraps,
  coffee grounds, tea bags (paper ones), crushed eggshells, fresh grass
  clippings.
- Browns are dry and dead, and rich in carbon (the energy source).
  Everyday examples: dry autumn leaves, straw, shredded cardboard,
  shredded newspaper, small twigs, untreated wood chips or sawdust.

The practical ratio: about 2 to 3 parts browns for every 1 part greens,
measured by volume, not weight. A bucket of scraps means two to three
buckets of leaves. You do not need to be exact; when in doubt, add more
browns. Almost every compost problem is too many greens, not too few.

## What never goes in

- Meat, fish, and bones: they attract rats and raccoons, and a home pile
  rarely gets hot enough to break them down safely.
- Cheese, dairy, fats, oils, and grease: same reasons.
- Dog or cat waste, and cat litter: pets can carry disease organisms
  that home compost does not reliably kill.
- Diseased plants and weeds that have gone to seed (flowered and formed
  seed heads): a cool pile will not kill the disease or the seeds, and
  you will spread them later.
- Treated or painted wood, and coal or barbecue ash: chemicals you do
  not want in your soil.

## Building and running the pile

1. Pick a spot with decent drainage, ideally on bare soil, out of any
   spot that floods. Shade or sun both work.
2. Start with a layer of bulky browns about 4 to 6 inches deep. Twiggy
   stuff at the very bottom helps air get in.
3. Add your greens. Chopping scraps into 1 to 2 inch pieces speeds
   everything up, because the bacteria work on surfaces.
4. Cover every addition of food scraps with 4 to 8 inches of browns.
   Buried food does not smell and does not attract animals. This one
   habit prevents most beginner problems.
5. Check moisture. Squeeze a handful of the mixed material from inside
   the pile (not the dry cover layer): it should feel like a wrung-out
   sponge, clearly damp but yielding at most a drop or two of water. Too
   dry, sprinkle water as you mix. Too wet, mix in dry browns. Gloves
   are a good habit, and always wash your hands after working the pile.
6. Turn the pile (mix it with a fork, bottom to top) every week or two
   if you want speed, or once or twice a month if you are patient.
   Turning feeds air to the bacteria. Skipping it does not ruin
   anything; it just slows things down.
7. Keep adding as scraps appear. When the pile reaches about 3 by 3 by
   3 feet, it can hold enough heat inside to really cook. Smaller piles
   still compost, just slowly and coolly.

A well-fed pile of that size warms up inside within days. The middle of
a working pile feels distinctly hot to a quick touch; a compost
thermometer will read about 130 to 160 degrees F (do not hold a bare
hand in material that hot). Heat is a bonus, not a requirement: it
speeds things up and helps kill weed seeds, but a cool pile still turns
into compost eventually.

## The honest timeline

Months, not weeks. Anyone promising two-week compost is selling something.

- A tended pile (good ratio, kept damp, turned regularly): roughly 2 to
  5 months. University sources vary in this range because climate,
  materials, and effort vary; warm weather is faster.
- A well-managed tumbler: as little as a couple of months. The sealed
  container holds heat despite its small size, and spinning it makes
  the frequent mixing easy, which is why it can beat an open pile.
- An ignored pile you just keep adding to: about a year, sometimes two.
  This is a legitimate method. Lazy composting works; it is only slow.

After the pile stops heating, let it sit and finish for at least 4 more
weeks (called curing). Half-finished compost can rob nitrogen from your
plants instead of feeding them.

## How you know it is done

Finished compost is dark brown, loose, and crumbly, smells like fresh
earth after rain, and no longer looks like what you put in. The pile will
have shrunk to about half its original height. A few stubborn bits
(eggshells, avocado pits, corncobs) may remain; sift them out and toss
them back into the next batch.

## Using it

Spread a layer about 1/2 to 1 inch deep over garden beds and work it
gently into the top of the soil, or ring it around existing plants,
keeping it a few inches away from stems. Mix some into the soil when
planting. Compost is far more forgiving than fertilizer, but more is
not better: stick to the 1/2 to 1 inch a year. Years of heavy
applications can build up excess nutrients in the soil.

## Troubleshooting

- Smells like rotten eggs, rancid butter, or vinegar: not enough air,
  usually because the pile is too wet or packed down. Fluff and turn the
  whole pile and mix in dry browns.
- Smells like ammonia (sharp, like window cleaner): too many greens.
  Mix in more browns.
- Rats, raccoons, or other animals: something meaty, oily, or dairy got
  in, or food is sitting exposed. Remove it, always bury new scraps
  under browns, and if it continues, switch to a closed bin; cover any
  vent holes with fine metal mesh (hardware cloth).
- Clouds of fruit flies: annoying, harmless. Bury scraps deeper under
  browns. Freezing scraps before adding them kills fruit fly eggs that
  ride in on fruit skins.
- Pile will not heat up: it is too small (under about 3 by 3 by 3 feet),
  too dry, or too brown-heavy. Grow it, water it while turning, or mix
  in fresh greens like grass clippings. Or it may simply be finished;
  done compost runs cool.
- Soggy mess: too much rain or too many wet greens. Turn it, add plenty
  of dry browns, and cover the top during long wet spells.
- Bone dry and nothing happening: sprinkle water as you turn until it
  passes the wrung-out-sponge test.
- Bugs, worms, and slugs everywhere inside the pile: good news, not a
  problem. They are decomposers doing the work.

## The loop this closes

The scraps from your kitchen become the dark, living soil that a tomato
seedling wants to grow in. Compost feeds the garden, the garden feeds
you, and the trimmings go back into the pile. Waste stops being waste.
Start the pile now and it will be ready for planting season.
