# Ratios and Mixing

A ratio says how much of one thing goes with how much of another. One
part cement to two of sand and three of gravel. Two or three buckets of
dry leaves for every bucket of kitchen scraps. Eight drops of bleach to a
gallon of water. Most of the mixing a household does, in the kitchen, the
garden, the workshop and the medicine cabinet, is a ratio, whether or not
the recipe calls it one.

Getting a ratio right is usually a matter of quality: a cake that rises,
concrete that does not crumble, compost that does not stink. In a few
places it is a matter of safety, and those places are where a beginner
needs to be most careful, because the mistake is invisible. Infant
formula mixed too weak or too strong looks like infant formula. A canning
recipe with a little less vinegar looks the same in the jar. Bleach poured
together with another cleaner "to make it stronger" can make a poisonous
gas.

This guide takes its building mixes from the Army's field manual
*Concrete and Masonry* (FM 5-428, 1998), its kitchen measurement from the
National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), its canning rules
from the US Department of Agriculture (USDA), its cement safety from the
Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) and the National
Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), and its dilutions,
formula and household chemical rules from the Environmental Protection
Agency (EPA), the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) and the Centers for
Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). All are United States government
publications. Where something is general practice, arithmetic or our own
reasoning rather than a published rule, the text says so.

Some neighbouring guides: [Units and Converting
Them](units_and_converting_them.md) is the foundation, because a ratio
is only as good as the units on both sides of it; [Why Canning Has

## First: where a wrong mix hurts people

- **Infant formula.** The FDA says to "Use the amount of water and number
  of powder scoops listed on the instructions of the infant formula
  label. Be sure to use the scoop provided by the manufacturer. Always
  measure the water first and then add the powder." Liquid concentrate
  must be mixed with an equal amount of water; ready-to-feed formula
  needs no mixing at all, so adding water to it is a mistake (our
  reading of the FDA's three forms). The FDA also advises against making
  homemade formula at all: it has had reports of babies hospitalised
  after being fed it. Formula is a recipe that must never be stretched,
  strengthened or improvised (our reading).
- **Canning.** USDA: "Do not alter vinegar, food, or water proportions in
  a recipe or use a vinegar with unknown acidity." And: "Use only recipes
  with tested proportions of ingredients." Its guide adds that the proportion
  of fresh food to other ingredients affects flavour "and, in many
  instances, safety." Never scale, halve, double or adjust a canning
  recipe; [Why Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md) explains
  what is at stake.
- **Bleach and other cleaners.** The CDC: "Never mix bleach with ammonia
  or other cleaners. This will produce a poisonous gas." Stronger is not
  better, and two products are not a recipe: mix only one product, with
  water, as its label says (our reading).
- **Pesticides and garden chemicals.** The EPA's household brochure: "Use
  the right amount. Labels indicate the correct amount to use. Using more
  can waste the product, your money and can cause harm to people, pets
  and the environment." If a label tells you to mix a product in another
  container, use all of the mixture, or label the new container. The
  EPA's advice on leftovers starts with mixing only enough for the job.
- **Food and drink containers.** The EPA's brochure says it is very
  dangerous to put products in food and beverage containers, because
  children think something in a familiar bottle is safe to drink. A
  mixed solution goes in a labelled container that could not be mistaken
  for a drink (our application of the same rule).
- **Wet cement.** OSHA: wet portland cement "can cause caustic burns",
  and "Employees cannot rely on pain or discomfort to alert them to
  cement burns". The burns can keep getting worse after the cement is
  off the skin. Gloves, boots and eye protection are below, in the
  concrete section.
- **Medicine doses.** A dose is not a ratio to scale up or down yourself,
  for a child, a pet or a larger person. Use the label or ask a
  pharmacist (general practice). [Units and Converting
  Them](units_and_converting_them.md) explains the single-letter unit
  mistake that turns a dose into an overdose.

## What a ratio is

A ratio is written with colons: **1:2:3** means one part of the first
thing, two parts of the second and three of the third. "Parts" can be
any unit at all, as long as it is the same unit for every ingredient and
the same way of measuring. The Army's concrete manual makes exactly this
point about its 1:2:3 mix: one part cement, two parts sand and three
parts aggregate, "regardless of the units of measure used (shovels,
cubic feet, wheelbarrows, and so forth)."

Three ways to say the same thing (arithmetic):

- **As parts.** 1:2:3 is six parts in all.
- **As a fraction of the whole.** The cement is 1 of the 6 parts, so one
  sixth of the dry mix; the gravel is 3 of 6, so half.
- **As a percentage.** One sixth is about 17 percent.

Two traps hide in the word "part":

1. **Parts by volume and parts by weight are different mixes.** The
   concrete manual gives the weights that let you convert: a standard
   sack of portland cement weighs 94 pounds and is one loose cubic foot;
   hydrated lime is 50 pounds and damp, loose mortar sand 85 pounds a
   cubic foot. So one bucket of cement to two of sand is 94 pounds to 170
   by weight, about 1 to 1.8, not 1 to 2 (arithmetic), and with lime the
   difference is bigger still (the mortar example below). A recipe should
   tell you which it means. If it does not, ask (general practice).
2. **The same volume does not always hold the same amount.** The manual
   warns that damp sand bulks up: moisture on the grains holds them
   apart, so a bucket of damp sand holds less sand than a bucket of dry,
   and the amount can vary widely, "often not in proportion to the
   moisture content". Its advice: "be very careful when proportioning by
   volume." Flour packed down into a cup and flour spooned in loosely
   are the kitchen version of the same problem (our example).

That is why both the Army and NIST prefer the scale. The manual: measurement by
weight "is the most reliable because the accuracy of volume measurement
depends on an exact knowledge of the amount of moisture in the sand."
NIST, for the kitchen: "Measuring ingredients by mass (weight) using an
appropriate kitchen scale is a best practice to improve recipe accuracy".

## Scaling: everything by the same number

To make more or less of anything, find one number, the **scale factor**,
and multiply every ingredient by it (arithmetic):

> scale factor = the amount you want / the amount the recipe makes

**Worked example: a stew for 10 from a recipe for 4.** The scale factor
is 10 / 4 = 2.5. Every ingredient times 2.5:

| Ingredient | Recipe for 4 | For 10 |
|---|---|---|
| Potatoes | 500 g | 1,250 g |
| Onions | 1 | 2 or 3 (2.5) |
| Water | 1.5 litres | 3.75 litres |
| Salt | 1 teaspoon | 2.5 teaspoons |

The mistakes that spoil a scaled recipe are nearly always one of three
(general practice): an ingredient missed and left at the old amount;
the factor applied twice to one line; or a fraction mishandled. Write the
factor at the top of the page, scale every line in writing before you
start, and tick each one off as it goes in.

Fractions of cups are where scaling goes wrong most easily. Three
quarters of a cup times 2.5 is one and seven eighths cups, which no
measuring cup shows. NIST's answer for converting a recipe is the
scale: measure the ingredient once with the recipe's own measuring tool,
weigh it in grams, and then scale the grams. Salt and strong spices are
worth adding a little at a time and tasting, since a mistake there is
hard to undo (general practice).

### What does not scale

- **Canning recipes, ever.** Above, and in [Why Canning Has
  Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md).
- **Jams and jellies.** USDA: "make one batch at a time, according to
  the recipe. Increasing the quantities often results in soft gels." It
  also says not to try to reduce the sugar in traditional recipes,
  because too little sugar prevents gelling and may let yeasts and
  moulds grow.
- **Pans and tins.** A recipe's quantities fit its pan's area, and area
  goes with the square of the width ([Units and Converting
  Them](units_and_converting_them.md) has the rule). Moving from a 9 inch
  round tin to a 12 inch one is not a third more but about 1.8 times the
  mixture, because 12 x 12 / (9 x 9) = 1.78 (arithmetic).
- **Cooking times and temperatures** do not scale with the amount. A
  double batch in a deeper pot needs longer and checking; it does not
  need twice the heat (general practice).
- **Doses**, as above.

### A check on the arithmetic

Before you trust a scaled number, check that it is the right size
(general practice, and the subject of [Estimating](estimating.md)). For
10 people from a recipe for 4, every amount should be a bit more than
double. If one line came out smaller, or ten times bigger, it is wrong.

If you use the calculator in this app, read the note about it in "How
the game models it" below: it works strictly from left to right.

## Strength: how concentrated a mixture is

### Percentages

A **percentage** concentration is the part that is the active ingredient,
out of the whole mixture, times 100 (arithmetic). It can be by weight or
by volume, and a label should say which. One tablespoon of something in a
quart of water is a ratio of 1:64, because a quart is 64 tablespoons (32
fluid ounces, each 2 tablespoons); as a share of the whole it is 1 / 65,
about 1.5 percent (arithmetic).

That example is real. The EPA's page on household hazardous waste gives
a simple glass cleaner: "Mix one tablespoon of vinegar or lemon juice in
one quart of water."

### Worked example: the same dose from two strengths of bleach

The EPA's table for disinfecting drinking water with plain, unscented
household bleach when the water cannot be boiled gives the amount for two
strengths of bleach:

| Water | 6% bleach | 8.25% bleach |
|---|---|---|
| 1 quart or litre | 2 drops | 2 drops |
| 1 gallon | 8 drops | 6 drops |
| 2 gallons | 16 drops (1/4 teaspoon) | 12 drops (1/8 teaspoon) |
| 4 gallons | 1/3 teaspoon | 1/4 teaspoon |
| 8 gallons | 2/3 teaspoon | 1/2 teaspoon |

Read it as a ratio. Stronger bleach, fewer drops: 8 drops at 6 percent
and 6 drops at 8.25 percent carry nearly the same amount of the active
chemical (8 x 6 = 48 against 6 x 8.25 = 49.5, arithmetic). The EPA adds:
double the amount if the water is cloudy, coloured or very cold; stir and
let it stand 30 minutes; the water should have a slight chlorine smell,
and if it does not, repeat the dose and wait 15 minutes more. Use only
regular, unscented bleach whose label says it is suitable for
disinfection, fresh or kept at room temperature for less than a year,
and never a scented, colour-safe or added-cleaner bleach. The full
method is in [Making Water Safe to Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md).

So a ratio is only as good as the strength of the thing you are
diluting. Read the percentage on the label every time you buy a new
bottle (our reading of the EPA's two columns).

### Worked example: a stock solution

For a large volume, it can be easier to make a strong **stock solution**
first and then dilute that. The EPA's method with high-test granular
calcium hypochlorite (HTH): add one heaping teaspoon, about a
quarter of an ounce, to two gallons of water and stir until it dissolves,
which makes a solution of approximately 500 milligrams per litre. Then
"add one part of the chlorine solution to each 100 parts of water you are
treating", about the same, it says, as 1 pint of the solution in 12.5
gallons of water.

Check the arithmetic: a gallon is 8 pints, so 12.5 gallons is 100 pints,
and 1 pint to 100 is the 1:100 ratio. The water ends up at about 500 /
101, so roughly 5 milligrams per litre (arithmetic). The EPA's cautions
come with it: make the stock where there is ventilation, wear eye
protection, and follow the label for handling and storage, because the
granules are "a very powerful oxidant."

### Worked example: a cleaning solution, and its limit

For cleaning mould off hard surfaces, the CDC gives a bleach solution of
"no more than 1 cup of household laundry bleach in 1 gallon of water".
That is at most 1:16, since a gallon is 16 cups (arithmetic). The words
"no more than" matter: this is a ceiling, not a target. The CDC says to
wear rubber boots, rubber gloves and goggles, and never to mix the bleach
with any other cleaner; keep the room aired while you work (general
practice). [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md) says when bleach is the wrong tool
altogether.

## Concrete and mortar

### Water is the ratio that decides strength

The Army's manual says concrete strength is largely determined by the
ratio of water to cement, and puts it plainly: "The basic ratio of cement
to water determines the concrete's strength; generally, the less water in
the mix, the stronger, more durable and watertight the concrete." It
explains that a sack of cement needs only about 2 and a half gallons of
water for hydration, the reaction that hardens it, and that the extra
water in a workable mix is there for workability; too
much "dilutes the cement paste (binder), resulting in weak and porous
concrete". Economical mixes run from 4 gallons at the least to 7 gallons
at the most per sack.

The manual writes this as the **water-cement ratio**, pounds of water to
pounds of cement. For watertight work, it gives the Corps of Engineers'
limits: 5.5 gallons per sack (a ratio of 0.48) for concrete exposed to
fresh water, and 5 gallons per sack (0.44) for salt water.

**Check the arithmetic.** A US gallon of water weighs about 8.34 pounds
(general knowledge), so 5 gallons is about 41.7 pounds, and 41.7 / 94 =
0.44, the manual's figure. Adding one more gallon to a one-sack batch to
make it easier to work moves the ratio up by about 0.09 (arithmetic),
which by the manual's rule makes the concrete weaker and less durable.

### The 1:2:3 mix

For a simple batch, the manual's method: one part cement, two parts sand
and three parts aggregate (gravel), measured with whatever you have,
shovels, buckets or wheelbarrows, as long as it is the same measure for
all three. Measure the mixing water accurately for every batch, and
allow for water already in wet aggregate.

**Worked example: mixing by hand** (the manual's method; the bucket
counts are our example). Use buckets of the same size: 1 of cement, 2 of
sand, 3 of gravel. The manual says to mix on a clean, level, moistened
surface with tight joints, or in a mixing box; spread the cement over the
sand and the gravel over the cement; turn the dry materials at least
three times until the colour is even; then add water slowly while
turning at least three times more, until it is the right consistency.
For a small job, bagged ready-mix concrete states its own water and its
yield on the bag; follow the bag (general practice).

The 1:2:3 mix comes from a 1998 Army manual. For footings, slabs that
carry a building, retaining walls or anything that holds people up, the
mix, the reinforcement and the thickness belong to your local building
code and, where it says so, an engineer (general practice).

### Mortar: ratios by volume, converted to weight

The same manual gives mortar types by volume. Type N, for general use in
above-grade masonry, is one part portland cement, one part hydrated lime
and six parts sand. It says to convert volume parts to weight by
multiplying each by its weight per cubic foot.

**Worked example** (arithmetic, from the manual's weights): Type N by
weight is 94 pounds of cement, 50 of lime and 6 x 85 = 510 of damp,
loose sand. As a weight ratio that is about 1 : 0.53 : 5.4, quite unlike
the 1:1:6 by volume. Both describe the same mortar; they are simply
counting in different units. That is why it matters which one a recipe
means.

### Wet cement burns

OSHA's booklet *Preventing Skin Problems from Working with Portland
Cement* (2008) is written for workers; its advice on protecting your skin
applies just as well to a weekend slab (our reading):

- Wet cement can cause caustic burns, which may result in blisters, dead
  or hardened skin, or black or green skin, and in severe cases go to
  the bone. They may not hurt at first, and they can get worse after
  contact has ended. OSHA advises anyone with a cement burn to see a
  health care professional immediately.
- Wear well-fitting gloves; it says butyl or nitrile gloves, rather than
  cotton or leather, are frequently recommended. Loose gloves let cement
  in, and gloves or clothes that cement gets inside make exposure worse.
- Tape long sleeves to the gloves, wear waterproof boots high enough that
  cement cannot get in, tuck trousers inside and tape the tops, and kneel
  on waterproof kneepads or dry boards, never in wet cement.
- Wear proper eye protection. If cement gets in your eyes anyway, the
  first aid in NIOSH's Pocket Guide to Chemical Hazards is to irrigate
  them immediately: flush them with plenty of clean water. Then get
  medical help (general practice).
- Wash skin that touches wet cement in clean, cool water, with a
  pH-neutral or slightly acidic soap. Do not use abrasives or waterless
  hand cleaners, and do not use lanolin, petroleum jelly or other
  skin-softening products, which can seal the cement onto the skin.
  Take off watches and rings, which trap cement against the skin.
- Change out of clothes that wet cement has soaked into.

Burns that need care are covered in [Treating Burns](treating_burns.md),
but a cement burn of any size is a reason to see a professional, as OSHA
says.

## Compost: a ratio you can be loose about

Not every ratio needs a scale. The EPA's guide to composting at home:
"add at least two to three times the volume of browns (such as dry
leaves) to the volume of greens (such as food scraps)", and keep food
scraps covered by four to eight inches of browns. For a five-gallon
bucket of kitchen scraps that means 10 to 15 gallons of leaves (our
arithmetic). Done by eye, with buckets, it works, because the pile tells
you when the ratio is off: the EPA says a pile with a bad odour may be
too wet or need more air, and the fix is more browns and a turn. [Your
First Compost](your_first_compost.md) has the rest.

## Fuel for two-stroke engines

Chainsaws, string trimmers and many small engines burn a mixture of
petrol and two-stroke oil, at a ratio the engine's maker sets: the
manual, the engine and often the oil bottle say what it is (general
practice). The ratio is usually given as fuel to oil, such as 50:1, and
the arithmetic works for any unit:

- At 50:1, a US gallon (128 fluid ounces) of petrol takes 128 / 50 = 2.56
  fluid ounces of oil.
- At 50:1, 5 litres takes 5,000 / 50 = 100 millilitres.
- At 40:1, 5 litres takes 125 millilitres.

Use the ratio your manual gives, not the one a neighbour uses, and do not
run a two-stroke engine on plain petrol (general practice). Mix fuel
outdoors, away from any flame (general practice). Refuel with the engine
cold and store fuel outside the house, as the Consumer Product Safety
Commission advises; the details are in [Keeping Things
Working](keeping_things_working.md).

## How exact is exact enough?

Different ratios forgive different errors (our framing):

- **Forgiving:** compost, garden mulches, most soups and stews. Close is
  fine, and you can correct as you go.
- **Moderate:** baking, mortar, concrete, a two-stroke mix, a cleaning
  solution. Measure every time; weigh where you can; keep to the stated
  limits.
- **Exact, every time, as written:** infant formula, canning recipes,
  medicines, drinking-water disinfection, and any chemical whose label
  gives the amount. These are the ones where you follow the instruction
  and do not improvise, scale or "improve" it.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Infant formula questions,** especially for a baby under three months
  old, born prematurely, or with a weakened immune system: the FDA says
  to contact the baby's paediatrician about any extra precautions.
- **Medicine doses:** the label, a pharmacist or a doctor (general
  practice).
- **Canning recipes:** tested recipes only, unaltered (USDA).
- **Structural concrete:** your local building code, and an engineer
  where the code requires one (general practice).
- **A cement burn:** a health care professional, immediately (OSHA).
- **Pesticides and other chemicals:** the amount on the label, no more.
  If the label does not cover what you want to do, do not do it (our
  reading of the EPA's rule).

## How the game models it

The game is full of fixed ratios, and in a couple of places it uses real
ones.

- **Recipes are fixed ratios, one batch at a time.** Each recipe on the
  Crafting page takes set amounts of each ingredient and makes a set
  amount of product, and each press of Craft makes one batch. There is
  no scaling: to make more, you craft again.
- **A real dilution.** Mix Insecticidal Soap takes 1 Soap Bar and 5
  Purified Water and makes 5 Insecticidal Soap Spray, each described as
  1 litre of 2 percent soap. The recipe's notes do the arithmetic: a 100
  gram bar in 5 litres of water is 100 g in 5.1 kg, 1.96 percent, and
  the weights of the five sprays add up to the weights that went in.
- **A real compost balance.** The compost recipes at a Composter
  (sawdust with seed press cake or wheat bran, with water) are balanced,
  according to the notes in the recipe file, at about 33 parts carbon to
  1 of nitrogen, inside the 20 to 40 range those notes give for a pile
  that composts well.
- **Not a real concrete.** Pour Concrete takes 2 Cement, 2 Gravel and 1
  Purified Water and makes 2 Concrete Blocks. There is no sand in it, and
  by the items' own weights 9.4 kg of ingredients make 24 kg of blocks.
  Mix Cement makes cement by stirring stone, clay and sand together;
  real portland cement, the Army manual explains, is made by heating
  its raw materials to 2,600 to 3,000 degrees F into clinker and grinding
  that to powder. Do not learn a concrete mix from the game.
- **The default settings.** In the Dev play mode the game starts in
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), crafting by hand does not use up
  its ingredients, so the ratios cost you nothing; switch Play mode to
  Normal to see them consumed. And while "Start every session from the
  default home" is on, which it is by default, only your character
  carries between launches, so what you made is gone at the next start.
- **The Calculator works left to right.** The app's Calculator (in the
  Platform tab) does each operation in the order you type it, so 2 + 3 x
  4 gives 20, not the 14 that arithmetic's usual order gives, and
  brackets give an error. For ratio work, do one multiplication or
  division at a time and press = after each.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the
water-cement ratio, the strength of a dilution changing with the
strength of what you dilute, measuring by weight against measuring by
volume, and every one of the exact-every-time mixtures above. In the game
nothing goes wrong if you get a mixture wrong, because you cannot.

## You own this when

- You follow infant formula, canning recipes, medicine labels and
  chemical labels exactly, and never scale or "improve" them.
- You never mix bleach with ammonia or any other cleaner, and you keep
  every mixed solution in a labelled container that cannot be mistaken
  for a drink.
- You can turn a ratio into a fraction and a percentage, and back.
- You know whether a recipe means parts by volume or by weight, and you
  prefer the scale.
- You can scale a recipe with one scale factor, in writing, and check the
  answer is the right size.
- You know that jams, pans and cooking times do not scale like the
  ingredients do.
- You can read a dilution table for two strengths of the same chemical,
  and explain why the stronger one takes fewer drops.
- You can make and use a stock solution at a stated ratio.
- You know that water decides the strength of concrete, you can mix a
  1:2:3 batch by hand, and you convert a mortar recipe from volume to
  weight.
- You wear gloves, boots and eye protection with wet cement, wash it off
  in clean cool water, and treat a cement burn as a reason to see a
  professional.
- You mix two-stroke fuel at your engine's own ratio.
- You know which ratios forgive you and which never do.
