# Light, Lenses and Mirrors

Light does only a handful of things. It travels in straight lines. It
spreads out as it goes, so it gets weaker with distance. It bounces off
surfaces. It bends when it passes from air into water or glass, and the
different colours in it bend by slightly different amounts. Every
shadow, every mirror, every pair of glasses, every rainbow and every
magnifying glass is those few rules at work.

Knowing them is useful in ordinary ways: putting a lamp where it does
the most good, choosing and using a magnifier, looking into a place you
cannot get your head into, signalling for help with a mirror, watching
an eclipse without hurting your eyes, and not misjudging how deep water
is. It also explains the two ways light hurts people at home: in the
eyes, and by starting fires.

The physics in this guide comes from the Navy's training manual for its
optical repair specialists, *Opticalman 3 & 2* (1979), which teaches
lenses, mirrors and prisms from the beginning. The safety advice comes
from NASA, the Food and Drug Administration (FDA), the Consumer Product
Safety Commission (CPSC) and the Army's survival manual, the colours of
the sky from the National Weather Service, the speed of light from the
National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST), and the eye from
the National Eye Institute. All are United States government
publications. Where something is general practice, arithmetic or our own
reasoning rather than a published rule, the text says so.

## First: where light hurts people

- **The sun in your eyes, worst of all through optics.** NASA says that
  outside the brief total phase of a total eclipse, it is "not safe to
  look directly at the Sun without specialized eye protection for solar
  viewing", and that looking at it through a camera lens, binoculars or
  a telescope without a special-purpose solar filter on the front "will
  instantly cause severe eye injury." It also warns that ordinary
  sunglasses, "no matter how dark, are not safe for viewing the Sun."
  Never point binoculars, a telescope or a camera viewfinder anywhere
  near the sun (general practice, from NASA's warning). To watch the
  sun, project it with a pinhole, below, or use eclipse glasses or a
  handheld solar viewer, which NASA says "ought to comply with the ISO
  12312-2 international standard". Even with those on, never look at the
  sun through binoculars, a telescope or a camera: NASA warns the
  concentrated rays "will burn through the filter".
- **Lenses and glass in sunlight start fires.** The Army's survival
  manual teaches lighting tinder with a lens from binoculars, a camera,
  a telescopic sight or a magnifying glass, by angling it "to
  concentrate the sun's rays on the tinder." That works whether you
  meant it to or not. The CPSC has recalled snow globes because "When
  exposed to sunlight, the snow globes can act as a magnifying glass and
  ignite nearby combustible materials", after two reports of them
  igniting nearby materials, and outdoor solar lights whose lens set
  mulch and dry grass alight in five reported cases. Keep magnifying
  glasses covered or in a drawer, and keep glass balls, globes, water
  bottles and concave mirrors off sunny windowsills and away from
  anything that burns (general practice, from the same physics).
- **Lasers.** The FDA says a laser beam shone into a person's eye "can
  instantly cause damage", that laser eye injuries "often do not cause
  immediate pain", and that a beam bouncing off mirrors, shiny switch
  plates or polished metal "will reflect off and still be hazardous to
  anyone in the room." Its tips: never aim a laser at any person or
  animal; never at a vehicle or an aircraft ("Pointing a laser at an
  aircraft is a federal crime"); do not buy laser pointers for children
  or let them use them as toys; do not buy or use a laser of more than 5
  milliwatts, or one without its power printed on the label.
- **Water is deeper than it looks.** Because light bends as it leaves
  the water, the Navy manual explains, a fish is not where it appears to
  be, but deeper. So is the bottom. Find out the depth by wading in
  carefully or with a pole, and never jump or dive into water you have
  not checked (general practice).
- **A signal mirror can dazzle a pilot.** The survival manual warns not
  to hold the flash on an aircraft's cockpit for more than a few
  seconds, because it may blind the pilot.
- **Any eye injury needs a professional.** The FDA's advice, for laser
  injuries, is to "Immediately consult a health care professional" if
  you suspect one. The same goes for anything that has gone wrong with
  an eye after looking at the sun (general practice).

## Light travels in straight lines

The Navy manual's first rules of light include that it travels in
straight lines as rays. A shadow is simply the place the straight lines
from the light cannot reach.

Light is also fast. NIST gives the speed of light in a vacuum as
exactly 299,792,458 metres a second. For anything that happens on Earth,
the light from it reaches you, for practical purposes, at once.

### The pinhole: an image without a lens

Because light goes in straight lines, a tiny hole is enough to make a
picture: light from the top of a bright object passes through the hole
and lands at the bottom of the screen behind it, and light from the
bottom lands at the top, so the picture is upside down (our
explanation).

This is the safe way to look at the sun. NASA describes a pinhole
projector: a small hole, such as one punched in an index card,
"projects an image of the Sun onto a nearby surface. With the Sun at
your back, you can then safely view the projected image. Do NOT look at
the Sun through the pinhole!"

**Worked example: an eclipse box.** NASA's version: tape aluminium foil
over a hole in one side of a cardboard box, punch a pinhole in the foil,
and tape white paper inside the box on the far side. Stand with the sun
behind you so that sunlight streams through the pinhole onto the paper,
and look at the image through another hole cut in the box. During a
partial eclipse the image is a crescent. You never look towards the sun
at all.

### Light spreads out: the inverse-square rule

Light from a lamp spreads out as it travels. The Navy manual's
explanation: at twice the distance, the light from a lamp covers four
times the area, so each part of that area gets one quarter as much.
Illumination falls off with the square of the distance.

**Worked example: a reading lamp** (arithmetic). A lamp 60 centimetres
from a page is moved to 30 centimetres. Half the distance gives four
times the light on the page. Moving it to 20 centimetres, a third of the
distance, gives nine times. Moving a lamp closer often does more than
buying a brighter bulb. The National Eye Institute lists brighter
reading lights among the first things to try when close work gets hard
with age.

The same rule is why grow lights hang close above the plants: a light
twice as high puts a quarter of the light on the leaves (arithmetic).
The rule is exact for a small source and only approximate close to a
large one such as a long tube or a broad panel, where the light falls
off more slowly at short range (general knowledge).

## Reflection

### The law of reflection

The Navy manual names the parts. The ray arriving at a mirror is the
incident ray, the ray leaving is the reflected ray, and the imaginary
line standing straight out of the mirror where the ray strikes is the
normal. The angle between the incident ray and the normal is the angle
of incidence; between the reflected ray and the normal, the angle of
reflection. The law:

1. The angle of reflection equals the angle of incidence.
2. The incident ray and the reflected ray lie on opposite sides of the
   normal.
3. The incident ray, the reflected ray and the normal all lie in the
   same plane.

A ball bouncing off the cushion of a pool table follows the same rule,
which is the manual's own example.

**Smooth and rough surfaces.** The manual calls mirror-like reflection
specular, and says it comes only from a polished, flat surface. A rough
surface, like a white wall or paper, scatters light in all directions
instead. That is why a white wall brightens a whole room evenly while a
mirror throws a single patch of light (our example).

**Light paths run both ways.** The manual's law of reversibility says a
ray sent back along its path retraces it exactly, through any number of
mirrors, prisms or lenses. One consequence: if you can see someone's
eyes in a mirror, they can see yours (our example).

### Flat mirrors

A flat mirror makes what the Navy manual calls a virtual image: it seems
to be behind the glass, as far behind it as the object is in front, but
there is nothing there to put a screen on. It exists only for the eye
that looks.

**Using a small mirror to see round a corner** (general practice, from
the law). An inspection mirror on a handle lets you look behind an
appliance, under a sink or into an engine bay. To see a spot, the mirror
has to face halfway between your eye and the spot, because the angles in
and out must be equal. Move the mirror, not your head, until the spot
appears, and remember that left and right are swapped in what you see.

**Why a window turns into a mirror at night.** The Navy manual says
smooth glass reflects about 4 percent of the light that falls on it,
more when the light strikes at a glancing angle. By day that small
reflection is lost against the bright view outside; at night, with the
room lit and the outside dark, it is all you see (our explanation).

### Curved mirrors

The Navy manual describes two kinds:

- **A convex mirror** bulges outwards and spreads light. It shows a wide
  view, which is why trucks and cars use them as rear-view mirrors, but
  it makes everything look smaller and so farther away. Federal motor
  vehicle safety standard 111 requires a convex mirror used as a car's
  passenger-side mirror to carry the words "Objects in Mirror Are Closer
  Than They Appear." Judge a gap with the flat mirror or by turning your
  head, not by the convex one (general practice).
- **A concave mirror** curves inwards. The manual says it acts on light
  the same way a converging lens does: it brings light to a focus.
  That is how a torch or a headlamp throws a beam, with the bulb at the
  mirror's focus. Turned the other way, a concave shaving or make-up
  mirror can bring sunlight to a hot spot, so it belongs out of direct
  sun (our extension of the fire warning above).

### Signal mirrors

The Army's survival manual is emphatic: "On a sunny day, a mirror is
your best signaling device." A polished metal cup or belt buckle will
do if you have no mirror. It notes that pilots have reported seeing
mirror flashes "up to 160 kilometers away under ideal conditions", and
says to practise now, "do not wait until you need it". If your mirror
has instructions on the back, follow them.

A way to aim a plain mirror (general practice, from the law of
reflection): hold out your other hand at arm's length, angle the mirror
until the bright spot of reflected sun lands on your hand, then raise
your hand towards the target and keep the spot on it while you move the
hand out of the way. Sweep the spot slowly across the target rather than
flashing it fast, and never hold it on a cockpit, as the manual warns.

## Refraction: light bends

Light slows down when it passes from air into water or glass. The Navy
manual defines a material's **index of refraction** as the speed of
light in a vacuum divided by its speed in the material, and gives these
values: air 1.0003, water 1.33, crown glass (ordinary optical glass)
1.52. Light in ordinary glass travels at only about two thirds of its
speed in air.

When light crosses at an angle from air into something slower, it bends
towards the normal; coming back out, it bends away (the Navy manual's
laws of refraction). That bend is behind three everyday effects.

- **The bent stick.** A straight stick standing in a glass of water
  looks bent at the surface. The Navy manual calls it an optical
  illusion created by refraction.
- **The shallow-looking pond.** For the same reason, the bottom of
  water looks closer than it is. Looking almost straight down, water
  looks about three quarters of its true depth, because 1 / 1.33 is
  about 0.75 (a standard physics result for nearly straight-down
  viewing, worked from the index; not from the sources). From an angle
  the effect is stronger. Hence the warning at the top.
- **The silver ceiling of the sea.** Light trying to leave water at too
  shallow an angle cannot get out at all and is reflected back. The Navy
  manual gives the critical angle for water as 48 degrees 36 minutes,
  measured from the normal: beyond it, the surface seen from below acts
  as a perfect mirror. The same total internal reflection is why, the
  manual explains, the prisms in binoculars need no silver coating on
  their reflecting faces. Optical fibres carry light the same way
  (general knowledge).

### Colours, rainbows and the blue sky

The National Weather Service explains that white light "is actually a
combination of colors", each with its own wavelength, violet the
shortest and red the longest. Each colour bends by a slightly different
amount, so a prism or a raindrop can spread white light into its
colours.

**Rainbows.** The weather service describes sunlight entering a
raindrop, reflecting off the inside of the drop and bending again on the
way out, concentrated along an angle of 42 degrees. To see one: "You
need to be standing with the sun to your back and the rain in front of
you", and the sun must be less than 42 degrees above the horizon, which
is why rainbows are morning and evening sights. A fainter secondary bow,
from two reflections inside the drops, sits at about 50 degrees with
its colours reversed.

**The blue sky.** The weather service explains that the molecules of
the air scatter violet and blue light most, a process called Rayleigh
scattering, and that the sky looks blue rather than violet because our
eyes are more sensitive to blue. At sunrise and sunset the light crosses
much more air, the blue is scattered out of the direct beam, and the
reds and oranges remain.

## Lenses

### Converging and diverging

A lens that is thicker in the middle than at the edges is a
**converging** (positive) lens: the Navy manual says parallel rays
passing through it come together at a point, the **principal focus**.
The distance from the lens to that point is its **focal length**. A lens
thinner in the middle is **diverging** (negative) and spreads light out.

### Dioptres

Lens strength is measured in **dioptres**. The Navy manual: a lens with
a focal length of 1 metre has a power of 1 dioptre, and in general the
power is 1 divided by the focal length in metres, positive for a
converging lens and negative for a diverging one. Its examples: a
converging lens of 20 centimetre focal length is +5 dioptres; a
diverging lens of 50 centimetres is -2.

The strength printed on shop reading glasses, such as +2.00, is in
dioptres (general knowledge), so a +2.00 lens has a focal length of 1 /
2 = 0.5 metres (arithmetic).

### Real and virtual images

The Navy manual separates two kinds of image. A **real image** is made
where light rays actually come together; it can be caught on a screen
or a sheet of paper, the way a camera lens forms its picture. A
**virtual image** is only seen by the eye looking through the lens or
into the mirror; nothing could catch it on paper.

**Worked example: finding a lens's focal length** (general practice,
from the manual's principal focus). Stand across a room from a bright
window, hold the lens up, and put a sheet of white paper behind it.
Move the paper until a sharp, small, upside-down picture of the window
appears. The distance from the lens to the paper is close to the focal
length; the farther away the window, the closer. Do this with a window,
never by focusing the sun on paper, which risks your eyes and a fire.

### Magnifiers

The Navy manual explains that a positive lens magnifies because it lets
the light from an object take up a larger angle at your eye than it
could without the lens. It works out magnifying power by comparing with
how big the object looks at 10 inches (about 25 centimetres), the usual
closest distance for clear seeing: the magnifying power is 10 inches
divided by the focal length in inches. In metric, about 25 centimetres
divided by the focal length (arithmetic).

**Worked example** (arithmetic). Your lens focused the window at 5
centimetres. Its magnifying power is about 25 / 5 = 5, a "5x" magnifier.
A lens with a 10 centimetre focal length is about 2.5x. The shorter the
focal length, the stronger the magnifier, and the closer you have to
hold it to the thing you are looking at.

To use one, hold it close to your eye and bring the work towards it
until it is sharp, or hold it just above the work and move your head;
either way the work sits a little inside the focal length, and you see
an upright, enlarged virtual image (general practice). Then put it away
out of the sun.

### Your eyes are lenses too

The National Eye Institute explains that presbyopia, trouble seeing
things up close, happens because the lens inside the eye hardens with
age and stops focusing light correctly on the retina. It says
"Everyone gets presbyopia as they get older", usually after age 45. In
the early stages it suggests holding reading material farther away,
using larger print, and brighter reading lights; later, some people use
over-the-counter reading glasses, or an eye doctor can prescribe
lenses. Eye doctors check for it as part of a comprehensive eye exam.

## Know where your own optics stops

- **Eye exams and prescriptions** are an eye doctor's work. Shop reading
  glasses help many people with close work, but they are not a
  substitute for an exam (the National Eye Institute's advice on
  presbyopia, and general practice).
- **Any eye injury, or any change in your sight after looking at the sun
  or a laser,** needs a health care professional straight away (FDA,
  and general practice).
- **Lasers beyond a labelled 5 milliwatt pointer** are not household
  tools (FDA).
- **Watching the sun through a telescope or binoculars** needs a proper
  solar filter fitted over the front of the optics, or a pinhole
  projector instead (NASA). Do not improvise a filter (general practice).

## How the game models it

The game's renderer follows real optics in a few places, and its items
and machines leave most of optics out.

- **The sky is blue for the real reason.** The game scatters light in
  each planet's air by colour, the Rayleigh scattering the weather
  service describes. Earth's air is set to scatter blue about six times
  as strongly as red, and the code's notes compare those strengths with
  real air's. You can switch this off in Settings > Graphics ("Scattering
  atmosphere"); it is on by default.
- **Water reflects more at a low angle.** The water shader uses water's
  index of refraction, 1.33, the Navy manual's figure, to reflect about
  2 percent of the sky when you look straight down and more and more
  towards a low, grazing angle, the way the Navy manual says glass
  reflects more of the light that strikes it at a glancing angle. Its
  reflection is drawn from the game's own sky.
- **Grow lights cover a fixed area.** A powered grow light lights an
  area of crop set by its watts: a 100 watt light covers about 0.58
  square metres of a crop that needs the game's reference amount of
  light, 17 moles of light per square metre a day, according to the
  game's lighting data. It reaches plots within 3 metres across the
  floor and none beyond. That reach is a game estimate, which the data
  file says was chosen because light falls off with the square of
  distance; within the reach, the game does not dim the light with
  distance.
- **Solar panels follow the sun's hour and the clouds.** A game panel
  makes nothing at night and its full rating at local noon, and one built
  on a planet's ground, under the weather, makes a quarter of it under a
  full overcast. Unlike a real panel ([Your First Solar
  Power](first_solar_power.md) explains tilting one towards the sun), it
  does not matter which way a game panel faces or how it is tilted.
- **Optical things you can make do nothing yet.** A Mirror (a glass pane
  coated with silver at a workbench), Binoculars, a Telescope and a
  Microscope are in the item list with recipes, but none of them shows
  you anything: there is no zoom, no magnifier and no way to light a
  fire with a lens. Under the default settings, the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode) makes the materials free, and while
  "Start every session from the default home" is on, which it is by
  default, only your character carries between launches, so anything
  you made is gone at the next start.

What the game does not model, so you do not learn it from the game:
water that looks shallower than it is, lenses and focusing, burning
glasses, rainbows, and the danger of looking at the sun. In the game
you can look straight at the sun and nothing happens. Do not carry that
habit outside.

## You own this when

- You never look at the sun, least of all through binoculars, a
  telescope or a camera, and you can make a pinhole projector to watch
  an eclipse.
- You keep magnifiers, glass globes and concave mirrors out of the sun.
- You never point a laser at a person, an animal, a vehicle or an
  aircraft, and you know the FDA's 5 milliwatt line.
- You check water depth before you wade or jump, because it is deeper
  than it looks.
- You can state the law of reflection and use it to aim a mirror, look
  round a corner, or signal for help.
- You know why a convex mirror makes things look farther away, and why
  a concave one can start a fire.
- You can explain the bent stick, the shallow pond and the silver
  underside of the water surface.
- You know why the sky is blue, why sunsets are red, and where to look
  for a rainbow.
- You can find a lens's focal length with a window and a sheet of paper,
  convert it to dioptres, and work out roughly how strong a magnifier
  it makes.
- You move a lamp closer before you buy a brighter one, and you can say
  why.
- You know when an eye problem is an eye doctor's job.
