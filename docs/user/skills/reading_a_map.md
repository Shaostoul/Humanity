# Reading a Map

A map is a drawing of the ground, made small enough to carry. Reading
one means doing that drawing backwards: looking at lines, colours and
numbers on paper and seeing the hill, the stream and the road they stand
for, then looking at the hill in front of you and finding it on the
paper. Done well, a map tells you how far it is, which way to go, how
steep the climb will be, where the water runs and where you are now.

The Army's map reading manual defines a map as a graphic representation
of a portion of the earth's surface drawn to scale, as seen from above.
Every part of that definition matters. **Drawn to scale** means you can
measure distances on it. **As seen from above** means you have to turn
a bird's view into the view from your own eyes. And **a portion** means
the map stops somewhere, and the next sheet may be the one you need.

Most of this guide comes from that manual (FM 3-25.26, *Map Reading and
Land Navigation*), from the US Geological Survey (USGS), which makes the
standard topographic maps of the United States, from NOAA's National
Centers for Environmental Information on the magnetic field, and from
the National Park Service and the US Forest Service on going outdoors
with a map. All are United
States government publications, in the public domain. Where something is
general practice or plain arithmetic rather than a published rule, the
text says so.

Two other guides sit beside this one. [Estimating](estimating.md) covers
pace counting, which is how you measure distance on the ground to match
the distance you measured on the map. [The Sky as an
Instrument](the_sky_as_an_instrument.md) covers finding true north
without a compass and, in detail, why a compass does not point there.

## First: a map in places where you could get hurt

Most map reading happens at a kitchen table, planning. The rest happens
outdoors, and there a mistake can mean a cold night, a fall or a long
search. A few habits matter more than any technique below.

- **Tell someone where you are going and when to expect you back.** The
  National Park Service says so on its page about the ten essentials to
  carry. If you do not come back, somebody knows where to start
  looking.
- **Carry a paper map, even if you have a phone.** The Park Service lists
  navigation (a map, a compass and GPS) among the ten essentials, says
  to know how to use a topographic map, compass or GPS before going out,
  and says: "Bring a physical map as a back up." It also says to pack an
  extra battery for your phone. Navigation is only the first of its ten;
  carry the other nine too: sun protection, extra clothing, a light,
  first-aid supplies, a way to make fire, a repair kit and tools, food,
  water and emergency shelter.
- **Check the date on the map.** A map shows the ground as it was when
  the information was gathered, not as it is today. The Army manual
  warns against relying mainly on man-made features (its word is
  "cultural") and vegetation, because they are "most likely to have
  changed since the map was last revised". A trail, a bridge or a fence
  on the map may be gone, and a new one may not be on it.
- **Read the steepness before you go, not on the slope.** Contour lines
  that touch or nearly touch mean a cliff. A slope whose contour lines
  are far apart at the top and close together at the bottom gets steeper
  as you go down, and from its top you cannot see the steep part below
  you. Both are explained in the contour section.
- **A map distance is a flat distance.** The Army manual says distances
  measured on a map do not take the rise and fall of the land into
  account, so the distance on the ground will be longer. On a steep
  route, plan for more time and more effort than the map distance
  suggests.
- **Do not plan on water from a dashed blue line.** On USGS maps, a
  solid blue line is a stream that runs all year and a blue dashed and
  dotted line is an intermittent stream. An intermittent stream may be
  dry when you get there (general practice).
- **Know where you are all the time, not just at the end.** The Army
  manual calls this the key to success in land navigation. Check your
  position against the map at every feature you pass. If you can no
  longer match the map to the ground, stop and work it out before going
  further; walking on in the hope that it will make sense later is how
  people get badly lost (general practice).
- **If you think you are lost, stop.** The US Forest Service's advice is
  "As soon as you realize you may be lost: stop, stay calm, stay put."
  Then think back over how you got there, get out the map and compass to
  work out directions, and make a plan before you move. It says that if
  you are not very, very confident of the route, it is always better to
  stay put, and to stay in place if night is falling, you are hurt or you
  are near exhaustion. Staying put is also why telling someone your
  route matters: it is where they will look (our reasoning).

## What a map shows, and what it leaves out

A map cannot show everything at its true size, or it would be
unreadable. So it uses symbols, and the symbols are often far bigger
than the things they stand for. The Army manual gives examples from a
1:250,000 scale map: the symbol for a building covers an area about 500
feet square on the ground, and a road symbol is as wide as a road about
520 feet across. The map is telling you a building is there, not how big
it is.

The same manual separates two kinds of map that a beginner meets all the
time:

- **A planimetric map** shows only where things are, side to side. It
  leaves out the shape of the land. A road atlas, a street map and most
  phone maps in their plain setting are planimetric (our examples).
- **A topographic map** shows where things are and also the shape of the
  land, usually with contour lines. It is the map for walking, building
  and judging where water goes.

The USGS puts it simply: the distinctive characteristic of a topographic
map is the use of contour lines to show the shape of the Earth's
surface.

## Read the margin first

The Army manual compares a map to a piece of equipment: before you use
it, read the instructions. On a map the instructions are in the margin,
and they are different on every map. The items a beginner most needs:

- **The scale.** A ratio such as 1:24,000, and the bar scales, which are
  printed rulers. The manual notes that maps have three or more bar
  scales, each in a different unit, and says to take care to pick the
  unit you need.
- **The contour interval.** The height between neighbouring contour
  lines, with its unit. The USGS says the contour interval is always
  noted below the bar scale.
- **The date the map information was collected.** On a military map it
  is just below the word LEGEND. The manual says this date matters for
  how well the map will match the ground, and that the printing date
  should not be used to judge when the information was gathered. A map
  printed last year can carry information from decades ago.
- **The legend.** What each symbol means on this map. The manual is
  blunt that symbols are not always the same on every map, so read the
  legend every time you use a new one.
- **The declination diagram.** The angle between true north, grid north
  and magnetic north for this map, covered in the direction section
  below.
- **The vertical datum.** What the heights are measured from. The Army
  manual says that in the United States, Canada and Europe it is mean
  sea level, but that in parts of Asia and Africa it may be a local
  assumed height with no connection to sea level, so check the note on
  every map.

## Scale: turning map distance into ground distance

### The ratio

A scale of 1:24,000 means that one unit on the map is 24,000 of the same
units on the ground. It does not matter which unit: one centimetre on the
map is 24,000 centimetres (240 metres) on the ground, and one inch is
24,000 inches. The USGS puts it the familiar way for its standard
1:24,000 maps: one inch on the map represents 2,000 feet on the ground.

To get a ground distance, measure on the map and multiply by the second
number. The Army manual's example: at 1:50,000, five units on the map is
5 x 50,000 = 250,000 units on the ground.

**Worked example.** On a 1:24,000 USGS map you measure 3.5 cm between a
trailhead and a lake. 3.5 x 24,000 = 84,000 cm, which is 840 metres. On
a 1:50,000 map the same 3.5 cm would be 1,750 metres. (Arithmetic.)

The words confuse people, so the manual spells them out: the larger the
number after the 1:, the smaller the scale. A 1:1,000,000 map is "small
scale": it covers a huge area with little detail. A 1:24,000 map is
"large scale": a small area in detail. If you want to see individual
streams and the shape of each hill, you want the larger scale.

**If a map has no scale**, you can work one out. Measure the distance
between two points on the map, find the real distance between the same
two points on the ground (by pacing, or from another map), and divide.
The manual's example: 4.32 cm on the map and 2.16 km (216,000 cm) on the
ground gives 216,000 / 4.32 = 50,000, so the scale is 1:50,000. Use the
same unit for both measurements before you divide.

### The bar scale

The bar scale is a ruler printed on the map, and for most work it is
easier than the arithmetic. The manual's method:

1. Lay the straight edge of a piece of paper on the map so it touches
   both points.
2. Make a tick mark on the paper at each point.
3. Move the paper to the bar scale. Put the right-hand tick on a whole
   number in the main part of the scale so that the left-hand tick falls
   in the finer part to the left of zero, which the manual calls the
   extension scale.
4. Read the whole number, then read the extension scale **from right to
   left**, and add the two.

For a winding road or stream, the manual pivots the paper: mark the
start, lay the edge along the first straight stretch, mark where the
road leaves the edge, hold that mark with a pencil point and swing the
paper to line up with the next stretch, and carry on. Only the first and
last ticks are measured.

**A copied or zoomed map keeps its bar scale and loses its ratio.** If a
map is photocopied at a different size, or shown on a screen at any
zoom, the printed ratio is no longer true, but the bar scale grew or
shrank along with everything else and still measures correctly. That is
our reasoning rather than a quoted rule, and it is why a phone map shows
a bar and not a ratio.

## Colours and symbols

Colours do most of the work on a topographic map. The Army manual lists
the usual meanings on a large-scale map, and warns that they may vary
from map to map:

| Colour | What it usually shows |
|---|---|
| Black | Man-made features such as buildings and roads, surveyed spot heights, and labels |
| Blue | Water: lakes, rivers, swamps and drainage |
| Green | Vegetation such as woods and orchards |
| Brown (red-brown on newer military maps) | Relief: the shape of the land, including contour lines |
| Red | Main roads, built-up areas and boundaries, on older maps |

The USGS describes its own maps the same way: contours in brown, streams
and other water in blue, roads in black and red. It adds an example that
matters outdoors: a perennial stream is a solid blue line and an
intermittent stream is a blue dashed and dotted line.

When the legend and this table disagree, the legend wins.

## Contour lines: height on flat paper

The USGS defines contour lines as imaginary lines connecting points of
the same elevation, usually measured from mean sea level. Walk along a
contour line on the ground and you would neither climb nor descend.

### The three kinds of line

The Army manual describes three:

- **Index contours.** Every fifth line, drawn heavier, and usually
  labelled somewhere with its height.
- **Intermediate contours.** The finer lines between index lines,
  normally four of them, unlabelled.
- **Supplementary contours.** Dashed lines at half the interval, used on
  fairly flat ground where the normal lines would be far apart.

### Reading a height

1. Find the **contour interval** and its unit in the margin. The manual
   insists on the unit: feet, metres or yards.
2. Find the nearest **labelled index line**.
3. Count the lines between it and your point, and **add or subtract the
   interval** for each one, depending on whether you are going uphill or
   down.

The manual's example: the index line is labelled 500 metres, the interval
is 20 metres, and the point sits on the second line above it. Its height
is 500 + 20 + 20 = 540 metres.

Three refinements from the same manual:

- **The top of a hill** is above the highest closed contour. Add half
  the interval: inside a 600 metre ring with a 20 metre interval, call
  the top about 610 metres.
- **A point between two lines**: if it is less than a quarter of the way
  across, give it the lower line's height; between a quarter and three
  quarters, add half the interval; more than three quarters, give it the
  upper line's height.
- **A depression** (a hollow with no outlet) is drawn as closed contour
  lines with small tick marks pointing down into it. The bottom is about
  half an interval below the lowest ring.

### Steepness

The rule both the USGS and the Army state: **contour lines close
together mean steep ground; lines far apart mean gentle ground.** The
USGS adds that a fairly flat area may have a 10 foot interval while
steep country may need 100 feet or more, just to keep the lines from
merging. So two maps can look equally crowded and describe very
different hills. Always check the interval before you judge a slope by
eye.

To put a number on a slope, use the rise and the run:

**Worked example.** On a map with a 10 metre interval, your route crosses
six contour lines going uphill in 300 metres of map distance. The rise is
6 x 10 = 60 metres. The slope is 60 / 300 = 0.2, which is 20 percent. The
Army manual gives a quick way to get degrees: multiply the rise by 57.3
and divide by the run, which it says is reasonably accurate for slopes
under 20 degrees. 60 x 57.3 / 300 is about 11.5 degrees. (The exact
angle, from the tangent, is 11.3 degrees.) [Square, Level and
Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md) explains ratios, percent and degrees
for slopes at home.

### The shape of a slope

Evenly spaced lines mean an even slope. Uneven spacing tells you how the
slope changes, and the Army manual names the two cases:

- **Concave**: lines close together at the top and wide apart at the
  bottom. Steep up high, easing off below. From the top you can see the
  whole slope.
- **Convex**: lines wide apart at the top and close together at the
  bottom. Gentle up high, steepening below. The manual's description is
  that from the top of a convex slope you cannot see most of the slope or
  the ground at its foot.

The convex slope is the one to respect. Walking down it, the ground gets
steeper as you go, and the steepest part is the part you could not see
when you set off. Plan the way down from the map, not from what the top
looks like. (That safety reading is ours; the shape and what can be seen
from the top are the manual's.)

## The shapes contour lines make

The Army manual teaches the land as a small set of features, each with a
recognisable pattern. Learn these and a contour map stops being a tangle.

| Feature | On the ground | On the map |
|---|---|---|
| Hill | High ground sloping down on all sides | Closed rings, the smallest one is the top |
| Saddle | A dip between two higher areas: high ground two ways, low ground the other two | An hourglass shape |
| Valley | A long groove, usually with water in it: high ground three ways | U or V shaped lines whose closed end points **upstream**, toward high ground |
| Ridge | A sloping line of high ground: low ground three ways | U or V shaped lines whose closed end points **away** from high ground |
| Depression | A hollow, low ground with higher ground all round | Closed rings with tick marks pointing down into it |
| Draw | A smaller stream course than a valley, with little or no level ground: a valley in the making | U or V lines pointing toward high ground |
| Spur | A short finger of high ground running off a ridge | U or V lines pointing away from high ground |
| Cliff | A vertical or near-vertical drop | Lines that touch, or merge into one line with tick marks pointing toward the low side |

The one rule worth memorising from that table: **where contour lines
cross a stream, they bend into a V or U that points upstream.** The
manual puts it as the closed end pointing upstream, toward high ground.
So you can tell which way water flows from contours alone, even where no
stream is drawn.

The manual also offers a way to compare what you see with what is on the
map, which it calls SOSES: the **shape** of a feature at its base, its
**orientation** (end on, side on or at an angle to you), its **size**,
its **elevation** compared with the features around it, and its
**slope**. Matching two or three of these at once is far more reliable
than matching one.

## Direction

### Three norths

The Army manual names three, and a map reader has to keep them apart:

- **True north**: toward the North Pole, along the lines of longitude.
- **Magnetic north**: where the north end of a compass needle points.
- **Grid north**: the direction of the vertical grid lines printed on
  the map.

On most maps, north is at the top. Check the declination diagram or the
margin to be sure (general practice).

### Bearings

The manual's term is azimuth: a direction measured in degrees
**clockwise** from north, from 0 to 360. East is 90, south 180, west
270.

The way back along a route is the **back azimuth**: add 180 degrees if
the bearing is 180 or less, subtract 180 if it is more. A bearing of 60
has a back bearing of 240; a bearing of 300 has a back bearing of 120.
The manual prints a warning box over this: a simple arithmetic mistake
here can have disastrous consequences. Check the sum twice.

### The declination diagram

The angle between magnetic north and true or grid north is the
declination. The Army manual says the diagram in the margin shows the
three norths as prongs that are **seldom drawn to scale**, so do not
measure the angle off the drawing: read the number printed beside it.
If you ignore the difference, it says, every bearing you carry between
map and ground will be wrong by the size of that angle.

Two things the diagram cannot tell you:

- **It is out of date.** Declination changes from year to year, and the
  number on a map was correct when the map was made. NOAA's National
  Centers for Environmental Information say the magnetic field changes
  with both location and time, which is why its models are revised at
  least every five years. Look up the current value for where you are.
  [The Sky as an Instrument](the_sky_as_an_instrument.md) works through
  how large the error is, using NOAA's calculator, and how to check a
  compass against the sky.
- **It is for the whole map sheet, not your exact spot.** NOAA says that
  a compass points along the local magnetic field, not at any single
  point, and that "A mile or two away the magnetic declination may be
  considerably different, requiring a different correction." It also
  notes that rare local anomalies of more than 10 degrees exist.

### Keep the compass away from metal

A compass reads any magnetism near it. The Army manual says nonmagnetic
metals do not affect it, and suggests these separation distances for
things that do:

| Keep the compass at least this far from | Distance |
|---|---|
| High-tension power lines | 55 metres |
| A truck (the manual's list is a field gun, truck or tank) | 18 metres |
| Telephone wires and barbed wire | 10 metres |
| A steel helmet or rifle | Half a metre |

At home the same applies to a car, a steel fence, a stove or a phone
(general practice). The manual also says to check a compass now and then
against a known direction, and not to use one that is more than 3
degrees out.

## Orienting the map

The manual defines an oriented map as one lying flat with its north and
south matching north and south on the ground. Once it is oriented, the
road that runs left on the map runs left in front of you, and matching
the two becomes much easier. There are two ways.

**With a compass.** In short: lay the edge of the compass along a
north-south line on the map, then turn the map and compass together
until the needle points to magnetic north as the declination diagram
shows it, which is the map's grid north turned by the declination angle.
The manual's two step-by-step methods are more precise; the key points
are that the compass measures magnetic directions, so the declination
must be allowed for, and that once the map is oriented you must not move
it, or it is no longer lined up. The manual adds that a small mistake in
orienting can send you in the wrong direction.

**By the land.** If you know roughly where you are, turn the map until
the features on it line up with the features you can see: the road, the
stream, the ridge, the far hill. The manual calls this terrain
association. It needs no compass, but it does need you to know your
approximate position first.

## Knowing where you are, and staying found

The Army manual says that if you set off without first knowing where you
are, everything after that is a gamble. So the first job is always to
fix your starting point on the map: a road junction, a bridge, a
summit.

From there, two methods, which work best together.

**By the land (terrain association).** You compare what the map says you
should see with what you do see, all the way. The manual calls this more
forgiving of mistakes than travelling by compass bearing alone, because
an error shows up as soon as the ground stops matching the map. It
offers three aids:

- **Handrails**: long features that run the way you are going, such as
  a road, a power line, a ridge or a stream. Keep one on your left or
  right and follow it.
- **Catching features**: something you cannot miss that tells you to
  turn, or that you have gone too far: a stream across your path, a road,
  the edge of a field.
- **An attack point**: an easy-to-find feature close to your goal, from
  which you go the last stretch carefully by bearing and pace count. The
  manual says 500 metres or less is most desirable.

**By bearing and distance (dead reckoning).** You follow a compass
bearing and count paces. The manual's warning is that errors add up the
farther you go without checking against something on the ground, so
confirm your position at every known feature and start the pace count
again from there. [Estimating](estimating.md) explains how to calibrate
and keep a pace count.

Two route-planning tips from the manual worth keeping:

- **The best checkpoints are long features that cross your route**, such
  as streams, roads, ridges, valleys and power lines, and then features
  with a height change of at least two contour intervals, such as hills
  and spurs. Do not rely mainly on other man-made features or on
  vegetation, which are the most likely to have changed.
- **Aim off on purpose.** If your goal is on a long feature that crosses
  your path, such as a road or a stream, the manual suggests aiming about
  10 degrees to one side. When you reach the feature, you know which way
  to turn. Aim straight at it and, when you do not see it, you will not
  know whether it is left or right.

## Worked example: planning a walk on paper

An illustration with made-up numbers, to show the steps in order.

You want to walk from a trailhead at a road junction to a small lake on
a 1:24,000 USGS map with a 20 foot contour interval.

1. **Margin.** Scale 1:24,000, contour interval 20 feet. The map is a
   few years old, so treat any trail drawn on it as a suggestion.
2. **Distance.** A paper strip along the trail measures 9.5 cm.
   9.5 x 24,000 = 228,000 cm, about 2.3 km on the map. Allow more on the
   ground for the climb.
3. **Height.** The trailhead sits just above the 1,200 foot index line.
   The lake is inside the 1,440 foot contour. The climb is about 240 feet,
   twelve lines. The lines bunch together in one stretch of about 400
   metres in the middle: that is the steep part.
4. **Shape.** Above the steep stretch the lines spread out again, so
   from the lake end the slope is gentle at the top and steep further
   down: convex. On the way back you will start down the gentle part and
   will not see the steep stretch until you reach it, so note on the
   map where it begins.
5. **Handrail and catching feature.** A stream drawn as a solid blue
   line runs beside the trail for the first kilometre. The contours bend
   upstream, toward the lake, so you walk upstream. Where the stream
   turns away, the trail crosses a draw: that is your cue to turn.
6. **Attack point.** A saddle about 300 metres from the lake. From the
   saddle, the lake is up the gentle slope to the north-east.
7. **Back azimuth.** If the bearing from the saddle to the lake is 50
   degrees, the bearing back to the saddle is 230.
8. **Tell someone** the route and when you will be back.

## Getting a real map

In the United States, the USGS publishes the standard topographic maps.
Its current series is US Topo: maps at 1:24,000, each covering 7.5
minutes of latitude by 7.5 minutes of longitude, free to download. The
USGS lists three free download tools (topoView, The National Map
Download Client, and the Map Locator in the USGS Store), and the USGS
Store is where to order a printed sheet.

Two things the USGS says about how current they are:

- US Topo maps are updated on a **three-year cycle**, a third of the
  country each year, with the goal of each map being current to within
  three years when published. It adds that there is considerable
  variation in how current individual features actually are.
- Older USGS maps, published before 2006, show some features such as
  trails, buildings and survey points that the newer maps are still
  adding over time. An old sheet and a new sheet of the same place can
  each show things the other does not.

For trails in a park or forest, the land manager's own map is often more
up to date than either (general practice). Outside the United States,
the national mapping agency of the country is the equivalent source
(general practice).

**Take care of the paper.** The Army manual says to carry a map where it
is protected from water, mud and tearing, such as a waterproof case, and
to mark it only lightly in pencil, so marks can be rubbed out without
smearing.

## GPS and phone maps

A GPS receiver or a phone tells you where you are without any of the
skills above, which is exactly why the skills matter when it stops. The
Park Service's advice is to bring a paper map as a back-up and an extra
battery, and to know how to use the map before you need it. The Army
manual notes that a GPS receiver has no inherent drift and updates its
own position, and describes it in the same chapter as the compass, as
one more piece of navigation equipment.

Practise reading the paper map on walks where the phone is working, so
that you can check one against the other and learn where you go wrong
(general practice).

## Know where map reading stops

- **Legal boundaries.** A topographic map is not a survey. Where exactly
  your land ends is a question for the deed and a licensed land surveyor
  (general practice).
- **What is underground.** No map you can buy shows buried pipes and
  cables reliably (general practice). Before any digging in the United
  States, the Pipeline and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration says
  to call 811 so that the lines can be marked; the details are in
  [Square, Level and Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md).
- **Dangerous terrain.** Avalanche slopes, glaciers, fast water and
  climbing routes need training and local knowledge that a map cannot
  give (general practice).
- **Somebody is missing.** Call the emergency services. Do not wait to
  work it out from the map (general practice).

## How the game models it

The game has one real map you can read, on the **Maps** page, which
opens on its Solar System view. Its first tab, **Planet**, draws real
places on Earth.

- **Two real places.** The game ships two regions, Seattle Center and
  Silverdale, Washington, built from OpenStreetMap data: roads, the
  outlines of buildings, and water. The files are in
  `data/maps/regions/`, and the map credits OpenStreetMap at the bottom,
  as its licence requires.
- **North is up.** The view is drawn with north at the top. You can zoom
  with the scroll wheel and drag to move.
- **A bar scale, not a ratio.** In the corner is a scale bar labelled
  with a round length, from 10 metres up to 5 kilometres, which changes
  as you zoom. That is the bar scale of this guide, and the reason a
  screen map shows a bar rather than a ratio. Try measuring a road on
  screen with the edge of a piece of paper and the bar.
- **Where you are on the planet.** The line at the bottom gives the
  region's name and the latitude and longitude of the point the region
  is drawn around.
- **Names.** Hold the pointer over a road to see its name. Names of
  water show at the starting zoom, and street names appear as you zoom
  in.
- **The same places in the world.** The same two regions are also built
  into the game's 3D Earth, at their real latitude and longitude, once
  the camera comes within about 40 kilometres of them.

What the game does not model, so you do not learn it from the game: the
Planet view is a **planimetric** map in the Army manual's sense. It has
no contour lines and no ground heights (only building outlines are
shaded by height, taller ones lighter), no legend or margin, no declination
diagram and no grid. The game has a Paper Map and a Compass in its item
list, and a compass can be crafted at a workbench, but neither does
anything yet: carrying them shows no map and no bearing. The bar at the
top of the screen shows compass letters as you turn; this guide has not
checked whether its N matches true north on a planet's surface, so do
not use it to practise declination. The game has a Navigation skill, but
nothing in the game levels it yet.

## You own this when

- You tell someone your route and return time, and carry a paper map as
  well as a phone.
- You read the margin of a new map before anything else: scale, contour
  interval and its unit, the date of the information, and the legend.
- You can turn a measurement on the map into a ground distance with the
  ratio and with the bar scale, including along a winding road.
- You can read the height of a point, a hilltop and a hollow from the
  contour lines.
- You can tell a steep slope from a gentle one, and a convex slope from
  a concave one, before you are standing on it.
- You can pick out a hill, saddle, valley, ridge, spur, draw, depression
  and cliff, and say which way a stream flows from the contours alone.
- You can give a bearing clockwise from north, work out its back
  bearing, and explain why the declination on an old map is not today's.
- You can orient a map by compass and by the land, and you keep the
  compass away from metal.
- You plan routes with handrails, catching features and an attack
  point, and you know where you are all the way.
