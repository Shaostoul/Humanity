# Firewood

Three decisions make up this whole subject: **how much wood you need, what
it is, and when you cut it.** Everything else is consequence. Get the
quantity wrong and you run out in February. Get the moisture wrong and you
burn twice the wood for half the heat and line your chimney with tar. Get
the species wrong and you have merely wasted effort, which is the mildest
failure here.

So, the short answer, and the rest of this guide is why.

**How much.** The Forest Products Laboratory puts the energy used to heat
an average-sized home at between 50 and 150 million Btu over a heating
season, and rates seasoned firewood at about 15.3 million Btu of *usable*
heat per cord once stove losses are taken off. Divide one by the other:
**roughly 3 to 10 cords a year**, with their own worked example of a
100-million-Btu season coming out at 6.5 cords. Six cords stacked four
feet high and sixteen inches deep is about 144 feet of wall. Go and look
at 144 feet of anything before you decide you have enough wood.

**Of what.** Locally, Douglas-fir, with bigleaf maple its equal and red
alder its easy-splitting understudy. Western redcedar is the one species
here you should mostly *not* burn for heat, for reasons in the species
section.

**Cut when.** As far ahead as you can stand. A year ahead is the working
answer; the real answer is whenever a moisture meter says the wood is
under 20 percent, and nothing else.

Where a number appears below it comes from a publication that measured
it, listed in the Sources at the bottom. Where something is a working
rule rather than a measured value, it says so.

Two things this guide deliberately does not do. It **does not teach tree
felling**, which is the part of firewood that kills people, and it says so
again with more force further down. And it does not repeat the companion
guides: species properties, moisture content and decay are in
[Working with Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md); axe and saw filing are in
[Sharpening](../making/sharpening.md); the heat you are trying to keep in
the building is in [Insulation and Heat Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md);
and the air the fire needs, plus the full treatment of carbon monoxide, is
in [Ventilation, Damp and Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md). That last
pairing matters more than it sounds and has its own section here.

## The unit problem, which is where people get cheated

Start here, because this is the one part of the subject with a legal
answer, and it is the part most buyers do not know.

**A cord is a defined volume.** NIST Handbook 130, the model regulation
that state weights-and-measures agencies adopt, defines it at section
2.4.1.2 as the amount of wood contained in a space of 128 cubic feet when
the wood is ranked, with individual pieces touching and parallel to each
other and stacked in a compact manner. Four feet by four feet by eight
feet. The words "ranked" and "compact" are doing work: a heaped pile of
the same wood occupies more space and is not a cord.

**Firewood may only be sold by that measure.** Section 2.4.3 requires
fireplace and stove wood to be advertised, offered for sale and sold only
by the cord or the cubic metre. The exceptions are narrow: packaged
natural wood under 0.45 cubic metres, which is an eighth of a cord or 16
cubic feet, is declared in litres; compressed or processed logs and bricks
over 6 inches in any dimension go by weight plus count; pellets and chips
under 6 inches go by weight.

**The informal units are prohibited outright.** Section 2.4.4 is titled
Prohibition of Terms and states that the listed terms shall not be used
when advertising, offering for sale, or selling wood for use as fuel. The
Connecticut Department of Consumer Protection, restating the same model
regulation as its state rule, gives the list as face cord, rack, pile,
truckload, or terms of similar import.

Here is why that ban exists, in one table. The only sourced row is the
first; the rest is arithmetic on the cord definition, using piece lengths
and a truck bed I chose for illustration.

| What you are offered | The stack it describes | Cubic feet | Fraction of a cord |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cord | 4 ft x 4 ft x 8 ft, ranked | 128 | 1 |
| "Face cord" or "rick" of 12 inch pieces | 4 x 8 ft face, 1 ft deep | 32 | one quarter |
| "Face cord" or "rick" of 16 inch pieces | 4 x 8 ft face, 16 in deep | 42.7 | one third |
| "Face cord" or "rick" of 24 inch pieces | 4 x 8 ft face, 2 ft deep | 64 | one half |
| Level pickup load, 8 ft bed, 4 ft usable width, 1.5 ft sides | 8 x 4 x 1.5 ft | 48 | about three eighths |

A face cord is not a unit. It is a face of unknown depth, and the same
words can mean a quarter of a cord or half of one depending on a number
nobody said out loud. **A "truckload" is worth even less as a promise,**
because it describes a vehicle rather than a volume: the level pickup
load above is about three eighths of a cord, and a heaped one is a
guess.

**What you are owed on delivery.** Section 2.4.5 requires a delivery
ticket or sales invoice whenever any non-packaged firewood is sold, and it
must carry the vendor's name and address, the purchaser's name and
address, the date delivered, the quantity delivered and the quantity the
price is based on if those differ, the price, and the identity of the wood
in the most descriptive terms commercially practicable, including any
quality claim made in the sale.

**What you are owed on species.** Section 2.4.2 allows a seller to declare
a species group, giving the regulation's own example of 50 percent hickory
and 50 percent miscellaneous softwood, and requires that declaration to be
accurate within 10 percent. So "mixed hardwood" that arrives as
cottonwood is not a matter of opinion.

The practical move: buy by the cord, get it stacked or stack it yourself
before the truck leaves, and measure the stack. Four by four by eight. If
the seller will not sell by the cord, they are not offering you a unit.

## Moisture content decides everything else

If you take one number from this guide, take this one: **under 20
percent.** EPA's Burn Wise programme states it plainly, that wood burns
best at a moisture content of less than 20 percent, and tells you not to
burn wet or green wood at all.

Here is what that is worth, from the Forest Products Laboratory's Fuel
Value Calculator. Gross heating value is the energy in the fuel; the
efficiency column is what a typical appliance gets out of it; the last
column is what you actually receive.

| Condition | Moisture content | Gross, million Btu per ton | Efficiency | Usable, million Btu per ton |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Green | 50% | 8.60 | 67% | 5.74 |
| Semidried | 30% | 12.04 | 74% | 8.95 |
| Air-dried | 20% | 13.76 | 77% | 10.56 |
| Ovendry | 0% | 17.20 | 80% | 13.80 |

Read the first and third rows together. **A ton of air-dried wood
delivers 84 percent more usable heat than a ton of green wood.** Same
tree, same species, same truck. And you paid to haul the difference,
because, as the same publication says, water commonly makes up half the
weight of a living tree.

Two separate mechanisms are stacked in that table, and it is worth pulling
them apart because they are fixed by the same action for different
reasons.

The larger one is simply **mass**. Green wood at 50 percent moisture on a
wet basis is half water by weight, so a ton of it contains half a ton of
fuel, and the gross column duly halves from 17.20 to 8.60. You are paying
to cut, split, stack, carry and haul water.

The smaller one is the **efficiency** column, which drops from 80 percent
ovendry to 67 percent green, a further penalty of about a sixth. That is
the latent heat of vaporisation: the fire must boil the water out of the
wood before the wood will burn. FPL is explicit that in the operation of
heating boilers the flue gas temperature is generally kept high enough to
prevent the water vapour condensing, and that the latent heat of
vaporisation is therefore **not available for making steam, hot water or
useful heat.** It goes up the chimney. You never get it back.

### The two different 20 percents

This trips people up and it is worth thirty seconds. Moisture content can
be expressed on a **dry basis**, as water weight divided by the wood's
ovendry weight, which is the convention in the Wood Handbook and in the
moisture meter you will buy; it is why green wood can read over 100
percent. Or it can be expressed on a **wet basis**, as water divided by
total weight, which the Fuel Value Calculator above uses and says so.

Converting between them is one line of arithmetic. Twenty percent on a
dry basis is 16.7 percent wet; twenty percent wet is 25 percent dry. So
the FPL "20 percent" row is slightly wetter wood than the EPA "under 20
percent" target that your meter is reading. The gap is small and both
point the same way, but knowing which convention a number uses is the
difference between reading a table and guessing at it.

### What wet wood does to the chimney

Smoke is unburned fuel. A fire that is boiling water is a fire that is not
hot enough to burn its own smoke, so the unburned fraction goes up the
flue, meets surfaces cooler than itself, and plates out. The Consumer
Product Safety Commission describes the result exactly: creosote, a tarry
substance condensed inside the chimney and chimney connectors.

That is the whole causal chain, and it is why moisture is a safety topic
rather than an economy topic. Wet wood makes smoke; smoke makes creosote;
creosote is what chimney fires burn. The creosote section below has the
numbers.

## Seasoning: how long, stacked how, covered how

**How long is the wrong question and everybody asks it anyway.** The
honest answer is that "a year" is a rule of thumb that happens to work in
most places. EPA says to season wood for at least six months. Washington's
Department of Ecology, which is writing for this climate specifically,
says to use wood that has been split, covered and stacked for at least one
year. USDA APHIS, surveying the trade for a different purpose entirely,
reports that seasoned wood has generally been air dried for six months to
a year, though wood cut in winter and dried under good conditions may be
considered seasoned after three months.

Three federal and state bodies, three different answers, all correct,
because drying time depends on species, piece size, climate and how the
stack is built. **The real answer is a moisture meter**, which EPA
recommends by name and which costs less than a wheelbarrow. Split a piece,
press the probes into the freshly exposed face, and read it. The outside
of a stacked round tells you nothing; the inside is what burns.

**Split it first, and split it soon.** Bark is the tree's own weather
seal and it works after death. Splitting opens the wet interior to the air
and multiplies the exposed surface. Green wood also splits more willingly
than seasoned wood, so the work is easier now than later.

**Stack it off the ground, and stack it single-row.** EPA's instruction is
to store wood outdoors, off the ground, with only the top covered, because
covering the sides traps moisture in. That last clause is the one people
get backwards: a tarp over the whole pile is a humidity tent. You want a
roof, not a bag. Off the ground matters for the same reason it matters in
[Working with Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md): serious decay needs
liquid water and wood above about 30 percent moisture content, and ground
contact is the most reliable way to supply both. Rot is fungi eating your
fuel before you do.

**Why the ends matter.** Wood is not equally permeable in all directions.
The Wood Handbook makes the point about water going in, that absorption is
most rapid in the longitudinal direction, meaning through the end grain,
and the same anisotropy governs water coming out. The ends are the
motorway; the sides are a footpath. Which gives three practical rules that
follow directly: cut to length before stacking rather than after, leave
the cut ends exposed to moving air rather than butted against each other
or against a wall, and expect the checks that open in the end grain as it
dries. Those checks are the wood telling you the ends are working.

**What this climate does to the ceiling.** Wood outdoors does not dry to
whatever you want; it equilibrates with the air. [Working with
Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md), quoting the Wood Handbook, gives
the outdoor equilibrium figures for Seattle as 12.0 percent in July rising
to 16.9 percent in December. So in western Washington, **under 20 percent
is achievable and 8 percent is not**, and a stack left uncovered through a
Silverdale winter climbs back toward 17 percent no matter how well it
dried in August. The locale's own rainfall record makes the same point
another way: December averages 252 mm of precipitation against 21 mm in
July. Cover the top, and bring a week's wood inside ahead of burning it.

## Heat by species, which is a density story

Here is the fact that dissolves most firewood folklore: **a pound of wood
is a pound of wood.** The Fuel Value Calculator gives ovendry wood a
single gross heating value of 17.2 million Btu per ton, about 8,600 Btu
per pound, with no species column at all. The Wood Handbook makes the
parallel point about moisture behaviour, that for most practical purposes
its equilibrium moisture content values may be applied to wood of any
species. The USDA APHIS firewood assessment says it from the trade side:
although some species are preferred based on characteristics like heat
production and splitting, all species of trees can be and are used as
firewood.

So the differences between species in a stove are not differences of
quality. **They are differences of density**, because a cord is a fixed
volume and denser wood puts more pounds of fuel into it.

The specific gravities below are from the Wood Handbook's Table 5-3b, at
12 percent moisture content, defined there as ovendry weight over volume
at that moisture content. The last two columns are my arithmetic on those
numbers: ovendry pounds per cubic foot is specific gravity times 62.4, a
cord is taken as holding about 80 cubic feet of solid wood, which is a
**rule of thumb** and not a measured value, and the heat is 8,600 Btu per
pound. The cross-check is reassuring: Douglas-fir comes out at 20.6
million Btu per cord against the Fuel Value Calculator's 20 million for
"seasoned firewood", so the rule of thumb is in the right place.

| Species | Specific gravity at 12% | Estimated million Btu per cord | Relative to Douglas-fir |
|---|---|---|---|
| Douglas-fir (Coast) | 0.48 | 20.6 | 1.00 |
| Bigleaf maple | 0.48 | 20.6 | 1.00 |
| Western hemlock | 0.45 | 19.3 | 0.94 |
| Red alder | 0.41 | 17.6 | 0.85 |
| Lodgepole pine | 0.41 | 17.6 | 0.85 |
| Sitka spruce | 0.40 | 17.2 | 0.83 |
| Grand fir | 0.37 | 15.9 | 0.77 |
| Black cottonwood | 0.35 | 15.0 | 0.73 |
| Western redcedar | 0.32 | 13.7 | 0.67 |

One naming note on that table: the shore pine that grows on this coast is
*Pinus contorta*, the same species as the handbook's lodgepole pine, in a
different variety. The row is close enough to use and not the same tree.

Notice the spread. From the best to the worst on that list is a factor of
about 1.5, not a factor of ten. A cord of cedar is not worthless; it is
two thirds of a cord of Douglas-fir, and it will heat your house if that
is what you have. The folklore that treats softwood as unburnable is
wrong, and it is wrong in a way the table makes obvious.

How to read that table for what is actually in these woods:

**Split for heat: Douglas-fir and bigleaf maple.** Tied at the top and
both abundant here. Douglas-fir is the default firewood of this region for
the straightforward reason that it is the region's dominant timber tree
and there is always some coming down.

**Split for heat, but burn it sooner: western hemlock.** Ninety four
percent of Douglas-fir's heat, which is fine. The catch is not in this
table but in the companion guide's: hemlock heartwood is rated slightly or
nonresistant to decay, so a hemlock stack that gets rained on is
composting. Cover it and burn it within the year.

**Split for heat, and enjoy the work: red alder.** Eighty five percent,
which is a real but modest penalty, in exchange for a wood that splits
cleanly, seasons fast and grows back everywhere a disturbance happens.
The locale species record notes that red alder coals are the standard wood
for smoking salmon in this region, which is a second use for the same
woodpile.

**Do not split for heat: western redcedar.** It is last on the table at
two thirds, but that is not the argument. The argument is that cedar is
the only species in this region whose heartwood the Wood Handbook rates
decay-resistant, and it has the least shrinkage of the lot, which is why
it is the wood for siding, shakes, fence boards, raised beds and anything
that has to sit outside and stay flat. **Burning structural cedar is
burning the most valuable board in the woods to make the least heat in the
table.** What cedar is genuinely excellent at is kindling: it splits into
thin straight sticks along the grain and lights from a match. Two working
cautions that are trade knowledge rather than sourced findings: cedar
snaps and throws sparks as it burns, so keep the screen closed, and cedar
dust is a respiratory hazard, which [Working with
Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) covers under the same NIOSH entry as
wood dust generally.

**Take it if it is down, do not go looking: black cottonwood, grand fir.**
Three quarters of Douglas-fir, wet and stringy when green, perfectly
serviceable once dry. A lot of handling per unit of heat.

**Oregon white oak is the exception to all of the above.** The locale
species record describes it as a hard, heavy wood used for fuel and tool
handles, which by the density logic would make it the best firewood here.
It is not in the Wood Handbook's table, so this guide gives it no number.
The same record describes it as local and patchy on the Kitsap Peninsula
rather than widespread, confined to dry prairie edges and open south
facing slopes, and it supports deer, squirrels and birds. Take it if a
tree is already down. Do not take one down for fuel.

## What a cord is worth against other fuels

Worth knowing before you commit a winter to it, because the answer is
lopsided in one direction and the catch is not in the price.

The Forest Products Laboratory's Fuel Value Calculator works the
comparison for a 100 million Btu heating season, at its own example
prices. **Those prices are illustrative and old, and every one of them has
moved**, so read the ordering rather than the dollars.

| Fuel | Appliance efficiency | Example price | Cost for the season |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seasoned firewood | 77% | $115 per cord | $747 |
| Natural gas | 80% | $7 per 1,000 cubic feet | $854 |
| Premium wood pellets | 83% | $120 per ton | $882 |
| Fuel oil #2 | 83% | $1.40 per gallon | $1,220 |
| Propane | 79% | $1.25 per gallon | $1,730 |
| Electricity | 98% | $0.08 per kWh | $2,390 |

FPL's own conclusion is that seasoned firewood is the least expensive
fuel, with natural gas and wood pellets a close second and third. And its
own caveat is the honest one: to decide whether firewood is the fuel of
choice you have to compare the heating systems too, and work out how much
time and effort you are willing to spend processing and handling the fuel,
because fossil fuel heating systems typically cost less up front, sometimes
significantly so, than firewood and wood pellet furnaces.

That is the shape of the trade. **Wood is cheap per Btu and expensive per
hour.** Nothing on that list except pellets asks you to cut, split, stack,
turn, carry and sweep. Six cords is not a purchase; it is a season of
weekends, and an oil tank is not.

For scale, the Energy Information Administration's 2012 survey found about
2.5 million United States households, 2.1 percent, using wood as their
main heating fuel, up from 1.7 percent in 2005, with a further 9 million
households, 7.7 percent, burning it as a secondary fuel. Together that is
roughly 500 trillion Btu a year, about the same as residential propane and
slightly less than fuel oil. Heating stoves are the commonest equipment
for households whose main heat is wood; fireplaces are the commonest for
secondary heat.

## Creosote and chimney fires

Fire is the ordinary failure of this whole subject, and the chimney is
where it happens.

The US Fire Administration's topical report on heating fires in
residential buildings puts the 2017 to 2019 average at **34,200 heating
fires a year in residential buildings, causing 165 deaths, 600 injuries
and $367 million in property loss**. Its separate figures for 2021 are
32,200 fires, 190 deaths, 625 injuries and $442 million. **Heating is the
second leading cause of all residential building fires, following
cooking**, and incidence peaks in January at 20 percent of the year's
total.

Now the number that matters for this section. In that same data,
**confined fires, meaning those confined to chimneys, flues or fuel
burners, accounted for 77 percent of residential building heating
fires**, and the report's own breakdown puts chimney or flue fires at
49.4 percent of the total against 27.2 percent for confined fuel burner
or boiler malfunctions. Just under half of every home heating fire in the
United States is a fire inside the chimney.

That is the whole case for the maintenance below, and it is unusual to get
a risk this concentrated and this cheap to fix. The other headline from
the same report belongs here too: in the *nonconfined* fires, the ones
that got out into the building, **the heat source was too close to
combustibles 27 percent of the time.** That is what the three foot
clearance rule exists to prevent, and it is worth reading as the second
half of this section: keep the fire inside the chimney, and keep the
building away from the fire.

**What creosote is and how it forms.** CPSC calls it a tarry substance
condensed inside chimneys and chimney connectors, and "condensed" is the
operative word. Smoke is unburned fuel in vapour and particle form. When
it touches flue surfaces below the temperature at which it stays airborne,
it deposits. So anything that makes more smoke, or a cooler flue, makes
more creosote.

**What actually reduces it**, in order of how much difference it makes:

1. **Burn dry wood.** This is most of it. See the section above: wet wood
   is smoke by definition, because the fire is spending its heat boiling
   water instead of burning the volatiles.
2. **Burn hot, not slow.** The classic mistake is damping a stove right
   down overnight for a long burn. A starved fire is an incompletely
   burning fire, and incompletely burned fuel is what ends up on the flue
   wall. It is also the same condition that produces carbon monoxide,
   which is the next section.
3. **Burn wood, and only wood.** USFA is direct: do not burn paper in your
   fireplace or wood stove. Paper, cardboard and rubbish burn fast, throw
   embers up the flue, and can ignite deposits already there. Treated,
   painted or salvaged construction lumber adds a chemistry problem to a
   fire problem.
4. **Have the chimney swept and inspected every year.** This one is not a
   preference. USFA says to have heating equipment and chimneys cleaned
   and inspected each year by a professional. CPSC says to have flues and
   chimneys inspected before each heating season for leakage and blockage
   by creosote or debris. CDC says to have the chimney checked or cleaned
   every year, and CDC is saying it for a different reason, which is the
   next section. Three federal agencies, three different remits, the same
   annual interval.

**And put the fire out before you sleep.** USFA's wording is exactly that:
put the fire out before you go to sleep or leave your home. CPSC adds the
matching rule about the other end of the burn, never to close the damper
or go to bed while the ashes are still warm, because an open damper helps
prevent the build-up of poisonous gases indoors.

**Ashes.** USFA: put ashes in a metal container with a lid, and place the
container outside at least 10 feet from your home. Not a cardboard box,
not a plastic bin, not the wheeled rubbish cart, and not on a wooden deck.
Ash holds live embers for days.

## Carbon monoxide, which is the part that kills

Everything above burns buildings. This burns people, quietly, in their
sleep.

Carbon monoxide is produced whenever carbon-based fuel burns. It is
colourless, odourless and tasteless, which is why USFA calls it the
invisible killer. CDC lists the early symptoms as headache, dizziness,
weakness, upset stomach, vomiting, chest pain and confusion, which is also
a description of winter flu, and that overlap is exactly the danger.

The numbers, from three agencies counting different things:

- **CDC**, on unintentional poisoning not linked to fires: more than 400
  Americans die each year, more than 100,000 visit an emergency
  department, and more than 14,000 are hospitalised.
- **USFA**: more than 150 people in the United States die every year from
  accidental non-fire-related carbon monoxide.
- **CPSC**, counting only heating systems, ranges and ovens, and water
  heaters: an average of about 85 deaths a year.

### The three ways a wood fire does it

**A blocked or damaged flue.** The exhaust has nowhere to go, so it comes
back into the room. A bird's nest, a collapsed liner, a chimney that was
never swept, or, as USFA specifically warns, a vent buried in snow or
debris. This is the failure mode the annual inspection above is really
for: the sweep is not only removing fuel for a chimney fire, they are
confirming the flue is open.

**A starved fire.** A fire damped down for a long slow burn is a fire
running short of oxygen, and incomplete combustion is what makes carbon
monoxide rather than carbon dioxide. The overnight damp-down that lays
creosote in your chimney is producing carbon monoxide at the same time, by
the same mechanism. Those are not two problems. They are one problem with
two symptoms.

**An unvented fire indoors.** CDC's rule is absolute: do not cook or burn
anything on a stove or fireplace that is not vented. A camp stove, a
charcoal grill, a patio heater or an unflued propane heater brought inside
during a power cut puts every combustion product into the room you are
breathing. This is the classic winter-storm death and it happens every
year.

### Where this meets the insulation guide

This is the pair those two documents form, and it is worth stating
directly.

[Insulation and Heat Loss](insulation_and_heat_loss.md) tells you to air
seal before you insulate, because air leakage is roughly a third of a
building's heat loss and sealing it is the cheapest comfort you will ever
buy. That advice is correct and you should follow it. But a wood stove
with a conventional flue is a **natural draft appliance**: it relies on
hot gas rising to pull exhaust up the chimney, and it takes the air it
needs from the room. Tighten the building around it, or add a powerful
range hood or an exhaust-only ventilation fan, and you can depressurise
the house enough that the flue reverses and the exhaust comes **down** the
chimney instead.

A leaky old house shrugs that off. A well-sealed one does not.
[Ventilation, Damp and Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md) has the full
treatment, including the fixes in order of how well they work and the
alarm requirements. The short version: if you air seal a house that has a
wood stove in it, get the combustion appliances checked afterwards, and
consider a dedicated outside air supply for the stove.

### Alarms

Not optional, and not the same device as a smoke alarm. USFA: install
carbon monoxide alarms in a central location outside each separate
sleeping area and on every level of your home. CDC: check the batteries
when the clocks change each spring and autumn, and **replace the alarm
itself following the manufacturer's instructions or every 5 years**. A
carbon monoxide sensor wears out on a schedule whether it has ever
detected anything or not.

If it sounds, get everyone outside into fresh air first, then call
emergency services from outside. Do not go back in to investigate.

## Wood smoke is a health problem, not a smell

It is easy to treat smoke as a nuisance, a thing neighbours complain
about. The evidence says otherwise, and the affected people are not
abstract.

EPA is direct that the biggest health threat from smoke is fine
particles, also called fine particulate matter or PM2.5. Those particles
are small enough to reach the eyes and the deep respiratory system, and
EPA attributes to them burning eyes, runny nose, and illnesses such as
bronchitis; the ability to make asthma symptoms worse and trigger asthma
attacks; and the ability to trigger heart attacks, stroke, irregular heart
rhythms and heart failure, especially in people already at risk. Wood
smoke also carries benzene, formaldehyde, acrolein and polycyclic aromatic
hydrocarbons.

EPA's list of who is at greater risk is worth reading slowly, because it
is probably a description of somebody on your street: children, teenagers
and older adults; people with lung disease including asthma and COPD;
people with heart disease; outdoor workers; people of low socioeconomic
status including people who are homeless; people with obesity or diabetes;
and pregnant women.

This is not an argument against heating with wood. It is the argument for
doing it properly, and the single biggest lever is the same one as
everywhere else in this guide: **dry wood, burned hot, in a certified
appliance, makes very little smoke.** EPA says exactly that, that dry
seasoned wood creates very little smoke and that the more efficiently you
burn, the less smoke is created.

### Burn bans

Where enough households burn wood in a valley that holds air still, the
smoke becomes a regional air quality problem and the response is a burn
ban. In western Washington, the Puget Sound Clean Air Agency holds the
authority to issue air quality burn bans for King, Kitsap, Pierce and
Snohomish counties, and describes wood smoke as a main contributor to
harmful fine particle pollution. Restating its two stages in our own
words, because a state or regional agency's text is not public domain:

- **Stage 1** stops all outdoor burning and all burning in wood-burning
  fireplaces, uncertified wood stoves and uncertified fireplace inserts.
  A certified stove may still be used, but it may not produce visible
  smoke.
- **Stage 2** stops burning in *any* wood-burning fireplace, wood stove or
  fireplace insert, certified or not, and in pellet stoves as well.

Both stages have an exemption for a household whose wood heat is its only
adequate source of heat, and the Washington Department of Ecology
describes the same structure statewide. Two practical consequences: **the
exemption has to be applied for and approved before a ban is called**, not
claimed during one, and if wood is genuinely your only heat, that
application is a piece of paperwork worth doing in September rather than
January. Ecology also notes that selling an uncertified wood stove is
illegal in Washington, although removing one is not required when a house
changes hands.

Find out how your local agency announces bans and sign up for the alert
before you need it.

## Getting the wood: the part that hurts people

### Felling

**This guide does not teach tree felling and you should not learn it from
a document.** That is a hard boundary, the same one
[Building a Fire Staff](../making/fire_staff_materials.md) draws around
fire spinning. Dropping a tree is a skill with a fatal failure mode, it is
learned in person from someone competent, and no amount of reading
substitutes. What follows is only enough to tell you what competence looks
like, so you can recognise its absence.

Federal workplace regulation, OSHA 29 CFR 1910.266, is the closest thing
to a public-domain statement of the standard of care, and four of its
provisions tell you most of what you need to know about why this is
dangerous.

**The dead standing tree is the most dangerous one to take.** The
regulation defines a **danger tree** as a standing tree that presents a
hazard due to conditions such as deterioration or physical damage to the
root system, trunk, stem or limbs, and the direction and lean of the tree.
It requires the employer to fell, remove or avoid each one; if it is
neither felled nor removed it must be marked, and **no work may be
conducted within two tree lengths of it.** Before felling, a danger tree
must be carefully checked for loose bark, broken branches and limbs, and
other damage.

Read that against the instinct that says a dead tree is the polite one to
cut because it is already dead. The dead tree is the one with rotten hinge
wood that will not steer, loose bark that will not hold a wedge, and
broken limbs waiting overhead. The regulation's answer to a danger tree is
to keep two tree lengths away from it.

**You must have a planned escape route before you start.** The regulation
requires the feller to plan and clear a retreat path before felling
begins, extending diagonally away from the expected felling line. Not
straight back, which is where the butt goes; diagonally.

**The cuts are specified because guessing at them kills.** An undercut in
each tree, sized so the tree will not split and will fall in the intended
direction, and a backcut leaving sufficient hinge wood to hold the tree to
the stump during most of its fall so the hinge can guide it. The hinge is
the steering. A tree cut through is a tree nobody is steering.

**Nobody works alone.** The regulation requires each employee to work
within visual or audible contact with another employee, and requires that
no one approach closer than two tree lengths to a tree being felled until
the feller says it is safe. A first-aid kit is required at each site where
trees are being cut.

If you are going to fell trees, get trained. Until then, cut wood that is
already on the ground, and stay two tree lengths away from anything
standing that looks wrong.

### The chainsaw

The same regulation is unusually specific about chainsaws, and its list is
a good self-audit whether or not you are anyone's employee.

- **Leg protection of cut-resistant material, such as ballistic nylon,
  covering the full length of the thigh to the top of the boot on each
  leg.** This is the one piece of equipment people skip, and it is
  protecting the limb the saw is closest to.
- **Foot protection** that covers and supports the ankle, and for chainsaw
  operators, built of cut-resistant material.
- **Eye and head protection** where there is potential for injury from
  falling or flying objects, which with a chainsaw is always.
- **Start it on the ground or otherwise firmly supported, with the chain
  brake engaged. Drop starting is prohibited.** Start at least 10 feet
  from where you fuelled it.
- **Both hands, thumbs and fingers encircling the handles**, throughout
  operation.
- **Shut it down or engage the chain brake whenever you carry it further
  than 50 feet.**
- **Shut it off or release the throttle before you start your retreat.**

On kickback, which is the mechanism behind the worst of these injuries:
CPSC describes it as the sudden swift movement of the saw blade up and
back toward the user, and estimated 105,000 chainsaw injuries requiring
medical attention in 1979, of which about 24,000 were kickback injuries.
That data is decades old and saws have changed a great deal since, chain
brakes and low-kickback chain among the changes; CPSC found at the time
that a redesigned chain cut kickback energy by as much as 74 percent. The
number is dated. The mechanism is not.

### Splitting and bucking

Less dramatic than a chainsaw and a great deal more routine, which is
exactly why people relax around it. Three points, all of them consequences
of things taught elsewhere:

**A dull axe is the dangerous axe.** That is the whole thesis of
[Sharpening](../making/sharpening.md), which also gives the axe bevel,
the filing direction, and the reason never to put an axe on a high-speed
dry bench grinder. This guide will not re-teach it.

**Split against something that will not throw the piece back.** A
chopping block that puts the workpiece at a height where a missed swing
goes into the block or the ground, not into your shin.

**Wood splits along the grain, and knots are where it refuses to.**
[Working with Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) explains why: wood is
enormously strong along the fibres and weak across them, and a knot is a
region where the fibres are distorted around a branch. A round that will
not split is usually a round with a knot in the line you are hitting. Move
the line, or set that round aside for a hydraulic splitter or a longer
burn.

## Moving firewood, and why it is a rule rather than a slogan

"Buy it where you burn it" sounds like a bumper sticker. It is a
quarantine rule, and the reasoning behind it is specific.

Insects and fungi live in wood. Felling a tree does not kill them. USDA
APHIS's risk assessment on the movement of firewood puts it plainly: pests
that attack live trees often survive tree felling, rough processing and
shipping, and pests may also attack the wood afterwards, in the landing,
during transport, or lying in a firewood yard. Seasoning does not solve
it either; the same assessment notes that wood infested with emerald ash
borer may hold both new and fully developed larvae, and that it can take
two successive emergence periods, meaning two summers, for all the adults
to emerge. Pine wood nematodes can survive 20 months in chipped wood.

The reason this matters is that firewood moves at highway speed and
insects do not. APHIS cites an estimate that firewood spread is frequently
150 to 200 miles, and surveys finding that between 8 and 57 percent of
campers bring firewood from home, often travelling 100 to 200 miles and
frequently crossing state lines. Research in the western states estimated
**330,000 campers moving firewood into national parks every year.** An
insect that disperses at its own flying speed gets two hundred miles in an
afternoon instead, and arrives somewhere nothing has evolved to resist it.

That is why the regulation exists. The same assessment reports that 28
states in the lower 48 have quarantine regulations covering the movement
of raw logs, with newer ones naming firewood explicitly, and lists the
organisms that prompted federal and state quarantines: emerald ash borer,
Asian longhorned beetle, spongy moth (the species APHIS calls gypsy moth
in that 2011 document), pine shoot beetle, sudden oak death, sirex wood
wasp, hemlock woolly adelgid and European larch canker. New York was the
first state to prohibit moving untreated firewood more than 50 miles.

**Heat treatment is the exception that proves the rule.** Firewood
imported into the United States must generally undergo heat treatment at
71.1 degrees C for 75 minutes. For domestic firewood, the assessment cites
thermotolerance work on emerald ash borer concluding that an internal wood
temperature of 60 degrees C for 60 minutes should be considered the
minimum for safe treatment. Certified heat-treated bundles carry a seal
and may travel.

So, in practice: **source your firewood locally, burn it where you got it,
and do not carry a load of it to a campsite or across a state line.** If
you are going camping, buy it at the campground or from a certified
treated source. Check whether your county or state is inside a quarantine
before you move any wood at all; APHIS and your state agriculture
department both publish the current areas.

## Storing it

**Away from the house.** USFA's wildfire guidance is to keep the first 5
feet around a home clear of anything flammable, and it names woodpiles
first on the list. By the arithmetic in the species table above, a cord of
seasoned Douglas-fir weighs close to a ton and a half, and every ounce of
it is dry fuel with an enormous surface area. Stacked against the house it
sits exactly where you least want it in a wildfire, and directly under the
eaves in an ember shower. Put the main stack well away, and keep only a
few days of wood close in.

**Off the ground.** Pallets, rails, gravel, anything. Ground contact wicks
water into the bottom course and holds it there, which puts that wood back
above the moisture content where decay fungi work. You are trying to keep
the fuel under 20 percent; the ground is trying to put it over 30.

**Not stacked against a wall, and this is two mistakes at once.** The
moisture half is straightforward: a wall blocks air on one side, so that
face of the stack does not dry, and the stack traps moisture against the
siding, which is the exact condition
[Working with Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) identifies as the
requirement for rot. The pest half is a working rule rather than a sourced
finding, but it is a widely held one in the trades: a woodpile against a
wall is a sheltered, undisturbed, damp habitat with a bridge straight into
the building, and the insects that like dead wood are the same ones you do
not want finding your framing.

**Top covered, sides open.** As above. A roof, not a bag.

**Rotate it.** Burn the oldest first. A stack that never turns over grows
a bottom layer that has been wet for three years and is now half compost.

## What can go wrong

- **The fire will not get going and the glass sooted up overnight.** The
  wood is wet. Test it with a meter; do not argue with it. Nothing else
  on this list is nearly as common.
- **You ran out in February.** Recount in cords, not in impressions. Three
  to ten cords a season is the range, and a face cord is a third of a cord
  or less.
- **The wood you bought is lighter than you expected.** Either it is wetter
  than it should be, in which case you paid for water, or it is a lighter
  species than you were told, which the 10 percent identity accuracy rule
  gives you standing to raise.
- **The chimney sweep found heavy creosote after one season.** You have
  been burning wet wood, damping the fire down, or both. Fix the wood
  first; it is the bigger of the two.
- **The carbon monoxide alarm goes off, or anyone in the house has a
  headache that clears when they leave.** Everyone outside, then call for
  help from outside. Then have the flue inspected before the next fire.
  Do not treat a single alarm as a false alarm.
- **You air sealed the house and now the stove smokes into the room on
  lighting.** That is backdrafting, and it is the interaction described
  above. Crack a window as a diagnostic, and read
  [Ventilation, Damp and Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md) for the
  real fix.
- **The stack is mouldy and the bottom course is soft.** Ground contact,
  or a tarp over the sides. Both hold water in.
- **The wood seasoned fine in summer and will not light in December.** It
  came back up with the weather. Cover the top and keep a week's worth
  indoors.
- **A neighbour complained about the smoke.** They may be right, and one
  of EPA's at-risk groups may live there. Check whether a burn ban is in
  effect and whether your wood is actually dry.
- **A round will not split no matter how hard you hit it.** There is a
  knot in your line. Move the line rather than the effort.

## You own this when

- You can say how many cords your house needs for a winter, and you
  arrived at that by dividing an energy figure by a heat content, not by
  guessing.
- You buy by the cord, you know a cord is 4 by 4 by 8, and you would
  refuse a "face cord" without asking how deep.
- You own a moisture meter, you test on a freshly split face, and "under
  20 percent" is a measurement rather than a hope.
- Your stack is off the ground, covered on the top only, open on the
  sides, cut to length with the ends exposed, and away from the house.
- You can explain why wet wood and a damped-down fire cause the same two
  problems, and name both.
- Your chimney is swept and inspected every year, and you know that just
  under half of United States home heating fires are chimney or flue
  fires.
- There is a working carbon monoxide alarm outside each sleeping area, you
  know how old it is, and you know that a wood stove in a tightened house
  is the specific thing that needs watching.
- You did not learn felling from a document, and you stay two tree lengths
  from any standing tree that looks wrong.
- Your firewood came from near where you burn it.

When those are true you have a fuel supply rather than a pile of wood, and
the understanding transfers further than firewood does. Knowing that the
unit is the thing to pin down before the price, that water in a fuel is
energy you have already spent, and that the failure which kills is quieter
than the failure which burns are the same three habits behind every
system you will ever run.

### A note on what is not cited

NFPA 211 is the standard the chimney trades actually work to, and many
building codes adopt it by reference. It is copyrighted and sold, so no
number in this guide comes from it. Every maintenance interval above is
instead taken from a federal agency, and it is worth noticing that the US
Fire Administration, the Consumer Product Safety Commission and the
Centers for Disease Control and Prevention independently arrive at the
same annual interval for three different reasons.

No chainsaw injury figure more recent than 1979 appears here. The current
estimates in circulation come from analyses of the Consumer Product Safety
Commission's National Electronic Injury Surveillance System published in
journals this guide could not open, so they are not quoted rather than
quoted second hand.

EPA's Burn Wise programme ran from 2009 to 2025, and EPA states that it
stopped updating the programme's content on 30 October 2025. Its pages are
still the best public-domain source on firewood moisture and wood smoke,
and they are cited as such, but they are now a historic record rather than
a maintained one. Expect the links to move.
