# Trading Fairly

A homestead never makes everything it needs. You trade eggs for
firewood, a day's work for the loan of a trailer, honey for a repair,
a surplus of squash for a neighbour's spare seedlings. Many of these
trades are small, friendly and done on a handshake, and many go fine.

A trade can go wrong in a few familiar ways: the two people meant
different things by the same words, one side did not know
the condition of what they were getting, the timing was vague, or the
payment turned out to be worth less than it looked. None of that needs
bad faith. It needs only a trade that was never quite agreed.

The test this guide uses for a fair trade is simple: **both sides would
do it again**, knowing everything they know afterwards. That is our
framing, not a published rule, and it asks more than "nobody cheated".
It asks that both people understood what they were getting, agreed a
value they can still defend, and got what was promised, when it was
promised.

The rules in this guide come from the National Institute of Standards
and Technology (NIST), which looks after the weights and measures that
trade depends on, the Federal Trade Commission (FTC), the Consumer
Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the US Department of Agriculture
(USDA), the Bureau of Labor Statistics (BLS), the Food and Drug
Administration (FDA) and the Internal Revenue Service (IRS), with one
line from the Constitution as the National Archives transcribes it.
All are
United States government publications, in the public domain. Laws on
trade, food and tax differ from country to country and from state to
state: what follows is the United States picture, and the habits carry
further than the laws do. Where something is general practice or our
own example, the text says so.

## 1. Say exactly what is being traded

### In a unit, not a word

Fair measure is old enough to be in the United States Constitution:
among the powers Article I gives Congress is the power to "fix the
Standard of Weights and Measures" (see [The Constitution of the United
States](../../reference/us-constitution.md)). The reason is the one
every trade runs into. Words like "a load", "a bag", "a bunch" or "a
good pile" mean different amounts to different people.

Firewood is the classic case, and [Firewood](firewood.md) explains it
in full: a cord is a defined volume, 128 cubic feet of stacked wood,
and the model regulation that states adopt bans informal terms for
firewood (one state's version lists face cord, rack, pile and
truckload), because the same words can mean a quarter of a cord or
half of one. The same habit works for everything: agree a count, a
weight or a volume, and say how it will be measured.

### Net, not gross

When something is traded by weight, the weight that counts is the
goods, not the container. NIST's handbook for checking packaged goods
says a declared net weight means the amount of the product exclusive
of wrapping materials. The container's weight is called the tare. If
you trade honey by the pound, weigh an empty jar, weigh the full one,
and subtract (our example of NIST's rule).

### On a scale both of you trust

Between neighbours, a kitchen scale you both look at is usually
enough (general practice). Selling to the public is different. NIST
says that a scale used for commercial purposes must comply with the
laws of the state it is used in; that all 50 states have adopted NIST
Handbook 44, NIST's specifications and tolerances for weighing and
measuring devices; and that before a scale can be used commercially,
it must be inspected and approved by an authorised body of that state. NIST counts as
commercial any scale used to establish the quantity of produce or other
articles offered for sale. So if you weigh what you sell at a stand or
a market, ask your state's weights and measures office what it
requires before you start.

### In what condition

Quantity is half of it. The other half is condition, and it is where
honest people can disagree after the fact.

- **Say what is wrong with it.** A chainsaw that is hard to start, a
  pump that leaks at one fitting, a ladder with a repaired rung: say so
  before the trade, not after (general practice). The other person is
  trading on what you tell them.
- **Use shared words for quality.** The USDA's Agricultural Marketing
  Service sets official grade standards for farm products, and it says
  large buyers such as grocery stores, restaurants and the military use
  those grades as a common language that makes business easier. Between
  neighbours, the same idea applies at a smaller scale: agree what
  "good", "seconds" or "for animal feed" means before the swap (our
  extension).
- **Show the records.** A machine that comes with its maintenance log
  is worth more, and is easier to judge, than one that does not (see
  [Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md)).
- **Let them test it.** Start the engine, run the pump, open the jar.
  Inspection before the trade is cheap dispute prevention (general
  practice).

### Things you may not trade at all

Some goods cannot be sold or passed on, however willing both sides are.

- **Recalled products.** The CPSC says its laws and regulations apply
  to anyone who sells or distributes consumer products, including
  individuals holding yard sales and flea markets, and that selling
  recalled products is unlawful unless they have been repaired in
  accordance with the recall. People who give away used products for
  free cannot knowingly pass on products that do not meet the law
  either. Before you sell or swap a used product, search the CPSC's
  recall listings; the CPSC's guide for resellers points to
  SaferProducts.gov for this.
- **Old cribs, and some other children's products.** The same CPSC
  guide says most cribs made before June 2011 may not be resold, and it
  lists durable infant and toddler products such as play yards, infant
  walkers, bath seats and toddler bed rails that are missing parts,
  wobbly or unstable as things not to sell. Its advice for a hazardous
  product is that it should be destroyed, not sold or given away.
- **Food, without checking the rules.** Food you grow or make for your
  own table is yours. Selling it is regulated, and mostly not
  federally: the FDA publishes the Food Code as a model, and it says
  local, state, tribal and federal regulators use it to write their own
  food safety rules. So the rules for selling eggs, honey, jam or
  canned goods depend on where you live. Ask your state's agriculture
  or health department before you sell food you made (general
  practice). The safety reasons behind the canning rules are in [Why
  Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md).

## 2. Agree what it is worth

There is no single right price for a dozen eggs or a day's work. A
fair price is one both sides can still defend once they have looked
around. Three anchors help.

### What the market says

For farm products, the USDA's Agricultural Marketing Service runs
Market News, which it describes as free, unbiased price and sales
information. It publishes thousands of reports a year with wholesale,
retail and shipping data, including for local and regional foods, as
well as fruit, vegetables, livestock, eggs, grain and hay. Wholesale
prices are lower than what a shop charges and retail prices higher
than what a farmer gets, so knowing which one you are looking at
matters (general practice). The prices at your nearest shop, farm
stand or market are the other half of the picture.

### What the work is worth

When one side of a trade is labour, the question is what an hour of
that work is worth. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' Occupational
Employment and Wage Statistics programme publishes wage estimates for
about 830 occupations, for the country as a whole, for each state and
for metropolitan and nonmetropolitan areas. A day of fencing, a morning of
electrical work and an afternoon of babysitting are different trades,
and the published wage for each in your area is a fair place to start
the conversation (our suggestion). Skill, tools brought and risk
taken are fair reasons to adjust it.

### What it costs to replace

For a used tool or machine, the anchor is often what it would cost to
buy one in the same condition, not what it cost new (general
practice). A machine with its manual, its spares and a record of its
maintenance sits at the top of that range.

### The middleman's share

Anyone who buys to resell has to buy for less than they sell for, or
they cannot keep going. That gap is not cheating; it pays for their
time, their stock and their risk. But it is a reason to trade directly
when you can, because a direct trade between two people who each value
what they get keeps that gap in the pockets of the people doing the
work. The game's trading post, described below, makes the size of that
gap easy to see. This paragraph is our own reasoning, not a published
rule.

### The would-you-do-it-again check

Before you agree, imagine each side learning the price everyone else
pays, a week later. If either of you would feel cheated, the price is
not fair yet. Adjust it now, while it is cheap to (our framing).

## 3. When work is part of the trade, write it down

Trades of goods are over in a moment. Trades of work stretch over days
or weeks, which leaves more room for misunderstanding. The FTC's
advice on hiring a contractor was written for paid home improvement,
but it makes a good checklist for any trade of work, paid in money or
in kind.

- **Get it in writing.** The FTC says that even if your state does not
  require a written agreement, you should ask for one. A written
  estimate should include a description of the work, the materials, the
  completion date and the price. Before signing, make sure the
  agreement includes who is doing the work and how to reach them, an
  estimated start and completion date, and any promises made in
  conversation about the scope of the work and the cost of labour and
  materials, and that all blank spaces are filled in.
- **Compare.** The FTC suggests getting more than one estimate, not
  automatically choosing the lowest, and asking for an explanation when
  estimates differ a lot.
- **Do not pay everything up front.** The FTC says not to pay the full
  amount at the start, notes that some states limit how much a
  contractor can ask as a down payment, and says never to make the final
  payment until the work is done and you are satisfied with it.

Between neighbours, a page in a notebook, signed by both of you, does
the job: who does what, by when, for what in return, and what happens
if it is late or goes wrong (our suggestion of the FTC's checklist,
cut down). Writing it down is not a sign of distrust. It is how two
people who trust each other find out, before they start, that they
had understood different things.

## 4. Settle up safely

### Hand over together

The safest trade is the one where both sides hand over at the same
time, in the same place, after both have looked at what they are
getting (general practice). If one side must go first, the one going
first is the one taking the risk, and the agreement should say so.

If you are meeting someone you do not know to trade, meet somewhere
public, in daylight, and tell someone where you are going (general
practice).

### Know what a payment is really worth

Cash in your hand is settled. Other payments may not be.

- **A cheque can turn out to be fake weeks after the money shows.**
  The FTC explains that by law banks have to make deposited funds
  available quickly, so seeing the money in your account does not mean
  the cheque was good, and a fake cheque can take weeks to be
  discovered. By then, you are
  the one who owes the bank. Its advice: do not rely on money from a
  cheque unless you know and trust the person you are dealing with.
- **Overpayment is a scam, not a mistake.** In the FTC's description,
  a buyer sends a cheque for more than the price and asks you to send
  back the difference. Its rule is blunt: do not accept a cheque for
  more than the selling price. And never send money from a cheque on
  by gift card, wire transfer or cryptocurrency to anyone who asks.
- **Payment apps are like cash once sent.** The FTC says scammers like
  mobile payment apps because once you send money, it is hard to get it
  back, and it advises double-checking the recipient's details before
  you pay.

### Signs that a trade is not what it seems

The FTC's list of the tactics used in home improvement scams works for
any trade of work: someone who knocks on your door because they are
"in the area", who says they have materials left over from another job,
who pressures you for an immediate decision, or who asks you to pay
everything up front or only in cash. Pressure to decide now is the one
to remember. A fair trade will still be fair tomorrow.

## 5. When it goes wrong

Even good trades go sideways: the wood is wetter than promised, the
job runs late, the machine fails a week later. The FTC's sequence for a
dispute over work is a sensible order for any trade:

1. **Try to resolve it with the other person first.** The FTC notes
   that many disputes can be settled at that level.
2. **Put it in writing.** Follow a phone call with a letter, and keep
   notes and copies of letters and documents.
3. **If that fails, get outside help.** For disputes with a business,
   the FTC lists your state attorney general or local consumer
   protection office and dispute resolution programmes. Between
   neighbours, a trusted third person both of you respect can do the
   same job (general practice). The Humanity Accord's approach to
   conflict is in [Conflict
   Resolution](../../accord/conflict_resolution.md).

A written agreement, as in section 3 of this guide, is what makes all
of this possible. Without one, a dispute is two memories against each
other.

## 6. Keep a record, and know the tax rule

Write down every trade worth remembering: the date, who with, what
went each way, in what quantity and condition, and anything still owed.
[Keeping Records](keeping_records.md) covers how.

In the United States, trades have a tax side too. The IRS defines
bartering as the exchange of goods or services, and says you must
include in gross income, in the year you receive it, the fair market
value of the goods or services you receive from bartering. Its example
is a plumber who trades plumbing work for a dentist's dental work. The
same page explains that a "barter exchange" is an organisation whose
members trade through it, which must file Form 1099-B, and that the
term does not include informal swaps of similar services on a
non-commercial basis, such as a babysitting co-op run by neighbourhood
parents. That is a statement about which arrangements count as a
barter exchange; the page does not say that informal swaps are free of
tax. How the rule applies to your own trades is a question for a tax
professional or the IRS, not for this guide; keeping a record of what
each trade was worth is what lets you answer it later (general
practice).

## Worked examples

### Eggs for firewood

A neighbour has firewood; you have more eggs than you can use.

- **The units.** "Some wood" becomes a stack 8 feet long, 4 feet high
  and 1 foot deep, split, which is 32 cubic feet: a quarter of a cord
  (the arithmetic is in [Firewood](firewood.md)). "Eggs" becomes six
  dozen.
- **The condition.** Seasoned or green? Which species? Firewood that is
  wet is worth less as fuel, and the firewood guide explains why.
- **The value.** Look at what eggs cost at the nearest farm stand and
  what a quarter cord costs locally, and see whether the two are in the
  same range. If not, change the quantities.
- **The timing.** "Six dozen eggs over six weeks, one dozen each
  Saturday; a quarter cord of split, seasoned wood, stacked by your
  shed, by 1 November." Written on one page, both names at the bottom.
- **The check.** Stack it and measure it: 8 by 4 by 1 feet.

The quantities and prices in this example are ours, for illustration.

### A day's work for a used chainsaw

A neighbour will give you their old chainsaw for a day of help
clearing their fence line.

- **Condition.** Start it, cut with it, ask when it was last serviced,
  and ask for the manual. Ask what is wrong with it.
- **Safety.** Look the saw's model up in the CPSC's recall listings
  before you take it. A saw with a missing or broken chain brake or
  guard is not a bargain.
- **Value.** Compare what a similar used saw sells for locally with
  what a day of your labour is worth, using the local wage for similar
  outdoor work as a guide.
- **Terms.** Which day, how many hours, who brings the tools and fuel,
  and what happens if it rains.

### Honey at the end of the drive

You have more honey than the household needs and want to sell some.

- **Before you start:** ask your state's agriculture or health
  department what the rules are for selling honey you produced, and
  ask the weights and measures office what scale you may use. Both
  differ by state.
- **Measure:** sell by net weight, not including the jar.
- **Label:** say what it is, how much, and who produced it, and keep
  your own record of each batch. That is general practice; your state's
  rules may require more.

## How the game models it

The game has two ways to trade, and both teach something.

- **The trading post.** Your home has a trading post, a machine you
  walk up to and use to buy and sell goods for credits. Every good has a
  base value in `data/trade_goods.ron`, and the post sells to you at
  125 percent of that value and buys from you at 50 percent, which its
  window states. Buy something with a base value of 100 credits and you
  pay 125; sell it straight back and you get 50, so the post keeps 75
  for its trouble. That is the middleman's share from section 2, made
  visible, and it is a reason to trade with other players when you can. The file's own notes say the
  values were set so that one credit is roughly a minute of unskilled
  work.
- **Quality changes the price.** Tools, clothing and furniture you make
  by hand are graded by your skill, and the trading post pays for each
  grade at a different rate: 0.4 times its usual price for poor work,
  1.5 times for good, 2.5 for excellent and 5 for a masterwork, and it will not buy
  defective goods at all. The grades are in `data/manufacturing.ron`.
  Condition is part of value, in the game as in life.
- **Trades with other players.** When you are connected to a server,
  the Trade page lets you trade items directly with another player.
  One of you proposes a trade and the other accepts; each side then
  lists items from their own backpack. Any change to either side's
  offer clears both confirmations, so nobody can agree to one offer and
  receive another. You can confirm only while you are actually carrying
  what you offered, and when both sides have confirmed, each player's
  game hands over its own side. A worn tool arrives worn and a graded
  item keeps its grade, so trading is not a free repair. The server
  records the trade, but it does not hold the goods; the check that
  you still carry what you offered is the guard.
- **What the Trade page does not show you.** It lists the other
  player's offer by name and count only, not the wear or grade that
  will arrive with it. So in the game, as in life, ask before you
  confirm.

**One setting changes what you keep.** While Settings > Gameplay >
"Start every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
during development, only your character carries between launches.
Credits, and goods you bought, sold or traded, start over at the next
launch; the game keeps a record of which trades it has already settled
so that none is handed over twice. Turn the setting off to keep your
goods and credits.

What the game simplifies: prices at the trading post are fixed, not set
by supply and demand; there is no haggling, no written agreement, no
trading of work, no measuring or weighing, and no tax. The Trading
skill exists in the skill list, but nothing in the game levels it yet.

The trading post's prices are in `src/systems/economy/mod.rs` and
`src/gui/pages/vendor.rs`, and the player-to-player trade in
`src/gui/pages/trade.rs` and `src/relay/storage/trading.rs`.

## You own this when

- Every trade you make names a quantity in a real unit, and how it
  will be measured.
- You trade by net weight, and you know whether your scale is good
  enough for selling.
- You say what is wrong with what you are trading, and you inspect
  what you receive before you agree.
- You check the recall list before you sell or give away a used
  product, and you know the rules for selling food where you live.
- You can find a reference price for goods and for work, and you can
  explain your price to the other person.
- Trades of work are written down: who, what, by when, for what, and
  what happens if it goes wrong.
- You never pay for all of a job up front, and never make the last
  payment before the work is done.
- You do not accept a cheque for more than the price, and you treat a
  payment app like cash.
- You walk away from pressure to decide now.
- You keep a record of every trade, and you know trades can have a tax
  side.
- Both sides of your trades would do them again.
