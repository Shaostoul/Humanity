# Making a Tool

Sooner or later a job needs a tool you do not have. The shop is shut,
or far away, or the tool does not exist, because the job is yours
alone. So you make one: a handle for a bare axe head, a wedge, a
mallet, a jig that holds the work at the right angle, a chisel ground
from a bar of good steel. People have always done this, and a home that
can make and repair its own tools depends less on a shop.

It is also a way to get hurt. The Occupational Safety and Health
Administration (OSHA) puts it bluntly in its booklet on hand and power
tools: "The greatest hazards posed by hand tools result from misuse and
improper maintenance." Improvising usually means using a tool for a job
it was not made for, which is misuse, and a home-made tool has never
been tested by anyone but you. This guide is about telling the two
apart: which tools are worth making, how to make the common ones well,
which ones you must never make or improvise, and where your own work
should stop.

It takes its safety rules from OSHA's regulations and booklet, the
armed services' manuals on hand tools (TM 9-243) and on metals and heat
treatment (TM 9-237), and the Consumer Product Safety Commission
(CPSC); its method for fitting a handle from the Forest Service's ax
manual. All are United States government publications, in the public
domain. Where something is general practice rather than a published
rule, the text says so.

Other guides cover the ground this one stands on, and it links to them
rather than repeating them. [Hand Tools](../making/hand_tools.md) is how
to use and judge a tool. [Sharpening](../making/sharpening.md) covers
edges and grinders. [Metals and Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md)
covers telling steels apart and heat treatment in full. [Working With
Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) covers grain and wood dust.
[Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](force_levers_and_mechanical_advantage.md) covers levers and
lifting. [Salvage and Reuse](salvage_and_reuse.md) covers where stock
comes from and what not to reuse. [Keeping Things
Working](keeping_things_working.md) covers jacks, ladders and looking
after what you have.

## First: should this tool be made at all?

### The improvisations that hurt people

OSHA's booklet gives four examples of how hand tools injure, and every
one is an improvisation or a tool kept past its life:

- "If a chisel is used as a screwdriver, the tip of the chisel may
  break and fly off, hitting the user or other employees."
- "If a wooden handle on a tool, such as a hammer or an axe, is loose,
  splintered, or cracked, the head of the tool may fly off and strike
  the user or other employees."
- "If the jaws of a wrench are sprung, the wrench might slip."
- If impact tools such as chisels, wedges or drift pins have mushroomed
  heads, "the heads might shatter on impact, sending sharp fragments
  flying toward the user or other employees."

The armed services' hand tool manual (TM 9-243) adds some that are
tempting when you are short of the right tool:

- **A file as a pry bar or a hammer.** "Do not use a file for prying.
  The tang end is soft and it bends easily. The body of the file is hard
  and very brittle. A light bending force will cause it to snap." And:
  "Do not hammer on a file. This is very dangerous because the file may
  shatter."
- **A screwdriver as anything else.** It says in capital letters not to
  use a screwdriver for prying, punching, chiselling, scoring or
  scraping, and not to use one near a live wire, to check a storage
  battery, or to find out whether a circuit is live.
- **A pipe over a wrench handle.** "Do not attempt to extend the handle
  in any way to increase the leverage on a wrench. Increased leverage
  may damage the wrench or the work." Nor strike a wrench with a hammer.

In each case the tool fails without warning, and the person holding it,
or someone beside them, is in the way of what happens next: a tip or a
fragment flies, a head comes off, a wrench slips (our summary of the
examples above).

### What you must never make or improvise

Some tools carry a load that, if it lets go, lands on a person. For
these, the rules leave no room for home-made versions, and neither
should you (our reading of the rules below):

- **Anything that lifts or holds a load over people.** OSHA's rule for
  slings (29 CFR 1910.184) says slings "shall not be shortened with
  knots or bolts or other makeshift devices", and for chain slings
  "Makeshift links or fasteners formed from bolts or rods, or other such
  attachments, shall not be used." A bolt through two links, a hook bent
  from bar, or a knot in a chain is exactly that.
- **Anything that holds up a vehicle or machine you are under.** A jack
  is for lifting; the load is then held on stands or blocking rated for
  it, never on bricks, concrete blocks or a stack of wood of unknown
  strength (general practice for the materials; the rules are in
  [Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md)).
- **Anything you climb.** OSHA's ladder rule for construction (29 CFR
  1926.1053) says ladders "shall not be tied or fastened together to
  provide longer sections unless they are specifically designed for
  such use", and that they "shall be used only for the purpose for
  which they were designed." The same rule covers job-made ladders and
  sets out how they must be built, which tells you a ladder is
  something built to a specification, not improvised. At home, buy a
  rated ladder (general practice).
- **Anything electrical that protects the wiring.** The CPSC's home
  electrical safety checklist (Publication 513, July 2008) says to
  install the correct size of fuse, and to have an electrician identify
  and label it if the size is unknown, because the wrong size "can allow
  too much current" to flow and overheat the wiring, a fire hazard. A
  coin, a wire or a bigger fuse in its place is exactly the dangerous
  improvisation that rule is about (general practice for the examples).
- **Anything that holds pressure,** such as a tank, a fitting or a hose
  for compressed air, gas or steam, and **anything whose job is to
  protect someone,** such as a guard, a harness, a helmet or a
  child's seat (general practice; [Salvage and
  Reuse](salvage_and_reuse.md) covers reused protective gear).

If failure would hurt someone, buy the rated part.

### The rule about your own tools

OSHA's general rule (29 CFR 1910.242(a)) makes an employer responsible
for the safe condition of tools used by employees, "including tools and
equipment which may be furnished by employees." It is written for
workplaces, but the principle carries home: if you lend or hand a
home-made tool to someone, or have someone working for you, it is still
your tool to answer for (our reading).

## Three kinds of making

1. **Improvising:** using something you have for a job it can safely do,
   such as a straight board as a fence for a saw, a sandbag as a weight,
   or a block of hardwood under a pry bar to spread its load (general
   practice).
2. **Making in wood:** handles, wedges, mallets, jigs, gauges and
   holders. This is the safest place to start (general practice).
3. **Making in steel:** forging, grinding and heat treating a chisel, a
   punch or a blade. It needs heat, hard steel and the most care.

## Eyes, hands and lungs at the bench

- **Eye protection for all of it.** OSHA's rule on eye protection (29
  CFR 1910.133) lists hazards from "flying particles, molten metal,
  liquid chemicals, acids or caustic liquids, chemical gases or vapors,
  or potentially injurious light radiation", and requires side
  protection where objects can fly. Tool making produces several of
  those at once.
- **Wood dust.** The CPSC's Art and Craft Safety Guide says wood dust
  can irritate the lungs, that some hardwood dusts are sensitisers, and
  that occupational exposures to some hardwood dusts, in furniture and
  cabinet making for example, "have been associated with cancer of the
  nasal cavities and sinuses". It advises a respirator approved by the
  National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH) or good
  ventilation. More is in
  [Working With Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md).
- **A forge.** The CPSC's guide says forging furnaces "may release
  metal fumes and toxic gases (e.g., carbon monoxide) and infrared
  radiation." Its advice: be aware of fire and heat hazards, keep
  combustibles away and have a fire extinguisher, ventilate, "Install
  carbon monoxide alarms", and wear protective clothing, gloves,
  earplugs, and infrared goggles or a face shield. Run a forge outdoors
  or in an open-sided shelter where you can. Indoors, run one only with
  real ventilation and a carbon monoxide alarm, and never in a closed
  space such as a shut garage (general practice for where to put it;
  [Firewood](firewood.md) covers carbon monoxide).
- **Galvanised and painted metal.** The Army's welding manual (TM
  9-237) says the fumes of burning paint, and of brazing, welding or
  cutting brass, lead, zinc and galvanised or cadmium-plated parts, "are
  filled with poisonous particles of oxides, lead, or zinc." Heating
  galvanised steel in a forge is the same hazard (our extension), so do
  not forge it; [Salvage and Reuse](salvage_and_reuse.md) has more.
- **Grinders.** Before you mount an abrasive wheel, inspect it and
  ring-test it: OSHA's booklet says to tap it gently with a light,
  non-metallic instrument, and that a wheel that sounds cracked or dead
  "could fly apart in operation." Check the wheel's speed rating against
  the machine: "Take care to ensure that the spindle speed of the
  machine will not exceed the maximum operating speed marked on the
  wheel." OSHA's rule (29 CFR 1910.215(d)(1)) requires the same check
  before mounting. Stand out of the plane of the wheel while it starts,
  because "An abrasive wheel may disintegrate or explode during
  start-up."
- **Keep the guards on.** OSHA's booklet: "Portable grinding tools need
  to be equipped with safety guards to protect workers not only from
  the moving wheel surface, but also from flying fragments in case of
  wheel breakage." Never take the guard off a hand-held grinder to fit
  a bigger wheel or reach an awkward spot (general practice, following
  that rule).
- **The bench grinder's rest.** OSHA's rule (29 CFR 1910.215(a)(4))
  keeps the work rest within one-eighth of an inch of the wheel, so the
  work cannot be jammed between them, and says "The adjustment shall
  not be made with the wheel in motion." The guard's adjustable tongue
  stays within one-quarter of an inch (1910.215(b)(9)). Always use eye
  or face protection, and never clamp a hand-held grinder in a vice.
  [Sharpening](../making/sharpening.md) covers grinding without ruining
  the steel.

## Making in wood

### Choosing the wood

The Forest Service's ax manual, *One Moving Part* (2020), says hickory
is the preferred wood for ax handles in America, "a dense, tightly
grained hardwood known for its durability and strength", and that oak
and ash are also suitable. For other handles that take a shock, those
three are the ones to look for (our extension). For a mallet head or a
wedge, any dense, dry hardwood will serve (general practice); [Working
With Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) explains density and drying.

**Then look at the grain.** The manual says the grain should
run parallel to the ax head, and "A handle with wood grain that runs
perpendicular to the direction of the ax head is more likely to break."
It warns against "runout", where the grain does not run the full length
of the handle, because "Areas where the grain runs short have an
increased chance of splitting or separating." Choose a blank whose
grain runs straight from end to end, along the direction of the force
(general practice for blanks other than ax handles).

The manual also settles an old argument: Forest Products Laboratory
research has shown that red, white, and mixed red and white hickory all
have the same strength characteristics, so the colour of a handle is
not a reason to reject it.

### Worked example: fitting a handle to an axe head

The ax manual calls this hanging an ax, and it says a properly hung ax
must meet three criteria: the head must be securely fixed to the
handle, in line with it, and at the right angle to it. "An ill-fitting
head is dangerous; it could become loose and separate from the handle,
potentially injuring you or another person."

Its steps, in short:

1. **Trial fit.** Hold the head beside the handle's shoulder to see how
   much will stick out above it, and trim the top if there is far too
   much, leaving extra to spare. The eye is slightly larger at the top,
   to take the wedge.
2. **Shape only the part inside the eye.** Use a rasp, then a scraper,
   knife or sandpaper, and remove wood a little at a time; "be careful
   not to remove too much wood." Never take wood off the whole length of
   the handle, only from the part that sits in the eye.
3. **Seat it by striking the handle, not the head.** Use a wooden or
   rubber mallet or a dead-blow hammer on the heel of the handle to draw
   the head up onto it. "Never pound an ax head with a metal hammer or
   mallet to loosen or remove the head from the handle." A drift pin is
   the tool for easing a handle out.
4. **Check alignment each time.** Sight down the cutting edge to the
   centre of the knob at the end of the handle, and lay the ax on a
   bench to check the angle: the edge should touch about at its middle
   or a little toward the heel, with the knob also touching. Adjust by
   removing wood from the side inside the eye.
5. **Wedge it.** Use a wooden wedge "about 3 inches long, depending on
   the depth of the ax eye" (so shorter for the smaller eye of a
   hatchet, our reading), reaching into the handle's kerf (the slot in
   its top) about one-half to three-quarters of the depth of the head;
   deepen the kerf with a saw if it is too shallow. Do not put linseed
   oil on the wedge, the manual says, because it can act as a lubricant
   and let the wedge slip out.
   Drive it firmly and evenly, saw off the excess, and leave a little
   handle standing above the head rather than cutting it flush.
6. **Pin it, if it is a working tool.** The manual says competition
   choppers and Forest Service employees pin the head with a roll pin
   through head and handle, about one-fifth of the way up from the
   bottom of the head, below the wedge, which "prevents the head from
   flying off, even if it is loose."
7. **Oil the handle,** not the wedge: several thin coats of boiled or
   raw linseed oil. If you oil a new handle before you hang it, keep the
   part that goes inside the head dry: the manual says to leave it free
   of oil or make sure it is completely dry before it goes in, because
   "Oil is a lubricant and can cause the ax head to slip on the
   handle."

The manual does not use the small metal crosstie wedges sold with many
axes: driven straight into the wood, they "crush the wood fibers" and
can split the handle. In the field, if one must be used, it goes along
each side of the wooden wedge, not across it.

The same method fits a hammer, a hatchet or a maul (general practice).

**If the head works loose, do not soak it.** The ax manual: "If the
head becomes loose, you may be tempted to soak the ax in water to make
the handle swell. This temporary fix is ill advised." The swelling wood
is crushed inside the eye, and once it dries the head is looser than
before. Its fix is to drive the wooden wedge farther down with a few
taps of a mallet, or to replace it with a thicker wooden wedge, and to
pin the head. A handle that is cracked or splintered is replaced: OSHA
(29 CFR 1926.301(d)) requires wooden handles to be kept free of
splinters or cracks and kept tight in the tool. The Forest Service's
older *Handtools for Trail Work* (2005), cited in [Hand
Tools](../making/hand_tools.md), describes soaking as a field fix for
the day; this guide follows the 2020 ax manual, which is the newer
advice and explains the damage.

Rags with linseed oil on them can heat up and catch fire by themselves:
dry them flat outdoors or keep them in a closed metal can of water, as
[Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md) explains.

### Worked example: a handle for a file

TM 9-243: "If a file is designed to be used with a handle, do not
attempt to use it without the handle. Holding the sharp tang in your
hand while filing can cause serious injury." A file handle is one
of the simplest tools to make, and a good first one (general practice for
what follows):

1. Cut a piece of straight-grained hardwood about the length of your
   palm and a little thicker than two fingers, and round it.
2. Fit a metal ring over the end the tang goes into, such as a short
   piece of copper pipe, to stop the wood splitting.
3. Drill a hole down the middle, in steps: first a small one as deep as
   the tang is long, then a larger one part of the way, so the tapered
   tang wedges in its narrow end.
4. Push the tang in by hand, then seat the file by tapping the end of the
   handle on the bench, not by hammering the file, which TM 9-243 says
   may shatter it.

### Wedges, mallets and jigs

A splitting wedge, a mallet for a chisel and a holding jig are the
other common wooden tools (general practice):

- **A mallet** is a dense hardwood head on a straight-grained handle,
  the handle wedged or tapered through the head like an ax. It is what
  a chisel handle should be struck with, as [Hand
  Tools](../making/hand_tools.md) explains.
- **Wooden wedges** for splitting or for tightening handles are cut so
  the grain runs along the wedge, never across it, or they snap.
- **Jigs and gauges**, such as a block that holds a chisel at a fixed
  angle for sharpening, a stop that sets the depth of a hole, or a
  stick marked with the measurements of a job, are tools only you will
  ever need, and making them is often the quickest route to accurate
  work. Mark each one with what it is for.

## Making in steel

### Which steel will make a tool

To be hardened through into a tool that holds an edge, steel needs
enough carbon. TM 9-237 gives the rule: alloy steels and plain carbon
steels "with a carbon content of 0.35 percent or higher" can be
hardened, while "Low carbon steel, wrought iron, and steel castings
cannot be hardened." (The same manual describes case hardening, which
adds carbon to the surface of a low carbon steel to give it a hard
skin; that is a separate process, not covered here.) It sorts plain
steels by carbon:

| Steel | Carbon | What TM 9-237 says it is for |
|---|---|---|
| Low carbon | Up to 0.30 percent | Soft and ductile; does not harden to any appreciable amount when quenched |
| Medium carbon | 0.30 to 0.45 percent | Parts that need surface hardness and strength, heat treated after making |
| High carbon | 0.45 to 0.90 percent | Tools, heat treated after they are made, to withstand wear |
| High carbon tool steel | 0.60 to 1.70 percent | Tools where high hardness is needed to keep a sharp cutting edge |

A bar of unknown steel can be roughly sorted with a grinder. TM 9-237's
spark test: low carbon steel throws "long yellow-orange streaks" with
some forked sparkles, and high carbon steel "a large volume of
brilliant, yellow-orange sparklers." Compare a spark from your bar with
one from a known mild steel nail and a known file, side by side (general
practice). [Metals and Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md) covers
identifying metals, and the limits of every test, in detail.

**Salvaged steel is unknown steel.** Old files, springs and blades are
usually good high-carbon steel, which is why people make tools from
them, though some cheap ones are only case-hardened mild steel, which a
spark test helps sort out (general practice). Good ones arrive hard. A
file, TM 9-243 says, is "hard and very brittle", and TM 9-237 says
annealing, heating above the critical temperature and cooling slowly, is
how steel is made soft. So salvaged tool steel is annealed before it is
shaped, and hardened and tempered again afterwards (general practice for
the order with salvaged stock).

### Heat treatment, in outline

The sequence is in TM 9-237 and set out in full, with its hazards, in
[Metals and Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md). In short:

1. **Anneal** to make it soft enough to work.
2. **Shape it,** by forging hot or by filing and grinding. Keep the
   steel cool when grinding a hardened piece, or you draw its temper
   ([Sharpening](../making/sharpening.md) explains).
3. **Harden** the working end. TM 9-237: "High carbon steel can be
   hardened by heating to a good red and quenching in water," and the
   quenched steel becomes "hard, brittle, and much stronger".
4. **Temper** it at once. TM 9-237: "After a steel is hardened it is
   too brittle for ordinary purposes," so it is reheated to a lower
   temperature, and "As the tempering temperature increases, toughness
   increases, and hardness decreases." Its Table IV gives the
   traditional oxide colours and the tools tempered at each: very deep
   straw, 480 degrees F, for punches, dies, knives and reamers; peacock
   or full purple, 540 degrees F, for augers and cold chisels for steel;
   bluish purple, 550 degrees F, for axes, cold chisels for iron,
   screwdrivers and springs.

Two warnings from TM 9-237 belong here. Never heat steel close to its
melting point: it becomes coarse and brittle, and "Steel in this
condition usually cannot be restored by any subsequent heat
treatment." And colours are only a guide: the manual says a pyrometer
or other temperature-measuring instrument should be used whenever
possible, "as estimating metal temperature by its color is not
accurate."

Quenching throws scalding water and steam, and anything wet that molten
or very hot metal meets can do the same. Wear a face shield, gloves and
long sleeves, use a quench tank that cannot tip, and keep your face out
of the line above it ([Metals and Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md)
gives these hazards in full).

### Worked example: a cold chisel from a bar

A cold chisel, for cutting metal with a hammer, is a good first steel
tool. In outline (general practice, built on TM 9-237's rules):

1. Start with a bar of high-carbon steel, checked by spark against a
   known file and a known mild steel nail.
2. Anneal it if it is hard, then forge or grind the cutting end to a
   blunt wedge. A cold chisel's edge is far blunter than a wood chisel's,
   because it cuts metal.
3. Grind a small bevel round the striking end. A struck tool's head
   spreads with use; a chamfer slows that down.
4. Harden only the cutting end, about the last few centimetres: heat it
   to a good red and quench it. Leave the striking end soft, so it
   spreads rather than shatters.
5. Polish a strip of the hardened end bright, and temper it: heat the
   shank behind it and watch the oxide colours run toward the edge.
   Quench when purple reaches the edge, the colour TM 9-237's table gives
   for cold chisels.
6. Test it gently first, on soft metal held in a vice, with eye
   protection and nobody in line with the chisel. Then use it.

From then on, look after it as the regulations say: OSHA's rule for
construction hand tools (29 CFR 1926.301) says impact tools "such as
drift pins, wedges, and chisels, shall be kept free of mushroomed
heads." Grind the spread lip off before it can break away. [Hand
Tools](../making/hand_tools.md) explains why a mushroomed head is so
dangerous.

## Testing what you made

Every home-made tool is untested until you test it (general practice):

- **Load it gently first,** well below what you will ask of it, with eye
  protection on and nobody in line with it.
- **Watch for movement** where parts join: a head creeping on a handle,
  a wedge working loose, a crack opening at a hole.
- **Mark it as home-made,** with what it is for, so nobody uses it for a
  job it was not made for. OSHA's ladder rule asks for the same thing of
  a defective ladder: it is marked or tagged and withdrawn from service.
- **Retire it when it shows damage,** exactly as you would a bought
  tool. TM 9-243's rule is to never use damaged tools.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Lifting gear, supports, ladders, pressure equipment, electrical
  protection and protective gear.** Buy the rated part (our reading of
  the OSHA and CPSC rules above).
- **Welding and anything that will carry a load across a weld.** A
  welded joint needs training to make and to inspect; [Metals and
  Alloys](../making/metals_and_alloys.md) explains why welds fail beside
  the weld (general practice for the training).
- **Heat treating a part whose failure hurts someone,** such as a
  spring, a hook or a vehicle part. A blacksmith or machine shop with
  the right steel and a controlled furnace (general practice).
- **A forge indoors.** Only with real ventilation and a carbon monoxide
  alarm, as the CPSC says, and never in a closed space such as a shut
  garage; outdoors or an open-sided shelter is better (general
  practice).
- **Working near electricity.** TM 9-243 says not to use a screwdriver
  near a live wire or to test whether a circuit is live; [Working Out
  Why Something Broke](working_out_why_something_broke.md) covers
  making a circuit safe.

## Traps

- **"It is only for a minute."** That minute is when the file gets used
  as a pry bar, the chisel as a screwdriver and the pipe on the wrench.
- **Hardening without tempering.** A quenched tool left untempered is,
  in TM 9-237's words, "too brittle for ordinary purposes".
- **Mild steel for a cutting tool.** It cannot be hardened, however well
  you quench it.
- **Hitting a hardened face with a hardened hammer.** Chips fly off
  both (general practice; TM 9-243 tells you to replace a hammer whose
  face is chipped).
- **Grain running across a handle or a wedge.** It snaps along the
  grain.
- **A metal hammer on an axe head.** Seat the handle with a wooden or
  rubber mallet on its heel.
- **Soaking a loose axe head.** The swelling crushes the wood and the
  head ends up looser. Reset or replace the wooden wedge.
- **A grinder without its guard.** Nothing is left between a burst
  wheel and you.
- **A wheel rated slower than the machine runs.** OSHA's booklet lists
  the speed check among the ways to keep a wheel from cracking.
- **Forging galvanised steel.** Zinc fumes. Do not.

## How the game models it

In the game, tools are something you make, use up and make again.

- **Which crafts need tools.** A craft you make by hand at a workbench,
  forge, anvil station or electronics bench may need tools: building
  furniture, doors and walls at a workbench needs a hammer and a hand
  saw; assembling machines needs an adjustable wrench and a flat
  screwdriver; smithing at the forge, and work at the anvil station,
  needs a hammer; soldering needs a soldering iron and pliers; and some
  recipes name their own, such as a utility knife for chopping food or a
  chisel and hammer for carving stone. The tools must be in your
  backpack, and each craft wears every tool it names by one use
  (`data/crafting/tools.ron`). A craft an automated machine makes needs
  no tools.
- **You can always make the first one.** The rule in the data is that a
  recipe that makes a tool never needs that tool itself, "so the first
  one can always be made" (`data/crafting/tools.ron`,
  `src/systems/crafting/tools.rs`). The hammer is made at a workbench
  and needs no tool at all.
- **The tool recipes** (`data/recipes.csv`). At a workbench: Craft
  Hammer and the two screwdrivers, each from 1 Iron Ingot and 1 Wood
  Plank at Metalworking level 1, and Craft Clamp. At the forge, each
  needing a hammer: the hand saw, chisel, pliers and adjustable wrench
  (from steel, Metalworking 2), the axe and pickaxe (2 Iron Ingots and a
  plank, Metalworking 2), the hatchet, shovel, hoe, rake, trowel and
  utility knife (Metalworking 1), the crowbar (2 Steel Ingots,
  Metalworking 2), the Metal File (1 Steel Ingot, Metalworking 2), the
  pipe wrench and pruning shears (Metalworking 2), the scythe, the Vise
  and the bolt cutter (Metalworking 3), and the anvil, from 10 Steel
  Ingots at Metalworking 4. The default family home
  has workbenches in its workshop and a forge and an anvil station in
  its forge room (`data/machines/home.ron`).
- **How long they last.** A tool breaks when its uses reach its
  durability: 200 uses for a hammer, an axe, an adjustable wrench or a
  pipe wrench, 180 for pliers, a hatchet or a shovel, 150 for a hand
  saw, a hoe, a chisel or a screwdriver, 120 for a utility knife, a
  rake, a Metal File or pruning shears, 250 for a bolt cutter, 300 for a
  crowbar or the Vise, before any adjustment for how well it was made
  (`data/items.csv`).
- **How well it was made.** A tool you make by hand is graded by your
  level in the recipe's skill, here Metalworking: half the uses for a
  Poor tool, the full number for Standard, 1.3 times for Good, 1.8 for
  Excellent and 2.5 for Masterwork, while a Defective one breaks the
  first time it is used (`data/manufacturing.ron`,
  `src/systems/crafting/quality.rs`). At level 1 the score runs from
  0.35 to 0.55 against the band edge of 0.40, so a beginner's tools come
  out Standard about three times in four and Poor the rest
  (arithmetic from those files); only higher levels reach Good and above.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, crafting
  uses up no materials and wears no tools. The Crafting page still asks
  for the materials and the tools to be in your backpack, and for the
  workbench or forge to be where you are, before it lets you press
  Craft; a recipe that needs a skill level still needs it, and the grade
  still depends on your skill. In Dev, while the "Enable dev cheats"
  switch is on (Settings > Animations, also on by default during
  development), the Crafting page also shows a "Dev: stock all
  materials" button that puts a stack of every recipe input in your
  backpack, ingots and planks included, and the Profile page a "Dev: max
  skills" button. To see tools wear out, switch Play mode to Normal.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character's name, appearance and
  clothes carry between launches, so the tools you made, their wear and
  the Metalworking level you earned start the next session as they were
  in the default home. Turn the setting off to keep them.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: a tool
is made in one step from ingots and planks, with no forging, hardening,
tempering, handle or wedge; the hammer, both screwdrivers, the axe,
hatchet, pickaxe, shovel, hoe, rake and trowel are made from Iron
Ingots, which the game describes only as a smelted iron bar
(`data/items.csv`), and if that were wrought iron or another low carbon
iron, TM 9-237 says it could not be hardened; there is no improvising,
so no tool is ever
used for the wrong job; no tool can be repaired; and nothing at the
forge, grinder or quench tank can hurt you.

## You own this when

- You can name the improvisations that hurt people, a chisel as a
  screwdriver, a file as a pry bar, a pipe on a wrench, a loose head, a
  mushroomed chisel, and you do not do them.
- You never make or improvise anything that lifts, holds up a vehicle,
  is climbed, holds pressure, protects a circuit or protects a person.
- You wear eye protection for all tool making, and you run a forge only
  with ventilation and a carbon monoxide alarm, preferably outdoors.
- You ring-test a grinding wheel, check its speed rating against the
  machine, keep the guards on, stand out of its plane at start-up, and
  keep the work rest close, adjusting it only with the wheel stopped.
- You choose straight-grained hickory, ash or oak for a handle, and can
  hang an axe head so it is tight, aligned and wedged, without hitting
  the head with a metal hammer.
- You can make a handle for a file, and use no file without one.
- You know that steel needs enough carbon, 0.35 percent or more, to be
  hardened by quenching, can sort a bar with a spark test, and anneal
  salvaged tool steel before shaping it.
- You always temper what you harden, and know what the tempering colour
  is telling you and why a pyrometer is better.
- You test a home-made tool gently before you trust it, mark it, and
  retire it when it is damaged.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- That the OSHA sling, ladder and fuse rules, and the CPSC's, mean
  never making or improvising lifting gear, supports, ladders, pressure
  equipment, electrical protection or protective gear; that a home-made
  tool you lend is still yours to answer for.
- Never holding a vehicle on bricks, blocks or wood of unknown strength;
  buying a rated ladder; the coin, wire or bigger fuse as examples.
- The three kinds of making and the examples of safe improvising; the
  summary of OSHA's four examples (each tool fails without warning, with
  the person in the way).
- Running a forge outdoors or in an open-sided shelter where possible,
  and never in a closed space; that forging galvanised steel is the same
  hazard as welding it.
- Never taking the guard off a hand-held grinder, following OSHA's rule
  that portable grinding tools have guards.
- That the ax manual's "depending on the depth of the ax eye" means a
  shorter wedge for a hatchet.
- Hickory, oak and ash for other handles that take a shock; any dense,
  dry hardwood for mallets and wedges; straight grain along the force
  for blanks other than ax handles; that the axe method fits a
  hammer, hatchet or maul.
- The file handle, mallet, wedge and jig details.
- Comparing sparks side by side with known samples; that old files,
  springs and blades are usually high-carbon steel, though some cheap
  ones are case-hardened mild steel; annealing salvaged tool steel
  before shaping it.
- The cold chisel procedure, including the chamfer, hardening only the
  cutting end and running the temper colour from the shank.
- The quench tank habits beyond those in Metals and Alloys.
- Testing, marking and retiring home-made tools.
- That welding and heat treating load-bearing parts belong to the
  trained; that a hardened hammer on a hardened face chips both.
