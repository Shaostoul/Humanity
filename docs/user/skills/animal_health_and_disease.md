# Animal Health and Disease

Animals cannot tell you they feel ill, and many show little until they
are quite sick, so by the time a sick goat looks sick to a stranger it
may have been ill for days (general practice). The keeper's main tool is
not a medicine chest but an eye trained on what normal looks like for
each animal: how it stands, eats, chews, moves, breathes and behaves.
Notice the change early, and a vet can often fix what would otherwise
kill.

This guide is about that early noticing, about the few measurements any
keeper can learn, about the diseases that pass from animals to people,
about keeping disease out of a herd, and about the law on animal
medicines. It is not a veterinary manual, and it does not teach
treatments: diagnosing and treating a sick animal is a vet's work, and
this guide is mostly about knowing when to call one.

Its safety rules come from the Centers for Disease Control and
Prevention (CDC), the US Department of Agriculture's Animal and Plant
Health Inspection Service (APHIS), the Food and Drug Administration
(FDA), the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) and
federal regulations; its pasture and parasite figures from the
Department of Agriculture's Natural Resources Conservation Service
(NRCS). Those are United States government works, in the public domain.
Its vital sign figures and bloat advice come from the extension services
of Virginia and Nebraska; those are copyrighted, so their facts are
restated here in our own words, never quoted. Where something is
general practice or our own reading, the text says so.

Three other guides sit beside this one. [How Animals Work](how_animals_work.md)
explains the bodies whose failures this guide is about. [Keeping
Animals](keeping_animals.md) covers the water, feed, shelter and pasture
that prevent most illness. [Handwashing and
Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) covers the habit that does most to
keep animal germs out of people. Poultry diseases, including bird flu,
are in [Chickens and Eggs](chickens_and_eggs.md).

## Know what normal looks like

APHIS advises looking at the health of your animals every day when you
can, because early detection is what stops a disease spreading. The
Virginia Cooperative Extension lists what to watch beyond the numbers:
behaviour, dung, eating habits, skin and coat, and body condition. Is
the animal alert, or dull with its ears drooping? Has it stopped eating
or drinking? Is it urinating more or less than usual? Is its coat rough
and poor?

Make those checks a habit, and write down what you see (general
practice):

- **At every feed.** Does every animal come to eat? An animal that hangs
  back, stands apart from the herd or lies down when the others are up
  is telling you something.
- **Cud.** Cattle, sheep and goats spend hours a day chewing the cud
  ([How Animals Work](how_animals_work.md) gives the hours). A ruminant
  that has stopped is worth a closer look (our reading).
- **Dung and urine.** Note what normal looks like for each species, so
  you notice scouring (diarrhoea), hard dry dung, straining, or none at
  all.
- **Movement.** Lameness, stiffness, staggering, or reluctance to get up.
- **Breathing.** Fast, noisy, laboured or open-mouthed breathing in an
  animal at rest.
- **Body condition,** felt by hand every month and recorded ([How Animals
  Work](how_animals_work.md) explains the scores).

## Taking vital signs

Temperature, pulse and breathing rate are the three numbers a vet will
ask for when you call, and the Virginia Cooperative Extension says your
vet may ask you to measure and report them in an emergency.

**Be safe first.** Taking a temperature puts you at an animal's back
end, within reach of its hind legs. The CDC says to avoid standing directly
behind farm animals or approaching from the rear even when the animal is
restrained. So have a helper hold the animal's head or tie it with a
halter, stand to one side of its hindquarters, close against its body,
never directly behind it, and keep a hand on its hip so you feel it shift
(general practice). With a cow or a horse, unless the animal is well
restrained and someone experienced has shown you how, leave it to your
vet (general practice).

**Temperature.** The Virginia Cooperative Extension's method:

1. Use a veterinary thermometer, conventional or digital.
2. Tie a long string securely to its end, so it cannot be lost inside
   the animal; on a horse the string can be clipped to the tail.
3. Moisten or lubricate the tip and insert it into the rectum.
4. Take it out after 2 to 3 minutes, or the time the maker gives, and
   read it.

Its normal adult ranges are 100.4 to 103.1 F (38.0 to 39.5 C) for
cattle, 102.2 to 104.9 F (39.0 to 40.5 C) for sheep and goats, 100.4 to
104 F (38.0 to 40.0 C) for pigs, and 99.5 to 101.3 F (37.5 to 38.5 C)
for horses; the Celsius figures are our conversions. It says an animal
counts as having a fever when its temperature is one degree outside the
normal range (the table is in Fahrenheit, so about half a degree
Celsius), but warns that hot weather and activity raise the reading: a
horse just ridden, or cattle moved on a hot day, can look feverish when
they are not. A high temperature in a resting animal can mean infection
or heatstroke. Low readings are less common but can also mean trouble.

The most useful number of all is your own animal's normal: take each
animal's temperature a few times when it is healthy and calm, and write
it down (general practice).

**Pulse.** Count beats for a full 60 seconds, with your fingers, not your
thumb, which has a pulse of its own. The Virginia Cooperative Extension
says to feel cattle and horses where an artery crosses the lower edge of
the jaw, sheep and goats on the inside of the hind leg (thigh), and that
a pig's pulse has to be felt at the heart. Its normal resting ranges, in
beats a minute: cattle 40 to 70, sheep and goats 60 to 90, pigs 60 to
100, horses 28 to 40.

**Breathing.** Watch or feel the flank rise and fall for a minute while
the animal is at rest. Normal rates, in breaths a minute: beef cattle 10
to 30 and dairy cattle 18 to 28, sheep and goats 12 to 20, pigs 8 to 18,
horses 8 to 16. Note any wheezing, rattling, groaning, or breathing that
is laboured, shallow or irregular.

**Water and blood.** For dehydration, pinch up a tent of skin on the neck
and let go: it should fall back within one to two seconds, and the gums
should be moist, not dry. Gums should be a medium pink, not whitish or
red, and colour should come back within one to three seconds after you
press a finger on them. Abnormal gum colour and a slow return, the
Virginia Cooperative Extension warns, can mean shock or internal
bleeding. That is a call to the vet now (our reading).

## Signs that cannot wait

Some problems can kill quickly. Call your vet at once for any of these
(general practice unless a source is named):

- **Bloat.** The Nebraska extension service describes the signs in
  cattle: the left side of the belly swelling, often the first sign;
  discomfort, with stamping, kicking at the belly and laboured breathing;
  frequent urination and dung; and collapse. It says acute bloat must be
  treated promptly, because delay in severe bloat can mean death, and
  that keepers should plan emergency treatment with their vet before the
  grazing season. It also warns: never drench a bloated animal (pour
  liquid down its throat), because it is likely to breathe the liquid in,
  which can kill it at once or cause pneumonia. Sheep and goats have the
  same rumen and the same risk (our reading). Treatments that release
  the gas through a tube or through the skin are for a vet, or for
  someone the vet has trained (our reading). How to prevent bloat is in
  [Keeping Animals](keeping_animals.md).
- **Down and unable to rise,** or straining without result.
- **Not eating or drinking,** especially with a dull, drooping look.
- **Heavy bleeding, a deep wound, or a broken leg.**
- **A difficult birth,** or a newborn that will not stand and suck.
- **Pale or white gums,** or the slow colour return above.
- **Several animals ill at once, or sudden deaths.** This is also a
  reason to report, below.

## Diseases you must report

Some diseases threaten every herd in the country, and APHIS keeps a
national list of the animal diseases that must be reported. It describes
two worth knowing.

**Foot-and-mouth disease** (not a human health threat, and not the
childhood illness with a similar name) affects cloven-hoofed animals:
cattle, pigs, sheep, goats and deer. APHIS says to look for fever,
blisters on the tongue and lips, in and around the mouth, on the udder
and around the hooves, which burst and leave raw patches; sticky, foamy,
stringy saliva; eating less; lameness and reluctance to move; abortion;
low milk yield; and heart disease and death, especially in newborn
animals. Other diseases cause blisters too, and only a laboratory can
tell them apart, so APHIS says to report mouth or foot blisters
immediately. It also asks travellers who visited
farms abroad to declare it on return and to keep away from livestock for
5 days afterwards.

**Scrapie** is a fatal brain disease of sheep and goats, one of the
transmissible spongiform encephalopathies. APHIS says it can take 2 to 5
years for signs to show, and an infected animal that looks healthy can
spread it. Its signs include poor coordination; stumbling, falling or
difficulty getting up; changes in behaviour; marked weight loss despite a
good appetite and good teeth; more sensitivity to noise and sudden
movement; tremors; star gazing; head pressing; a high-stepping or
bunny-hopping gait; and intense rubbing that leaves matching bare
patches on both sides of the body. There is no treatment.

**Who to call.** APHIS says owners who suspect a disease should contact
their vet first. Vets report listed diseases to the state animal health
official and to APHIS. APHIS's list of reportable diseases gives its
Foreign Animal Disease Hotline for after hours and weekends:
866-536-7593. Its 2007 biosecurity fact sheet adds a rule worth keeping: do not wait, but report serious or
unusual animal health problems to your vet, your local extension office,
or state or federal animal health officials. Write the vet's number and
the hotline inside the barn door (general practice).

## Diseases that pass to people

The CDC's warning is short: "Even healthy animals can carry diseases."
It lists diseases that farm animals can spread to people, among them
anthrax, bovine tuberculosis, brucellosis, Campylobacter, Cryptosporidium,
E. coli, influenza, leptospirosis, Listeria, MRSA, orf (sore mouth), Q
fever, rabies, ringworm, Salmonella and vesicular stomatitis. OSHA
explains how people catch such diseases: by direct contact with animals
or their products, such as manure or a placenta; by eating or drinking
animal products such as raw meat or raw milk; through bites; and
indirectly, from contaminated soil, food or water.

Some people are at higher risk of serious illness. For germs carried in
raw milk, the CDC names children under 5, adults over 65, pregnant women
and people with weakened immune systems; for Q fever at birthing time it
adds people with heart valve disease or other heart problems.

Three diseases matter especially to anyone keeping goats, sheep or
cattle.

**Q fever.** The CDC explains that the bacteria behind it naturally
infect goats, sheep and cows, and are found in their birth products
(placenta and birth fluids), urine, dung and milk; people catch it by
breathing dust contaminated with those. Some never fall ill; others get
fever, chills, tiredness and aching muscles, and some develop a chronic,
life-threatening form. There is no Q fever vaccine in the United States.
Its fact sheet for livestock birthing exhibits adds that Q fever may
lead to miscarriage in pregnant women. If you fall ill after time with
sheep, goats or cattle, the CDC says to see your healthcare provider and
say so.

**Orf (sore mouth).** The CDC says orf mostly affects sheep and goats,
which get sores around the lips and muzzle and in the mouth. People
catch it through a cut or scrape that touches an infected animal or its
equipment, especially when bottle feeding, tube feeding or shearing, and
get painful sores on the hands and forearms. It advises rubber or latex
gloves when handling sheep and goats, especially with cuts on your
hands or when touching the mouth, and frequent handwashing.

**Raw milk.** The CDC says raw (unpasteurised) milk and products made
from it can carry Campylobacter, Cryptosporidium, E. coli, Listeria,
Brucella and Salmonella, and that good practices on a farm can reduce
contamination but cannot guarantee safety. Illness can be diarrhoea,
cramps and vomiting, or in some cases Guillain-Barre syndrome or
haemolytic uraemic syndrome, which can lead to paralysis, kidney
failure, stroke or death. Its advice is to choose pasteurised milk and
dairy products. The CDC's Q fever page says the same: do not consume raw
milk or raw milk products. If you keep a milking animal, ask your
extension office for a tested way to pasteurise its milk before anyone
drinks it, and never give raw milk to children, pregnant women, older
people or anyone with a weakened immune system (general practice from
the CDC's warnings).

**Rabies.** The CDC says any mammal can get rabies, and that vaccines
for livestock exist, rabies among them; ask your vet whether yours need
it. An animal that suddenly behaves strangely, drools, or cannot swallow
should not be handled bare-handed or examined in the mouth until a vet
has seen it (general practice). If a wild or unfamiliar animal, or one
of your own that is acting strangely, bites or scratches you, the CDC's
rabies advice is to wash the wound at once with soap and water for 15
minutes and to talk to a healthcare or public health professional
urgently; [How Animals Work](how_animals_work.md) and [Bleeding and
Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md) have more.

### Habits that protect you

The CDC's rules for people who work with farm animals:

- **Wash your hands with soap and running water** after touching animal
  body fluids such as saliva, birth tissue or birth fluid; after handling
  milk or eggs; after cleaning stalls or dung; after handling feed, bowls,
  supplies or equipment; and after touching fences or buckets. Use an
  alcohol-based hand sanitiser with at least 60 percent alcohol only when
  soap and water are not available. Adults should supervise handwashing
  for children under 5.
- **Wear protective equipment, such as masks, gloves and boots,** for any
  job that touches animal body fluids, including cleaning stalls and
  helping an animal give birth.
- **Cover open wounds and cuts** before working with animals.
- **Keep a pair of shoes and gloves for the animals,** stored outside the
  house; scrub your shoes and change clothes before and after visiting
  another farm's animals.
- **Do not eat or drink in animal areas,** and do not take babies' things
  (toys, dummies, bottles, pushchairs) into them.
- **Supervise children,** keep their hands from their mouths, and keep
  children under 5 from touching chicks, ducklings and other live
  poultry.
- **Keep livestock out of the house.**

### Birthing season

Birth brings the placenta, birth fluids and newborn animals, which the
CDC's fact sheet names as ways people catch Q fever, and its Q fever
page advises avoiding contact with animals especially while they are
giving birth. Its fact sheet on Q fever at livestock birthing
exhibits says pregnant women, people with weakened immune systems, and
people with heart valve disease or other heart problems should avoid
working at birthing exhibits; the same risk is there at lambing and
kidding at home (our reading). For those who do the work, it lists
disposable gloves, plastic arm-length sleeves, goggles, boots and
respiratory protection (an N95 respirator recommended) when helping with
births, cleaning birthing areas and disposing of birth products; it adds
that the wearer should be trained to use a respirator, and should ask a
doctor first whether wearing one is safe for them. It says to remove
placentas, bedding and anything soaked with birth fluids promptly, and
to work with your vet to find the cause of any abortion or stillbirth.

APHIS's scrapie advice adds: confine ewes and does that are giving birth
so they have little contact with other animals; remove placentas, fluids
and soiled bedding as soon as possible after the birth while wearing
protective equipment; disinfect the birthing area between births; and do
not feed milk or colostrum from possibly exposed ewes or does to lambs
or kids.

### Bites, scratches and kicks

The CDC says to wash a bite or scratch at once with soap and warm water,
because even a wound that does not look deep can become infected, and to
seek medical attention if the wound is serious (bleeding that will not
stop, muscle or bone showing, a bite over a joint, extreme pain or being
unable to move), if it becomes red, painful, warm or swollen, or if your
last tetanus shot was more than 5 years ago. If you are kicked, get away
from the animal as quickly as possible and seek medical attention,
especially for a kick to the head.

## Keeping disease out

APHIS calls the everyday measures biosecurity. Its 2007 fact sheet for
livestock and poultry owners names bringing in new animals, or mixing
your animals with others, as one of the greatest risks, and gives these
rules:

- **Keep new animals apart for 30 days.** The same goes for your own
  animals coming back from a fair, show or sale.
- **Keep your distance.** Limit visitors, give them one way in, keep them
  away from the animals unless it is necessary, and give them clean
  boots and clothes if they must go near. Post a sign.
- **Keep it clean.** Clean clothes, disinfected boots and washed hands for
  everyone, family included; keep equipment and vehicles clean, and
  control birds and rodents.
- **Don't haul disease home.** After visiting other farms, sales or
  petting zoos, clean your vehicle's tyres and equipment, and change
  clothes and wash your hands before going back to your own animals.
- **Don't borrow disease from your neighbour.** Do not share equipment
  without cleaning and disinfecting it first.

For sheep and goats, APHIS's scrapie page recommends a closed herd:
buying as few outside animals as you can, from other closed herds, and
mixing as little as possible with animals at markets, fairs and shows.
The CDC adds routine veterinary visits and vaccination, planned with your
vet, and care for the farm's dogs and cats too.

### Worms

The NRCS handbook says effective control of the parasites that live in
and on livestock is needed for efficient livestock production, and it
treats the pasture as the first defence against worms: rest
pastures for at least 20 days and graze no closer than 4 inches (10 cm)
from the ground to break stomach-worm life cycles, with longer rests for
sheep and goats; give goats 30 to 45 days of rest and a 4-inch stubble
against the barber pole worm, which lives near the soil surface. Clean
water helps too.

The barber pole worm causes anaemia (too little blood), and the handbook
describes the check keepers use for it in goats: the colour of the
inside of the lower eyelid, scored on the FAMACHA chart from 1 (bright
red, little sign of worms, do not deworm) to 5 (critical, with a high
chance of death). It pairs the eyelid with four more checks, together
called the five-point check: the back (body condition), the tail (a
dirty, dung-caked rear), the jaw (a soft swelling under it called bottle
jaw) and the nose (discharge). It recommends faecal egg counts to see
whether deworming worked, changing the active ingredient, and
considering culling an animal that keeps getting reinfected. Learn the
eyelid check from your vet or a trained person, and let the vet choose
the dewormer and the dose (general practice).

## Medicines: the law, and the reasons for it

**Antibiotics need a vet.** From 11 June 2023, the FDA says, every new
package of a medically important antimicrobial for animals (the
antibiotics that also matter in human medicine) carries a prescription
label, and once the old over-the-counter stock ran out, all of them need
the authorisation of a licensed vet. Its questions and answers for
farmers say the change covers cattle, pigs,
chickens, turkeys, horses, sheep, goats, cats and dogs, and drugs for
food animals and pets alike. You may still buy a prescribed drug from a
supplier, but you need the prescription.

**The vet-client-patient relationship.** Any use of a drug other than as
its label says needs what federal rules call a valid
veterinarian-client-patient relationship, and the FDA says prescriptions
come through one too, with details that vary by state. Under 21 CFR
530.3(i), that means a vet has taken responsibility for medical judgements about your
animals and you have agreed to follow the vet's instructions; the vet
knows the animals well enough to make at least a preliminary diagnosis;
the vet is available for follow-up; and the vet has recently seen the
animals, or visited where they are kept. The FDA's leaflet for sheep and
goat keepers says to build that relationship and set treatment plans for
common conditions before illness strikes.

**Use a drug only as its label says, unless your vet directs otherwise.**
Federal rules (21 CFR 530.11) do not permit "Extralabel use in an animal
of an approved new animal drug or human drug by a lay person (except when
under the supervision of a licensed veterinarian)", nor extra-label use
of a drug in or on animal feed. Extra-label use means a different
species, a different illness, a different dose, frequency or route, or a
different withdrawal time from what the label says (21 CFR 530.3(a)).

**Withdrawal times.** A drug given to an animal leaves residues in its
meat, milk and eggs for a time. The FDA explains the withdrawal time as
"the time from when the animal was last treated with the drug to when
the animal can be slaughtered for food or the animal's milk or eggs can
go to market", and says that if it is followed, food from the treated
animal is safe. It varies with the drug, the dose, how it is given and
the kind of animal, and when a vet prescribes a drug for extra-label use
in a food animal, federal rules require the vet to set a substantially
extended withdrawal period and to make sure the treated animal can be
identified (21 CFR 530.20). The same wait applies to the milk and eggs
your own household eats (our reading).

The FDA's short version is T.A.L.K.:

1. **Talk** to your vet before giving any drug.
2. **Ask** whether the drug is approved for your species.
3. **Look** at the label: the dose, how often, for how long, by which
   route, and the withdrawal time.
4. **Keep** complete treatment records: which animal, which drug, how it
   was given, why, and when its food is safe again.

Mark a treated animal so nobody milks it or collects from it by mistake,
and keep the records as [Keeping Records](keeping_records.md) describes
(general practice).

**Needles.** Injections bring a hazard of their own for the keeper. OSHA
warns that a needlestick can inject a vaccine with live organisms, a
hormone or another drug into you, and lists how to
prevent it: use an approved sharps container, never remove a needle cap
with your mouth, avoid recapping needles, and wear gloves. If you do
stick yourself with an animal drug, wash the wound, read the label, and
see a doctor, taking the label with you (general practice). Keep animal
medicines locked away from children (general practice).

## When an animal dies

Wear gloves to handle a dead animal, and do not touch its fluids
bare-handed (general practice, from the CDC's protective equipment
advice). If an animal dies suddenly or for no reason you can see, ask
your vet whether a post-mortem examination is worth doing, because the
cause may threaten the rest of the herd (general practice). APHIS pays
for scrapie testing of up to 30 adult sheep or goats per flock a year,
and owners can report a dead sheep or goat and ask for help submitting
samples. How a dead animal must be disposed of is set by state and local
rules; ask your state animal health official or extension office
(general practice).

## Know where your own work stops

- **Diagnosis, prescriptions and treatment:** your vet. Your part is to
  notice, measure, report clearly and follow the instructions.
- **Bloat, a difficult birth, a wound, a down animal:** your vet, at once.
- **Blisters in the mouth or on the feet, nervous signs in sheep or
  goats, sudden deaths or many sick animals:** your vet and the state
  animal health official, or the APHIS hotline.
- **Your own health:** a doctor for bites, needlesticks and any illness
  after animal contact; your health department for a possible rabies
  exposure.
- **Medicines:** never extra-label without your vet; never feed a drug in
  feed in a way its label does not allow; always the withdrawal time.

## Traps

- **"It looks healthy, so it is safe."** The CDC says healthy animals can
  carry diseases. Wash your hands every time.
- **Waiting to see.** Many animals show little until they are quite
  sick. A change is the signal; act on it.
- **Standing behind an animal to take its temperature.** Stand to the side,
  with the animal held.
- **No baseline.** A temperature means little if you never took one when
  the animal was well.
- **Drenching a bloated animal.** It can kill it.
- **Raw milk for the family.** The CDC says it can carry serious
  infections, especially for children, pregnant women, older people and
  anyone with a weakened immune system.
- **Pregnant women at lambing and kidding.** The CDC says they should
  avoid working at livestock birthing exhibits, and the risk is the same
  at home (our reading).
- **Leftover antibiotics.** Using a drug without your vet, in a way the
  label does not say, breaks federal rules and can leave residues in your
  food.
- **Forgetting the withdrawal time.** Write it down the day you treat.
- **New animals straight into the herd.** Thirty days apart first.
- **Recapping needles.** OSHA says to avoid it; holding a cap in one hand
  and a needle in the other is an easy way to stick yourself (our
  reading).

## How the game models it

The game does not model animal illness. Nothing in it makes an animal
sick, gives it worms, or passes a disease from an animal to you, and the
home's animals never need a vet.

- **Animals have health only for combat.** Each species has hit points in
  `data/creatures.csv` (15 for a hen, 50 for a sheep, 55 for a goat), and
  they lose them only when something attacks them. There are no vital
  signs to take.
- **Food from animals can make you ill.** Drinking the goats' milk, which
  is not pasteurised, carries a 5 percent chance of food poisoning, and
  spoiled milk always poisons you. Eating raw meat carries a 40 percent
  chance, and meat spoils after 24 game hours; cooking removes the risk
  (`data/food_system.ron`). Food poisoning is a status effect that drains
  your health for a while.
- **The game treats cheese and butter as safe.** Its cheese and butter,
  made from the same raw goats' milk, carry no risk in the game. Real
  cheese and butter made from raw milk can carry the raw milk's germs, as
  the CDC warns.
- **No animal medicines.** There are no drugs for animals, no withdrawal
  times and no needles.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, materials
  are free while the Creative mode switch on the Inventory page is on,
  which it is unless you turn it off, so cooking needs no milk or meat.
  The risk of eating raw or spoiled food is the same in every mode.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches, so
  the food in your pack, and any milk you collected, is gone at the next
  launch.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: illness,
parasites, bloat, birth, the diseases that pass to people, reporting,
medicines and their law, and the daily looking that catches trouble
early. In real keeping, those are where animals and people get hurt.

## You own this when

- You look at every animal every day and keep notes on what is normal for
  each one.
- You can take a temperature, a pulse and a breathing rate safely, from
  the side, with the animal held, and you know each animal's healthy
  baseline.
- You can check for dehydration with a skin pinch and for shock with gum
  colour and refill time.
- You know the signs of bloat and never drench a bloated animal.
- You know the signs that must be reported, and have your vet's number
  and the APHIS hotline written in the barn.
- You wash your hands after every contact, keep animal shoes and gloves
  outside the house, and wear protective equipment for birth fluids and
  dirty stalls.
- You keep pregnant women, people with weakened immune systems and people
  with heart valve disease away from birthing animals.
- You do not drink raw milk or give it to anyone at higher risk, and you
  wear gloves with sheep and goats.
- You quarantine new animals for 30 days and run as closed a herd as you
  can.
- You manage pasture to break worm cycles and leave dewormer choices to
  your vet.
- You have a vet who knows your animals, use drugs only as labelled or as
  your vet directs, follow every withdrawal time, keep treatment records
  and use a sharps container.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- That many animals show little until quite sick.
- The daily checks at feeding time, and writing them down; a ruminant
  that stops chewing the cud as a concern.
- Reading the extension's one degree of fever as Fahrenheit, about half a
  degree Celsius.
- Applying the CDC's birthing exhibit advice to lambing and kidding at
  home.
- Holding the animal, standing to the side with a hand on its hip, and
  leaving cows and horses to the vet unless trained; learning each
  animal's own healthy temperature.
- That abnormal gums mean calling the vet now.
- The list of signs that cannot wait, beyond bloat; applying the cattle
  bloat advice to sheep and goats; tube and trocar treatment being for
  the vet.
- Writing the vet's number and the hotline in the barn.
- Asking the extension office for a tested way to pasteurise milk, and
  never giving raw milk to people at higher risk.
- Not handling a strangely behaving, drooling animal's mouth until a vet
  has seen it, and treating a bite from your own animal that is acting
  strangely the way the CDC treats one from an unfamiliar animal.
- That a recapped needle is an easy way to stick yourself.
- Learning the eyelid check from a trained person and leaving the
  dewormer to the vet.
- That withdrawal times apply to food your own household eats; marking
  treated animals.
- What to do after a needlestick; locking medicines away.
- Gloves for dead animals, asking about a post-mortem, and asking about
  disposal rules.
- The Celsius conversions, which are arithmetic.
