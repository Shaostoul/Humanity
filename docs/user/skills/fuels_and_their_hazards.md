# Fuels and Their Hazards

Almost every household keeps fire in a can. Gasoline for the mower and
the generator, propane for the grill, natural gas in the pipes, kerosene
for a heater, diesel or heating oil, a bag of charcoal, a stack of wood.
Each one packs a great deal of energy into a small space, which is
exactly why it is useful and exactly why it is dangerous.

Each fuel also tends to hurt people in its own particular way. Gasoline
gives off vapour that drifts along the floor to a pilot light. Propane
leaks sink and wait in low places. Natural gas leaks rise and wait for a
light switch. Kerosene is safe enough until someone pours gasoline into
the heater. Charcoal looks harmless once it is glowing, and indoors it
kills with carbon monoxide. Knowing the way your fuel fails is most of
keeping it from failing.

This guide explains the few numbers that predict how a fuel behaves,
goes through the common household fuels one at a time, and then covers
storing, moving and refuelling them, spills, leaks, fuel fires, and where
your own work stops. It is written for the United States, with US
rules; the physics is the same everywhere.

Several guides carry parts of this subject, and this one links to them
rather than repeating them. The liquid fuels in depth, with a full table
of flash points, kerosene grades, lamp oils, alcohols, static and what to
do if someone swallows fuel, are in [Fire Performance
Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md). Wood is in
[Firewood](firewood.md). Burning fuel safely indoors is in [Heating a
Home Safely](heating_a_home_safely.md), and outdoors in [Making and
Controlling Fire](making_and_controlling_fire.md). Refuelling small
engines and oily rags are in [Keeping Things
Working](keeping_things_working.md), the gas water heater and fuel
vapours in [Heating Water](heating_water.md), generators in [Choosing and
Running a Generator](choosing_and_running_a_generator.md) and [Where Your
Own Electrical Work Stops](where_your_electrical_work_stops.md), carbon
monoxide alarms in [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md), and getting rid of old fuel in
[What Not to Compost, Burn or Pour
Away](what_not_to_compost_burn_or_pour_away.md).

Its sources are the Occupational Safety and Health Administration
(OSHA) and the other federal rules in the Code of Federal Regulations,
the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH),
NOAA's CAMEO Chemicals database, the Consumer Product Safety Commission
(CPSC), the US Fire Administration (USFA), the Pipeline and Hazardous
Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA), the Federal Emergency Management
Agency's Ready.gov and the Federal Register, all US federal and in the
public domain; and Britain's Health and Safety Executive and Poison
Control, restated in our own words. Where something is general practice
or our own reading rather than a published rule, the text says so.

## First: the rules for every fuel

1. **Keep fuel out of the house.** The CPSC: "Never store fuel for your
   generator in the home." It says gasoline, propane, kerosene and other
   flammable liquids belong outside living areas, in properly labelled,
   non-glass safety containers, and not near a fuel-burning appliance
   such as a gas water heater in a garage. The USFA: "Store oil, gasoline,
   paints, propane and varnishes in a shed away from your home."
2. **In a container made for that fuel, labelled, closed.** Never a drink
   bottle, a milk jug or a food container: the CPSC calls any container
   not designed for fuel, such as a jug or a plastic bag, extremely
   dangerous (restated from a page summary). Keep containers closed,
   upright and out of the reach of children and pets (USFA, CPSC).
3. **Away from heat, sun, flame and sparks.** The USFA: "Never store
   materials that can cause a fire in the sun or near an open flame or
   heat source." The CPSC adds equipment that throws sparks, such as a
   mower or a generator, to the list of things not to store fuel beside.
4. **Refuel engines off and cold.** The CPSC: "Before refueling the
   generator, turn it off and let it cool down. Gasoline spilled on hot
   engine parts could ignite." The same goes for heaters, mowers and saws
   ([Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md)).
5. **Fill cans outdoors, on the ground.** The USFA: "Only fill portable
   gasoline containers outdoors in an airy area. Make sure to place the
   container on the ground when you fill it." [Fire Performance
   Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) explains the static spark
   that a can sitting on a truck bed liner can make.
6. **Never on a fire.** No gasoline or other liquid fuel to start or
   revive a fire, ever ([Making and Controlling
   Fire](making_and_controlling_fire.md)).
7. **Burn fuel only where it was meant to burn.** Every fuel here makes
   carbon monoxide when it burns. Nothing that burns fuel runs indoors
   unless it was built and installed to vent outside ([Heating a Home
   Safely](heating_a_home_safely.md)).
8. **Smell gas: get out, then call.** Details under Leaks below.

## Four numbers that explain every fuel

You do not need chemistry to use these, only the idea behind each.

### Flash point: when a liquid starts giving off fuel to the air

OSHA defines a liquid's flash point as "the minimum temperature at which
a liquid gives off vapor within a test vessel in sufficient concentration
to form an ignitable mixture with air near the surface of the liquid".
Below it, the liquid has to be heated, or spread thin on a wick, before a
spark will light it. Above it, the air just over the liquid will already
burn.

The figures for the common fuels:

| Fuel | Flash point | What that means at home |
|---|---|---|
| Gasoline | -45 F (-43 C), NIOSH | Ignitable vapour at any temperature you will ever meet |
| Kerosene sold to the US specification | 100 F (38 C) or higher, as [Fire Performance Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) explains | Safe from a spark when cool; not when hot, sprayed or soaked into something |
| Diesel | 125 F (52 C), CAMEO | Hard to light with a match when cool; burns readily once heated or soaked into a wick |
| No. 2 fuel oil (heating oil) | 136 F (58 C), CAMEO | As for diesel |

(The Celsius figures are our conversion of the Fahrenheit ones.)

Heat takes the margin away. OSHA's rule for workplaces makes a liquid
that is heated to within 30 F of its flash point be handled as though it
were the next, more dangerous kind (29 CFR 1910.106(a)(19)). A can of
diesel in a hot car, kerosene on sun-baked pavement or lamp oil next to a
stove is no longer the fuel its label describes (our reading).

### Flammable range: how much fuel in the air will burn

A fuel vapour burns only when it is mixed with air between two limits:
below the lower one there is too little fuel, above the upper one too
little air (general knowledge). The limits are small numbers:

- gasoline: 1.4 to 7.6 percent vapour in air (NIOSH);
- propane: 2.1 to 9.5 percent (CAMEO);
- methane, the main part of natural gas: 5 to 15 percent (CAMEO).

Read the lower figure the right way round. Gasoline needs only 1.4 parts
in a hundred of the air to be explosive, which is why a little spilled
fuel in a closed garage or basement is not a little danger (our reading).
And a room too full of gas to burn is not safe either: as it airs out, it
passes back down through the range (our reading).

### How heavy the vapour is: where a leak goes

CAMEO describes each fuel's vapour against air:

- **Gasoline and kerosene vapour is heavier than air.** For gasoline,
  CAMEO warns that the vapour "may travel considerable distance to a
  source of ignition and flash back." For kerosene and fuel oil, it says
  most vapours spread along the ground and collect in low or confined
  areas, such as sewers and basements.
- **Propane is heavier than air too,** about one and a half times as
  heavy, and CAMEO's verdict is "will sink". A propane leak pools in a
  basement, a crawlspace, a ditch or the bottom of a boat (examples ours).
- **Methane is lighter than air** and rises (CAMEO).

This is why a can of gasoline beside a water heater is dangerous even
when nothing is spilled on the heater (our reading), and why the CPSC
reported in 2003 that gas water heaters lighting flammable vapours were
involved in nearly 800 home fires a year, with an average of five deaths
and 130 injuries.

### Autoignition temperature: hot enough to light without a flame

This is the temperature at which a fuel lights by itself in hot air in a
test flask, with no spark or flame. CAMEO's figures include 853 F for
gasoline, 842 F for propane, 444 F for kerosene and 350 to 625 F for
diesel. Notice the surprise: the heavier fuels, hard to light with a
match, light by themselves at lower temperatures than gasoline does. A
hot exhaust or engine part in open air usually has to be a good deal
hotter than the flask figure before it lights anything, so read these as
a floor, not a trigger; [Fire Performance
Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) explains why. Fuel spilled on a
hot engine is still how refuelling fires start (rule 4).

### And one smell, added on purpose

Natural gas and propane have almost no smell of their own; CAMEO calls
methane "a colorless odorless gas". So the law adds one. For natural gas
in distribution lines, federal pipeline rules require that the gas
"must contain a natural odorant or be odorized so that at a concentration
in air of one-fifth of the lower explosive limit, the gas is readily
detectable by a person with a normal sense of smell" (49 CFR 192.625(a)).
OSHA's rule for liquefied petroleum gas sets the same one-fifth target
(29 CFR 1910.110(b)(1)(i)). PHMSA describes the result as the
"distinctive 'rotten egg' smell". In other words, you are meant to smell
a leak long before it can burn; the rules are written for "a normal
sense of smell", and not everyone has one (our reading).

## The common fuels, one at a time

### Gasoline

The most dangerous fuel most households keep, because it is always
giving off vapour that will burn.

- **How it hurts people:** vapour reaching a pilot light, a furnace, a
  spark from a tool or a switch; flash fires when it is poured on a fire;
  burns from refuelling a hot engine; and flame jetting, which is a flame
  outside a can lighting the vapour inside it and throwing burning fuel
  out of the spout. The CPSC, explaining its rule on fuel cans in the
  Federal Register: "Flame jetting typically injures people other than
  the person holding the container."
- **Where it lives:** never indoors. The CPSC said in 2003 that gasoline
  should never be stored or used indoors, in a basement or a garage,
  where its vapours can ignite, and should be kept in tightly closed,
  properly labelled, non-glass safety containers away from ignition
  sources and out of reach of children (restated from a page summary).
  A shed away from the house (USFA).
- **Its container:** buy a proper gasoline can. Federal rules require
  portable gasoline containers to have child-resistant closures (16 CFR
  part 1460, made under a 2008 act of Congress, Public Law 110-278); cans
  made on or after 22 December 2022 must meet the current version of that
  standard. Portable fuel containers made after 12 July 2023 must also
  carry a flame mitigation device, such as a flame arrestor, the
  Federal Register's example of "a screen that quenches and cools a flame
  so that it cannot pass through" (88 FR 2206; 16 CFR part 1461). Gasoline
  cans are usually red (CPSC). An old can without those parts is worth
  replacing (our reading).
- **What it is for:** engines. [Fire Performance
  Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) cites the maker's safety
  data sheet, which says to use it only as a motor fuel: not as a cleaner,
  not to kill weeds, not on a fire (examples ours).
- **Never siphon it by mouth.** Poison Control warns that gasoline drawn
  into the lungs while siphoning can cause pneumonia, coma and death, and
  that a tiny amount is enough (restated). If anyone swallows fuel, call
  Poison Control at 1-800-222-1222 at once and do not make them vomit, as
  [Fire Performance Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) explains.

### Kerosene

Safe from a spark at room temperature, which is why people trust it too
much.

- **How it hurts people:** gasoline mixed into it, the wrong grade, and
  fumes from heaters burning it indoors. The CPSC: "Even small amounts of
  gasoline or other volatile fuels or solvents mixed with kerosene can
  substantially increase the risk of a fire or an explosion." Its heater
  rules are in [Heating a Home Safely](heating_a_home_safely.md).
- **Its container:** its own, meant for kerosene, never a gasoline can or
  one that ever held gasoline. Kerosene containers are usually blue and
  gasoline containers usually red (CPSC), so the colour tells everyone
  which is which.
- **Buy the right grade:** water-clear 1-K kerosene from a dealer who
  certifies it, for a kerosene heater; the CPSC warns that different
  grades can look the same.

### Diesel and heating oil

Hard to light and slow to evaporate, and so easy to be careless with.

- **How they hurt people:** a spill that soaks into soil, a rag or your
  sleeve burns like a wick whatever the weather, and stays wet (and
  flammable) far longer than gasoline would (our reading of what [Fire
  Performance Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) explains for
  kerosene and lamp oil, which behave the same way). Their vapour is
  heavier than air and collects in basements and other low spaces
  (CAMEO).
- **What not to do:** the diesel maker's sheet cited in [Fire
  Performance Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) says to use it
  only as a motor fuel. Like any liquid fuel it never goes on a fire
  (CPSC), and it is not a fire starter (our reading).

### Propane and butane

Stored as a liquid under pressure, used as a gas. CAMEO describes propane
as a colourless gas that is "Easily ignited", whose vapour is heavier
than air, and whose containers "may rupture violently and rocket" under
prolonged exposure to fire or heat. Butane, which CAMEO notes is used in
cigarette lighters, behaves the same way. CAMEO adds a less obvious
hazard: liquid escaping from a cylinder chills as it evaporates and can
cause frostbite.

The CPSC's gas grill fact sheet sets out the rules for the LP gas
cylinders used with grills (LP gas is liquefied petroleum gas, which the
fact sheet calls liquid propane):

- **Storing:** always upright; "Never use or store an LP gas container
  indoors."; never store a spare cylinder under or near the grill; never
  keep a filled one in a hot car or car trunk.
- **Moving:** secure and upright.
- **Refilling:** only by your LP gas dealer or a qualified service
  station operator. "DO NOT FILL THE CONTAINER YOURSELF." It suggests
  using a cylinder exchange.
- **Checking for leaks:** whenever you reconnect a cylinder or smell gas.
  Open the cylinder valve fully and brush a soapy solution, one part
  water to one part liquid detergent, on the connections; bubbles mean a
  leak. Turn off the gas and tighten the connection; if that does not
  stop it, close the cylinder valve and take the grill to your LP gas
  dealer or a qualified appliance repair person. Never use a flame to
  look for a leak: the fact sheet says no lighted cigarettes, matches or
  open flames near a leaking grill.
- **If the grill's flame goes out:** turn off the gas at the cylinder,
  open the lid and wait at least 5 to 10 minutes for the gas to clear
  before relighting, because propane can build up under the grill racks.

Two more points from OSHA's rule for LP gas at work, which we take as
good practice at home (our reading): the valves of cylinders in storage
are kept closed, and an "empty" cylinder stored inside is counted as a
full one (29 CFR 1910.110(f)(2)(iv) and (v)). An empty cylinder still
holds gas (our reading of why).

### Natural gas

Piped to the house, so you never handle it, only live with it.

- **How it hurts people:** a leak indoors meets a spark: a light switch,
  a garage door opener, a phone, a pilot light. Ready.gov: "Natural gas
  leaks and explosions cause a significant number of fires after
  disasters."
- **Know your shutoff before you need it.** Ready.gov says every member
  of the household should know how to shut off the gas, that the
  procedure depends on your meter so your gas company should show you,
  and not to actually turn the gas off when practising. If you do turn
  it off: "only a qualified professional can turn it back on. NEVER
  attempt to turn the gas back on yourself."
- **Dig with care.** Gas service lines run underground to the meter.
  PHMSA: "Every digging project requires that you call the one-call
  center before digging," even planting trees or shrubs, and dialling
  811 reaches it; the call and the marking are free.

### Charcoal

Clean-looking once it is glowing, and lethal indoors. The CPSC:
"Never burn charcoal in homes, tents, vehicles, or garages." The federal
labelling rule for charcoal sold in shops requires a carbon monoxide
warning on the bag, "Because inhalation of the carbon monoxide produced
by burning charcoal indoors or in confined areas can cause serious injury
or death" (16 CFR 1500.14(b)(6)(ii)(A)). Lighter fluid goes on before the
charcoal is lit and never afterwards (CPSC), and cooled coals go in a
metal can with a lid (USFA). [Heating a Home
Safely](heating_a_home_safely.md) explains why a grill is never a heater.

### Wood and wood pellets

Wood is covered in [Firewood](firewood.md): moisture, creosote, chimney
fires, ashes and carbon monoxide.

Bulk wood pellets have a hazard few people expect. In a 2012 safety
alert, Britain's Health and Safety Executive reported at least nine deaths
in Europe since 2002 from carbon monoxide after people entered wood
pellet storage areas, three of them since 2010 at what it calls domestic
sites, one a householder who went into the storeroom for his boiler.
Stored pellets give off carbon monoxide and use up oxygen as they age,
more when they are freshly made and when they are warm. Its advice
(restated): never enter a pellet store or put your head into a pellet
hopper; keep the store ventilated; and before anyone goes in, ventilate
it and check the carbon monoxide and oxygen levels with a suitable
meter. A few bags of pellets in a ventilated shed are a very different
thing from a sealed bulk store, but the same gas comes off them (our
reading).

### Alcohol, lamp oil, camp fuel and gel fuel

These are covered fully in [Fire Performance
Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md): methanol's nearly invisible
flame and its poison, gel fuels that splatter when refilled, lamp oil
that looks like a drink to a child, and the flash point of each.

## Storing fuel at home

Put together from the rules above, a household's fuel store looks like
this (our summary of the sources):

- **A detached shed or an outdoor cabinet,** away from the house: not
  the house, the basement or a garage, and never beside a gas water
  heater or anything else with a flame (USFA, CPSC).
- **Shaded and ventilated,** away from the sun and from anything with a
  flame, a pilot light or a motor that sparks (USFA, CPSC).
- **Each fuel in its own proper container,** closed and labelled:
  gasoline in a red can with a child-resistant cap, kerosene in a blue
  one, propane cylinders upright with their valves closed (CPSC, OSHA).
- **Only as much as you will use** in a season (general practice).
- **Out of reach of children** (USFA).
- **An extinguisher rated for flammable liquids nearby,** class B or a
  multipurpose A-B-C (the USFA's classes; keeping one by the store is
  general practice). For a garage, the USFA suggests a heat alarm rather
  than a smoke alarm.

## Moving fuel

- **Fill on the ground,** never in a car, a trunk or a truck bed (USFA;
  [Fire Performance Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) explains
  the static).
- **Closed, upright and stable** in the vehicle, and taken out as soon as
  you get home, to a cool, ventilated place away from children (CPSC,
  restated from a page summary).
- **Propane cylinders secured upright,** never left in a hot car (CPSC).

## Spills

- **Small spills:** the USFA: clean the area, and put the containers in
  an airy place. Soak up anything more than a splash with sand or cat
  litter, as [Fire Performance
  Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md) describes, and keep every
  flame and spark away until the vapour has cleared (our reading of
  CAMEO's flashback warning).
- **A spill you cannot control:** the USFA: "If you cannot control the
  spill, or are unsure about cleanup and disposal, call the fire
  department."
- **Fuel on your clothes:** change before you go near any flame; a splash
  of kerosene or diesel stays wet and burns like a wick ([Fire
  Performance Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md)).
- **Rags:** rags with oil-based finishes can heat up and catch fire by
  themselves; [Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md) says
  how to store them. The USFA puts fires from spontaneous combustion or
  chemical reaction at an average of 1,700 home fires a year.
- **Old fuel and empty containers:** a household hazardous waste
  collection, never a drain or the ground ([What Not to Compost, Burn or
  Pour Away](what_not_to_compost_burn_or_pour_away.md)).

## Leaks: if you smell gas

PHMSA's instructions for the "rotten egg" smell of natural gas in a home:

- **Do not** start an engine of any kind, strike a match or make any
  flame, use a telephone or mobile phone, or turn any light switch,
  garage door opener or other electrical switch on or off: "these can
  ignite airborne gases."
- **Do** make sure gas appliances are turned all the way off, leave the
  area, telephone 911 from a neighbour's house or somewhere well away from
  the leak, explain the situation, and warn others against going in.

Ready.gov's version, for any household: if you smell gas or hear a
blowing or hissing noise, open a window and get everyone out quickly;
turn off the gas at the outside main valve if you can; and call the gas
company from a neighbour's home. Getting everyone out comes first; if the
smell is strong, do not stop for the appliances or the window (our
reading of the two together). Then stay out yourself until the gas
company or the fire department says the house is safe (our reading of
PHMSA's warning to keep everyone out of the leak area). If the gas was
turned off, only a qualified professional turns it back on (Ready.gov).

A propane leak gets the same response (our reading of PHMSA's advice,
which is written for natural gas), with one difference: propane sinks, so
it gathers in basements, crawlspaces and other low places. Never go down
into one to look for the leak (our reading).

## When fuel is burning

- **Get out and call 911.** The USFA's extinguisher checklist, in [Making
  and Controlling Fire](making_and_controlling_fire.md), allows fighting
  only a small fire in one object, with everyone warned, the fire
  department called and a clear way out. A fire fed by a can of fuel is
  rarely that small for long (our reading).
- **No water on burning liquid fuel:** it can spread it ([Fire
  Performance Fuels](../making/fire_performance_fuels.md)). The CPSC's
  kerosene heater label says not to move the heater, try to smother it or
  put water on it, and to get out and call the fire department.
- **A propane cylinder in or near a fire:** get everyone well away and
  tell the fire department it is there; CAMEO's warning that such
  containers may rupture violently and rocket is the reason (our reading).

## Worked example: sorting out a household's fuel

An illustration with made-up details. A household has a petrol mower, a
small generator for outages, a gas grill, a kerosene heater for the
workshop and a bag of charcoal. Today the fuel is where it landed.

1. **What they find.** A plastic milk jug of gasoline by the furnace in
   the basement, an old red can with no cap on the spout in the attached
   garage beside the gas water heater, a spare propane cylinder under the
   grill on the deck, a blue can of kerosene, and a half-used bag of
   charcoal by the back door.
2. **The milk jug.** It is the most dangerous thing in the house: wrong
   container, gasoline vapour, a burner a few feet away. They carry it
   out with the windows open and nothing switched on or off near it, pour
   it into a proper can outdoors, on the ground and away from anything
   that could spark, and take the jug to the household hazardous waste
   collection (rules 1, 2 and 5; what to do with the jug is general
   practice).
3. **The cans.** A new gasoline can with a child-resistant cap and a
   flame arrestor, filled on the ground at the station and carried home
   upright and closed in the trunk, then straight out to the shed. The
   blue can is for kerosene only, and they mark the mower's red can and
   the heater's blue can so nobody ever mixes them up (CPSC).
4. **The shed.** A small ventilated shed 30 feet from the house: the
   gasoline, the kerosene and the charcoal on a shelf, a class B rated
   extinguisher by the door, nothing that sparks inside.
5. **The propane.** The spare cylinder moves from under the grill to an
   upright spot outdoors, away from the grill and the house, valve closed
   (CPSC, OSHA). They brush soapy water on the grill's connection after
   reconnecting the cylinder; no bubbles.
6. **Refuelling.** The generator is refuelled only after it has been off
   and cooled, outdoors, from the can, on the ground (rule 4). The
   kerosene heater is filled outdoors when cold, to below the full mark
   ([Heating a Home Safely](heating_a_home_safely.md)).
7. **The plan.** Everyone knows where the gas meter is and has watched the
   gas company show them the shutoff, and knows that once it is off only
   a qualified professional, such as the gas company, turns it back on
   (Ready.gov).

## Where your own work stops

Each of these belongs to the gas company, a licensed professional, the
fire department or a hazardous waste collection. That is our
recommendation for a household, built on the sources given with each.

- **Gas meters, regulators, lines and appliance connections:** the gas
  company or a qualified installer. After any shutoff, only a qualified
  professional turns the gas back on (Ready.gov).
- **Filling a propane cylinder:** the dealer or a qualified service
  station operator, never you (CPSC).
- **A grill or appliance that still leaks after you tighten it:** close
  the cylinder valve and take it to the dealer or a qualified repair
  person (CPSC).
- **Any smell of gas indoors:** out, call 911 or the gas company from
  away from the house, and back in only when they say so (PHMSA,
  Ready.gov; staying out until then is our reading).
- **A spill you cannot control,** or one you do not know how to clean up:
  the fire department (USFA).
- **Any fuel fire bigger than a wastebasket:** 911 (USFA's checklist).
- **Digging anywhere:** call 811 first (PHMSA).
- **Bulk pellet stores and boilers:** installed and serviced by a
  competent person, and entered only after ventilating and testing the
  air (UK Health and Safety Executive, restated).
- **Old fuel, leaking cans and corroded containers:** a household
  hazardous waste collection or the fire department ([What Not to
  Compost, Burn or Pour Away](what_not_to_compost_burn_or_pour_away.md)).

## Traps

- **"It's only diesel, it won't burn."** A rag or a sleeve soaked in it
  will.
- **"The can is empty."** An empty can is full of vapour; an "empty"
  propane cylinder still holds gas.
- **"A splash of gas will get the brush pile going."** That is how flame
  jetting and flash fires happen.
- **"The garage is basically outside."** Not with a gas water heater in
  it, and the CPSC says not in a garage at all.
- **"I'll top up the heater with what's in the red can."** Even a little
  gasoline in a kerosene heater raises the risk of fire or explosion
  (CPSC).
- **"Any jug will do."** The CPSC calls jugs and bags for fuel extremely
  dangerous, and a container that has held fuel is never safe for
  drinking water again, however well it is rinsed ([Storing Water
  Safely](storing_water_safely.md)).
- **"I'll find the leak with a lighter."** Soapy water, and never a flame.
- **"I'll switch the light on to see where the smell is coming from."**
  The switch can be the spark.
- **"I'll just suck on the hose to get it started."** Gasoline in the
  lungs can kill.

## How the game models it

The game has fuels to make and burn, and models what a liquid fuel can
be stored in and how much a generator burns. It models none of the
hazards.

- **Refined Fuel.** The home has a fuel refinery in its forge room that
  turns crude oil into Refined Fuel (its recipe: two crude oil into two
  Refined Fuel, 30 seconds). It runs by itself, takes its crude oil from
  home storage only, never from your backpack, and fills its own 200
  litre drum. The recipe asks for Engineering level 2, but a machine
  that runs by itself skips that check: owning the machine is enough
  (`data/recipes.csv`, `data/machines/home.ron`,
  `src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`). The home's backup generator has a 200
  litre drum of its own, and you move fuel from one drum to the other
  through your backpack, with the Take and Store buttons on each
  machine's card.
- **Charcoal.** Coal, the game's charcoal, is listed as a fuel too, and
  recipes at the smelter, kiln and forge use it up, several of them
  leaving wood ash (`data/items.csv`, `data/recipes.csv`); [Making and
  Controlling Fire](making_and_controlling_fire.md) has more. It is not
  in the flammable class, so it never goes in a drum and the generator
  cannot burn it.
- **Containers remember.** The game sorts what a container may hold by
  class, and fuel, oil and solvents are the "flammable" class, needing a
  sealed vessel. A container that has once held fuel can never hold food
  or drinking water again, even after it is cleaned, though it can still
  hold fuel, and its machine card says so in a warning line
  (`data/containers/content_classes.ron`,
  `src/systems/inventory/containers.rs`, `src/lib.rs`). That is the real
  rule too: see [Storing Water Safely](storing_water_safely.md).
- **A generator that burns anything flammable.** The backup generator is
  modelled on a 1.8 kilowatt inverter set burning 1.125 litres an hour
  while it runs, and it runs only when its circuit is short of power and
  the batteries on that circuit are below a quarter full, or there are
  none (`data/machines/home.ron`, `src/systems/electrical.rs`). It burns
  whatever flammable-class item is in its drum: Refined Fuel, but also
  Crude Oil, Glue and Paint, which its Store button offers too, so in
  this build a can of paint can run the house (`data/items.csv`). That is
  a known bug, BUG-154; a real generator runs only on the fuel its engine
  was made for (general knowledge). Under Settings > Gameplay > "Ship
  life support", the default, Station-supplied, ties a circuit with a
  feed from the ship to the ship's reactor, which counts as its supply,
  so a backup generator on such a circuit never needs to run.
- **No hazards at all.** There are no flash points, vapour, spills,
  leaks, static, fuel fires or carbon monoxide. The generator and its drum
  sit indoors, in the home's plant room, and make no exhaust; in real life
  that is the arrangement that kills people in a power cut. The game's
  data includes a table of gases with their flammable ranges
  (`data/chemistry/gases.csv`), but no game system reads it yet.
- **Starting from the default home.** Settings > Gameplay > "Start every
  session from the default home" is off by default, so the fuel you
  refined or moved stays in the home's drums between launches. With the
  setting on, only your character carries between launches, and the next
  session starts with the default home's machines and drums as they were,
  whatever fuel you refined or moved.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the smell
of a leak, vapour creeping to a pilot light, the can left in the sun, the
child who drinks from the jug, and the generator that has to run outside.

## You own this when

- No fuel is stored in your home, and each fuel outside is in its own
  proper, labelled, closed container.
- You can say why gasoline's flash point, its flammable range and the
  weight of its vapour make a basement can dangerous.
- You fill cans on the ground, carry them closed and upright, and take
  them out of the car when you get home.
- You refuel engines and heaters only when they are off and cold, and
  never put anything but 1-K kerosene in a kerosene heater.
- You know propane's rules: upright, outdoors, never filled by you, and
  leak-tested with soapy water, never a flame.
- You know where your gas shutoff is, and that once it is off only a
  qualified professional, usually the gas company, turns it back on.
- You would never light charcoal indoors, or pour liquid fuel on a fire.
- If you smell gas, you get everyone out without touching a switch, call
  from outside, and nobody goes back in until the gas company or the
  fire department says so.
- You know where your nearest household hazardous waste collection is,
  and to call 811 before you dig.
