# What Soil Is

Put a spade into the ground and turn it over. What comes up tells you
more about what will grow there than the seed packet does. Soil is not
an inert material that holds plants upright while you add fertilizer.
It is ground-up rock, dead things, water, air, and a very large amount
of life, and the proportions of those five decide most of what you
will experience as a gardener: how often you water, whether a bed
drains after rain, how deep roots go, and whether the fertilizer you
bought does anything at all.

This guide is about reading what you already have. It is the thing to
understand before you test your soil, before you plan a planting
calendar, and before you spend money correcting a problem you have not
identified. Nothing here is hazardous, and nothing here needs
equipment. Most of it you can do this afternoon with your hands, a
spade, and a jar.

## What soil is actually made of

Take a spadeful of good topsoil and sort it by volume. USDA Natural
Resources Conservation Service describes the surface layer as roughly
half solids and half pore space. Of the whole volume, about 45 percent
is mineral particles, meaning ground-up rock; about 5 percent or less
is organic matter; and the remaining 50 percent is pore space, filled
with water and air in a ratio that changes every time it rains.

Four things follow from that, and they are worth sitting with.

**Half of good soil is nothing.** The pore space is not wasted volume,
it is the working part. Roots grow into it, water moves through it,
and air reaches the roots and the microbes through it. When you
compact soil, what you destroy is this half.

**Water and air share one space.** They are in direct competition. A
soil holding all the water it can hold is a soil with no air in it,
which is why waterlogged ground kills plants that are not adapted to
it. NRCS notes that once more than about 80 percent of the pore space
is filled with water, soil respiration falls to a minimum and nitrogen
starts being lost to the air as gas.

**Organic matter is the smallest ingredient by a wide margin**, at 5
percent or less, and it matters far out of proportion to that. There
is a section on it below.

**It is alive, and that is not a metaphor.** NRCS defines soil health
as "the continued capacity of soil to function as a vital living
ecosystem that sustains plants, animals, and humans." The bacteria,
fungi, worms and arthropods in the ground are not passengers. They are
what turns dead material into plant food and glues loose particles
into crumbs.

## Sand, silt and clay are sizes, not substances

This is the single most useful thing in the guide, and almost everyone
gets it slightly wrong at first. Sand, silt and clay are not three
different materials. They are three size ranges of the same ground-up
rock. USDA defines them by diameter:

| Name | Diameter | How it behaves in your hand |
|---|---|---|
| Sand | 2.0 down to 0.05 mm | Gritty. You can see individual grains with the naked eye |
| Silt | 0.05 down to 0.002 mm | Smooth, like flour. Invisible without magnification |
| Clay | smaller than 0.002 mm | Sticky when wet, hard when dry |

Anything 2 mm or larger is not counted in the texture at all. USDA
calls the material below 2 mm the "fine earth" and treats everything
above it separately as rock fragments, which are described by how much
of the soil volume they occupy.

Those three lines look like a small difference. They are not. Because
the particles get smaller by roughly a thousandfold from sand to clay,
the surface area goes up enormously. NC State Extension publishes a
comparison that makes the point better than any argument: one gram of
coarse sand offers a surface area of about 23 square centimeters,
while one gram of clay offers about 8 million.

Surface area is where everything happens in soil. Water clings to
surfaces. Nutrients stick to surfaces as charged ions. Microbes live
on surfaces. So a clay particle does thousands of times more chemistry
than a sand grain of the same weight, and a soil's clay content
largely decides how much water and how many nutrients it can hold.

The flip side is drainage. Water moves fast through the large gaps
between sand grains and slowly through the tiny gaps between clay
plates. NRCS gives these average steady-state infiltration rates, the
speed at which water soaks in once the soil is already wet:

| Soil type | Water soaks in at |
|---|---|
| Sand | more than 0.8 inches per hour |
| Sandy and silty soils | 0.4 to 0.8 inches per hour |
| Loam | 0.2 to 0.4 inches per hour |
| Clayey soils | 0.04 to 0.2 inches per hour |

Those are averages and NRCS is explicit that they should not be
generalized to all soils, but the spread is the point: water enters a
sandy soil several times faster than a clayey one, and at the extremes
of that table more than ten times faster.

This is why the same watering habit produces a drought in one garden
and root rot in another. Sandy ground drains fast, dries fast, warms
early in spring, and loses nutrients downward with the water. Clay
ground holds water and nutrients, stays cold and wet late into spring,
and turns to a brick when it dries. Silt sits between the two: it
holds more water than sand and lets it through faster than clay.

One honest complication: clay holds a lot of water but does not give
all of it back. NRCS points out that a great deal of the water held by
clay is bound too tightly for plants to use, while organic matter can
hold up to 90 percent of its own weight in water and releases nearly
all of it. A clay soil can be wet and a plant in it can still be
thirsty.

## Texture is the proportions, and you are not going to change it

Soil texture is simply the percentage of sand, silt and clay, by
weight, in the fine earth. USDA sorts every possible combination into
twelve texture classes: sands, loamy sands, sandy loams, loam, silt
loam, silt, sandy clay loam, clay loam, silty clay loam, sandy clay,
silty clay, and clay. They are laid out on a triangular chart, the
texture triangle, where each corner is 100 percent of one separate.

Note where "loam" sits. A loam is not an equal mix and it is not a
quality rating. USDA defines it as 7 to less than 27 percent clay, 28
to less than 50 percent silt, and 52 percent or less sand. It is a
particular corner of the chart that happens to balance the useful
behaviors of all three sizes.

Texture is effectively permanent. NC State Extension makes the
arithmetic vivid: a typical mineral soil 6 inches deep across one acre
weighs about 2 million pounds, so shifting the sand content by one
percentage point means adding 10 tons of sand, and a change you would
actually notice means something closer to 100 tons.

So stop trying to fix texture. You cannot afford it and it will not
work. The sand you dig into a clay bed does not make it sandy, it fills
the gaps and makes it denser. What you can change, and change a great
deal, is structure.

## Structure is a different thing, and compaction is what quietly ruins ground

Texture is what the particles *are*. Structure is how they are
*arranged*. Soil particles stick together into units, and USDA calls a
unit that formed through soil development a ped. The surfaces of peds
survive repeated wetting and drying, which is what distinguishes them
from a clod you made with a plow.

Good topsoil typically has granular structure, roughly rounded crumbs.
NC State Extension describes it as looking like chocolate cookie
crumbs, which is exactly right and is the image to hold. Those crumbs
leave large gaps between them for air and water, and small gaps inside
them that hold water. Soil with no structure at all is either loose
single grains, like beach sand, or massive, a single coherent block.

Structure is what compaction destroys, and compaction is the failure
that does the most damage while announcing itself the least. It has no
symptom of its own. It shows up as poor drainage, stunted plants,
puddles that sit for days, roots that turn sideways at a certain depth,
and it gets blamed on all sorts of other things.

The measurement behind it is bulk density, the oven-dry weight of soil
per unit volume. NRCS publishes the values at which roots start to
struggle, and they depend on texture:

| Texture | Ideal for plant growth | Restricts root growth |
|---|---|---|
| Sand, loamy sand | under 1.60 g/cm3 | over 1.80 g/cm3 |
| Sandy loam, loam | under 1.40 g/cm3 | over 1.80 g/cm3 |
| Silt, silt loam | under 1.40 g/cm3 | over 1.75 g/cm3 |
| Clay, more than 45 percent clay | under 1.10 g/cm3 | over 1.47 g/cm3 |

NRCS notes the table does not apply to red clayey soils or to volcanic
ash soils, which matters in parts of the western United States.

You will not measure this at home, and you do not need to. The useful
lesson is in the shape of the table: clay soils reach a
root-restricting density at a far lower number than sandy soils do,
which is the technical version of "clay compacts easily and sand does
not." Clay particles are small and plate-shaped, so pressure slides
them into alignment, especially when wet.

Which gives you the two rules that actually protect soil, both of them
free. Do not work soil when it is wet. Do not walk on your beds. NRCS
also notes that bulk density normally rises with depth anyway, because
deeper layers have less organic matter and less root activity, so the
subsoil starts out denser before you add anything to it.

## Horizons, and what topsoil really means

Dig a deep enough hole and you will see the soil change in bands as
you go down. Those bands are horizons, and USDA gives them letters:

- **O** is an organic layer, mostly leaves, needles, twigs and moss in
  various stages of decay, lying on top.
- **A** is the mineral topsoil: rock particles thoroughly mixed with
  humified organic matter. It is usually the darkest layer and holds
  most of the roots.
- **E** is a bleached, lighter layer, found in some soils, where clay,
  iron and aluminum have been washed downward, leaving sand and silt
  behind.
- **B** is the subsoil, where the material washed out of the layers
  above accumulates. It is often more clayey, denser, and a different
  color.
- **C** is little-altered parent material: the sediment, weathered
  rock or geologic deposit the soil formed in.
- **R** is bedrock, coherent enough that hand-digging with a spade is
  impractical.

Not every soil has all of them, and USDA sets the lower boundary of
soil, for classification purposes, at an arbitrary 200 cm.

"Topsoil" in the ground means the O and A horizons, and it is where
the organic matter, the biology and the roots are. "Topsoil" in a bag
or on a truck means whatever the seller decided to call topsoil. NC
State Extension warns plainly that there are no standards for material
sold under that name, that it can bring in weed seeds and disease, and
that dumping a layer of it over compacted ground creates a new
drainage problem where the two layers meet. Buying topsoil is not a
shortcut past the work.

## pH, and why most crops want a narrow band

pH measures how acid or alkaline the soil solution is. Seven is
neutral, lower is acid, higher is alkaline. The scale is logarithmic,
so each whole number is a tenfold change: pH 5 is a hundred times more
acid than pH 7, not 40 percent more.

USDA puts the full range of soils from slightly below 2.0 to slightly
above 11.0, and names the bands like this:

| Class | pH |
|---|---|
| Extremely acid | 3.5 to 4.4 |
| Very strongly acid | 4.5 to 5.0 |
| Strongly acid | 5.1 to 5.5 |
| Moderately acid | 5.6 to 6.0 |
| Slightly acid | 6.1 to 6.5 |
| Neutral | 6.6 to 7.3 |
| Slightly alkaline | 7.4 to 7.8 |
| Moderately alkaline | 7.9 to 8.4 |

The scale continues past both ends of that table. USDA calls anything
below 3.5 ultra acid and anything above 9.0 very strongly alkaline.
Soils that extreme do exist, but they are uncommon and they are not
the sort of thing a bag of lime fixes.

pH matters because it controls what is chemically available, not what
is present. A soil can hold plenty of a nutrient that the plant cannot
take up because the pH has locked it away. NC State Extension notes
that lowering pH increases the solubility of manganese, zinc, copper
and iron, and that below about 5.5 those, along with aluminum, can
reach levels that damage plants. So a very acid soil can poison a crop
as well as starve it.

Most food crops want the slightly acid to neutral band. NC State
Extension describes a pH of 6.5 to 7.0 as what is often considered
ideal for most plants, while adding the caveat worth repeating: the
optimum differs by species, and it is worth looking up the plants you
actually intend to grow. Blueberries and azaleas are genuinely happy
near 5.0. Asparagus tolerates up to about 8.0.

What makes soil acid is mostly rain and time. NC State Extension
describes soils across the southeastern United States being acidified
over thousands of years by acids arriving in rainfall, by the decay of
plant and animal residues, and by the steady washing of basic cations
such as calcium and magnesium down out of the root zone. Where rainfall
is low, those bases are not washed away, carbonates accumulate, and
soils tend alkaline instead. The parent rock matters too: soil formed
over limestone starts with a large reserve of calcium carbonate.

Raising pH means adding lime, lowering it usually means adding
elemental sulfur, and both are slow and easy to overdo. That belongs
to Testing and Correcting Soil, not here. The point of this section is
that you should know your number before you buy anything.

### A regional note: the Pacific Northwest leans acid

The canonical world for this project sits in the Pacific Northwest,
and forest soils there tend toward the acid end. You can check that
against the actual survey rather than taking anyone's word for it.
USDA's Official Series Descriptions give the reaction range for each
mapped soil, and three of the common Puget lowland soils, all formed
under Douglas fir, western hemlock, western redcedar and red alder,
read as follows: Alderwood, pH 5.1 to 6.5; Everett, pH 4.5 to 6.0;
Kitsap, pH 5.1 to 7.3. Alderwood receives about 1000 mm of rain a
year, which is the mechanism in one number.

That is a regional fact, not a universal one, and even within that
region Kitsap runs up into the neutral range. If you garden elsewhere,
look up your own ground in USDA's Web Soil Survey rather than assuming
it behaves the same way.

## Organic matter, the 5 percent that does the heavy lifting

Organic matter is the decomposing and decomposed remains of living
things, plus the living organisms themselves. NRCS calls it the most
important single indicator of soil health, and for a component that is
5 percent or less of the volume, the list of what it does is
absurdly long: it holds water like a sponge and gives nearly all of it
back, it feeds the microbes, it releases nutrients as it breaks down,
it buffers acidity, it glues particles into the aggregates that give
soil its structure, it reduces crusting and compaction, and it makes
water soak in rather than run off.

Some numbers to anchor it. NRCS reports that soils holding 2 to 5
percent organic matter can maintain that level under ordinary
management, and that in temperate regions roughly 1.5 percent of the
organic matter present mineralizes each year, which is to say it is
continuously being consumed and must be continuously replaced. Each
percentage point of organic matter in the top 6 inches of a
medium-textured soil releases roughly 10 to 20 pounds of nitrogen, 1 to
2 pounds of phosphorus and 0.4 to 0.8 pounds of sulfur per acre per
year.

And the sobering one, which explains why building organic matter is a
long game: an acre of soil 6 inches deep weighs about 2 million pounds,
so one percent of organic matter is about 20,000 pounds of it. Since
roughly 10 pounds of raw organic material decomposes down to 1 pound
of stable organic matter, raising an acre by a single percentage point
takes on the order of 100 tons of material. You do this over years, with
compost and mulch and roots left in the ground, or you do not do it.

There is a reason forest soil is often less rich than it looks. NRCS
explains that soils under forest commonly carry relatively low organic
matter, because trees put far less root mass into the ground than
grasses do and do not die back and decompose every year. Most of the
carbon in a forest is standing up in the trunks. Grass, by contrast,
dies back top and root every season and puts that material straight
into the upper soil, which is why grassland soils are the deep dark
ones.

## How to tell what you have, today

### The ribbon test

This is the method soil surveyors actually use in the field, and USDA
publishes the flowchart for it. It takes practice, and it is worth the
practice, because it needs nothing but your hands and a little water.

1. Take about 25 grams of soil into your palm, roughly a heaped
   tablespoon, and pick out any stones.
2. Add water a few drops at a time and knead it until every lump is
   broken down. It is ready when it feels like moist putty: plastic and
   moldable, not runny.
3. Squeeze it into a ball. If it will not hold a ball at all, and it is
   neither too wet nor too dry, it is **sand**. Stop.
4. Put the ball between your thumb and forefinger and push it upward
   with your thumb into a flat ribbon of even thickness, letting the
   ribbon hang out over your finger until it breaks under its own
   weight. If it will not form a ribbon at all, it is **loamy sand**.
   Stop.
5. Measure the ribbon you got before it broke. Under 2.5 cm is a weak
   ribbon, 2.5 to 5 cm is medium, over 5 cm is strong. More clay makes
   a longer ribbon.
6. Now wet a fresh pinch of the soil thoroughly in your palm and rub it
   with a finger. Judge whether it feels distinctly gritty, distinctly
   smooth, or neither.

Put the two answers together. Weak ribbon plus gritty is a sandy loam;
weak plus smooth is a silt loam; weak plus neither is a loam. Medium
ribbon gives you the three clay loams in the same order: sandy clay
loam, silty clay loam, clay loam. A strong ribbon gives you sandy clay,
silty clay, or clay.

USDA is candid that this is a skill rather than a measurement, and that
certain soils will fool you. In humid climates clay can be cemented by
iron oxide into aggregates that feel like fine sand until you rub them
for a long time, and some granite-derived soils contain soft grains
that only reveal themselves as clay under prolonged rubbing. If a
result surprises you, rub harder and try again.

### The jar test

The jar test is the other method people are taught, and it works on the
same principle as the laboratory hydrometer method USDA uses: bigger
particles settle out of water faster than small ones. Put two or three
inches of soil in a straight-sided jar, pick out the stones and roots,
fill the jar most of the way with water, add a small squirt of
dishwashing liquid to stop the clay clumping, cap it, and shake it hard
for several minutes. Then set it down and leave it alone. The sand
drops out almost immediately, the silt settles over the next few hours,
and the clay may take a day or more to clear. Measure the three bands
and you have rough percentages to take to the texture triangle.

Treat the timings as a rule of thumb rather than a measured standard.
The published settling times vary from source to source, organic matter
floats rather than settling and will sit on top, and the same cemented
clay aggregates that fool the ribbon test will settle out early here
and read as sand. The jar test is a good way to *see* your soil's
three fractions separated out, which is genuinely worth doing once. The
ribbon test is the one to learn for real use.

### Look at the structure while you are there

Dig a spadeful from a spot you have not walked on and let it fall apart
on its own. Does it break into rounded crumbs, or into hard angular
blocks, or not at all? Crumbs are good structure. A solid mass that
needs force to break, especially with a flat, smooth face where it
gives way, suggests compaction. Look for where the roots stop, and for
whether they turn sideways at a particular depth, which usually means
they have hit something they cannot get through.

## One thing to check before you grow food in unfamiliar ground

Soil texture and pH are gardening problems. Contamination is a health
problem, and it is invisible.

EPA notes that lead levels are typically higher in soil near roads,
because of decades of leaded gasoline exhaust; along the dripline of
buildings painted before 1978; and near industrial sites, smelters and
hazardous waste sites. Their practical advice for growing food is
worth following: test the soil you intend to use for food crops; keep
food beds at least 10 feet away from buildings, roads and driveways;
and use raised beds or containers of clean soil where levels are
higher. EPA also notes that root vegetables such as carrots and leafy
greens take up more lead than fruiting crops like tomatoes, peppers
and cucumbers, and that produce should be washed thoroughly, root crops
peeled, and the outer leaves of leafy vegetables discarded.

Many state university extension offices will test soil for lead for a
modest fee, and that is the right place to start if you have any doubt
about the history of the ground.

## So what is "good soil"?

The honest answer is that it depends what you are growing, and anyone
who gives you a single answer is selling something.

A blueberry wants soil at about pH 5.0, which NC State Extension gives
as its preference and which would starve most vegetables. Asparagus, by
the same source, tolerates up to about 8.0. Beyond those two, the
familiar examples are ordinary gardeners' knowledge rather than
measured values, and each is worth confirming for the variety you
actually plan to grow: that carrots want loose, deep, stone-free ground
because the root forks around any obstacle, that rice wants the standing
water that would kill a tomato, and that lavender and most Mediterranean
herbs want the fast-draining, lean ground a squash would sulk in.

What is nearly always true is narrower and less exciting: a soil that
lets water in, holds some of it without staying saturated, lets air
reach the roots, has enough organic matter to feed its own biology and
hold itself together in crumbs, and sits at a pH where the nutrients
present are actually available. Get those and the texture you happen to
have stops mattering very much. Which is the useful conclusion, because
the texture is the one thing you were never going to change anyway.

You now know what to call what you have, why it behaves as it does, and
which of its properties you can move. The next step is to measure the
things you cannot judge by hand, which is pH and nutrients, and that is
Testing and Correcting Soil.
