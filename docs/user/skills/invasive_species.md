# Invasive Species

Most of the living things around a home belong where they are. The
moss, the alders, the voles and the woodpeckers grew up together over
thousands of years, and each has its checks: something that eats it,
something that competes with it, a season that does not suit it. A few
plants and animals arrived only recently, nearly always carried by
people, and found a place with none of those checks. Some of them take
over. They crowd out what was there, kill trees, clog water pipes, eat
crops, carry disease or, in a few cases, hurt the person who touches
them.

This guide is about recognising those few, keeping them from spreading,
and knowing what is yours to do about them and what is not. It is also
about something easier to forget: you, your boots, your firewood, your
trailer and your fish tank are how most invasive species travel.

Its definitions come from the federal executive order on invasive
species, published in the Federal Register, and from the National
Invasive Species Information Center, run by the US Department of
Agriculture's National Agricultural Library. Its pests and their signs
come from the USDA's Animal and Plant Health Inspection Service (APHIS),
its water and pet advice from the US Fish and Wildlife Service (FWS), its
safety advice from the National Park Service (NPS), the Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA), the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
(CDC) and the Health Resources and Services Administration (HRSA), and
its rules from the Code of Federal Regulations. Those are United States
government works, in the public domain. The worked examples of a state's
rules come from the Washington State Noxious Weed Control Board, because
this project's sample place, Silverdale, is in Washington; a state body's
pages are copyrighted, so its facts are restated in our own words. Where
something is general practice or our own reading, the text says so.

This guide sits beside [How an Ecosystem Holds
Together](how_an_ecosystem_holds_together.md), which looks closely at how
four invasive plants around Silverdale change the structure of the land,
and [The Species Where You Live](the_species_where_you_live.md), which is
how you learn what belongs. [Taking Without Ruining
It](taking_without_ruining_it.md) covers the seeds you carry on your boots,
and [Pests and Disease](pests_and_disease.md) covers garden pests,
including what to do when one looks new.

## What "invasive" means, exactly

The federal definition is in Executive Order 13112, as amended in
December 2016 by Executive Order 13751. An invasive species is, with
regard to a particular ecosystem, "a non-native organism whose
introduction causes or is likely to cause economic or environmental harm,
or harm to human, animal, or plant health."

The same order defines the words inside that sentence:

- **Non-native** means an organism, including "its seeds, eggs, spores,
  or other biological material capable of propagating that species", that
  is outside its natural range.
- **Introduction** means, as a result of human activity, the intentional
  or unintentional escape, release, spreading or placement of an organism
  into an ecosystem where it is not native.

Read closely, that is three tests at once (our reading): the species is
not native here; people brought it, on purpose or by accident; and it
does harm, or is likely to.

The third test is the one people forget. **Non-native does not mean
invasive.** The National Invasive Species Information Center (NISIC) is
careful about this: tomatoes and hot peppers came from South America,
lettuce was first grown by the Egyptians, and domestic cows are not native
to North America either, but in a farm setting they are considered
beneficial. Most of a vegetable garden is non-native, and that is fine.

The first test has a catch of its own: **with regard to a particular
ecosystem.** A species can be native in one place and invasive in another,
and even native in one strain and invasive in another. [How an Ecosystem
Holds Together](how_an_ecosystem_holds_together.md) walks through reed
canarygrass, whose invasive populations in North America are thought to be
non-native strains or hybrids of a grass that also has native strains.

The project's sample place shows the proportions. Of the 133 species in
the Silverdale locale data, 106 are recorded as native, 13 as introduced
(brought by people, but not designated invasive there) and 14 as
invasive.
Invasive species are a small share of what you see. They matter because
of what they do.

## How they arrive

NISIC says invasive species are spread primarily by human activities,
often unintended. It names the main routes: ships' ballast water,
firewood, accidental release and people; insects in wood, shipping pallets
and crates; garden plants that escape into the wild; and unwanted pets
let loose. Four real cases show the range.

**In firewood.** The emerald ash borer, a beetle from Asia, was first
found in North America in 2002 in south-east Michigan. APHIS says it has
killed or weakened tens of millions of ash trees and has been detected in
38 states and the District of Columbia. The beetle spreads on its own,
but APHIS says people spread it over long distances when they move
infested ash firewood, logs or nursery stock.

**On anything left outdoors.** The spotted lanternfly, native to Asia, was
first found in Pennsylvania in 2014 and likely arrived on goods shipped
from overseas. APHIS calls it a hitchhiking pest: it lays its eggs on any
hard surface, including grills, vehicles, trailers, firewood, outdoor
furniture, bikes and toys. It is now in 19 states and the District of
Columbia.

**In a shop-bought fish-tank plant.** In March 2021 the US Geological
Survey confirmed a report from a Seattle pet store of invasive zebra
mussels attached to and inside marimo moss balls, a plant product sold for
aquariums. FWS says contaminated moss balls were found in 46 states by 27
April 2021. Retailers pulled them, and to FWS's knowledge no mussels got
into a waterbody from that incident.

**As livestock gone wild.** Feral swine, also called wild pigs, wild boars
or razorbacks, descend from escaped or released pigs that Europeans first
brought to the United States as food, APHIS says, and their numbers have
spread across the country.

And on you. The National Park Service notes that seeds lodge in boots and
cling to clothing, and that dirty cars, boats and trailers carry seeds and
plant fragments; [Taking Without Ruining It](taking_without_ruining_it.md)
has the detail and what to do about it.

## The harm they do

[How an Ecosystem Holds Together](how_an_ecosystem_holds_together.md)
makes the structural case: Himalayan blackberry holding the seedling
layer, Scotch broom locking a site for years, English ivy smothering the
ground and climbing the trunks, reed canarygrass turning a wetland into
grass. NISIC puts the stakes in one line: invasive species can lead to the
extinction of native plants and animals, destroy biodiversity and
permanently alter habitats. The cases above show the other kinds of harm
in the executive order's definition:

- **Money and food.** APHIS puts the minimum annual cost of feral swine
  damage in the United States at $3.4 billion, with an estimated $2.5
  billion of damage and control costs in agriculture alone. The spotted
  lanternfly feeds on grapevines, hops, stone fruit trees and hardwoods,
  and the sugary fluid it excretes grows sooty mould on the plants.
- **Water supplies.** FWS lists what zebra mussels do once established:
  disrupt the food chain, smother native mussels, change the water
  chemistry, clog water intakes and delivery systems, and litter beaches
  with sharp shells.
- **Health.** APHIS says feral swine carry at least 30 viral and bacterial
  diseases and nearly 40 parasites that can pass to people, pets, livestock
  and wildlife, and it found 8 percent of feral swine infected with
  brucellosis, which can sicken livestock and people. And one invasive
  plant, giant hogweed, can burn your skin badly; it has its own section
  below.

## Recognising one

You cannot recognise what does not belong until you know what does. [The
Species Where You Live](the_species_where_you_live.md) is how to build that
knowledge for your own place. On top of it, three kinds of list tell you
what to look for:

- **The federal noxious weed list.** The USDA designates federal noxious
  weeds in 7 CFR 360.200, and giant hogweed is on it. Under 7 CFR 360.300,
  no person may move a federal noxious weed into or through the United
  States, or between states, without a permit.
- **Your state's noxious weed list.** Washington is a worked example. Its
  state weed law (chapter 17.10 of the Revised Code of Washington) has
  the state board keep the official list in three classes. Class A weeds
  are newcomers that are still rare in the state, and landowners must
  eradicate them completely, roots included. Class B weeds are common in
  some parts of the state and absent from others, and a landowner may have
  to control them depending on where they are and what the county board
  has chosen. Class C weeds are already widespread; the state does not
  require their control, though a county may. Washington also keeps a
  quarantine list of plants that may not be sold, bought, traded or
  transported in the state, seed packets and wildflower mixes included,
  with a civil penalty of up to $5,000 per violation. Your own state's
  rules will differ; the county or state weed board is where to ask.
- **Pest alerts.** APHIS publishes a page for each invasive pest it is
  fighting, with what to look for and how to report it.

The rule that comes out of all three: **the rarest invasive species are
the most important to report.** The Washington board makes this point
about its Class A weeds, the ones you are least likely to see; APHIS says
early detection is a critical step against the spotted lanternfly. A
newcomer found early, while there are only a few, can be wiped out. Found
late, it can only be managed.

### What to look for: three examples

**Emerald ash borer**, from APHIS: the adult is a metallic green beetle
with a coppery-red abdomen, about half an inch long. Its larvae feed under
the bark of ash trees and leave S-shaped tracks; the adults come out in
spring through D-shaped exit holes. A tree that has it may show dead
branches near the top, heavy branching on the trunk, vertical cracks in
the bark, and woodpeckers peeling bark to get at the larvae.

**Spotted lanternfly**, from APHIS: adults are about an inch long and half
an inch wide, with brown forewings spotted black and scarlet hind wings.
In autumn, winter and spring, look for egg masses about an inch long,
holding 30 to 50 eggs, that look like wet grey putty when new and then
like a smear of cracked mud, on trees, bricks, stones, fences, grills,
equipment and vehicles. A sticky, shiny residue with a vinegar smell on
plants below is another sign. Its favourite plant is another invader,
tree-of-heaven.

**Feral swine**, from APHIS: thinner than farm hogs, with thicker, coarse,
bristly hair and longer tusks. You are more likely to see their signs:
ground rooted up in patches, muddy wallows, rubbed tree trunks, trails
through thick cover, hoof prints near water, and droppings like a dog's
with bits of acorn, grain, hair or feathers in them.

When you find something you think is invasive, FWS's advice to boaters
and anglers works for any species (our reading): note its exact location,
take a photograph if you can, and report it. Then leave it where it is
(general practice): carrying it home or to an office to be identified is
one more way to spread it.

## What to do

### Do not move it

Most of the damage invasive species do is done by the next place they
reach, so the first rule is to be a dead end, not a carrier.

- **Firewood.** APHIS's advice: buy only local firewood or certified
  heat-treated firewood. In practice that means not bringing firewood
  home from a trip or taking your own on one (general practice).
- **The law on moving firewood depends on where you are.** APHIS removed
  its federal emerald ash borer quarantine in a rule that took effect on
  14 January 2021, but other federal quarantines still cover firewood. The
  Asian longhorned beetle's list of regulated articles begins with
  "Firewood (all hardwood species)", and APHIS's page on that beetle says
  "please don't move firewood out of quarantined areas" and asks people
  in them to follow state and federal laws. The spongy moth quarantine
  (the regulation, 7 CFR 301.45, still calls it the gypsy moth) counts
  firewood among the "outdoor household articles" that may not be taken
  across a state line from a quarantined area into one that is not
  without a certificate or a signed self-inspection checklist. States and
  counties can add rules of
  their own, so check before you haul wood (general practice).
- **Vehicles, trailers and outdoor things.** In a spotted lanternfly area,
  APHIS asks you to keep car windows closed when parked, to park about 15
  feet from trees in a quarantine zone if you can, to check doors, sides,
  bumpers, wheel wells, grills and roofs before you leave a parking lot or
  work site, and to check outdoor items for egg masses before you move or
  store them. Egg masses you find should be smashed and scraped into a
  zip-top plastic bag holding hand sanitiser or rubbing alcohol, which is
  then sealed and put in the rubbish.
- **Boots and clothes.** Brush off the seeds before you leave a weedy
  place, not when you reach a clean one ([Taking Without Ruining
  It](taking_without_ruining_it.md)).
- **Plants and seeds.** Never move a plant on your state's quarantine list
  or a federal noxious weed. And do not dump garden waste in the woods or
  along a road (general practice); [How an Ecosystem Holds
  Together](how_an_ecosystem_holds_together.md) explains how dumped yard
  waste spreads English ivy.

### Report it

- **Plant pests.** APHIS asks the public to report what they see, and to
  contact their State Plant Health Director, who can help identify a pest,
  explain management programmes and say what restrictions apply in your
  state. Each pest page has its own reporting form; for the emerald ash
  borer the line is 1-866-322-4512.
- **Weeds.** Your county weed board (in Washington, 38 of the 39 counties
  have one).
- **Fish, mussels and water plants.** FWS says to report a suspicious
  species to your state's natural resource agency, or through the US
  Geological Survey's online sighting form.
- **Feral swine.** APHIS takes sightings online and through an app.

### Control it, or get help to

The Washington board's advice on a weed you have found, restated: first
find out exactly what it is; then whether the law requires you to control
it (it recommends controlling invasive weeds regardless, as a good
neighbour); then choose methods. It describes an integrated approach that
combines tools:

- **Mechanical:** pulling, digging, mowing, tarping and mulching, tilling.
  Hand-pulling a small patch disturbs the least soil and spares the plants
  you want.
- **Cultural:** planting and seeding the plants you want, improving the
  soil, managing water and light, so the site suits them better than the
  weed.
- **Biological:** approved insects that feed only on one weed, or grazing
  animals, which also eat the plants you want, so plan to replant.
- **Chemical:** a herbicide chosen carefully, used exactly as its label
  says (more on that below), with help from the county board in choosing
  one.

Two habits from the same advice make the difference between a project and
a habit of losing: work on a section you can actually finish, then move on;
and **replant bare ground with what you want before the weeds come back.**

Above all, do not let an invasive plant set seed. [How an Ecosystem Holds
Together](how_an_ecosystem_holds_together.md) gives the Forest Service's
figures for Scotch broom: seeds that last at least 5 years in the soil and
possibly 30, and several thousand seeds a year from one shrub. A plant that
seeds once can keep coming back for a decade. Bag flower heads and seed
heads and put them in the rubbish rather than the compost or a brush pile
(the NPS says this for giant hogweed; applying it to other invasive
plants is general practice).

## Water, boats, pets and aquariums

The water routes are the easiest to close, because the steps are simple.

**Clean, drain, dry.** FWS asks boaters and anglers to do this every time
they leave a body of water:

1. **Clean** off visible plants, animals and mud from the boat, trailer
   and gear before leaving the water access. Hot water kills more than
   cold: FWS recommends water of at least 120 F, while avoiding contact
   with your skin and checking that the equipment can take the heat.
2. **Drain** the motor, bilge, livewell and every other part that holds
   water, before you leave.
3. **Dry** everything for at least five days, or wipe it with a towel,
   before using it somewhere else.

FWS does not recommend bleach or other chemical disinfectants for boats
and gear: they can damage equipment, harm the environment and people, and
may not work. Never carry water, animals or plants from one waterbody to
another, and never release live bait or fish into a new water. Put unused
bait, worms and fish parts in the rubbish.

**Pets and aquariums.** FWS's advice: buy from reputable dealers, and
never release aquarium fish, other animals or plants of any kind into a
natural body of water. Find an unwanted pet a new home instead, through a
pet shop, an animal shelter or another owner. For a water garden: put it
away from streams and anywhere that floods, and choose native or
non-invasive plants. FWS's phrase for it: "If you don't know it, don't
grow it!"

### Worked example: a moss ball in the fish tank

Suppose you bought a marimo moss ball for a tank and later hear about the
zebra mussel problem. FWS's instructions, in order:

1. **Look.** Inspect the moss ball for mussels. If there is any sign of
   them, destroy it, and report it to your state natural resource agency
   and to the USGS Nonindigenous Aquatic Species database.
2. **Destroy** it in one of three ways: freeze it in a sealed plastic bag
   for at least 24 hours; or put it in boiling water for at least one full
   minute; or soak it for 10 minutes in unscented household bleach diluted
   to one third of a cup per gallon of water, or for 20 minutes in
   undiluted white vinegar.
3. **Dispose** of it and its packaging in a sealed plastic bag in the
   rubbish. Pour any bleach, vinegar or boiled water down a household
   drain, never a storm drain, which leads straight to streams.
4. **Drain and clean the tank.** Move the fish to a separate container of
   clean water, treat the tank water with bleach at the same strength for
   10 minutes before pouring it down a household drain, and clean the tank
   and its gravel, decorations and filter by one of FWS's methods: hot
   water at 120 F for at least two minutes, kept off your skin (FWS's
   boat-cleaning advice warns to avoid skin contact with water that hot);
   a 24-hour soak in half a cup of
   salt per gallon; or a 10-minute soak in the same bleach solution, after
   which FWS says to replace the filter media and use a dechlorinating
   product before the fish go back.

**Bleach and vinegar are alternatives, never a mixture.** The CDC says
never to mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners, because it makes a
poisonous gas, and HRSA's poison-prevention advice is that bleach should
not be mixed with anything other than water. Use one method, rinse, and
open a window while you work (the window is general practice). If anyone
breathes fumes and feels ill, get them into fresh air (general practice)
and call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222.

## Some invasive species can hurt you

### Giant hogweed: never mow it

Giant hogweed is in the carrot family, the same family as parsley, dill
and cilantro. The NPS describes a grown plant as up to 15 feet tall, with
leaves that can spread 5 feet wide and thick stalks topped with feathery,
umbrella-like flower heads; it calls it Queen Anne's lace on steroids.

Its sap is the danger. The NPS says chemicals in the leaves, roots, stems,
flowers and seeds react with skin so that it blisters severely when
exposed to sunlight; that sap in the eyes can cause temporary or even
permanent blindness; and that breathing it can cause breathing problems.
Its advice:

- **Never mow or string-trim it.** That sprays the sap everywhere, and the
  plants grow back from the roots anyway.
- **Cover up completely** if you do control it: gloves, long sleeves,
  trousers, shoes and eye protection.
- **If sap gets on your skin, wash it off immediately** and keep that skin
  out of the sun for at least a couple of days.
- **Cut flower heads carefully, bag them and put them in the rubbish.**
  The roots have to be dug out or the plants treated with a herbicide, and
  a colony can take up to 5 years to clear because of regrowth from seeds
  and roots.
- **Calling local authorities or a professional is sometimes the best
  course**, the NPS says. Giant hogweed is a federal noxious weed; report
  it to your county or state weed board (reporting is general practice).

If sap gets in an eye, rinse it with plenty of clean water and get medical
help at once (general practice). And do not touch or taste any large plant
with white umbrella-shaped flowers that you have not identified: [The
Species Where You Live](the_species_where_you_live.md) explains why that
family includes some of the most poisonous plants in North America, poison
hemlock among them, which is itself on the Silverdale invasive list.

### Feral swine: keep your distance

APHIS calls feral swine "a dangerous and destructive invasive species". It
says they have been aggressive towards, and have even attacked, farmers,
golfers, hikers and picnickers, and that they become more aggressive when
they connect people with food, through handouts and badly kept rubbish. So:

- **Do not feed them, and do not leave food or rubbish where they can
  reach it.**
- **Keep away.** Watch from a distance, report the sighting and leave
  removal to the professionals; APHIS Wildlife Services helps landowners
  on request. (Keeping your distance is general practice.)
- **If you hunt or handle them, protect yourself.** APHIS says the most
  common way their diseases reach people is through handling and butchering
  them or eating undercooked meat: always wear gloves to handle a carcass,
  and cook the meat to an internal temperature of 160 F. If you feel ill
  after contact with feral swine or their meat, contact your physician or
  health department immediately. Hunting them is governed by state rules,
  which you need to check first (general practice).
- **Watch your pets and livestock.** APHIS says they can catch diseases
  from feral swine, their droppings, or feed and water containers the pigs
  have used; signs include fever, lethargy, swollen joints, and breathing
  and reproductive problems. Call a vet at once.

### Herbicides: the label is the law

When a weed needs a chemical, the label is not a suggestion. The EPA says
every pesticide label carries the statement "It is a violation of Federal
law to use this product in a manner inconsistent with its labeling", and
sums it up: "the label is the law." Herbicides are pesticides. The label
tells you who may use the product and where, how, how much and how often.

- **Read the whole label before you buy**, and buy only a product labelled
  for your weed and your site (our reading of the EPA's point).
- **Keep it in its original container**, never a food or drink container,
  and out of children's reach; HRSA's poison-prevention advice says the
  same for every household chemical.
- **Some herbicides may only be applied by a licensed applicator**, the
  Washington board notes, and your county weed board can help you choose.
- **If someone swallows it, breathes it or gets it in their eyes or on
  their skin**, call 911 if they are unconscious, having trouble breathing
  or having convulsions; otherwise follow the label's first-aid directions
  and call Poison Help at 1-800-222-1222, as the EPA advises.

## Where your own work stops

Spotting, reporting, not spreading and controlling small patches on your
own land are yours. Past that, hand over:

- **Anything that might be a newcomer**: a federal noxious weed, a weed on
  your state's top-priority list, or a pest with an APHIS alert. Report it
  first; the people who run eradication programmes may want it left as it
  is, or handled their way.
- **Giant hogweed**, unless you are equipped and confident, and even then
  report it.
- **Feral swine.** APHIS Wildlife Services, and your state's wildlife
  agency for hunting rules.
- **Any herbicide whose label you cannot follow exactly**, and anything
  that needs a licensed applicator.
- **A dying ash or any large tree.** A tree killed by a borer becomes
  dangerous to fell or to stand under; that is an arborist's job (general
  practice).
- **Invasive animals or plants in a lake, river or wetland.** The state's
  natural resource agency.

## Traps

- **"Just this one load of firewood."** That load is exactly how the ash
  borer travelled.
- **Letting a pet go "to give it a chance".** FWS asks you never to, and
  released pets are one of the routes NISIC names.
- **Composting seed heads**, or dumping garden waste in the woods.
- **Cutting a stand once and calling it done.** The seed bank and the
  roots outlast one cut by years.
- **Mowing or strimming giant hogweed.**
- **Mixing bleach with anything but water.**
- **Calling every non-native plant invasive.** Your tomatoes are
  non-native; the definition needs harm.
- **Waiting to be sure before reporting.** A photograph and a location
  cost nothing, and the rare ones matter most.

## How the game models it

The game does not model invasive species.

- **No species are native or invasive in the game.** Nothing spreads from
  one place to another, nothing arrives on firewood, boots or boats, and
  there are no weed laws, quarantines or reporting.
- **The locale data knows.** The Silverdale species file
  (`data/locales/silverdale_wa/species.json`) records each of its 133
  species as native, introduced or invasive, and the Library's guides use
  it. The game's simulation does not read it yet.
- **Garden weeds teach the seed-bank lesson.** Weeds in a soil bed come up
  from a seed bank in the soil and, if you let them stand, set seed and
  refill it (`data/garden/weeds.ron`). The file's calibration follows a
  real study: a bank run down to 5 percent over five years climbs back to
  about 90 percent of where it started after one season left uncontrolled.
  That is this guide's rule in the garden: never let them set seed. You
  hoe weeds while they are small and lay mulch to stop new ones. The file
  says what it leaves out, too: perennial weeds that come back from roots,
  which is how Himalayan blackberry and giant hogweed survive being cut.
- **Garden pests arrive on their own.** The pests in
  `data/garden/pests.ron` come onto any bed with their host crops in a
  slow trickle; nothing you carry or move brings one in, though in real
  life people carrying things is how most invasive species travel.

**Two settings change this.** In the default Dev play mode (Settings >
Gameplay > Play mode), materials are free, so hoeing needs no hoe and
mulch costs nothing from your pack. And while "Start every session from
the default home" is on, which it is by default during development, only
your character carries between launches, so your garden, its weeds and
its seed bank start fresh each time.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the
arrival, the spread, the harm to other places, and the duty to stop it.
In the game a weed is your problem only while it is in your bed. In real
life, a weed you let seed becomes your neighbour's.

## You own this when

- You can give the federal definition in your own words, and explain why
  a tomato is non-native but not invasive.
- You know where your state's noxious weed list is and which county or
  state board to call.
- You buy firewood where you burn it, and you check vehicles and outdoor
  things for hitchhikers when you leave an infested area.
- You clean, drain and dry a boat and gear every time, and you never move
  water, fish or plants between waters.
- You would never release a pet or an aquarium plant, and you know what to
  do with a contaminated moss ball, using bleach or vinegar, never both.
- You report a suspected invasive with a photograph and an exact location
  rather than waiting to be sure.
- You never let an invasive plant set seed, and you bag seed heads for the
  rubbish rather than the compost.
- You would never mow giant hogweed, and you know what to do if its sap
  touches your skin.
- You keep your distance from feral swine, and wear gloves and cook to 160
  F if you handle them.
- You read and follow a herbicide label exactly, and keep it in its
  original container.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- Reading the federal definition as three tests.
- Applying FWS's advice on reporting a sighting to species on land, and
  leaving a suspected invasive where it is rather than carrying it to be
  identified.
- Not carrying firewood to or from a trip; checking state and county
  rules on moving firewood, and on hunting feral swine, before you act.
- Reporting giant hogweed to a county or state weed board.
- Not dumping garden waste in the woods; bagging seed heads of invasive
  plants other than giant hogweed for the rubbish.
- Opening a window while using bleach or vinegar; rinsing an eye with
  water and getting medical help if hogweed sap reaches it.
- Keeping your distance from feral swine.
- Reading the whole herbicide label before buying, and buying only a
  product labelled for your weed and site, our reading of the EPA.
- Leaving dead or dying large trees to an arborist.
