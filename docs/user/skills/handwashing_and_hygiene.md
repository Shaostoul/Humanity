# Handwashing and Personal Hygiene

Almost everyone reading this already washes their hands and is confident
they do it properly. That confidence is the problem this guide exists to
address, because handwashing is one of those skills where the gap between
"I do that" and "I do that correctly" is wide, invisible from the inside,
and paid for in illness.

It is worth the twenty minutes it takes to read, for three reasons.

The first is size of effect. Very little else you can do for your health
is this cheap and returns this much, and the numbers further down are
specific enough to check.

The second is that this is the one link in the sanitation chain that
holds when everything else fails. Your water treatment can fail, your
toilet can fail, your refrigeration can fail, the supply chain that
brings you clean anything can fail. Soap and twenty seconds still works.
It works in a flood, in an outage, on a homestead with no plumbing, and
in a house where the tap runs fine. That is exactly the property you want
from the thing you rely on most.

The third is that the common beliefs about it are wrong in specific,
correctable places. Most people believe hand sanitiser is a slightly less
convenient equal of soap. It is not, and the section on that is the one
most likely to change what you actually do.

A boundary before anything else: this guide is about not getting sick and
not making other people sick. It is not medical advice, and it does not
replace a clinician. If someone in your household has persistent
diarrhoea, a fever, or a wound that is getting worse rather than better,
that is a doctor's problem and not a hygiene problem.

## How big the effect actually is

Every figure in this section comes from the US Centers for Disease
Control and Prevention, which publishes the pooled results of the
handwashing literature. They are theirs, not ours.

CDC reports that handwashing reduces the number of people who get sick
with diarrhoea by 23 to 40 percent, and "reduces diarrheal illness in
people with weakened immune systems by 58%." It reduces respiratory
illnesses such as colds in the general population by 16 to 21 percent,
and reduces absenteeism due to gastrointestinal illness in schoolchildren
by 29 to 57 percent. (Those three ranges are CDC's figures, restated here
without its dashes rather than quoted.)

Stated the other way round, CDC says handwashing "can prevent about 30%
of diarrhea-related sicknesses" and "about 20% of respiratory
infections."

The global picture is where the size becomes hard to argue with. CDC
states that "about 1.8 million children under the age of 5 die each year
from diarrheal diseases and pneumonia," and that handwashing with soap
"could protect about 1 out of every 3 young children who get sick with
diarrhea, and almost 1 out of 5 young children with respiratory
infections."

Against that, CDC puts "estimated global rates of handwashing after using
the toilet" at "only 19%."

The World Health Organization looks at the same behaviour inside
hospitals and reports that hand hygiene improvement programmes can
prevent up to half of avoidable infections acquired during health care,
and return on average sixteen times what they cost to run. WHO also
reports that compliance among health workers themselves averaged just
under 60 percent in intensive care units, splitting sharply between
higher-income and lower-income countries. (WHO material is copyrighted,
so it is restated here rather than quoted.)

Read those two paragraphs together. Even the professionals who are
trained, audited and reminded do it a little over half the time. This is
not a skill people fail at because it is hard. They fail at it because it
is boring and invisible, and nothing makes the failure obvious until
somebody is ill.

## Where the germs come from

Two routes account for nearly all of it.

The first is faecal. CDC's framing of the scale is worth keeping in your
head: a single gram of human faeces, which CDC notes is "about the weight
of a paper clip," "can contain one trillion germs." That gets onto hands after
using the toilet, after changing a nappy, after handling animals or
animal waste, and less obviously after handling raw meat, which carries
the gut organisms of the animal it came from.

The second is respiratory. Coughing, sneezing and nose-blowing put
organisms on your hands, and touching a surface someone else coughed on
puts them there too.

Neither route makes you ill by being on your skin. Skin is a good
barrier. CDC names the step that completes the journey: you get ill when
you "touch your eyes, nose, and mouth with unwashed hands," or "prepare
or eat food and drinks with unwashed hands."

So hands are not the destination. Hands are the bridge. Washing them is
how you take the bridge down.

## The five moments that matter most

CDC publishes eleven key times to wash. They group naturally into five
moments, and the grouping is not arbitrary: it follows the two routes
above rather than following habit. Habit says wash when your hands look
dirty. The evidence says wash when your hands have just crossed one of
these boundaries, whether or not anything is visible.

**1. After the toilet, and after anyone else's.** CDC: "After using the
toilet," and "After changing diapers or cleaning up a child who has used
the toilet." This is the moment with the largest single effect and the
one the 19 percent figure is about.

**2. Before food touches your hands or your mouth.** CDC: "Before,
during, and after preparing food," and "Before and after eating food."
Note *during*. The moment you put down raw meat and pick up the salad is
a moment, and it is the one people skip because they are in the middle of
something.

**3. After coughing, sneezing or blowing your nose.** CDC: "After blowing
your nose, coughing, or sneezing." This is the whole of the respiratory
16 to 21 percent, and it is the moment people most reliably forget
because nothing on the hands looks different afterwards.

**4. Around illness and around wounds.** CDC: "Before and after caring
for someone at home who is sick with vomiting or diarrhea," and "Before
and after treating a cut or wound." Both directions matter. Before
protects the wound or the patient from you; after protects you and
everyone else from them.

**5. After the dirty jobs you do not think of as dirty.** CDC: "After
touching an animal, animal feed, or animal waste," "After handling pet
food or pet treats," and "After touching garbage."

If you only fix one thing after reading this guide, fix number 3. It is
the cheapest correction on the list and the one almost nobody makes.

## The technique, and why each part is there

CDC's five steps: "Wet your hands with clean, running water (warm or
cold), turn off the tap, and apply soap." Then "Lather your hands by
rubbing them together with the soap." Then "Scrub your hands for at least
20 seconds." Then "Rinse your hands well under clean, running water."
Then "Dry your hands using a clean towel or an air dryer."

That is the whole procedure. What follows is why each step is in it,
because the reasons are what let you adapt the procedure when conditions
are not ideal.

**Soap removes, it does not kill.** This is the single most useful thing
to understand. Soap and water lift dirt, grease and organisms off the
skin so the rinse can carry them away. CDC draws the contrast plainly
against sanitiser, which works by killing. Removal is why soap handles
things that resist killing, and it is why the rinse is not an optional
tidy-up at the end. If you lather and then wipe rather than rinse, you
have loosened everything and left it on your hands.

**Friction is the working part.** The soap does the chemistry; your hands
do the mechanics. Lather is evidence that soap and water are properly
mixed on the skin, not an end in itself.

**Twenty seconds is a duration, not a gesture.** CDC: "Scientific studies
show that you need to scrub for 20 seconds to remove harmful germs and
chemicals from your hands." Time yourself once, honestly, against a
clock. Most people are at five to eight seconds and are genuinely
surprised. CDC suggests humming "Happy Birthday" twice as a timer, which
sounds childish and works.

**Cover the whole hand, including the parts the standard motion misses.**
CDC: "Make sure to scrub all areas of your hands, including your palms,
backs of your hands, between your fingers, and under your fingernails."
Palm-to-palm rubbing, which is what most people actually do, touches
almost none of that.

The thumb is the clearest example. Wrap one hand around the other thumb
and rotate; that motion does not occur naturally in any part of ordinary
handwashing, which is why the thumb gets skipped. CDC names the pattern
directly in its hand hygiene guidance: "Remember to clean your thumbs,
finger tips, and in between the fingers," because "these areas are
commonly missed when cleaning hands with alcohol-based hand sanitizer."
The same geometry applies to soap.

**Water temperature does not matter.** CDC: "Warm and cold water remove
the same number of germs from your hands." Hot water buys you nothing
except a greater chance of damaging your skin, which is its own problem
later in this guide. Wash in whatever is comfortable.

**Antibacterial soap buys nothing either.** CDC: "Use plain soap and
water to wash your hands. Studies have not found any added health benefit
from using antibacterial soap." Plain soap is cheaper, gentler and
equally good. This matters practically, because plain soap is the one you
can store in bulk, improvise, or eventually make.

**Drying is part of the wash, not the cleanup afterwards.** This is the
step people treat as optional and it is not. CDC puts it simply: "Germs
spread more easily when hands are wet, so make sure to dry your hands
completely, whatever method you use."

The measurement behind that statement is worth knowing, because it is
much larger than people expect. Patrick, Findon and Miller, writing in
*Epidemiology and Infection* in 1997, had subjects touch surfaces with
washed but undried hands and counted what transferred: roughly 68,000
organisms onto skin, 31,000 onto food, and 1,900 onto utensils. After
drying, the same contacts transferred 140, 655 and 28. That is a
reduction of about 99.8, 94 and 99 percent respectively, achieved by
nothing more than a towel. CDC cites this study in its own evidence
listing for hand hygiene.

So a careful twenty-second wash followed by wiping your hands on your
jeans undoes a large part of what you just did. Dry on something clean,
and dry completely.

## Soap versus alcohol sanitiser

This is the section most likely to correct something you believe.

Sanitiser is genuinely useful. It is fast, it needs no water, and in
settings where the alternative is not washing at all it is clearly
better than nothing. But it is not a substitute for soap and water, and
the ways it fails are specific and predictable.

**It does not kill everything.** CDC, naming names: "Soap and water are
more effective than hand sanitizers at removing certain kinds of germs
like norovirus, Cryptosporidium, and Clostridioides difficile."

Those three are not obscure. They are, respectively, the most common
cause of outbreak vomiting illness, a water-borne parasite with a tough
outer shell that also shrugs off chlorine, and a gut organism that forms
spores. CDC states each case separately elsewhere: "Hand sanitizer does
not work well against norovirus." "Alcohol-based hand sanitizers are not
effective against Crypto." "Alcohol-based hand sanitizer doesn't kill
*C. difficile*."

Note what those three have in common. Each survives in a form that
resists chemical attack. Soap does not care, because soap is not
attacking them. It is lifting them off and washing them down the drain.

**It does not remove chemicals.** CDC: "Hand sanitizers might not remove
harmful chemicals, such as pesticides, and heavy metals like lead." If
you have been handling fuel, paint, garden chemicals or old paint dust,
sanitiser is the wrong tool entirely.

**It fails on dirty or greasy hands.** CDC: "Hand sanitizers may not be
as effective when hands are visibly dirty or greasy." Its instruction is
blunter than that: "Don't use hand sanitizer if your hands are visibly
dirty or greasy," and the examples it gives are "after gardening, playing
outdoors, fishing, or camping."

This one matters enormously for the homesteading reader, because it
describes essentially every task you will do outdoors. A film of soil or
oil physically shields organisms from the alcohol. Sanitiser on muddy
hands is theatre.

**The concentration is a hard threshold.** CDC: "use a hand sanitizer
with at least 60% alcohol to clean your hands." Below that it does not
reliably work, and plenty of products on shelves are below it. CDC's
healthcare guidance also gives a ceiling: alcohol content "must not
exceed 95% alcohol content by volume," because alcohol needs some water
present to denature proteins. Read the label; the percentage is the only
thing on it that matters.

**The technique is different too.** CDC: "Apply the gel product to the
palm of one hand," "Cover all surfaces of hands," "Rub your hands and
fingers together until they are dry. This should take around 20 seconds."
If it dries in four seconds you did not use enough. And the same missed
spots apply, which is the context CDC's thumbs-and-fingertips warning was
written for.

**One safety note.** CDC: "Supervise young children when they use hand
sanitizer to prevent swallowing alcohol, especially in schools and
childcare facilities."

The honest summary: sanitiser is the tool for clean-looking hands away
from a sink. Soap and water is the tool for everything else, and the only
tool for the moments that matter most. CDC's own line is the one to
remember: "Washing hands with soap and water is the best way to get rid
of germs in most situations."

## Washing when water is scarce

This is the homesteading case and the emergency case, and it is where the
skill stops being automatic.

**The water for washing does not have to be drinking water.** This
surprises people and it is the fact that makes handwashing possible when
your drinking supply is short. CDC states that during "a boil water
advisory or 'do not drink water' advisory, it may be safe to wash hands
with tap water," and adds the conditional upgrade: "Wash your hands with
soap and water that has been boiled or disinfected if local authorities
or your doctor recommend extra caution."

There is a hard floor under that, though. Washing water must not be
sewage-contaminated. CDC's companion guidance on bathing draws the line
explicitly: "Do not bathe in water that may be contaminated with sewage
or toxic chemicals," and "Do not bathe in rivers, streams, or lakes that
are contaminated by flood water, human sewage, or animal waste." It also
warns that "open wounds and rashes that have been exposed to contaminated
water can become infected." Water that would merely give you an upset
stomach if you drank it is acceptable for washing. Water carrying sewage
is not, at any point in the chain.

**Running water beats a basin, and you can improvise running water.**
Washing in a shared bowl recontaminates everyone's hands from the same
water. The fix is a container that pours.

CDC's own emergency instruction is the simplest version: "If clean tap
water is not available, create a temporary handwashing station by using a
large water jug." A jug with a small hole near the base, or a bottle with
a pinhole punched in the cap and the cap loosened to let air in, gives
you a thin controllable stream. The rule is that your hands never touch
the container or the water supply, only the stream.

**The tippy tap is the developed version of that idea**, and CDC
publishes build instructions for it in several languages. A systematic
review of tippy taps by Mbakaya, Kalembo and Zgambo in *BMC Public
Health* in 2020 describes the design as a three or five litre jerry can
of water hung from a wooden frame, with a string from the neck of the can
down to a stick at ground level. Press the stick with your foot, the can
tips, and a small hole releases a stream. Soap hangs from the frame
alongside. Nothing you touch with dirty hands is something you touch
again with clean ones.

The same review reports the number that makes it worth building: a good
hand wash at a tippy tap can use about 50 mL of water, against up to
500 mL at a tap. A ten to one saving is the difference between
handwashing being affordable on a hauled water supply and not. The review
also reports school programmes where handwashing rose from a few percent
to near-universal after tippy taps were installed, alongside large falls
in reported stomach complaints.

**Soap stretches too.** CDC's global WASH materials note that "a soapy
water handwashing solution can be made by mixing water with liquid or
powdered soap," which turns a small amount of soap into many washes.

**Greywater discipline.** The water that has run over your hands is now
greywater, and where it goes is part of the same problem. Put the catch
basin where the runoff cannot reach a well head, a spring, a stream, or
the surface of anything you are going to eat raw. Site the whole station
downhill and well away from your drinking water source. This is a topic
in its own right and it is flagged again at the end of this guide.

## Soap without buying soap

Soap is simpler than it sounds and more dangerous to make than it sounds.

The FDA, which has to define soap precisely for regulatory purposes, puts
it plainly: "Ordinary soap is made by combining fats or oils and an
alkali, such as lye." What you get is "alkali salts of fatty acids," a
molecule with one end that mixes with water and one that mixes with fat.
That double nature is the whole trick, and it is why soap lifts grease
and the organisms riding in it off your skin. FDA adds the reassuring
part: "When made properly, no lye remains in the finished product."

It also notes that most modern cleansers are not soap at all: "Most body
cleansers, both liquid and solid, are synthetic detergent products."
For handwashing purposes this does not matter. Both work.

**Now the part that does matter.** The alkali is lye, and lye is one of
the more dangerous substances a household will ever handle. NIOSH lists
sodium hydroxide under the synonyms "caustic soda, lye, soda lye, sodium
hydrate," names the target organs as "eyes, skin, respiratory system,"
and lists the effects as "irritation eyes, skin, mucous membrane;
pneumonitis; eye, skin burns." The first aid entries are the tell: "Eye:
irrigate immediately. Skin: water flush immediately." NIOSH sets the
recommended exposure limit at a ceiling of 2 mg per cubic metre and
considers 10 mg per cubic metre immediately dangerous to life and health.

In plain terms: lye burns skin on contact, blinds on contact with eyes,
and the reaction of lye with water releases enough heat to matter.

So this guide is not going to teach soapmaking in a paragraph, because a
paragraph is exactly the amount of instruction that gets someone hurt.
Soapmaking is a real, learnable, worthwhile skill with its own protective
equipment, its own procedure and its own mistakes. Learn it from a source
that treats it as such. What belongs here is only this: you can make
soap, it is not mysterious, and the danger is entirely in the lye rather
than in the soap.

## Nails, hair, clothing and bedding

Briefly, because these matter less than the twenty seconds but are not
nothing.

**Nails.** CDC: "Dirt and germs can live under fingernails and contribute
to the spread of some infections, such as pinworms," and "more dirt and
bacteria can gather under long nails than short nails." The instructions
are short: "Keep nails short and trim them often," and "scrub the
underside of nails with soap and water (or a nail brush) every time you
wash your hands." CDC also says not to bite or chew nails, and not to cut
cuticles, since they "prevent infection."

On artificial nails, CDC's healthcare guidance is unambiguous, and it is
the strongest statement it makes on any of this: "germs can live under
artificial fingernails both before and after using an alcohol-based hand
sanitizer and handwashing."

**Hair, clothing and bedding.** Keep hair out of food and off wounds, and
wash it regularly. Change and wash clothing that has been in contact with
soil, animals, or anyone's illness before wearing it again. Wash bedding
regularly, and wash it promptly after any illness involving vomiting or
diarrhoea. None of this needs a schedule you will not keep. The principle
is that fabric which has touched a contaminated thing is now a
contaminated thing, and it stays that way until it is washed.

## Hand care, because cracked skin is an infection route

This is the failure mode created by doing everything else right.

Washing strips the skin's natural oils. Do it often enough and the skin
cracks, and cracked skin is worse than a nuisance. CDC's assessment in
*Emerging Infectious Diseases* is blunt: "damaged skin more often harbors
increased numbers of pathogens. Moreover, washing damaged skin is less
effective at reducing numbers of bacteria than washing normal skin." The
same review notes that "soaps and detergents have been described as the
most damaging of all substances routinely applied to skin," and that
"more washing and scrubbing are unlikely to be better and may, in fact,
be worse."

So the damage is self-reinforcing. Broken skin carries more organisms and
is harder to clean, which invites more washing, which breaks it further.

CDC's occupational guidance gives thresholds. Hand dermatitis "is made
worse by wet work: when hands are wet for more than 2 hours per day, hand
washing more than 20 times per day, or wearing gloves for more than 2
hours per day." It presents as "redness, dry skin, itching, burning,
blisters, and crusts," and in 2020 it was "the second most common
nonfatal occupational illness." That is not a rare complaint.

What to do about it:

- **Moisturise deliberately, not occasionally.** CDC: "Keep hands well
  moisturized with thick creams and ointments like plain petrolatum or
  petroleum." Thick and boring beats pleasant and thin.
- **Choose fragrance-free.** CDC recommends a product with few
  ingredients and no fragrance "to reduce the risk of irritation," and
  suggests looking for "ingredients like dimethicone or glycerin to help
  protect the skin."
- **Use sanitiser as the gentler option when hands are already clean.**
  This is counterintuitive and it is CDC's position: "Compared to soap and
  water, alcohol-based sanitizers remove fewer natural protective oils and
  cause less drying and damage." When there is nothing to remove, the
  gentler tool is the right one.
- **Treat stinging as information.** CDC: "If alcohol-based sanitizer
  burns, this is because the skin is already cracked and damaged." That is
  a signal to increase moisturising, not to stop cleaning your hands.

Two things make this worse that are not sourced here, so take them as
**rules of thumb**: cold dry weather, and harsh or strongly scented soap.
Both are consistent with the mechanism CDC does describe, which is loss of
the skin's protective oils, but neither number is one we can point at. If
your hands crack every winter, moisturise ahead of the season rather than
in response to it.

## The rest of the chain

Handwashing is one link. Three others carry most of the remaining risk,
and each is a topic in its own right that is not written yet. Here is
what each one is, and why it is not covered here.

**Human waste.** Everything in the faecal route above starts somewhere,
and that somewhere is a toilet, a latrine or a hole in the ground.
Getting this wrong contaminates groundwater and surface water for
everyone downhill of you, which is a larger and slower failure than a
dirty hand. CDC's emergency sanitation guidance makes one point that
belongs in both topics: "install handwashing stations at every sanitation
facility, regardless of the type of sanitation facility installed." The
two topics are physically joined, and the handwashing station is part of
the toilet, not a separate amenity. Siting, depth, distance from water,
composting and pathogen die-off times all belong to that guide.

**Greywater.** Washing water, laundry water and shower water are not
sewage, but they are not clean either. The EPA's research on onsite
non-potable reuse treats greywater as a stream that can be reused for
non-drinking purposes given appropriate treatment, and the pathogens it
tracks in that work include norovirus, rotavirus, *Campylobacter jejuni*
and *Cryptosporidium*. EPA points out that there is no single national
rule for this: it has produced a guidebook for state and local
jurisdictions to write their own. So greywater is simultaneously a public
health question and a legal one whose answer changes by state, which is
exactly why it needs its own guide rather than a paragraph.

**The pathogens that matter.** Everything above is written in terms of
"germs," which is a useful simplification and a limiting one. The
organisms behave differently: some are killed by chlorine and some are
not, some survive on surfaces for hours and some for months, some need a
thousand cells to make you ill and some need ten. Knowing the specific
short list is what lets you reason about a situation this guide did not
anticipate. That is a guide of its own.

Two related guides are already written and worth reading alongside this
one: **Making Water Safe to Drink**, for what to do about a source you do
not trust, and **Storing Water Safely**, for keeping a supply that stays
drinkable. Neither is repeated here.

## What can go wrong

- **You wash thoroughly and then dry on your trousers.** The most common
  way to waste a good wash. Wet hands transfer organisms by orders of
  magnitude more than dry ones, and a dirty towel is a fresh source. Dry
  completely, on something clean.
- **You use sanitiser after gardening, butchering or working on an
  engine.** Soil and grease shield organisms from alcohol. Wash.
- **You use sanitiser during a vomiting illness in the house.** This is
  the norovirus case, and it is precisely where sanitiser is weakest.
  Soap and water, every time, for everyone.
- **Your sanitiser is under 60 percent alcohol.** Check the label. Many
  scented and novelty products are.
- **Everyone washes in the same basin of water.** The second person is
  washing in the first person's runoff. Pour instead: a jug, a bottle
  with a hole, a tippy tap.
- **You wash constantly and your hands crack.** You have created a new
  infection route. Moisturise heavily, switch to sanitiser when hands are
  merely routine rather than visibly soiled, and do not reduce washing at
  the five key moments.
- **You washed your hands but not the tap.** If you turned the tap on
  with contaminated hands you will turn it off with clean ones. Use a
  towel or your wrist, or fit a lever tap. This is a real route and it is
  why CDC's first step includes turning off the tap before lathering.
- **You are relying on antibacterial soap to make up for a short wash.**
  It does not. Twenty seconds of plain soap beats five seconds of
  anything.
- **Handwashing facilities exist but nobody uses them.** The most common
  failure at the household scale, and it is a placement problem rather
  than a willpower problem. A station beside the toilet and beside the
  kitchen gets used; one across the yard does not.

## How you know it worked

- You can name the five moments without looking, and moment 3, after
  coughing or sneezing, is one of them.
- You have actually timed your own wash against a clock at least once,
  and corrected it.
- Your routine includes backs of hands, between fingers, under nails and
  both thumbs, deliberately, because none of those happen by accident.
- You dry completely, on something clean, every time.
- You know the three things sanitiser does not handle: norovirus,
  *Cryptosporidium* and *C. difficile*, and you know it also fails on
  dirty or greasy hands and on chemicals.
- You have checked that the sanitiser in your house is at least 60
  percent alcohol.
- If your water is limited, you have a station that pours rather than a
  basin that sits, and its runoff goes somewhere that is not uphill of
  your drinking water.
- Your hands are not cracked.

When all of those are true you have taken the most-used bridge between
the world's germs and your body out of service, using soap, water and
twenty seconds. There is no other intervention in this entire curriculum
with that ratio of effect to effort, which is the only real argument this
guide has been making.
