# The Year of Wild Food

A wild food calendar is not a table you memorise. It is a rhythm you
learn by watching one place for a year.

That distinction sounds like a nicety and it is not. A table tells you
that salmonberry ripens in late June. The rhythm tells you that
salmonberry ripens about a month after it flowers, that it flowers when
the hummingbirds arrive, and that both of those happen earlier in a warm
open clearing than in the shaded creek bottom two hundred metres away.
The table is wrong somewhere every year. The rhythm is never wrong,
because it is a relationship between things you can see rather than a
promise about a date.

This guide is about **when**, and only about when. It is not about what
is edible, not about how to identify anything, and not about whether you
should put it in your mouth. Those are separate topics and they are
separate on purpose. See the last two sections before you go outside.

The worked example throughout is one real place:
`data/locales/silverdale_wa/phenology.json`, which ships with this
project and holds the year at Silverdale, Washington as sixty-eight
dated events across twelve months. The reading copy of it, month by
month, is [Where You Are: Silverdale,
Washington](../locale/silverdale_wa.md). This document does not repeat
that calendar. It teaches you how to read one, how to tell a strong
entry from a weak one, and how to build your own wherever you live.

## Phenology is a real word for a real thing

The study of when living things do what they do has a name. Phenology,
which the National Park Service defines as the study of seasonal
life-history stages in plants and animals: leaf-out, flowering,
fruiting, nesting, migration, the falling of leaves.

The useful part is what the science found out about the mechanism,
because it explains why your calendar keeps being slightly wrong.

**Plants do not read the calendar. They count warmth.** The standard
measure is accumulated growing degree days. Take the amount by which
each day's mean temperature exceeds a base temperature below which the
organism does nothing, and add those amounts up across the season. A
plant does not open its buds on 3 April. It opens them when the running
total reaches a threshold, and the calendar date that happens on is
simply whatever date the weather delivered that year.

**Warmth alone does not explain it either.** Two other things are in
the mechanism. The first is chilling. A Forest Service study of a young
Douglas-fir plantation in western Washington found the terminal buds
needed roughly 1,200 hours between 0 and 5 C before they would respond
to warmth at all, and only then did temperatures above 5 C force bud
burst by accumulating heat. A mild winter can therefore delay spring
rather than advance it, which is not what anyone expects.

The second is day length. Forest Service work on coast Douglas-fir
found that in the warm parts of the species range, growth cessation in
autumn is driven mainly by short photoperiods rather than by cold, so a
warmer climate extends the season only slightly. Day length is the one
input that no weather can change. February has February's light no
matter how warm the afternoon is, which is the same fact that governs
the vegetable garden and is worked through in [The Growing Calendar
Where You Live](the_growing_calendar.md).

Here is the finding from that Douglas-fir plantation that should
permanently cure you of trusting a date. In two of the three years
studied, trees on the **cooler** microsites broke bud two to four days
**earlier** than trees on the warmer ones. Not later. Earlier. Within
one plantation, on one hillside, in the same year. The authors
concluded that simple models of bud burst are inaccurate and that you
need chilling, heat forcing, photoperiod and spring freeze events
together to predict the sequence.

If a research plantation with instruments cannot get this right from
temperature alone, your notebook will not get it right from a date.

## Key off what you can see, not off the day of the month

The practical conclusion is that you should learn **indicators**: the
visible events that tell you where the year actually is, as opposed to
where the calendar says it is.

An indicator is any observable thing that reliably comes before, with,
or after the thing you want. It works because it integrates the whole
winter you actually had. It does not need to know the temperature
record, because it has already lived through it.

At Silverdale the best one is Indian plum. The Forest Service records
that it flushes light green leaves and white flowers much earlier than
the other plants it grows among, and explains why it does that: it
gathers light for fruit set before the deciduous shrubs around it leaf
out and shade it. The consequence for you is that when a bare grey
thicket suddenly shows pale green, spring has started here. That is a
better signal than any date, because a cold year moves the leaves and
does not move March.

The whole first half of the year has that structure, and the calendar
file marks the sequence for you:

- Hazel catkins on bare branches, the earliest flowering shrub here,
  through January to March. Nothing else is doing anything yet.
- Indian plum leaves, the first green in the understory, February into
  March.
- Skunk cabbage lighting up the wet ground, before most other growth.
  Where it grows, the water table is at or near the surface, which
  makes it a map as well as a clock.
- Red alder flowering. In Snohomish County, a Puget Sound lowland
  county close enough in latitude and elevation to read across,
  flowering begins 29 March and the flowers have all fallen by 12
  April.
- Alder leaf bud burst on 3 April in that same record, fully leafed out
  by 2 May. This is the canopy closing over the creek bottoms, which
  changes the light on the ground for the rest of the summer.

Three kinds of indicator are worth learning deliberately.

**What is in leaf.** Leaf-out is the strongest single marker of where
spring is, because it is unambiguous and it happens once. First green in
the understory, then the shrub layer, then the canopy.

**What is in flower.** Flowering is what tells you a fruit is coming and
roughly how long you have. Salmonberry ripens 30 to 36 days after
pollination, so the bloom is a countdown you can actually read. Flowers
also carry other information: the Forest Service notes that salmonberry
flowering aligns with rufous hummingbird migration, so the bloom tells
you the birds have arrived as well.

**What the ground is doing.** Saturated, drying, cracked, frozen, or
soft after the first rain. This is the indicator that governs autumn
here, because the mushroom season and the salmon runs are both triggered
by rain rather than by date.

One caution about indicators that a beginner will hit within a season.
An indicator tells you the year has reached a stage. It does not tell
you that a particular plant three valleys away has reached that same
stage. Indicators travel badly across distance, elevation and aspect,
which is why the answer is always the same: watch your own ground.

## The shape of the year in a marine climate

Before any of the sequences make sense you need the shape of the year
they sit inside, because the shape is what makes this place different
from the one the field guide was written for.

**There is a wet half and a dry half, and the split is extreme.** At
Silverdale, October through March delivers 1,154 mm of the annual 1,446
mm. That is 79.8 percent of the year's water falling in the six cold,
dark months, which is closer to four fifths than to three quarters.
December alone brings 252 mm, nearly as much as April through September
put together, which manage 294 mm across six months.

The dry half is genuinely dry. July gets 21 mm and August 28 mm, against
a July mean high of 24.7 C. People who move here are surprised by this
in both directions: surprised that they have to irrigate in a place
famous for rain, and surprised that anything ripens at all in a place
that is bone dry through the whole ripening season.

**The frost window is wide and the average date is a trap.** The last
spring frost falls on 29 March on average, which means that in half of
all years it falls later than that. It comes as early as 5 March and as
late as 24 April. The first autumn frost averages 14 November, ranging
from 26 October to 1 December. Between them sits a median of 227
frost-free days.

Those six dates are the backbone of the whole calendar, and they are the
only kind of entry on it that is measured rather than estimated. More on
that below, because it matters more than it sounds.

**Light collapses faster than temperature does.** Silverdale runs from
8.4 hours of daylight in December to 16.0 in June. September still
averages 22.2 C for a high and it is already down to 12.6 hours of
light. That gap between a warm month and a dark one is why autumn here
feels like it should have more growing in it than it does.

Put those three together and you get the local shape: a long, mild,
generous season at both ends, pinched hard in the middle by drought, and
pinched at the shoulders by light rather than by cold.

## Spring: the tightest window and the one people miss

Spring at Silverdale carries very little food and it carries it very
briefly. Counting every entry that carries food, whether fruit, seed or
harvest window, the calendar file lists two in March, five in April and
seven in May. Compare that with twelve in each of August and September
and you can see the shape of the problem: the spring
window is thin, it is early, and by the time most people start thinking
about foraging it has closed.

The sequence runs roughly like this. Nettle shoots reach cutting size
around March and stay worth taking into May. Bracken croziers uncoil
between March and early May. Knotweed pushes its reddish shoots up in
April. Cattail shoots follow into May. Camas comes into bloom from late
May.

**Why a shoot is only worth taking for a short time.** This is the part
that a table cannot convey, and it is the central idea of spring.

A shoot is a plant spending stored energy to build new tissue fast. For
those few weeks it is tender, low in fibre, and concentrated. Then the
plant switches from growing to defending and reproducing, and the same
tissue becomes tough, fibrous, bitter, or actively unpleasant.

Nettle is the clean example. The calendar's rule is to cut the top few
inches while the plant is still under about knee height, and to stop
once it flowers. Older nettle develops gritty mineral deposits that
irritate the urinary tract, so this is not a matter of the leaves merely
getting tough. The plant stops being food. That transition is driven by
the plant's development, which means it is driven by accumulated warmth,
which means it does not happen on a date. It happens when the plant gets
there. You have to be watching.

Note also how the calendar handles the honest limits of its own
evidence. The nettle entry is labelled local consensus rather than
published range, and it says why: the Forest Service gives flowering
from late May to October but publishes no shoot emergence date, so the
start of that window is a regional generalisation rather than a
measurement. The file will not dress it up.

**One spring entry is a warning rather than a harvest.** The calendar
lists bracken croziers, and its entry is explicit that the Forest
Service records western brackenfern as mutagenic and carcinogenic in
rats and mice, with all parts including the spores implicated, to the
point that face masks are recommended for people working in dense
stands. It is widely eaten as a fiddlehead and, on that evidence, it
should not be. Its value on this calendar is as an indicator instead:
croziers in the open are often killed by late spring frost, so a frosted
bracken patch is telling you that ground is a frost pocket.

That is not foraging advice and this guide is not the place you learn
it. It is here because a spring section that listed the shoots and
quietly left that one out would be teaching you something false by
omission.

**And one spring event is the most important timing lesson in the whole
dataset.** Common camas blooms from late May, and that bloom is the only
few weeks in the year when it can be told apart from meadow death camas,
which grows in the same ground and whose bulbs are indistinguishable.
Once the leaves die back and the seed disperses, nothing above ground
marks which is which.

The lesson is not about camas. It is about the structure: **the
identification and the harvest do not have to happen on the same day,
and for the dangerous cases they must not.** You walk the ground when
the plant is identifiable, you mark what you found, and you come back
later and take only what you marked. The calendar is what carries an
identification forward across the months when identification is
impossible.

How you actually tell those two plants apart is not taught here. It is
taught in the identification guides, and the reasoning behind that split
is set out in [The Species Where You Live](the_species_where_you_live.md).

## Summer: the berry sequence

Summer is when a calendar is easiest and most fun, because the events
are conspicuous and they arrive in a reliable order even though they
arrive on unreliable dates.

The order at Silverdale, from the calendar file:

- **Indian plum** disperses first, beginning around May and nearly
  finished by July. Most people never see it because it is over before
  they start looking. Only the female plants fruit.
- **Salmonberry** is the first fruit anyone notices, June into July.
  The calendar's practical note is worth more than its dates: colour is
  not ripeness. Salmonberries run yellow through orange to deep red and
  all of those can be ripe, so you judge by how easily the fruit lifts
  off. Because a patch ripens over several weeks rather than all at
  once, the patch yields for far longer than any single plant does.
- **Trailing blackberry**, the native one, June through August. Small,
  seedy, far more strongly flavoured than the invasive Himalayan, and it
  trails along the ground on thin wiry stems rather than making thickets,
  so it is easy to walk straight over. Worth looking down for in July.
- **Thimbleberry** through mid to late summer. A ten-year study recorded
  earliest ripe fruit on 30 June and latest on 3 September, which is a
  two-month spread for one species, recorded by people who were watching
  on purpose.
- **Red huckleberry**, roughly July through September, often growing
  straight out of a rotting stump.
- **Himalayan blackberry**, August into September. It ripens about a
  month after its native cousin and bears far more heavily.
- **Salal**, August through October, and it hangs on the plant long
  after ripening, so its window is unusually forgiving.
- **Blue elderberry** from about late August.
- **Evergreen huckleberry** last of all, ripening July to September and
  often still hanging into October.

Two things to take from that list, neither of which is a date.

**The sequence is more reliable than the calendar.** Trailing blackberry
before Himalayan, salmonberry before thimbleberry, everything before
evergreen huckleberry. In a cold year the whole sequence shifts later
together. In a warm one it shifts earlier together. The order holds. So
if you know where you are in the sequence, you know what is coming next
without knowing today's date at all.

**The overlap is the resource, not any single species.** Look at the
count of ripe fruit entries per month rather than at the species: one in
May, four in June, eight in July, ten in August, nine in September, five
in October. The abundance is not one plant bearing heavily. It is many
plants overlapping, which is also why a single failed species barely
registers, and why the season is far longer than any one shrub's.

Note the honest disagreement the file preserves rather than smooths
over. The red huckleberry entry records that the Forest Service table
gives August to September for Washington while the local record says
July through September, and concludes that late July is the early edge
rather than a reliable start. A calendar that reported one number and
hid the other would look more confident and be worth less.

## Autumn: the richest and most crowded part of the year

Autumn here is not a winding down. It is the busiest stretch on the
calendar by a clear margin, and everything arrives at once.

September carries twelve food entries, four salmon runs and five hazard
entries at the same time. October carries nine food entries, four runs
and six hazards. Nothing else in the year is close to that density.

Four things converge.

**Fruiting shifts from berries to keepers.** Salal, evergreen
huckleberry and blue elderberry come on late, hold longer, and dry and
cook better than the soft summer fruit. The nuts land in the same weeks:
hazelnuts through September, acorns falling around October. The calendar
notes the practical problem with hazelnuts, which is that squirrels and
jays strip them within days of ripeness, so the answer is to watch the
husks from August and pick slightly early, then cure them, rather than
waiting for a clean drop that never comes.

**The fungi arrive, on rain rather than on a date.** The National Park
Service records for Olympic National Park, directly across Hood Canal,
that mushrooms can be found nearly year round but the peak season runs
from the start of the fall rains until the first major frosts. That is a
season with two moving ends: it opens whenever the first sustained rain
after the summer drought happens to fall, and it closes at the first
hard frost, which the climate normals put anywhere from 26 October to 1
December. In a year when the rain returns in early September the season
starts a month earlier than in a year when it holds off.

**The salmon come in.** Chum run late October into December, peaking in
November, and they spawn in the lowest and most accessible reaches of
the local creeks. Coho run September into December, and the calendar
file flags them as the one run to watch the weather forecast for rather
than the calendar: they hold at the creek mouths and wait for the first
heavy rain to raise the water, so a dry October delays them and a big
early storm brings them in days.

**The first frost lands somewhere in the middle of all of it.** Ten
percent of years have frozen by 26 October. Half by 14 November.

Two consequences follow from that pile-up and they pull in opposite
directions. Autumn is when a year's worth of storable food is available
and it is also when you have the least time and light to process it,
because September is already down to 12.6 hours of daylight and October
brings 131 mm of rain. What you do with a glut you cannot eat is the
subject of [Keeping What You Grew](keeping_what_you_grew.md).

Two more things share the season and are worth knowing about even
though neither is food. Black bears feed hardest in September and
October, in the same weeks and often on the same fruit. And the autumn
mushroom flush brings the dangerous species up in the same woods, on the
same rain, as the good ones, which is exactly why mushrooms are a
separate discipline with its own guide and not a paragraph in this one.

## Winter, which is not empty

People assume the calendar goes blank. At Silverdale it does not. The
file lists seventeen events in January and twenty in December, and
several of them are food.

**What is still standing.** Madrone berries ripen from early autumn
through December or January, which makes them one of very few fruits
left when the year closes and disproportionately important to birds.
Salal fruit persists on the stem until December. Oregon grape is
evergreen, so the plants are findable in every month even when they are
not bearing, and marking a good stand in winter costs nothing. Bigleaf
maple seed disperses from October all the way into January, with some
still hanging in March.

**The one harvest unique to deep winter.** Bigleaf maple sap runs.
Washington State University Extension records that Pacific Northwest
maples are tapped December to February, when freeze and thaw events
drive the flow, and that collection is generally limited to a three to
four week window. It needs nights below freezing and days above, which
here means a hard clear snap rather than a typical grey wet January
week. So the usable window inside the season is short and unannounced,
and you have to be ready to take it when it comes. Flow stops at bud
break.

**What the tide does, and why it is the most useful winter fact here.**
This one is genuinely counter-intuitive. Dyes Inlet gets roughly the
same number of deep minus tides every month of the year. In the summer
half they fall around midday. In the winter half they fall in the middle
of the night, between roughly 22:00 and 02:00 from November through
February. The tide table will show you winter lows as deep as any in
June and they are entirely real, but they are in the dark and in the
cold. The lowest tide of 2026 is 3.7 feet below datum at 23:51 on 24
December.

Across a whole year there are only about 22 to 26 daylight minus tides
at or below 2.0 feet below datum, and they cluster in runs of four to
six days around the new and full moon between about mid April and mid
August. That is the sort of fact worth marking on a calendar a year
ahead. The tide side of all this is worked through properly in [Reading
the Tide](reading_the_tide.md).

**What the low light costs you.** December gives 8.4 hours of daylight
and 252 mm of rain; January 8.9 hours and 236 mm. That is not just
unpleasant. It is a hard limit on how far you can walk, how long you can
work outside, how much you can see, and how quickly conditions change on
you. A winter trip that would be trivial in July is a different
undertaking in December, and the deep tides being at midnight is the
sharpest example: the calendar file's own advice is to treat a winter
minus tide as an expert trip rather than a family one.

**And the hazards do not sleep.** The calendar carries more hazard
entries in November and December than in any summer month. Water hemlock
keeps its tuberous root in the ground all twelve months, with nothing
above ground in winter to show where it is. Poison hemlock stands as a
green rosette through winter, at the exact time of year when a field
edge has little else green in it. Poison oak drops its leaves and keeps
its oil on the bare stems, which removes the only identification
character most people know, in the same season people gather firewood.

Winter is not the empty part of the calendar. It is the part where the
calendar stops helping you identify anything.

## Confidence: the best idea in the file

This is the part to steal for anything you build yourself.

Every one of the sixty-eight events carries a label saying **what kind
of claim it is making**. The file's own definitions:

- **measured**: computed from raw observations or predictions, or taken
  from a NOAA normal that was itself computed from observations.
- **published_range**: a named source states this range, though usually
  for a region larger than Silverdale.
- **local_consensus**: the species record and regional sources agree,
  but no single publication gives the date.
- **estimated**: a regional generalisation applied here with no local
  source, and the event says so.

The file states the principle plainly: nothing in it dresses an estimate
as a measurement, and where the honest answer is that the timing varies
too much to pin down, the event says that instead of giving a date.

Now look at how those sixty-eight actually break down. Eight are
measured. Forty-one are published ranges. Eighteen are local consensus.
One is an estimate.

And here is the finding that should change how you read every calendar
you are ever handed. **All eight measured entries are weather or water.**
The frost dates, the growing season length, the wet season, the summer
drought, and the three tide entries. Every single biological event on
this calendar, every leaf-out, every bloom, every berry, every salmon
run, is a published range or a local consensus. Not one of them is
measured here.

That is not a defect in the file. It is an accurate report of the state
of the evidence. NOAA has run a weather station here for decades and
publishes tide predictions for this inlet, so the physical year is
measured. Nobody has run a thirty-year phenology study in Kitsap County,
so the biological year is borrowed from regional publications and
neighbouring counties and read across.

You can see the seams if you look. The alder dates are unusually
precise, 29 March for first flower and 3 April for bud burst, because
they come from a Snohomish County record close enough in latitude and
elevation to use directly. The Indian plum bloom is given as January to
May because that is the Forest Service range across the whole species
distribution, and the file narrows it to February through April here and
says that it is doing so. Twenty-four of the sixty-eight events carry no
source URL at all, and those are the ones assembled from agreement
rather than from a publication.

**So the question to ask of any calendar, including one printed in a
beautiful book, is: which kind of claim is this?** A date computed from
thirty years of observations at this station and a regional
generalisation borrowed from four hundred miles away are not the same
claim, and they should not be printed in the same typeface without a
label. Most calendars do not tell you. That silence is the thing to
notice.

## The three things this calendar refuses to say

The file names three things a reader might expect and will not find, and
each refusal teaches something real. Quoted from the file:

**"no month-by-month biotoxin season: closures have no season and the
calendar must not imply the tide answers that question."**

Marine biotoxins come from algal blooms, which the state health
department tests for continuously, and a closure can appear in any
month. The water looks clear during a toxic bloom, and cooking and
freezing do not destroy the toxin because it is in the meat. The lesson
is about the structure of the question: a good tide tells you the water
will be low. It tells you nothing whatsoever about whether the shellfish
are safe, and a calendar that printed both side by side would invite
exactly that inference. **A separate authority answers a separate
question, and it has to be checked live, on the day.**

**"no mushroom dates: fruiting here is triggered by rain, not by the
calendar, so a date would be a fabrication."**

This is the difference between an event that is scheduled and an event
that is triggered. Leaf-out is scheduled, loosely, by accumulated warmth
and light, which vary within a range. Fungal fruiting here is triggered
by a specific weather event whose arrival varies by a month or more. A
date is the wrong shape of answer for a triggered event. The right
answer is the trigger, which is why the file gives you the trigger and
the frost window that ends it, and no month.

**"no fixed shellfish beach seasons: those are set beach by beach and
annually, and reprinting last year's would be worse than silence."**

Worse than silence is the key phrase. A stale rule that looks current is
more dangerous than no rule at all, because it removes the prompt to go
and check. Anything set annually by an agency belongs in a live lookup,
never in a document that ships.

Those three refusals generalise into one question you can put to any
calendar: **what is this document quietly implying it can answer, that
it cannot?** The honest ones tell you. Most do not.

## Keep your own

Everything above is preparation for the only thing that actually works.

**Walk the same route, in the same week, for one year, and write down
what you see.**

That is the whole method. It is unglamorous and there is no shortcut,
and at the end of it you will know more about the timing of your own
ground than any published source can tell you, because no published
source is about your ground.

How to do it so it is worth having:

**Pick one route and do not change it.** Twenty minutes is plenty. It
should include several habitats if you can manage it: some woodland
edge, some open ground, some wet ground, some shoreline if you have one.
The value comes from repetition, so a short route you actually walk
weekly beats an ambitious one you walk four times.

**Go on a schedule, not on inspiration.** Weekly is the useful interval
for spring, when things change in days. Fortnightly is enough for
midsummer and midwinter. The trap is that you naturally go out when the
weather is nice, which quietly biases your record toward the mild weeks
and away from exactly the conditions that move the dates.

**Record the stage, not the impression.** Write "first open flowers on
three plants" rather than "flowering". Standard stages are easy and
worth using: first green leaf tip, leaves unfolding, first open flower,
full flower, first ripe fruit, most fruit ripe, leaf fall. The point is
that a stage is comparable between years and an impression is not.

**Record the date and the place precisely,** and record the absences
too. "Checked, not yet" on 12 March is a data point. A gap in the
notebook is not.

**Note what was happening at the same time.** This is what turns a list
of dates into a rhythm. If you write down that the alder was in flower
in the same week the nettle reached cutting height, you have learned a
relationship that will still be true in a year when both are three weeks
late.

**Expect the second year to be the useful one, and the third to be
better.** One year gives you dates. Two give you a spread. Three start
to give you a sense of what a normal year even looks like, and the
honest answer will be that there is not really a normal one.

**Then check your record against the published ranges rather than the
other way round.** If your ground runs consistently earlier or later
than the regional source, that offset is real information about your
site, and it is more valuable than the published number was.

If you want your observations to be worth something beyond your own
notebook, the USA National Phenology Network runs Nature's Notebook,
which is the national programme for exactly this and has been collecting
standardised observations since 2008. A note about why this project does
not use its data, because the reason is instructive rather than
critical: USA-NPN releases its data under a Creative Commons Attribution
4.0 International licence, which is genuinely open but is not the public
domain. An attribution condition attaches to every copy of anything it
is bundled into, so this project checked the terms and then deliberately
did not use it, and the phenology file says so on its face. That
constraint is ours and not yours. You have no release bundle to keep
clean, so for your own notebook the network is a good place to send your
observations and a good place to see what your neighbours are recording.

## What this guide does not qualify you to do

It does not qualify you to eat anything. Not one thing.

Everything above is about **when**. It says nothing about **what**, and
nothing at all about **whether**. Those are different questions with
different failure modes, and the failure mode for this one is mild while
the failure mode for those is not. Being wrong about a date costs you a
wasted walk. Being wrong about an identification can kill you.

The separation is deliberate and it runs through this whole collection.
Identifying a plant to species, knowing what is reliably edible where
you live, recognising the poisonous ones, mushrooms as their own
higher-stakes discipline, harvesting without ruining the resource, and
shoreline and shellfish harvest are each a topic of its own with its own
hazards. Go to them from the beginning and let them teach it properly.
Nothing here is a substitute for any of them, and knowing that
salmonberry ripens in late June is not permission to pick something red
in late June.

Two things in particular that this guide has deliberately left alone:

- **How to tell camas from death camas.** This guide told you that the
  bloom is the only window in which the question can be answered and
  that you must mark the plants and come back. It did not tell you what
  to look at, because that belongs to the identification topic.
- **Which mushrooms.** This guide told you that fruiting is triggered by
  rain and gave you no dates. It said nothing about species, because
  fungi are a higher-stakes discipline than plants and are taught
  separately for that reason.

Three things to carry out of here instead:

- **A calendar is a rhythm, not a timetable.** Learn the sequence and
  the indicators and you will be right in a year that no table
  predicted.
- **Ask any calendar what kind of claim each entry is making.** Measured,
  published range, local consensus, estimate. If it will not tell you,
  that is your answer.
- **Some questions a calendar cannot answer at all,** and a good one
  says so out loud instead of filling the gap with a plausible month.

The foundations under all of that are in [The Species Where You
Live](the_species_where_you_live.md), which is the guide to learning the
living things around you, and which is likewise not a foraging guide.

### Labelled in the text as rules of thumb, not sourced

- That indicators travel badly across distance, elevation and aspect.
  The underlying variation is documented in the Forest Service sources
  above; the practical warning is a generalisation from them.
- That weekly observation is the right interval in spring and
  fortnightly in midsummer and midwinter, and that the second and third
  years of a notebook are the useful ones. These are ordinary
  observing-practice advice, not measured findings.
- That a shoot is tender and worth taking because the plant is spending
  stored energy quickly, and becomes tough when it switches to
  reproduction. The specific nettle consequence is from the phenology
  file's own entry; the general principle is a rule of thumb.
