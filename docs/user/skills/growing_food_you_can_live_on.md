# Growing Food You Can Live On

A tomato is a wonderful first success and a nutritionally minor one. Here
is the number that makes the point. Using USDA food composition data, a
pound of raw tomatoes holds about 82 calories. A pound of potatoes holds
about 350. A pound of dry beans holds about 1,550.

You cannot live on tomatoes. That is not a criticism of the tomato, and
Your First Tomato is still the right place to begin, because a first
success teaches watering, light and patience faster than any reading
does. But if the ambition is to feed yourself rather than to garnish a
meal, the crops change, the arithmetic changes, and the honest numbers
are worth seeing before you plan a garden around them.

This guide covers the three crops that carry a beginner furthest:
**potatoes** for calories, **dry beans** for protein and long storage,
and **winter squash** for calories that keep for months without
electricity. They are chosen because each one is forgiving, each one
stores without a freezer, and together they are the classic backbone of
a subsistence garden in most of the temperate world.

One honest thing first, and it is a bigger one than usual: this guide
tells you what a plot can produce, not what a person should eat. No three
crops make a complete diet. Feeding yourself entirely from a garden is a
serious nutritional project involving fats, vitamin B12, and much else,
and it is a question for a dietitian, not for a garden guide. What
follows is the scale, so you can see what a given piece of ground
actually does.

## What the numbers actually say

Every figure below comes from a university extension service or from
USDA food composition data. The multiplication is ours and we show it, so
you can check it or redo it with your own local figures.

**Calorie density (USDA FoodData Central, per 100 grams raw, converted to
pounds by us at 453.6 grams to the pound):**

| Food | Calories per 100 g | Calories per pound | Protein per 100 g |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tomato, red ripe, raw | 18 | about 82 | 0.9 g |
| Potato, flesh and skin, raw | 77 | about 350 | 2.1 g |
| Winter squash, butternut, raw | 45 | about 205 | 1.0 g |
| Dry beans, black, mature seeds, raw | 341 | about 1,550 | 21.6 g |

**Yield per 100 feet of row (extension figures):**

| Crop | Yield | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Potatoes | 200 to 300 lb | Iowa State (20 to 30 lb per 10-foot row) |
| Potatoes | 75 to 100 lb | Utah State |
| Dry beans | 20 to 25 lb | Utah State |
| Winter squash | 75 to 100 lb | Utah State |

Look at the two potato rows. Two real extension services, both giving
home-garden guidance, differ by a factor of three. That is not an error
in either one. Iowa is deep prairie soil with a long humid summer; Utah
is arid, high, and short-seasoned. Everything about garden yield depends
on soil, water, season length, variety and how well the season goes, and
a first-year garden on unimproved ground will land at the bottom of any
published range or below it.

So treat every yield figure you ever read, including these, as a planning
estimate rather than a promise, and plan on the low number.

**Putting them together (our arithmetic):**

| Crop | Calories per 100-foot row | Days of one adult's calories |
|---|---|---|
| Potatoes, Utah figure | 26,000 to 35,000 | 13 to 18 |
| Potatoes, Iowa figure | 70,000 to 105,000 | 35 to 53 |
| Dry beans | 31,000 to 39,000 | 15 to 19 |
| Winter squash | 15,000 to 20,000 | 8 to 10 |

The "days" column uses 2,000 calories a day, which the FDA states is
"used as a general guide for nutrition advice," while noting that your
own needs "may be higher or lower and vary depending on your age, sex,
height, weight, and physical activity level." A working adult doing
physical labor needs more.

Two cautions on that table. The winter squash row is generous, because
the yield figure is whole fruit and you do not eat the skin or the seed
cavity, so the real number is lower. And the dry bean row understates
what beans are for: their value is the 21.6 grams of protein in every
100 grams, which works out to roughly 4.3 to 5.4 pounds of protein from
a 100-foot row, alongside a food that keeps for years in a jar.

**How much ground is that?** Utah State spaces potato rows 30 to 36
inches apart and Iowa State says 2 to 3 feet, so a 100-foot potato row
plus the walking space beside it occupies roughly 300 square feet. Bean
rows at 18 to 24 inches occupy roughly 200 square feet per 100 feet of
row. Winter squash is planted in mounds 4 feet apart, but the vines
sprawl well past the row, so its real footprint is considerably larger
than the row length suggests. Do not scale these figures up across a
whole plot as though the whole plot were productive row; a real garden
loses ground to paths, edges, shade and the corner where nothing grows.

## Potatoes: the calorie crop

Potatoes produce more food energy per square foot than almost anything
else a beginner can grow, they store for months without a freezer, and
they are grown from pieces of potato rather than from seed.

**Planting.** Utah State: plant 2 to 3 weeks before your last frost date,
once soils are at least 50 F. Buy certified seed potatoes rather than
using supermarket potatoes, which are often treated to suppress
sprouting. Cut them so each piece weighs at least 2 ounces and has one or
more eyes, the small dimples that sprout. You need 8 to 10 pounds of seed
potatoes for every 100 feet of row. Seed potatoes are frequently treated
with fungicide before sale, so plant them, do not eat them, and keep them
away from the kitchen and out of reach of children.

**Spacing.** Utah State: 4 to 6 inches deep, 10 to 12 inches apart in the
row, rows 30 to 36 inches apart. Iowa State's version is a furrow 4
inches deep with pieces about 1 foot apart and rows 2 to 3 feet apart.
Either works.

**Growing.** Iowa State: at least six hours of direct sun each day, 1
inch of water per week through the season, and 1 to 2 pounds of
all-purpose garden fertilizer such as 10-10-10 per 100 square feet. Keep
cultivation shallow, 2 inches or less, because the tubers form near the
surface and a deep hoe cuts them. Pull soil up around the stems as they
grow, which is called hilling, so developing potatoes stay covered.

**Harvest.** Iowa State: 90 to 120 days after planting for a full crop.
You can steal a few new potatoes earlier once they are more than an inch
and a half across, but use those within ten days; they have no skin to
speak of and will not store.

**Curing and storage.** Utah State: cure the tubers at 55 to 60 F with
high humidity for 2 to 3 weeks after harvest, then store in the dark at
40 to 45 F through the winter. Iowa State's long-term figure is 40 F at
90 percent relative humidity, with a two-week cure at 50 to 60 F and 85
to 90 percent humidity first. Do not store potatoes with apples or other
fruit, which give off a ripening gas that shortens their storage life.

**The one real safety rule.** Potatoes exposed to light turn green and
produce solanine, a natural toxin. Utah State: the chemical "tastes
bitter after being cooked," and you should "discard very green tubers."
Cut away small green patches generously; throw out badly greened
potatoes. Store them in the dark, always. This is also why potato leaves
and fruits are not food; the plant is in the nightshade family and only
the tuber is edible.

## Dry beans: the protein crop

A dry bean is the same plant as a green bean, left on the vine until the
seed inside the pod is fully mature and hard. That one change turns a
perishable vegetable into a food that stores for years at room
temperature in a jar, and it is the single easiest protein a beginner can
produce.

**Planting.** Utah State: about 3 to 4 ounces of seed plants 100 feet of
row. Sow 1 inch deep, 2 to 3 inches apart, in rows 18 to 24 inches apart.
Beans need soil and air temperatures above 60 F; they rot in cold wet
ground and they will not take a frost at either end of the season.

**Timing matters more than for most crops.** Dry beans must stay on the
plant to the very end of the season, so they need the whole season. Utah
State's warning is worth taking seriously: dry beans planted after July 1
"generally will not mature in cooler areas of Utah but will produce
mature seeds in the warmer regions of Southern Utah." Translate that to
where you live by counting the frost-free days you get and comparing them
to the days-to-maturity on the packet, with a few weeks of margin. If the
margin is thin, choose the earliest variety you can find.

Unlike snap beans, there is no picking through the summer. The whole crop
comes in at once.

**Harvest and drying.** Utah State: "Pull up the plants and lay in a row
in the garden for 5 to 7 days. Once plants are dry, remove the pods,
shell out the seeds and allow some additional time for the seeds to dry
further." If rain is forecast during those days, move the plants under
cover; a soaked, half-dried bean crop molds.

**Storage.** Sealed containers in a cool dry place. Beans that are
properly dry rattle hard and cannot be dented with a fingernail.

**Dry beans must be fully cooked, and this is a real safety rule, not a
matter of taste.** Raw and undercooked dry beans contain a natural toxin
called phytohaemagglutinin. The FDA's Bad Bug Book records that red
kidney beans carry the most of it, and that as few as four or five
improperly cooked red kidney beans can cause severe vomiting within a few
hours, followed by diarrhea. Kansas State University Extension gives the
procedure: soak the beans for at least five hours, drain and discard the
soaking water, then boil in fresh water for at least 30 minutes. The
toxin breaks down at boiling temperature in about 10 minutes, and the
longer 30 minutes is the margin researchers advise.

And the counterintuitive part: **do not cook dry beans in a slow cooker.**
K-State's wording is blunt: "Don't use a slow cooker: it likely won't get
hot enough." A slow cooker held below boiling can leave the toxin intact
while making the beans soft enough to eat, which is how most poisonings
happen. Boil first on the stove, then move them to the slow cooker if you
want to. Canned beans from a store are already cooked and are not
affected by any of this.

**A bonus.** Dry beans are self-pollinating, which makes them one of the
best crops to save your own seed from. See Saving Your Own Seeds. Once
you grow an open-pollinated dry bean, you never need to buy that seed
again.

## Winter squash: the storage crop

Winter squash is not a variety of squash so much as a moment of harvest.
It is left on the vine until the rind hardens into armor, and that armor
is why it keeps for months in an unheated room with no processing at all.
It costs you space, not effort.

**Planting.** Utah State: plant 4 to 6 seeds in mounds 4 feet apart, 1 to
2 inches deep, once soils reach 65 F. After the seedlings have two
leaves, thin to two plants per mound.

**Harvest.** Utah State: squash are mature "when fruits are fully
colored, when vines begin to die back, and when the rind is hard and
impervious to scratching from a fingernail." Iowa State adds that mature
squash have dull rather than glossy surfaces. Cut, do not pull, and leave
about a 1-inch stem on each fruit; a squash that loses its stem rots at
the scar. Bring them all in before a hard frost.

**Curing.** Iowa State: cure winter squash, except acorn types, at 80 to
85 F and 80 to 85 percent relative humidity. Do not cure acorn squash.
Curing hardens the skin and heals small scratches, and it is the
difference between a squash that keeps and one that goes soft in
November.

**Storage.** Iowa State: a cool, dry, well-ventilated place at 50 to 55 F.
Not the fridge, and not near apples or pears, which give off ripening gas
that shortens storage. How long they keep depends entirely on type: Iowa
State gives acorn about 5 to 8 weeks, butternut 2 to 3 months, and
Hubbard 5 to 6 months. Utah State agrees on the pattern, noting that
buttercup and banana squash store longer than butternut and acorn.

If you want food in February, that table is the whole decision. Grow the
long-keeping types.

## The honest counterweight

Set the guides side by side. Your First Tomato is a container, six hours
of sun, one plant, and a season that ends with something delicious that
holds roughly 82 calories a pound. This guide is rows, a real plot,
months of storage, and food that holds four to nineteen times as much
energy per pound.

Both are real gardening. The difference is that the first one teaches you
to garden and the second one starts to feed you, and it would be dishonest
to let the first stand in for the second. A garden that produces salad is
a hobby with benefits. A garden that produces a winter's worth of
potatoes, beans and squash is the beginning of not needing permission to
eat.

It is also, plainly, more work and more ground. A 100-foot row is not a
balcony. If you have only a balcony, grow the tomato and the herbs, and
use this guide as the map for the day you have soil, or as the reason to
go looking for a community garden plot.

## What can go wrong

- **The yield came in far under the table.** Expected, especially in year
  one. The published figures assume decent soil, adequate water and a
  full season. Improve the soil (see Your First Compost), water more
  consistently, and compare next year, which is the only comparison that
  means anything.
- **Potatoes came out small and few.** Usually not enough water during
  the weeks when tubers are forming, or not enough sun, or the plants
  were crowded. Potatoes are thirsty; the 1 inch a week is not optional.
- **Potatoes green in storage.** Light got in. Store them in the dark, in
  a box or a paper sack, and discard badly greened tubers.
- **The dry beans never dried.** The season ended first. Either the
  variety needed more days than you have, or planting was too late. Next
  year plant at the earliest safe date with the earliest variety you can
  find. Beans caught by frost while still green are not wasted; shell and
  eat them fresh as shell beans.
- **Beans molded on the drying plants.** Rain during the field-drying
  days. Move plants under cover next time.
- **Winter squash went soft by November.** Either it was picked immature,
  or it lost its stem, or it was not cured, or it was stored too warm or
  next to fruit. Check those four in that order.
- **Vines took over the garden.** They do. Winter squash is a land
  animal. Plant it at the edge where it can sprawl outward, or grow a
  bush variety, or plan for it next year.

## How you know it worked

- You harvested a crop you had to find somewhere to store, rather than
  eat that week.
- Potatoes are in the dark at around 40 F, dry beans are in sealed jars,
  and cured squash is on a shelf at around 50 F.
- In February you cook a meal whose main ingredient you grew in July.

That last one is the real threshold. Growing food in season is pleasant.
Eating in winter from ground you tended in summer is a different
capability, and it is the one that makes a household harder to squeeze.
