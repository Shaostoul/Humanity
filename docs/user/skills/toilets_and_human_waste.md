# Toilets and Human Waste

A flush toilet hides a whole system. Clean water comes in, a pipe takes
the waste away, and somewhere out of sight a sewer plant or a septic
system and the soil under it finish the job. Most people never think
about any of it until one part stops: the power goes out and the well
pump with it, a water main breaks, an earthquake cracks the pipes under
the house, or a winter of rain floods the drainfield. Then the question
arrives all at once. Where does everyone go to the toilet tonight, and
where does it go after that?

This guide answers it in the order you will meet it: what to do the day
the toilet stops; how to build and run a two-bucket toilet; what goes in
the bucket (sawdust, lime, bleach) and what does not; where the waste is
kept and where it may go; how far it has to stay from wells and water;
what a septic system needs to keep working; and why composting human
waste is a long, careful process with rules of its own. The worked
example is Silverdale, in Kitsap County, Washington, where the
simulation is set, and the rules described are Washington's and Kitsap
County's.

It leans on other guides and does not repeat them. [Where Water
Goes](where_water_goes.md) explains how a septic system works, the signs
that one has failed, and what failing systems have done to the shellfish
beds of Dyes Inlet. [The Germs That Matter](the_germs_that_matter.md)
names the germs in human waste and how each one travels, and [When Food
or Water Makes You Sick](when_food_or_water_makes_you_sick.md) covers
looking after someone with diarrhoea and when they need a doctor.
[Handwashing and Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) covers the hands,
which are the other half of every toilet. [Greywater](greywater.md)
covers washing water, which is a different stream with its own rules.
[Wells and Groundwater](wells_and_groundwater.md) has the distances that
protect a well, [Making Water Safe to
Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md) what to do about water you no
longer trust, and [Soil Chemistry](soil_chemistry.md) which bag of
"lime" is which.

Its sources are the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC)
and its National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH),
the National Institutes of Health's digestive diseases institute
(NIDDK), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), the Consumer
Product Safety Commission (CPSC), the Federal Emergency Management
Agency's Ready.gov site, the National Park Service and the federal
sewage sludge rule in the Code of Federal Regulations, all US federal
and in the public domain (the one partner's material in the Park Service
article is noted under Sources); and, restated in our own words, the
World Health Organization (WHO), the Washington State Department of
Health, the State Board of Health's on-site sewage rule, the Washington
State Department of Ecology's biosolids rule, the Kitsap Public Health
District, and the emergency toilet guidance of Clallam County,
Washington. Where something is general practice or our own reading, the
text says so.

## The day the toilet stops

1. **Work out which half has failed:** the water coming in, or the pipes
   taking waste away. They need different answers (below).
2. **After an earthquake, a flood or a sewage backup, do not flush.** On
   a public sewer, wait until officials say the sewer is working, because
   a break under the street cannot be seen from the house. On a septic
   system, wait until the tank and the pipes under the house have been
   checked and work (below).
3. **If only the water is off, and the drains are known to work,** a
   toilet can still be flushed by hand with a bucket of water (below).
4. **Otherwise, set up a two-bucket toilet:** one bucket for pee, one for
   poo, each with a lid.
   With small children in the house, keep the bathroom door shut and
   latched, with the latch out of the child's reach, and every lid on:
   an open bucket of water or waste can drown a toddler (below).
5. **Put a handwashing station beside it,** and use it every time.
6. **Keep the waste covered, contained and away** from food, water,
   children, pets and rats.
7. **Nothing goes on the ground or into water.** Not in the yard, a
   ditch, a creek or the inlet.
8. **Store it and wait for disposal instructions,** or, on a septic
   system that is known to work, empty it into your own tank (below).

The rest of this guide is the reasoning behind those eight, and the
detail.

## Why it matters

Many of the germs that give people diarrhoea leave the body in faeces.
The CDC names Salmonella, E. coli O157 and norovirus among them, and
says a single gram of human faeces, about the weight of a paper clip,
can contain a trillion germs. The WHO ties poor sanitation to the spread
of cholera, dysentery and other diarrhoeal illness, and also to typhoid,
polio and infections with intestinal worms (restated).
[The Germs That Matter](the_germs_that_matter.md) names the ones that
matter in a Kitsap household and how each one travels.

The WHO puts the job of sanitation in one idea: keep human excreta,
faeces and urine, away from human contact, so that the germs in it never
reach a new person (restated from its 2018 guidelines). Every rule in
this guide is a way of doing that. A few examples of what happens when it
fails, each from a source below:

- **Into the well.** One way the CDC lists for norovirus to reach
  drinking water is "when a septic tank leaks into a well".
- **Into the bay.** Just under two thirds of Dyes Inlet, below
  Silverdale, is classified Prohibited for shellfish harvest by the state
  Department of Health, for a wastewater treatment plant outfall and for
  nonpoint pollution, the kind of pollution the EPA says includes faulty
  septic systems ([Where Water Goes](where_water_goes.md), classification
  as of 4 September 2026).
- **Into the garden.** The CDC says roundworm and whipworm eggs reach the
  soil when infected people go to the toilet outside, or when people
  "use infected human feces as fertilizer", and that the eggs become
  infective as they mature in the soil.

An archived CDC page on sanitation in emergencies, written for disaster
response in low- and medium-income countries, improvised settlements
and cholera-prone areas, says it plainly: "prevention of open
defecation and waste containment are critical to reduce the spread of
disease."

## Which half has failed

**The water is off, but the drains work.** The well pump has no power, a
water main has broken, or the utility has shut the supply for repairs.
The pipes that carry waste away are fine. In this case the toilet still
works if you supply the water yourself: pour a bucket of water briskly
into the bowl and it will flush; washing water that has already been
used once will do for this; and a few lidded buckets filled before a
planned outage save carrying later (all general practice). One
exception: in a power cut, if the house's septic system relies on a
pump, the Kitsap Public Health District asks you to cut water
use as far as you can, to avoid overloading the system (Storms and
Flooding page, read 5 October 2026). The bucket toilet below is how you
cut it.

**Small children and stored water.** The Consumer Product Safety
Commission warns that 5-gallon buckets are a serious threat to toddlers,
who can topple in headfirst and cannot get out, that a child under 3 can
fall headfirst into a toilet, and that about two thirds of drownings in
the home, outside pools, happen in bathtubs. It asks that young
children be kept out of the bathroom when nobody is watching them, with
a latch on the door out of their reach, and: "Be sure all containers
with liquids are emptied immediately after use." So with a baby or a
toddler in the house, keep flushing water in buckets with their lids on
rather than in a filled bathtub, keep the toilet buckets' lids on too,
and keep the bathroom door latched (our reading of the CPSC's advice).

**The drains may be broken.** After an earthquake, Clallam County's
emergency toilet guidance says not to flush until officials say it is
safe. For homes on a public sewer, it points out that a sewer line can
break under the house, under the street or anywhere on the way to the
treatment plant, and that a break under the house or the street may be
hard or impossible to find. For homes on septic, it says not to assume
the system works even if you have power and water, because the pipes
under the house and the tank itself are at risk in the shaking, and not
to flush until the tank and the waste pipes have been checked and work
(all restated, guidance updated March 2015). The same goes for a flood:
the EPA says not to use the sewage system until the water in the
drainfield's soil is lower than the water around the house. And if
sewage is already coming back up a drain, every flush pushes more of it
into the house.

**Not sure.** Treat it as broken until the pipes have been checked and,
on a public sewer, officials say the sewer is working (our reading). A
bucket costs nothing if it turns out the drains were fine. Sewage on the
bathroom floor costs a great deal.

## The two-bucket toilet

Clallam County, on the Olympic Peninsula next door, teaches the
twin-bucket toilet for the weeks or months after a large earthquake,
when sewer and septic lines may be broken. The version here follows its
guidance, prepared with Jefferson County's emergency management
department (restated).

**What you need, bought before you need it:**

- Two buckets of 3.5 to 5 gallons, each with a lid that closes tightly,
  and one more bucket per household member as needed (Clallam's
  guidance).
- A toilet seat made to fit a bucket.
- A sack of dry cover material: sawdust, wood shavings or chips, finely
  shredded paper, dried leaves, peat moss or coir. Clallam's guidance
  suggests about a gallon bag of it a day.
- Toilet paper, disposable gloves, soap, hand sanitiser, towels or
  wipes, and nappies if there is a baby (Clallam's guidance).
- Heavy rubbish bags and ties. Ready.gov's basic emergency kit includes
  "Moist towelettes, garbage bags and plastic ties (for personal
  sanitation)". A bag lining the poo bucket makes a full one easier to
  lift out when the waste is to be stored rather than emptied into a
  septic tank, but a bag is only a liner, never the storage: each tied
  bag goes straight into a spare bucket or bin with a tight lid (general
  practice). Clallam's guidance counts a bag on its own, used as the
  toilet, among the obsolete substitutes.
- A water jug with a tap, or a bottle with a hole in the cap, for
  washing hands ([Handwashing and Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md)
  shows how to make one).

**Setting it up.** Label one bucket for pee and the other for poo;
numbers 1 and 2, or yellow and brown, work as well. Put them somewhere
private, with the cover material and a scoop beside them, and the
handwashing station next to them. The same archived CDC page, in its
advice for improvised settlements:
"Install handwashing stations at every sanitation facility, regardless
of the type of sanitation facility installed." A handwashing station
across the yard does not get used ([Handwashing and
Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md)).

**Using it.**

- **Pee in one, poo in the other.** Clallam's guidance gives the reason:
  keeping them apart lowers the disease risk and the smell, and makes
  each bucket easier to handle. It says most of the germs are in the poo,
  and that the bad smell comes from the pee once the two are mixed.
  Mistakes happen; keep trying.
- **Toilet paper goes in the poo bucket.**
- **Cover each deposit with a scoop or two of the dry material,** unless
  you plan to empty the bucket into your own septic tank (below).
- **Lid on after every use.** Move the seat from bucket to bucket.
- **Wash your hands every time.** Then the seat and anything else hands
  touched, when someone in the house is ill (below).

**How fast it fills.** Clallam's guidance says each person makes only 4
to 10 ounces of poo a day, and that two people fill a 5-gallon bucket,
poo and cover material together, in about a week, counting a bucket as
full at four fifths. Tie off a lining bag before it gets too heavy to lift
cleanly, and put it straight into a lidded bucket or bin (general
practice).

That is why the spare buckets matter. Clallam's starting kit for a
family of four is six, two to start and one more for each person,
adding more as needed. At the rate Clallam gives, four people fill a
poo bucket every three or four days. The Washington State Department
of Health puts an adult's urine at about 1.2 litres (40.6 fluid ounces)
a day, so the pee bucket fills about as fast, and six buckets hold
roughly a week to ten days of waste with nowhere to empty it (our
arithmetic). If disposal instructions could take weeks, keep more
lidded buckets or bins.

**What does not go in.** Clallam's guidance keeps nappies, adult
incontinence pads, sanitary pads and bagged pet waste out of both
buckets: double-bag them and put them in the rubbish.

**For someone who cannot squat or balance.** Clallam's guidance suggests
a bedside commode set over the bucket for the injured or frail.

**When someone in the house is ill** with vomiting or diarrhoea, the
toilet is where it spreads. The CDC's steps for norovirus, applied to
the seat, the lids and the floor around the buckets: put on rubber or
disposable gloves; wipe up any mess with paper towels and put them in a
plastic rubbish bag; disinfect with a bleach solution of 5 to 25
tablespoons of household bleach (5 to 8 percent) in a gallon of water,
left on for at least 5 minutes; then clean the whole area again with
soap and hot water; and wash your hands. That last clean also leaves no
bleach on a seat someone will sit on (our reading). Open a window while
you work, as the CDC's advice for cleaning with bleach asks, and heed
its warning: "Never mix bleach with ammonia or other cleaners." [The
Germs That Matter](the_germs_that_matter.md) and [When Food or Water
Makes You Sick](when_food_or_water_makes_you_sick.md) cover the rest of
looking after a sick household.

## Sawdust, lime and bleach: what goes in the bucket

**Sawdust and other dry cover.** Its job, in Clallam's guidance, is to
soak up moisture and keep the smell down while the waste waits for
disposal. It does not make the waste safe; it makes it bearable to keep
(our reading). Clallam's guidance also warns that cover material damages
a septic system, so leave it out of any bucket you mean to empty into
your own tank.

**Lime.** Lime does kill germs in waste, by making it strongly alkaline.
The federal rule for treating sewage sludge counts lime treatment as a
process that significantly reduces germs when "Sufficient lime is added
to the sewage sludge to raise the pH of the sewage sludge to 12 after two
hours of contact." Washington's rule for spreading septage on land
allows the same idea, holding pH 12 for at least thirty minutes, with
two pH tests on every load (restated). Septage is that rule's word for
what is removed from septic tanks, portable, vault and pit toilets and
similar systems that take only household sewage (WAC 173-308-080). The
Washington State Department of Health adds, for the solids from
composting toilets, that lime added before the material is tilled into
soil may help reduce germs, though it says more work is needed on lime
for that material (restated).
Three things follow, and they are why this guide does not put lime in a
household bucket (our reading):

- **The kinds of lime strong enough to do this are dangerous to handle.**
  Hydrated lime and quicklime burn skin and eyes, and quicklime heats up
  when it gets wet. Garden lime, ground limestone, is a different
  chemical. [Soil Chemistry](soil_chemistry.md) tells the three apart and
  has the first aid if one gets in your eyes.
- **It must never go into a septic system.** Kitsap's sewage rules forbid
  putting strong acids or bases into an on-site sewage system (Ordinance
  2025-01, section 6.G), and strong lime is a strong base.
- **A storage bucket does not need it.** The Washington county guidance
  this guide follows uses dry cover and a tight lid, and leaves the
  treating to whoever collects the waste.

**Bleach.** Use it to clean the seat and the floor, mixed as above, and
never pour it into the buckets. Urine contains ammonia, and the
Washington State Department of Health warns that bleach and ammonia
together give off toxic chloramine gas, naming toilet bowls and nappy
pails as places to take care, and that bleach with an acid, such as a
toilet-bowl cleaner, gives off chlorine gas (restated). So wipe up urine
with paper towels, or with soap and water, first, and then use bleach
mixed with water and nothing else (our reading). The Kitsap Public
Health District's homeowner's guide says not to pour strong chemicals,
bleach among them, into a septic system (restated), and bleach must
never meet ammonia or other cleaners (the CDC, above).

## Where it goes

**Storing it.** Clallam's guidance is to keep the waste in its buckets,
each under a tight-sealing lid; a tied liner bag goes into a lidded
bucket or bin as well (above). Keep them away from food, drinking water,
children and pets, out of the sun, in a garage or shed rather than the
house (general practice). Rats and flies are the other way stored waste
spreads germs, so a lid that clips shut matters more than it seems
([Rats, Flies, Mosquitoes and Ticks](rats_flies_mosquitoes_and_ticks.md)).

**Getting rid of it.** Clallam's guidance is to keep the waste stored
until the authorities say how to get rid of it. In Kitsap County the
body to ask is the Kitsap Public Health District, on 360-728-2235, and
the county's KitsapALERT warning system is how officials reach you in an
emergency ([Emergency Shelter](emergency_shelter.md) has how to sign
up). Do not guess where it may go.

**Emptying it into your own septic tank.** Clallam's guidance allows
this for households on septic, if you can find the tank's lid and lift it
safely (old concrete lids can be too heavy), and if no cover material
went into the poo bucket. Keep children and pets away from the open
tank, and put the lid back and secure it as soon as you are done. The
same guidance warns that the tank itself, and the pipes under the house,
are at risk in an earthquake, so after one have the tank checked before
you rely on it (our reading). Two more warnings come with it:

- **Never lean into or climb into a septic tank.** The EPA: "Only
  trained specialists should clean or repair septic tanks because tanks
  may contain dangerous gases." The WHO's 2018 guidelines name the gases
  that come off decomposing sludge, among them hydrogen sulphide,
  methane, carbon dioxide and ammonia, and say that breathing them can
  kill (restated). If anyone collapses in or at the open tank, do not go
  in or lean in after them: stay back and call 911. NIOSH, writing about
  confined spaces at work: "More than 60% of confined space fatalities
  occur among would-be rescuers". ([First Aid Until Help
  Arrives](first_aid_until_help_arrives.md) has the same rule for gas.)
  Methane burns, so no cigarette, match or flame near the open tank
  either (general practice). Lifting the lid from the ground to pour in a
  bucket is as far as a householder goes (our reading).
- **An open or broken lid is a hole a child can fall into.** The Kitsap
  Public Health District's septic safety sheet asks owners to keep lids
  sound and fastened, and to teach children not to play near them
  (restated, undated sheet).

**Never onto the ground or into water.** Kitsap's sewage rules say
sewage must go only to an approved sewer, septic system or other system
the health officer accepts (such as a portable toilet, a holding tank or
a certified pumping truck), never onto the ground or into surface water
or groundwater, and never left to back up into a building (Ordinance
2025-01, section 6.B, in force from 17 March 2025). The same
rules define sewage as including urine and faeces.

**What about urine?** Clallam's guidance says urine carries almost no
health risk, and that the poo bucket holds most of the germs. In Kitsap
County, though, urine counts as sewage, and sewage may not go on the
ground (our reading of the definition and section 6.B above). Keep it in
its lidded bucket, and pour it into a working toilet or your own septic
tank, or follow the disposal instructions you are given.

## How far from wells and water

**At home, do not dig.** Clallam's guidance says not to dig a pit privy
after a disaster: the soils of this region are waterlogged for part of
every year, so germs from a pit can readily spread to the properties
next door (restated).
Washington does allow pit toilets as a permanent system, but only under
its Department of Health standards (DOH 337-016, August 2025): with
permits from the local health officer before it is built and used; the
same distances from wells and water as a septic drainfield; never where
surface water floods; at least 4 feet of soil between the bottom of the
pit and the highest seasonal water table or any restrictive layer (the
state rule's term for a layer that holds back water, air and roots, such
as hardpan, bedrock or some compacted soils; water soaks through one
only slowly, if at all); never in medium sand or anything coarser; and
a pit no more than 5 feet deep, completely covered by its building
(restated). Around Silverdale that rules out a great deal of ground: on
the Alderwood soils, which cover about half the land here ([Where Water
Goes](where_water_goes.md)), dense till, a restrictive layer of this
kind, begins roughly 20 to 40 inches down ([Choosing Where to
Build](choosing_where_to_build.md)), which leaves far less than 4 feet
of soil under any pit (our reading).

**The distances that apply to a septic drainfield** are set by the
state's on-site sewage rule, chapter 246-272A WAC, as revised with
effect from 1 April 2025. Measured from the edge of the drainfield, they
include at least 100 feet from a well, from surface water and from a
private spring, and 200 feet from a spring or surface water that
supplies public drinking water; and from the septic tank, at least 50
feet from a private drinking water well and 100 feet from a public one
(restated). A pit toilet must keep the drainfield's distances. [Wells
and Groundwater](wells_and_groundwater.md) has the well's side of the
same rules, and [Choosing Where to
Build](choosing_where_to_build.md) shows what they do to a small lot.

**Away from home, outdoors.** Where there is no toilet at all, on a
hike or a camping trip, the National Park Service teaches the cathole:
"walk 200 feet or 70 large steps away from trails, campsites, and any
water source such as a lake or stream", then "dig a six to eight inch
hole", bury the waste completely, and pack out the toilet paper. Where a
hole cannot or should not be dug, it teaches the waste bag instead, a
toilet in a bag that you carry out (restated). It also says the right
method depends on where you are, so check before you go. The cathole is
for a person passing through wild land, not for a household living in
one place (our reading).

## What a septic system needs

[Where Water Goes](where_water_goes.md) explains how a septic system
works: the tank settles the solids, and the soil of the drainfield does
the treating. It also lists the signs of failure, from sewage backing up
to a patch of unusually green grass. What follows is what the system
needs from you, especially when the water or the power is off.

- **Water to carry the waste.** Clallam's guidance notes that a septic
  system only works with plenty of water flowing through it to carry the
  waste along.
- **Power, if it has a pump.** A system that relies on an electric pump
  stops moving sewage when the power goes. The Kitsap Public Health
  District asks the owners of such systems to use as little water as
  they can during an outage, so as not to overload it, and suggests a
  backup supply of power, a generator for example (restated, Storms and
  Flooding page, read 5 October 2026). Run any generator by the rules in
  [Choosing and Running a
  Generator](choosing_and_running_a_generator.md).
- **Soil that is not waterlogged.** The same page says a system may not
  work when its drainfield is saturated or flooded, and that using it
  then can push sewage back into the house or up to the surface. Spread
  water use through the day and week, cut back on laundry, flushing and
  bathing, fix leaks, and send downspouts and surface water away from the
  drainfield (restated).
- **After a flood,** the EPA's list: do not drink well water until it is
  tested; do not use the system until the water in the drainfield's soil
  is lower than the water around the house; do not have the tank pumped
  while the ground is saturated, because an emptied tank can float out
  of the ground; have it inspected if you suspect damage; keep away from
  any pump or other electrical part of the system that was under water
  until it is dry and clean, and have its electrical connections checked
  for damage before the power goes back on; and check that the tank's
  cover is still secure and its inspection ports are not blocked or
  damaged. The EPA also warns that whenever the water table is high or
  flooding threatens the system, sewage can back up into the home, and:
  "The only way to prevent this backup is to relieve pressure on the
  system by using it less."
- **Only what bodies make, and toilet paper.** The Kitsap Public Health
  District's homeowner's guide says not to flush cat litter, cigarettes,
  wipes, paper towels, plastics, nappies or hygiene products; to keep
  food, hair and grease out of the drains, and go easy on the garbage
  disposal; not to use strong chemicals such as bleach, or drain
  cleaners; and that it does not recommend commercial septic additives,
  which can harm the system (restated).
- **Regular inspection.** Kitsap's rules require the owner to have a
  standard system evaluated at least once every three years, and an
  alternative system at least once a year; the owner of an alternative
  system must also keep a monitoring and maintenance contract with a
  provider the health district certifies (Ordinance 2025-01, section
  13.C). The ordinance (section 5) counts a septic tank feeding a
  gravity drainfield as standard even when a pump lifts the liquid from
  the tank up to the drainfield, and calls any other design
  alternative; the district's septic page describes alternative systems
  more loosely, as ones with pumps, treatment devices or other parts a
  typical gravity system lacks (restated). Your record drawing, or the
  district, will say which yours is. The district's online record search
  has the record drawing for your property, which shows where the system
  is (septic safety sheet). The health district also inspects a septic
  system before a property is sold: in 2025 it carried out 1,743 of these
  inspections and found significant problems in 450 of them, 27 percent
  (septic page, read 5 October 2026).
- **Someone to tell when it fails.** The owner must report a septic
  failure to the health district within three days (section 13.C).

## Composting human waste: why it is slow and careful

People do turn human waste into compost, in composting toilets, and it
can be done safely. It is not a faster version of the garden heap, and
Washington treats it accordingly.

**Germs die with heat and with time, and a toilet rarely supplies the
heat.** The Washington State Department of Health's standards explain
that germs die off at temperatures above 122 F (50 C) held for long
enough, but that such temperatures are rarely reached when human
excreta is composted in a composting toilet. Without the heat, it is a
matter of time: most bacteria, viruses and protozoa die off within
several months, while some, such as the common roundworm and
*Cryptosporidium*, a parasite that causes diarrhoea, stand up to harsh
conditions and survive for longer. Fresh waste mixed into older
material can also contaminate the finished product.
The same standards say a composter at 41 F or colder does no composting
at all; it only stores what goes in until it warms up (all restated,
DOH 337-016, August 2025). The normal December and January day here
averages about 40 to 41 F, halfway between the normal high and low
(NOAA's 1991-2020 normals for Bremerton, the nearest station with a
temperature record, as [Silverdale, Washington](../locale/silverdale_wa.md)
gives them), so an unheated composter sits at or near that line for much
of the winter (our reading).

**What "hot enough" means in the federal rule.** For sewage sludge, the
federal rule's standard for composting that further reduces germs is
55 C (131 F) or higher held for three days in a vessel or an aerated
pile, or for 15 days or longer in a turned row, turned at least five
times while it is that hot (40 CFR Part 503, Appendix B, restated). A
bucket of sawdust and waste in a corner reaches nothing like that (our
reading).

**Why the garden is the worst place for mistakes.** The roundworm eggs
above are the reason: they reach people through soil and the vegetables
grown in it, which is why the CDC lists using human faeces as fertiliser
among the ways they spread, and why its advice for avoiding them
includes washing, peeling and cooking raw fruit and vegetables.

**Washington's rules, in order (restated):**

- **A composting toilet needs a permit.** The Department of Health's
  standards require permits from the local health officer before a
  composting toilet is installed and used. A manufactured model must be
  one the department has registered, after testing to the standard
  NSF/ANSI 41; a home-built one must follow the department's
  published standards (DOH 337-016; WAC 246-272A-0110).
- **It does not replace the drains.** Where a house has running water
  and makes greywater, a composting toilet may only be used together
  with an approved system for that greywater, or a public sewer
  (DOH 337-016). [Greywater](greywater.md) has the greywater rules.
- **What comes out of it is septage,** the same word the state uses for
  what is removed from septic tanks and from pit and vault toilets
  (above). Under the Department of Ecology's biosolids rule, the solids
  from a composting toilet that takes only household waste count as
  septage, and must either go to a permitted facility or be managed
  under the rule for septage applied to land (WAC 173-308-193).
- **Not on the lawn or the vegetable garden.** That rule forbids
  applying septage to a lawn, a home garden or a place the public uses.
  Where it is applied, it must stay 100 feet from surface water and from
  wells, and a food crop whose edible part touches the treated soil may
  not be harvested for 14 months, or 20 to 38 months for root crops,
  depending on how long the septage sat on the surface before it was dug
  in (WAC 173-308-270). The Department of Health's standards advise
  that, as a rule, what comes out of a composting toilet should not go on
  lawns or gardens, and name the other ways out: a licensed septage
  pumper, or the municipal rubbish where the jurisdiction allows it.

So a household that wants to compost its own waste needs a permitted
system, the health district's agreement, and somewhere for the finished
material to go that is not the vegetable bed (our summary of the rules
above). The garden compost heap is a different thing; [Your First
Compost](your_first_compost.md) covers it, and keeps dog and cat waste
out of it too.

## Worked example: an earthquake near Silverdale

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on.

A strong earthquake shakes Kitsap County on a February morning. The
power is out. The household, two adults and two children, lives outside
Silverdale on a well, with a gravity septic system: a tank and a
drainfield, no pump. With no power the well pump has stopped, so there
is no running water either.

1. **Which half has failed?** Both may have. The water has stopped with
   the power. The drains could be cracked under the house or at the tank,
   and nobody can see that from inside. They do not flush (Clallam's
   guidance).
2. **The toilet.** From the garage shelf come six buckets with lids
   (two to start, and one more for each of the four of them), the bucket
   seat, a bale of pine shavings, bags, gloves and toilet paper. They
   mark two of the buckets, set them up in the bathroom, put a water jug
   with a tap and the soap on the sink beside them, and show the children
   how it works. The lids stay on and the bathroom door stays shut and
   latched between visits, because the younger child is two. The
   shavings go on the poo bucket after every use, because they do not
   yet know whether the septic system works.
3. **Water.** Their stored drinking water is for drinking and cooking.
   Handwashing uses the jug, filled from the rain barrel: water for
   washing hands need not be drinking water, but it must not be water
   sewage may have reached ([Handwashing and
   Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md)).
4. **Day three.** The younger child has diarrhoea. An adult wearing
   gloves, with the window open, wipes up with paper towels, then cleans
   the seat, the lids and the floor with bleach solution mixed to the
   CDC's norovirus strength, and after 5 minutes washes them again with
   soap and hot water. Everyone washes with soap and water at every
   visit, not sanitiser ([The Germs That
   Matter](the_germs_that_matter.md)). They watch the child for signs of
   dehydration, and will call the doctor if the diarrhoea goes on for
   more than a day or the child cannot keep drinks down (NIDDK; [When
   Food or Water Makes You Sick](when_food_or_water_makes_you_sick.md)
   has the signs to watch for).
5. **Day five.** The power is back and the well runs. A septic service
   certified by the health district checks the tank and the pipes and
   finds them sound. Now they can flush again. The stored pee goes down
   the toilet. The poo bags have shavings in them, so they cannot go in
   the tank; tied, in their lidded buckets in the garage, they wait while
   the household rings the Kitsap Public Health District and does what
   it says with them.
6. **What they do not do.** They do not dig a pit at the bottom of the
   garden, empty a bucket in the ditch, put the bags out with the
   recycling, or spread anything on the vegetable beds.

## Know where your own effort stops

- **Sewage backing up into the house, or surfacing in the yard:** stop
  using water. On a septic system, call a septic professional certified
  by the health district, and report the failure to the Kitsap Public
  Health District within three days. On a public sewer, call the sewer
  utility that bills you (general practice).
- **A septic tank that needs cleaning or repair:** that is a trained
  specialist's job, because of the gases in it (the EPA). Lifting a lid
  from the ground to pour in a bucket is as far as a householder goes.
- **Someone who has collapsed in or at an open septic tank:** do not go
  in or lean in after them. Stay back and call 911 (NIOSH, above).
- **Sewage on the floor after a flood:** for cleaning up, the CDC says
  to wear rubber boots and plastic gloves (and rubber gloves and goggles
  for anyone who has to go into floodwater); to throw away what cannot
  be disinfected, such as wall coverings, cloth, rugs and drywall; to
  clean hard surfaces with soap and water and then disinfect them with 1
  cup of bleach to a gallon of water, with windows and doors open,
  goggles on, and the bleach mixed with water and nothing else (the
  CDC's advice for cleaning with bleach); and to wash clothes that
  touched flood or sewage water in hot water and detergent before
  wearing them again. More than a small area, or any sign that the
  structure or the wiring got wet, is a job for professionals (general
  practice).
- **A well that flood water or sewage may have reached:** do not drink
  from it until it has been tested ([Testing Water](testing_water.md),
  [Making Water Safe to Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md)).
- **An outhouse, a pit toilet or a composting toilet:** get the permit
  before you build anything.
- **Someone ill with diarrhoea that is bloody, that lasts more than two
  days in an adult or more than a day in a baby or child, or that comes
  with signs of dehydration:** see a doctor, and for a baby under 12
  months with diarrhoea, see one right away. Those day counts are
  NIDDK's; the CDC's general food poisoning page says more than three
  days and its E. coli page more than two, so for an adult act on the
  two. [When Food or Water Makes You
  Sick](when_food_or_water_makes_you_sick.md) has the full lists.

Yours to do: buying the buckets before the earthquake, knowing where
your septic tank and drainfield are and when they were last inspected,
keeping the waste contained, and washing hands.

## Traps

- **"The power's back, so the toilet's fine."** After an earthquake,
  the pipes can be broken whatever the power is doing. On septic, have
  the tank and the pipes checked; on a public sewer, wait until officials
  say the sewer is working.
- **"One bucket will do."** In a pinch, but Clallam's guidance counts the
  single-bucket toilet among the obsolete substitutes: keeping pee and
  poo apart lowers the disease risk and the smell, and makes each bucket
  easier to handle.
- **"A tub of water for flushing is handy."** Not with a toddler in the
  house. Lidded buckets, and a latched bathroom door.
- **"Sawdust makes it safe."** It makes it dry and bearable. The germs
  are still there.
- **"A scoop of lime in the bucket."** The kinds strong enough to kill
  germs burn skin and eyes, and may not go in a septic tank.
- **"A splash of bleach in the bucket."** Urine and bleach can give off
  a toxic gas. Bleach is for cleaning surfaces, mixed with water and
  nothing else.
- **"Just dig a hole at the bottom of the garden."** Not in this
  region's wet soils, and not without a permit.
- **"Pee's sterile, it can go on the lawn."** In Kitsap County, urine is
  sewage under the health district's rules.
- **"I'll just lift the lid and look in."** Never lean into a septic
  tank.
- **"I'll reach in and pull him out."** Call 911. NIOSH counts would-be
  rescuers as more than 60 percent of the people who die in confined
  spaces at work, and a septic tank is a confined space (our reading).
- **"The composting toilet makes free fertiliser for the vegetables."**
  Not in Washington: its product does not go on a home garden.
- **"Septic tanks look after themselves."** Kitsap requires an
  evaluation at least every three years for a standard system, and every
  year for an alternative one.

## How the game models it

The game turns human waste into fertiliser at the press of a button, and
skips everything in between.

- **Your waste is a reading, not a bucket.** On the Inventory page your
  Waste reading rises slowly while you live, filling in about three days
  of game time, and each solid meal adds to it. Above 75 you get the
  Unsanitary effect, which slows you a little. The HUD shows the reading
  when it passes half full (`src/systems/food.rs`,
  `data/status_effects.csv`, `src/gui/pages/hud.rs`).
- **One button empties it.** The Inventory page's Compost and draw off
  urine button turns every 25 points of Waste into one bag of Fertilizer
  at once and empties the reading. That is the same Fertilizer the home's
  Composter makes from plant material, and the garden uses it the same
  way (`src/systems/food.rs`, `data/garden/nutrients.ron`).
- **Urine collects in an unseen tank.** While you live, your urine fills
  a tank at one person-day a day. No machine in the home stands for it,
  because the home has no urine-diverting toilet yet, and no screen shows
  its level. It holds about two weeks, and the overflow is lost. The same
  button draws off each whole person-day as one Stored Urine, which the
  garden can use as quick nitrogen, but never on a crop within a month
  of its harvest (`src/systems/food.rs`, `data/items.csv`,
  `data/garden/nutrients.ron`, `data/home_outline.json`).
- **The house has a toilet and a septic tank.** In the default home the
  toilet is one of the machines that draws on the home's water, 42
  litres a day, and a waste line runs from it to a septic tank
  (`data/machines/home.ron`). Nothing in the game fills that tank, pumps
  it, inspects it or makes it fail.

**The default settings.** The play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play
mode; Normal, unless you choose another) does not change any of this:
your Waste reading rises in every mode, and the button works the same.
The Waste reading and the urine tank are saved with your game, and
"Start every session from the default home" is off by default, so the
next launch brings them back where you left them (`src/save_load.rs`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: the
months to years that real human waste takes to become safe, and the
heat it needs; Washington's rule that what a composting toilet produces
does not go on a home garden; whether your health district lets you use
urine on your own crops at all; the roundworm eggs, the norovirus and
the rest of the germs in [The Germs That
Matter](the_germs_that_matter.md); and every failure in this guide, from
a broken pipe to a flooded drainfield. In the game, the waste is gone
the moment you press the button. In real life, it is still there, and
still yours to deal with.

## You own this when

- You can tell whether it is the water or the drains that has failed,
  and you know not to flush after an earthquake or a flood until the
  pipes have been checked and, on a public sewer, officials say the
  sewer is working.
- You have two lidded buckets and one more for each person, and more if
  disposal could take weeks, a bucket seat, bags, dry cover material,
  gloves and soap in the garage now, not after the earthquake.
- You keep lids on and the bathroom latched when there is a small child
  in the house.
- You can set up a two-bucket toilet with a handwashing station beside
  it, and explain why pee and poo go in separate buckets.
- You know what sawdust, lime and bleach each do, and which of them go
  in the bucket.
- You keep stored waste sealed and away from food, water, children, pets
  and rats, and you wait for disposal instructions.
- You would empty buckets into your own septic tank only if it is known
  to work, with no cover material in them, never leaning in, and with
  children well away.
- You know that nothing goes on the ground or into water, urine
  included, in Kitsap County.
- You know the cathole rule for the backcountry, and that it is not a
  rule for home.
- You know where your septic tank and drainfield are, whether the system
  has a pump, whether the district counts it as standard or
  alternative, when it was last inspected, and what it needs in a power
  cut and a flood.
- You would never go into, or lean into, a septic tank after someone
  who has collapsed there; you would stay back and call 911.
- You can explain why composting human waste takes heat, time and a
  permit, and why its product does not go on the vegetable garden.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- Flushing by pouring a bucket of water into the bowl, using washing
  water for it and filling lidded buckets before a planned outage;
  lining the poo bucket with a bag when the waste is to be stored, tying
  the bag off before it is too heavy, and putting it straight into a
  lidded bucket or bin; storing full buckets away from food, drinking
  water, children and pets, out of the sun, in a garage or shed; no
  flame near an open septic tank; calling the sewer utility about a
  backup on a public sewer; and calling in professionals for sewage
  cleanup beyond a small area or where the structure or wiring got wet,
  are general practice.
- That a broken drain should be assumed until the pipes have been
  checked and, on a public sewer, officials say it is working; that
  flushing water belongs in lidded buckets rather than a filled bathtub,
  and the bathroom latched, when a small child is in the house (from the
  CPSC's advice); that the last soap-and-water clean leaves no bleach on
  the seat; that sawdust makes waste bearable rather than safe; that a
  household storage bucket does not need lime and that strong lime is a
  strong base under Kitsap's rule; how fast a family of four fills six
  buckets (our arithmetic from Clallam's and the Department of Health's
  figures), and keeping more if disposal could take weeks; that bleach
  belongs on surfaces rather than in the buckets, and that urine is wiped
  up before bleach goes on; that a septic tank should be checked after
  an earthquake before buckets are emptied into it; that lifting the lid
  from the ground is as far as a householder goes; that a septic tank is
  a confined space in NIOSH's sense; that for an adult the 2-day line
  for diarrhoea comes before the CDC's 3-day one; that urine counts as
  sewage under Kitsap's definition and may not go on the ground; that the
  cathole is for travellers, not households; that the Alderwood till
