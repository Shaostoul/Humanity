# Keeping Things Working

A pump, a mower, a furnace or a hammer does not usually fail all at
once. It wears, clogs, dries out, loosens or corrodes, and for a while
it keeps working anyway. Maintenance is the work you do in that while:
the cleaning, oiling, checking, tightening and replacing that keeps a
machine working and lets you see trouble coming before it stops you.

On a homestead it is also some of the cheapest work there is. A new
filter costs less than a new furnace, and ten minutes on a Saturday costs
less than a broken pump on the coldest night of the year. Done well,
maintenance turns most breakdowns into chores you chose the time for.

This guide takes its ideas about which maintenance is worth doing from
NASA's guide to maintaining its buildings and equipment, its safety
rules from the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA),
the armed services' joint hand tool manual and the Consumer Product
Safety Commission (CPSC), and its household examples from the Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA), the ENERGY STAR programme, the US Fire
Administration and the Federal Trade Commission (FTC). All are United
States government publications, in the public domain. Where something
is general practice rather than a published rule, the text says so.

It sits between two other guides. [Working Out Why Something
Broke](working_out_why_something_broke.md) is what to do when a
machine has stopped; this guide is about keeping it from stopping.
[Keeping Records](keeping_records.md) is how you remember what you did,
and maintenance is where records pay off most.

## First, make it safe to work on

Most maintenance means putting your hands where the machine does its
work: on the blade, the belt, the fan, the wiring or the fuel. OSHA's
rule on controlling hazardous energy (29 CFR 1910.147) defines
servicing and maintenance to include lubrication, cleaning and
unjamming, and it exists because those are exactly the moments a
person is exposed to a machine starting up unexpectedly. Oiling a
chain or clearing a clogged chute is not a small job from the
machine's point of view.

The full procedure for isolating a machine is in [Working Out Why
Something Broke](working_out_why_something_broke.md), and it applies
here unchanged. In short: take away every source of energy at a real
isolator (a breaker, a valve, an unplugged cord in your own hand), not
at the machine's on/off switch; **lock or label the isolator so nobody
turns it back on while you work**, because a breaker someone else can
switch on is not isolation; release the energy that stays behind after
you switch off; and check that it is dead before you touch it. For
wiring, that check means a voltage tester, not the switch. The
diagnosis guide explains the lock and the label, and why a breaker
lock-off beats a strip of tape.

Five hazards come up again and again in maintenance work.

### Guards

Guards come off for maintenance, and the danger is that they do not
go back on. OSHA's general rule for machines (29 CFR 1910.212)
requires guarding against hazards such as those created by point of
operation, ingoing nip points, rotating parts, flying chips and sparks,
and it says guards shall be affixed to the machine where possible. An
ingoing nip point is where a belt meets a pulley or two rollers meet:
it pulls in whatever touches it, including a sleeve or a finger.

The CPSC's advice for lawn mowers puts it in household terms: do not
remove any safety devices, check safety features often, and repair or
replace them if needed.

Before you switch a machine back on after working on it, OSHA's
lockout rule (29 CFR 1910.147(e)) asks for three things: that the work
area is clear of anything that does not belong there and the machine's
components are operationally intact; that "all employees have been
safely positioned or removed"; and that the people affected are told
before the machine is started. In practice: every guard back on, every
tool picked up, every bolt you took out put back, **nobody near the
machine, children included, and everyone in the household told before
you start it.** (The household version is our reading of the rule,
which is written for workplaces.)

### Stored energy

A machine that is switched off can still hold energy: a pressure tank
full of water, a spring under tension, a raised part held up by
hydraulics, a hot engine or exhaust, a charged capacitor. The diagnosis
guide covers how to release it and when to stop instead. Two cases
belong here because they are routine maintenance jobs.

**Anything raised on a jack.** OSHA's rule for jacks (29 CFR 1910.244)
starts before the lift. The jack must have "a rating sufficient to lift
and sustain the load", and where there is no firm foundation, "the base
of the jack shall be blocked". Then, after the load has been raised, it
shall be cribbed, blocked, or otherwise secured at once. The armed
services' hand tool manual (TM 9-243) says the same things in its own
words: be aware of the jack's capabilities, especially its load
capacity; and, in capital letters, never get under a load that is
supported only by a jack, because any jack can fail. When jacking a
vehicle, it tells you to make certain nobody is under it, set the hand
brake, block the wheels at the other end from the one you are lifting,
and put supports under the vehicle once it is raised, so it cannot drop
if the jack fails.

So: check the jack's rated load against what you are lifting, put it on
firm, level ground or a solid block, never on soft soil or a slope, and
hold the load with stands or blocking rated for it, not with the jack.
A jack is for lifting; a stand or blocking is for holding. (Level ground
and stands rated for the load are general practice; the rest is the
rule and the manual.)

**Springs.** A garage door's counterbalance spring, a recoil starter,
a spring-loaded clutch: these can release suddenly when a fastener is
undone. If you cannot see how the energy in a spring will be let out
safely, stop. That is general practice, and for garage door springs in
particular, a job for someone trained.

### Fuel and exhaust

Small engines bring fire and carbon monoxide into maintenance work.

- **Refuel cold.** The CPSC says never to refuel a mower while it is
  running or while the engine is hot, and for generators to turn the
  engine off and let it cool, because gasoline spilled on hot engine
  parts could ignite.
- **Store fuel outside the house.** The CPSC says never to store
  generator fuel in the home: gasoline, propane, kerosene and other
  flammable liquids should be stored outside living areas in properly
  labelled, non-glass safety containers, and not near a fuel-burning
  appliance such as a gas water heater in a garage.
- **Run engines outdoors only.** The CPSC says never to use a generator
  inside a home, garage, crawlspace or shed, even with fans running or
  doors and windows open, because carbon monoxide can build up quickly
  and linger for hours after the engine stops, and you cannot see or
  smell it. Testing a mower or a chainsaw after a service is the same
  hazard: do it outside, away from doors and windows. (Applying the
  generator advice to other small engines is our extension of what the
  CPSC says; the exhaust is the same.)
- **Disable it before you touch the blade.** The CPSC's way to disable a
  mower is to remove the ignition wire from the spark plug, or the plug
  itself. On many mowers the blade turns the engine, and an engine
  turned by hand can start.

### Compressed air

Blowing dust out of a motor or a filter with an air compressor is a
common shortcut. OSHA (29 CFR 1910.242) allows compressed air for
cleaning only when reduced to less than 30 p.s.i., and then only with
effective chip guarding and personal protective equipment. The air
throws dust and chips at whatever is in the way, which is usually the
face of the person holding the nozzle. Wear eye protection, turn the
pressure down, and never point it at a person, including yourself.
(The last two habits are general practice.)

### Ladders

Gutters, roof vents, chimneys, light fittings and smoke alarms all put
you on a ladder, and the ladder brings a hazard of its own: a fall.
OSHA's ladder rule for construction work (29 CFR 1926.1053) is a
good checklist for home use:

- Set a straight or extension ladder so its foot is out from the wall
  by about one quarter of its working length (the distance along the
  ladder from the foot to where it rests at the top). That puts it at
  about 75 degrees.
- If you are climbing onto a roof or platform, the side rails should
  stick up at least 3 feet (0.9 m) above the edge you step onto, so you
  have something to hold as you step off and back on.
- Only on stable, level ground, and never on a slippery surface unless
  it is secured.
- Rest the top with both side rails supported equally, against
  something solid, not on one rail.
- Never load a ladder beyond its rated capacity, which is on its label;
  that load counts you, your tools and what you carry.
- Never move, shift or extend a ladder while anyone is on it.
- Do not use the top, or the top step, of a stepladder as a step.
- Face the ladder going up and down, keep at least one hand on it, and
  do not carry anything that could make you lose your balance.
- Near exposed electrical equipment, use a ladder with nonconductive
  side rails, which rules out aluminium.
- **Look up before you raise a ladder.** Carry it and raise it well clear
  of overhead power lines, including the line from the pole to the
  house; a ladder that touches one, of any material, can kill the person
  holding it (general practice).
- Inspect it periodically and after anything that could have damaged
  it. A ladder with broken rungs, split rails, corroded parts or other
  defects is marked or tagged "Do Not Use" and taken out of service
  until it is repaired.

The geometry of the one-quarter rule is in [Square, Level and
Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md).

## Four ways to look after a machine

NASA's guide to maintaining its facilities and equipment (its
Reliability-Centered Maintenance Guide, 2008) sorts maintenance into
four kinds. The names sound like engineering jargon, but each one is a
decision every householder already makes, mostly without noticing.

1. **Run it until it breaks.** NASA calls this reactive maintenance:
   breakdown, repair, fix-when-fail, or run-to-failure. You do nothing
   until it stops, then you repair or replace it.
2. **Service it on a schedule.** Preventive maintenance: regularly
   scheduled inspection, adjustments, cleaning, lubrication, parts
   replacement, calibration and repair, at set intervals of time,
   operating hours or cycles. NASA notes that it is done regardless of
   the machine's condition. Changing the oil every so many hours is
   preventive maintenance.
3. **Watch its condition and act on what you see.** NASA calls this
   predictive testing and inspection, or condition monitoring: judging
   a machine's condition by testing, visual inspection and performance
   data, so that maintenance is scheduled only when the machine's
   condition calls for it. Checking a furnace filter every month and
   changing it when it is dirty, rather than on a fixed date, is
   condition monitoring.
4. **Fix the cause so it stops happening.** Proactive maintenance:
   better design, installation, procedures, workmanship and scheduling,
   using root-cause analysis to stop the same failure coming back. When
   the same belt keeps wearing out because the pulleys are misaligned,
   aligning the pulleys is proactive maintenance.

NASA's point is that none of these is right for everything, and that
choosing among them is the actual skill.

### Running to failure is allowed, if you choose it

NASA says plainly that run-to-failure can be used effectively when it
is a conscious decision, made by weighing the risk and cost of the
failure against the cost of the maintenance that would prevent it. Its
examples include small electric motors that nothing critical depends
on, restroom exhaust fans, and items where the consequences of failure
are negligible.

At home, a bedside lamp, a spare extension cord or a garden sprinkler
can reasonably run until it breaks. A well pump in winter, a freezer
full of the harvest, the only stove in the house or a smoke alarm
cannot. The question to ask of each machine is not "how old is it" but
"what happens to us if this stops tonight". That way of sorting is our
reading of NASA's guidance, applied to a house.

NASA also names the cost of running to failure: if the item fails and
repair parts are not available, delays ensue. For the machines that
matter, that is the argument for keeping spares on the shelf: a belt,
a filter, a fuse, a spark plug, a pump seal. Which spares to keep is
general practice and depends on what you own; the machine's manual
usually lists the parts that wear.

### Why "replace it before it fails" is not always better

It feels as if replacing everything on a schedule must be safer than
waiting. NASA's guide reports that it often is not, and the reason is
worth knowing.

It describes three studies of how equipment actually fails, labelled
in the guide as United Airlines (1968), a Swedish study (1973) and the
US Navy (1982). In all three, most failures were what NASA calls
random rather than wear-out with age: random failures were between 77
and 92 percent of the total, and age-related failures the remaining 8
to 23 percent. Simple, single-piece items such as tyres, brake pads
and structural members are the kind that often do wear out with age,
especially where mechanical wear or metal fatigue is at work. Complex
equipment mostly does not have a clear wear-out age.

The surprising part is what scheduled overhauls do to complex
equipment. NASA's guide says that in many cases scheduled overhaul
increases the overall failure rate by introducing a high infant
mortality rate into an otherwise stable system. In plain terms, a
machine that has just been taken apart and put back together is more
likely to fail soon afterwards than it was before. Why, in our own
explanation: a part goes in slightly wrong, a new part is faulty, a
seal is disturbed. NASA puts it
bluntly: timed maintenance can often result in unnecessary and in some
cases harmful maintenance.

What that means at home:

- **Replace wearing parts on schedule.** Filters, oil, belts, blades,
  brake pads, toilet flappers and batteries wear out with use and time.
  These are the parts the manual gives intervals for, and the intervals
  are worth following.
- **Do not take apart what is working well just because it is old.** A
  motor that runs quietly and cool is not improved by being opened. Look
  at it, listen to it, and keep notes instead.
- **After any service, check the machine works, and check it again
  soon.** The first hours after maintenance are when a mistake shows.
  The habit of checking a new part rather than trusting it is in the
  diagnosis guide.

## Your manual is the schedule

Most machines you own come with their own maintenance schedule, and
the manufacturer's manual is where it lives: what to oil, with what, how
often, which filter, which spark plug, what pressure, what to do before
storing it for the winter. A general guide like this one cannot replace
it, and the numbers in the manual win over anything general.

The CPSC's mower advice gives the habit in three parts: read the
owner's manual to become familiar with the machine, keep it in a safe
place so it is handy next time, and check it for routine maintenance,
checking oil levels and fluids, and what to do when the mower is
stored for the off-season.

If you have lost a manual, it is usually worth searching for the model
number with the word "manual" (general practice). Write the model and
serial numbers into your maintenance log the day you get the machine,
before the label wears off or gets painted over.

### Records, and the warranty

The FTC's advice on car warranties makes two points that hold well
beyond cars. First, you usually do not have to use the dealer for
maintenance or repairs to keep the warranty: the FTC says it is illegal
for a dealer to deny warranty coverage because routine maintenance or
repairs were done by someone else, and the warranty stays in effect if
you use aftermarket or recycled parts (though if a defective or badly
fitted part damages something the warranty covers, that damage can be
refused, once the maker shows the part caused it). There is an
exception, in the FTC's words: "But if the warranty says that the work
will be done for free, the dealer or manufacturer can make you use
repair facilities it chooses." Read your warranty for that clause.
Second, the warranty company could ask for your maintenance records, so
keep records of repairs and maintenance, such as oil changes, tyre
rotations, belt replacements, new brake pads and inspections; without
them, the FTC says, "your claim might be denied."

The FTC page is written about cars, and about work done by someone
other than the dealer. If you do the work yourself, keep the receipts
for the oil and parts and write the date and the hours or mileage in
your log; that is general practice and the way to show the work was
done. [Keeping Records](keeping_records.md) gives the shape of a good
maintenance log, borrowed from the federal rule for aircraft
maintenance records.

## Look, listen, feel, smell

Condition monitoring sounds technical, and NASA's version uses
vibration analysers and infrared cameras. Its core, though, is in the
ways NASA lists for using the data, among them "Trend analysis", "Data
comparison" and "Tests against limits and ranges". At home, you are the
instrument, and your records are the trend.

- **Listen** to each machine when it is running well, so you know what
  normal sounds like. A new squeal, knock, grind or hum is a change.
- **Feel** for heat and vibration, carefully and only where it is safe
  to touch. A motor or bearing that is running much hotter than it
  used to is telling you something.
- **Look** for leaks, rust, cracks, frayed cords, loose fasteners,
  rubbed wires and worn belts.
- **Smell** for burning, hot insulation, fuel or exhaust indoors.
- **Measure** what you can: hours on the hour meter, the meter readings
  on your water and electricity, how long the pump runs to fill the
  tank. A change in a number you record every week is the earliest
  warning you will get.

Those habits are general practice; the reasoning behind them, that a
trend shows trouble before a failure does, is NASA's. When something
does change, the diagnosis guide is the next step.

## A year of maintenance for an ordinary home

These are the jobs with published advice behind them. Your own house
will have others: write them into the same calendar.

### Every month

- **Test every smoke alarm.** The US Fire Administration says to test
  smoke alarms monthly. For an alarm on a 9-volt battery, replace the
  battery at least once every year; for a hardwired alarm, replace its
  backup battery at least once a year. It says a smoke alarm with a dead
  or missing battery is the same as having no smoke alarm at all.
- **Test the carbon monoxide alarm's batteries.** The CPSC says to
  test the batteries of a CO alarm monthly.
- **Test the ground-fault (GFCI) outlets.** The CPSC says to test each
  GFCI at least once a month, after installation and after a power
  failure; its lamp test is described in [Working Out Why Something
  Broke](working_out_why_something_broke.md).
- **Check the furnace and air conditioner filter.** ENERGY STAR's
  advice is to inspect, clean or change the air filters in a central
  air conditioner, furnace or heat pump once a month, and it says a
  dirty filter can increase energy costs and damage the equipment,
  leading to early failure.

### Every time you use it

- **The clothes dryer's lint filter.** The US Fire Administration says
  you are at higher risk of a dryer fire if you do not clean the lint
  filter and the dryer vents. Its advice: never use the dryer without
  the lint filter, clean the filter before and after each cycle, clean
  the back of the dryer where lint builds up, check that the vent
  behind it is not damaged, crushed or restricted, and make sure the
  outdoor vent cover opens when the dryer runs.
- **Hand tools after each job.** Wipe them clean and dry, oil bare
  steel, put edges away covered, and touch up an edge before it is
  dull. The detail is in [Hand Tools](../making/hand_tools.md) and
  [Sharpening](../making/sharpening.md).

### Every season

- **Heating and cooling check-ups.** ENERGY STAR recommends having a
  contractor do annual pre-season check-ups: the cooling system in the
  spring and the heating system in the fall, when contractors are less
  busy. Its list of what a check-up includes is a useful picture of the
  machine: tightening electrical connections, lubricating moving parts,
  checking the condensate drain, cleaning the coils, checking the
  refrigerant, and on the heating side checking the gas or oil
  connections, burner and heat exchanger. Most of that list is the
  contractor's work, not yours; the filter is the part ENERGY STAR
  lists as yours to do.
- **Gutters and drainage.** The EPA's guide to mould and moisture says
  to clean and repair roof gutters regularly, to make sure the ground
  slopes away from the building's foundation so that water does not
  enter or collect around it, and to keep air conditioning drip pans
  clean and their drain lines flowing. The EPA says "regularly";
  checking with the seasons, and after big storms, is our suggestion.
- **Engines before storage.** The mower's manual, or the chainsaw's or
  the generator's, says what to do before the machine sits for the off
  season. The CPSC points you to it for exactly this.

### Every year

- **Chimneys and heating equipment.** The US Fire Administration says
  to have heating equipment and chimneys cleaned and inspected each year
  by a professional, and to keep anything that can burn at least 3 feet
  from heat sources. Why wet firewood makes the creosote a chimney
  inspection looks for is in [Firewood](firewood.md).
- **Smoke alarm batteries.** As above, at least once a year for 9-volt
  and backup batteries.

### Every few years

- **Toilet flappers.** The EPA says the rubber flapper in a toilet tank
  wears out, should be checked periodically, and should be replaced at
  least every five years. It suggests taking the old one, or a picture
  of it, to the hardware store so the new one fits. That is a small
  example of a good spares habit: know the exact part before you need
  it.
- **Smoke alarms themselves.** The US Fire Administration says to
  replace the whole smoke alarm every 10 years. Alarms with a sealed
  10-year battery are replaced according to the manufacturer's
  instructions.

## Worked example: a mower through one year

A walk-behind petrol mower shows the whole pattern in one machine.

1. **Before each use:** check the oil level and the fuel, with the
   engine cold, and look over the guards and the blade-brake control.
   The CPSC says to fill the tank before starting and never to refuel a
   running or hot engine.
2. **Before any work near the blade:** stop the engine, wait for the
   blade to stop completely, and pull the lead off the spark plug. The
   CPSC says the blade must come to a complete stop before you clear a
   jammed chute, because your fingers could be amputated if you do it
   while the blade turns.
3. **On the manual's schedule:** change the oil, clean or replace the
   air filter, sharpen or replace the blade, replace the spark plug.
   These are wearing parts, the kind that age-based replacement suits.
4. **Before winter storage:** whatever the manual says about fuel, oil
   and storage. Record what you did and the date.
5. **When something changes:** a new vibration usually means a bent or
   unbalanced blade, a hard start often means stale fuel or a fouled
   plug. Those are the general-practice first suspects; work through
   them with the diagnosis guide rather than replacing parts at random.

The general petrol-engine routine in steps 3 to 5 is general practice
and varies by engine; your manual wins.

## Worked example: a hand tool kit

Hand tools are the easiest machines to keep and the easiest to ruin.
The armed services' hand tool manual (TM 9-243), covered in depth in
[Hand Tools](../making/hand_tools.md), sets out the habits: keep tools clean
and free of rust, nicks, burrs and breaks; give metal a light coat of
oil; keep each tool in its proper place, with an inventory list in the
box checked after each job; never use a damaged tool; and use each tool
only on the job it was designed for. The Forest Service's guide to
hand tools adds the storage rules (saws stored straight, hung or laid
flat, because storing one bent can bow it; never in a wet sheath; raw
linseed oil on wooden handles) and the scheduling advice most worth
copying: at the end of each day, pick out the tools that need
maintenance and set a time to repair or replace them.

**Rags with linseed oil on them can catch fire by themselves.** As the
oil dries it gives off heat, and a crumpled rag or a pile of rags can
hold that heat until it ignites, hours later, with nobody near it
(general knowledge). OSHA's rule for construction sites (29 CFR
1926.252(e)) requires that oily rags "be kept in fire resistant covered
containers until removed from worksite". At home, do the same: spread
used rags flat outdoors, away from the house, until they are dry and
stiff, or put them in a metal can with a tight lid, filled with water,
and never leave them in a heap, a bin or a pocket (general practice).
The same goes for rags used with oil-based stains and varnishes. Ask
your local waste service how to get rid of them.

A broken tool should be marked so nobody picks it up by mistake. OSHA's
rule for jacks says one that is out of order shall be tagged and not
used until it is repaired, and its ladder rule says the same for a
defective ladder. A strip of tape reading "broken, do not use" on a
cracked hammer handle is the household version (general practice).

## Know where your maintenance stops

Plenty of maintenance is yours to do. Some is not, and the published
advice draws the line in several places:

- **Gas and oil burners, heat exchangers, refrigerant.** ENERGY STAR
  lists these among the contractor's tasks, and the US Fire
  Administration says chimneys and heating equipment should be cleaned
  and inspected by a professional each year.
- **The dryer's installation and servicing.** The US Fire
  Administration says to have a dryer installed and serviced by a
  professional; the lint filter and the visible vent are yours.
- **Anything inside an electrical panel, and anything with a large
  capacitor.** See the diagnosis guide.
- **Springs you cannot see how to release safely,** garage door springs
  above all (general practice).
- **Work under a raised vehicle without stands or blocking.** Not ever.
- **Anything whose failure would hurt somebody other than you,** such
  as brakes on a vehicle others ride in, if you are not trained to do
  it (general practice).

Knowing that a job is past your skill is part of maintenance. Writing
it into the calendar so the professional visit actually happens is the
other part.

## Traps

- **"It is still working."** A machine running on a clogged filter or
  low oil is working until it is not. Check, do not assume.
- **Over-maintaining.** Taking apart a machine that is running well can
  cause the failure you were trying to prevent. Follow the manual's
  intervals for wearing parts, and watch the rest.
- **Leaving a guard off "just for now".** Put it back before the
  machine runs, every time.
- **Maintenance that lives only in your head.** If it is not written
  down, you will not know when it was last done.
- **No spares for the machines that matter.** The cheap part you do not
  have is the one that stops the expensive machine.

## How the game models it

The game models wear on the things you carry and use by hand, and on
one thing in the garden.

- **Tools wear out by use.** Each craft you make yourself that needs a
  tool wears every tool it names by one use, and the tools have to be
  in your backpack. A tool breaks when its uses reach its durability:
  200 uses for a hammer or an adjustable wrench, 150 for a hand saw or
  a flat screwdriver, 120 for a utility knife, before any adjustment
  for how well it was made. Cooking that chops, such as soup or stew,
  wears the utility knife. A craft that an automated machine makes
  needs no tools at all. The rules for which crafts need which tools
  are in `data/crafting/tools.ron`, and the durabilities in the
  `durability` column of `data/items.csv`.
- **Other hand work wears things too.** Hoeing weeds wears the hoe by
  one use for every 10 square metres hoed (a hoe lasts 150 uses), and
  cleaning out a container with soap wears the soap bar by one use.
  The hoe's figure is in `data/garden/weeds.ron`.
- **Row covers wear out in the weather.** A row cover laid over a bed
  wears out after 365 garden days on it, the low end of the 1 to 3
  years its source gives for the lightweight grade; taken off sooner,
  it goes back into your pack whole. That is in
  `data/garden/pests.ron`.
- **How well a tool was made changes how long it lasts.** A tool you
  make by hand is graded by your skill, and the grade multiplies its
  durability: half for a poor tool, 1.3 times for a good one, 1.8 times
  for an excellent one, 2.5 times for a masterwork, and a defective
  tool breaks the first time it is used. The grades are in
  `data/manufacturing.ron`.
- **You can see the wear.** In the Inventory, a tool's details show
  "Uses left" as a count out of its total, which is the condition
  monitoring of this guide in its simplest form: check it before a big
  job, not halfway through.

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), and in Dev, as in Creative,
  materials are free: crafting and hoeing need no tools and wear none,
  and laying a row cover takes no pieces from your pack. Only the soap
  bar wears in every mode. To see tools wear, switch Play mode to
  Normal.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches, so
  a worn tool starts the next session as it was in the default home.
  Turn the setting off to keep your tools, and their wear, from one
  session to the next.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: there
is no way to oil, sharpen or repair a tool, so a game tool simply runs
until it breaks. And the machines and buildings of your home do not
wear: in the game, furnaces, pumps, generators and walls need no
servicing. Real homes are the other way round, and most of the work of
keeping one running is the maintenance the game leaves out.

## You own this when

- You make a machine safe before you service it, the same way you would
  before a repair, with the isolator locked or labelled, and you put
  every guard back, clear everyone away and tell the household before it
  runs again.
- You use a jack rated for the load, on firm ground, and you never get
  under anything held up only by a jack.
- You refuel engines cold, store fuel outside the house, and run
  engines only outdoors.
- You dry oily rags flat outdoors or keep them in a closed metal can of
  water, never in a heap.
- You set ladders at one in four with both rails resting evenly, stay
  off the top and the top step of a stepladder, look up for power lines,
  and tag a damaged ladder out of use.
- You can say, for each machine you depend on, whether you run it to
  failure, service it on a schedule, or watch its condition, and why.
- You follow the manual's intervals for wearing parts, and you do not
  take apart what is working well just because it is old.
- You keep spares for the machines whose failure would hurt.
- You test smoke alarms, CO alarms and GFCIs monthly, check the furnace
  filter monthly, and clean the dryer's lint filter every load.
- You keep a maintenance log, with receipts, for every machine that
  matters.
- You know which jobs belong to a professional, and you book them.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- Sorting machines by "what happens if this stops tonight", and the
  household examples of what can and cannot run to failure, are our
  reading of NASA's guidance.
- Which spares to keep, finding a lost manual by model number, and
  writing model and serial numbers into the log on day one are general
  practice.
- Keeping receipts and dated log entries for maintenance you do
  yourself is general practice; the FTC page speaks of work done by
  someone other than the dealer.
- The listen, feel, look, smell and measure habits are general
  practice; the idea that a trend gives warning is NASA's.
- Stopping at springs whose release you cannot see, garage door springs
  in particular, taping a broken tool as "do not use", and leaving
  safety-critical work on vehicles others ride in to the trained are
  general practice.
- The mower's first suspects (a bent blade for vibration, stale fuel or
  a fouled plug for hard starting) and the general petrol-engine routine
  are general practice; the engine's own manual takes precedence.
- That on many mowers turning the blade turns the engine, which can
  then start, is general knowledge, as it is in the diagnosis guide.
- Which jobs fall in which season of the maintenance calendar, beyond
  the intervals the sources give, is our arrangement.
- Aluminium ladders being conductive is general knowledge; OSHA's rule
  is stated in terms of nonconductive side rails. The 75 degrees for the
  one-quarter rule is arithmetic (the angle whose cosine is one
  quarter).
- Wearing eye protection and never pointing compressed air at a person
  are general practice, beside OSHA's pressure limit.
- Clearing children away and telling the household before a machine is
  restarted is our household reading of OSHA's lockout release rule.
- Jacking on firm, level ground and holding the load on stands rated for
  it are general practice, beside OSHA's rating and firm-foundation
  rules.
- Testing other small engines outdoors is our extension of the CPSC's
  generator advice.
- Resting the ladder's top against something solid, finding the rated
  load on the ladder's label, and keeping a raised ladder clear of
  overhead power lines are general practice, beside OSHA's ladder rule.
- That rags with linseed oil, oil-based stain or varnish on them can
  heat up and catch fire on their own is general knowledge; drying them
  flat outdoors or keeping them in a closed metal can of water, and
  asking the local waste service, are general practice, beside OSHA's
  rule for oily rags on construction sites.
