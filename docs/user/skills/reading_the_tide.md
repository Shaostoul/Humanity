# Reading the Tide

The tide is the most predictable dangerous thing you will ever stand
next to. It is published years in advance, to the minute, for free. And
it still drowns people, every year, on this shoreline and every other.

So the urgent part goes first.

**Before you walk out onto anything the water can cover:**

1. **Know whether the tide is rising or falling right now, and what
   time the next high water is.** Not roughly. Look it up before you
   leave. A falling tide invites you out; a rising one closes the way
   back while you are facing the other direction.
2. **Never let a bar, a spit, a mudflat or a beach below a bluff be
   your only way back.** Pick the route home first and make it one that
   works at any water level. On a spit or a bar the water does not
   arrive from in front of you. It arrives behind you, and you find out
   when you turn around.
3. **If someone or something goes in the water, do not follow them in.**
   Throw, reach, row, call. This is not caution, it is arithmetic, and
   the next section has the numbers.
4. **Stay out of the narrow passage.** Wide water is slow. A throat
   between two basins runs faster than you can swim, on a schedule that
   is not the same as the schedule of the tide table.
5. **The printed number is a prediction, not a measurement.** Wind and
   air pressure move the real water away from it, sometimes by more
   than a foot. Leave margin.

Everything below this is the explanation. Do those five things first.

Local numbers in this guide are for the head of Dyes Inlet on Puget
Sound, the place the simulation is built on.
[Where You Are: Silverdale, Washington](../locale/silverdale_wa.md) has
the rest of the setting, including the full hazard list. The method
transfers anywhere. The numbers do not.

## What actually kills people here

**Being cut off.** A spit, a bar or a flat is walkable for a few hours
either side of low water and underwater the rest of the time. The
mistake is not misreading the table, it is not looking at it, then
walking out on a falling tide and losing track of time. When the water
comes back it comes back across the low ground first, so the path you
came in on floods before the ground you are standing on does. You can be
dry and stranded and still in serious trouble.

**Going in after a dog, a child, or a dropped thing.** This is the one
that turns a bad afternoon into a funeral, and it is worth understanding
why before you are standing there deciding.

**Current in a narrow passage.** Dyes Inlet is a basin that fills and
empties through one throat, Port Washington Narrows. All of the water
that covers the inlet has to get through that gap twice a day, so it
moves fast there. NOAA's current station at the Warren Avenue Bridge
(station PUG1510) predicts peak currents of 4.54 knots on the flood and
4.01 knots on the ebb for 2026, with typical daily maxima of about 2.3
knots. Nobody swims against 4.5 knots. Nobody swims against 2.

**Cold water, all year.** Puget Sound does not get warm. NOAA's nearest
published water-temperature record in this basin is Tacoma, station
9446484, about 47 km south of Silverdale. Over all of 2025 its hourly
record ran from 7.7 C (45.9 F) in February to 15.6 C (60.1 F) in July,
and no calendar month averaged warmer than 13.7 C (56.7 F) or colder
than 9.0 C (48.2 F). That is a measurement from Tacoma, not from Dyes
Inlet, and the difference matters; more on that further down.

Set that against what the National Weather Service says cold water does
to a body. Involuntary gasping and hyperventilation "can be triggered by
water as warm as 77 F (25 C)." Puget Sound never came within 17 F of
that ceiling all year. Physical incapacitation, which NWS defines as "a
loss of muscular control in the arms, legs, hands and feet," can be
"felt within seconds of entering cold water."

CDC's occupational safety guidance for commercial fishing puts the whole
sequence in three numbers. In the first minute, "you gasp and
hyperventilate." Over "the next 10 minutes, it becomes more difficult to
use your hands, arms, and legs. Swimming failure and drowning can
occur." Hypothermia proper takes about an hour.

Read those two paragraphs together and the fourth item on the urgent
list stops being advice and becomes a fact about your body. You do not
have an hour. You have about ten minutes of useful swimming, and the
first of those ten you will spend unable to control your breathing. A
person in the water without flotation usually drowns from losing muscle
control, not from getting cold. That is why you throw something that
floats instead of adding a second person to the water.

## What the tide actually is

Two bodies pull on the ocean: the moon and the sun. The sun is far more
massive, but tide-raising force falls off with the cube of distance, not
the square, and NOAA's Ocean Service puts the result plainly: "the sun's
tide-generating force is about half that of the moon, and the moon is
the dominant force affecting the Earth's tides."

Because there are two of them, their effects add and cancel through the
month. NOAA: "When the sun, moon, and Earth are in alignment (at the
time of the new or full moon), the solar tide has an additive effect on
the lunar tide, creating extra-high high tides, and very low, low tides
both commonly called spring tides." A week later, at the quarter moons,
"the solar tide partially cancels out the lunar tide and produces
moderate tides known as neap tides." Two of each per lunar month.

Spring tides have nothing to do with the season. They are the big ones,
and they are also the ones that expose the most beach. If you want the
lowest water of the month, you want a spring tide. So does everybody
else, which is why the worst days for getting cut off are the best days
for being out there.

## Two highs and two lows, and they are not the same size

This is the part that catches people here, so it gets its own section.

NOAA sorts coastlines into three patterns. Diurnal: one high and one low
a day. Semidiurnal: "two high and two low tides of approximately equal
size every lunar day." Mixed semidiurnal: "two high and two low tides of
different size every lunar day." And: "The U.S. West Coast tends to have
mixed semidiurnal tides, whereas a semidiurnal pattern is more typical of
the East Coast."

Puget Sound is firmly in the mixed camp. Working from the published
harmonic constituents at NOAA's Bremerton station, the form factor is
0.942, which sits inside the standard mixed-mainly-semidiurnal band.

What that means on the ground is in one pair of numbers from Bremerton.
The mean difference between the two daily HIGH waters is 0.273 m, about
11 inches. The mean difference between the two daily LOW waters is
0.870 m, about 2.9 ft. The two lows are wildly unequal. One of them is
merely low. The other is the one that opens up the beach.

The practical consequence: **"the tide was fine yesterday at this hour"
is not an argument.** Yesterday at this hour you may have had the lower
of the two lows and today you have the higher one, three feet of water
different, at nearly the same clock time. The tide does not repeat daily
here. It runs on the lunar day, which NOAA gives as 24 hours and 50
minutes, so the whole pattern slides about 50 minutes later each day
while alternating between a generous low and a stingy one. Check today's
table for today.

## What the number is measured from

A tide table says "3.2 ft." Three point two feet above what?

Above a **tidal datum**: an average water level computed from 19 years
of observations, called the National Tidal Datum Epoch, currently 1983
to 2001. NOAA defines the ones you will meet:

| Datum | NOAA's definition | Bremerton, above MLLW |
|---|---|---|
| **MHHW**, mean higher high water | "The average of the higher high water height of each tidal day observed over the National Tidal Datum Epoch" | 3.571 m (11.7 ft) |
| **MHW**, mean high water | "The average of all the high water heights observed over the National Tidal Datum Epoch" | 3.298 m (10.8 ft) |
| **MSL**, mean sea level | "The arithmetic mean of hourly heights observed over the National Tidal Datum Epoch" | 2.077 m (6.8 ft) |
| **MLW**, mean low water | "The average of all the low water heights observed over the National Tidal Datum Epoch" | 0.870 m (2.9 ft) |
| **MLLW**, mean lower low water | "The average of the lower low water height of each tidal day observed over the National Tidal Datum Epoch" | 0 by definition |

In the United States, **MLLW is the zero**. NOAA CO-OPS: "The nautical
chart will show the depth of water relative to a standard datum level
(in the U.S. this is Mean Lower Low Water)." Tide predictions are given
against it too. So a chart that says 6 ft means 6 ft when the water is
at MLLW, and the real depth is the charted depth plus whatever the tide
table says at that moment.

Two consequences worth holding on to.

**MLLW is an average of the lower lows, not the lowest low.** Half the
lower low waters are below it. The predicted number can be negative, and
a negative number is not an error, it is water below chart datum. At
Bremerton the lowest astronomical tide in a 19-year prediction run is
-1.406 m, about -4.6 ft.

**Which end of the range you care about depends on what you are doing.**
Depth under a keel is measured from MLLW. Clearance under a bridge or a
cable is measured from mean high water, because that is the conservative
end for something you might hit with a mast. A reader who does not know
which datum a number uses cannot use the number.

The full span is worth knowing for its own sake. At Bremerton the mean
range, high water to low water, is 2.428 m (8.0 ft). The great diurnal
range, from mean higher high to mean lower low, is 3.571 m (11.7 ft).
The total observed swing on record, from the lowest water ever measured
to the highest, is 5.987 m, just under 20 ft.

## Reading the table, and how fast the water moves

A tide table gives you four times a day and a height at each. A tide
curve joins them. Between the two you have to estimate, and the
traditional mariner's estimate is the **rule of twelfths**.

It is a **rule of thumb**, not a NOAA publication. I could not find it
in any federal source, and it is quoted here as a convention that
experienced boaters use, not as something anyone measured. It says:
split the time between high and low water into six equal parts, and the
water moves one twelfth of the total range in the first part, two in the
second, three in the third, three in the fourth, two in the fifth, and
one in the last.

The shape is the point. **The water moves fastest in the middle of the
tide and slowest at the turn.** Half of the entire range goes past in
the middle two parts.

Put Bremerton's great diurnal range of 11.7 ft through it and, on a tide
that takes six hours to fall, roughly 5.9 ft of water moves in the
middle two hours, and about 2.9 ft in the fastest single hour. Three
feet of water, in an hour, while you are looking at a clam.

Two honest limits on the rule. First, it assumes the rise and the fall
each take about six hours and are symmetric, and here they are not:
NOAA's prediction offsets put high water inside Dyes Inlet 28 minutes
after Seattle and low water 53 minutes after, so the ebb runs about 25
minutes longer than the flood. Second, on a mixed tide the successive
intervals are not equal to begin with. Use the rule to understand the
shape, use the published curve for the actual numbers.

## Current is not height, and current is what gets you

A tide table tells you how HIGH the water is. A current table tells you
how FAST it is moving and in which direction. They are different
measurements, from different stations, and they do not peak at the same
time.

NOAA's terms: a current that "moves toward the land and away from the
sea" floods; one that "moves toward the sea away from the land" ebbs.
Between them is slack water, "a brief slack period, which can range from
seconds to several minutes."

For the Silverdale area the station is **PUG1510, Port Washington
Narrows at the Warren Avenue Bridge**, a harmonic current station 8.7 km
away, measured in the bin 1.22 m (4 ft) below the surface. Mean flood
sets 286 degrees true, mean ebb 99 degrees true. For 2026 NOAA predicts:

| | Speed |
|---|---|
| Maximum flood | 2.337 m/s (4.54 knots) |
| Maximum ebb | 2.064 m/s (4.01 knots) |
| Mean of the daily maximum floods | 1.205 m/s (2.34 knots) |
| Mean of the daily maximum ebbs | 1.160 m/s (2.26 knots) |

That is 731 flood events and 706 ebb events in the year, so this happens
roughly four times a day, every day, forever. It is not a storm
condition. It is Tuesday.

The geometry is the reason. The open inlet is wide and slow; the throat
is narrow, and every cubic metre of water has to go through it. Drowning
risk here concentrates in the throat, not on the open water, and that is
true of every narrow passage on every tidal coast.

**Slack water is the only safe window to cross**, and to find it you
have to look up the CURRENT prediction, not the tide prediction. Slack
generally falls near high or low water, but "generally" is not a time,
and in a place like this the offset between the two is exactly the thing
that catches somebody who checked the wrong table.

## A derived number is not a measured one

Here is the honest hole in everything above, and it is worth more to you
than any of the numbers.

**NOAA has a station inside Dyes Inlet and publishes no datums for it.**
Station 9445901, Tracyton, is 4.67 km from Silverdale and the only
station actually in the inlet. It is a subordinate station: it exists to
produce predictions by applying offsets to a reference station, and its
datums API returns nulls. There is no measured MHHW for Dyes Inlet.

So the local data file carries three things instead of one, on purpose:

- The **reference station**, Seattle (9447130), whose datums actually
  drive the Dyes Inlet predictions.
- The **nearest station that does publish datums**, Bremerton (9445958),
  10.7 km away in a different inlet. Those are the measured numbers used
  throughout this guide.
- A **derived estimate** for Dyes Inlet, computed by applying NOAA's own
  subordinate-station ratios (1.06 on high-water datums, 0.95 on
  low-water datums) to the Seattle values: MHHW about 3.67 m, MHW about
  3.39 m, MLW about 0.82 m.

The file labels that last block `is_published: false` and says so in
words: use it for planning, and for anything you would wade or anchor
by, use the live predictions for station 9445901 or the published
Bremerton datums.

The habit to take from this is a question, and you should ask it of
every number anyone hands you: **was this measured here, or calculated
from somewhere else?** A derived number can be perfectly good and still
be the wrong thing to trust your footing to. The tell is whether the
source will tell you which it is. Ours does. Many will not.

The same applies to the water temperatures further up. Those are
Tacoma's, because Tacoma has a sensor and Dyes Inlet does not. A shallow
enclosed inlet in August is warmer than the main basin. Treat them as
the shape of the year, not as the temperature of the water in front of
you.

## The table is a prediction

This is the one thing in this guide most likely to be forgotten, so it
gets said plainly.

**A tide table predicts the astronomical tide only.** NOAA CO-OPS: "we
can only predict the astronomical tides; water level changes created by
the gravity of the Moon, and the relative motions of the Earth, Sun and
Moon." And: "We cannot predict the effect that wind, rain, freshwater
runoff, and other short-term meteorological events will have on the
tides."

The weather pushes the water around, in both directions:

- **Wind.** NOAA: "Strong offshore winds can move water away from
  coastlines, exaggerating low tide exposures. Onshore winds may act to
  pile up water onto the shoreline, virtually eliminating low tide
  exposures."
- **Barometric pressure.** NOAA: "High-pressure systems can depress sea
  levels, leading to clear sunny days with exceptionally low tides.
  Conversely, low-pressure systems that contribute to cloudy, rainy
  conditions typically are associated with tides than are much higher
  than predicted."

Notice which way that cuts. The bright, still, high-pressure day that
looks safest is the day the water is lowest and you go furthest out. The
grey low-pressure day is the one where the water comes back higher than
the number said.

Bremerton has the receipt. The highest water ever recorded there, 4.74 m
above MLLW on 27 December 2022, was 0.446 m, about 1.5 ft, ABOVE the
highest tide astronomy alone can produce. That extra foot and a half was
storm surge, and no tide table anywhere predicted it.

Build margin into your plan for that. Not because a foot and a half is
usually there, but because the day it is there is the day you would not
have wanted to be on a spit.

## Looking up the real prediction

Everything above is method. This is where you get today's actual number.
All of it is free and none of it needs an account.

1. **Find your station.** NOAA's interactive map at
   https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/map/index.html lets you browse
   water level, current, and temperature stations by location. The tide
   prediction search at
   https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/tide_predictions.html accepts a
   station name (three characters minimum), a seven-digit station ID, or
   latitude and longitude in decimal degrees.
2. **Read the tide prediction** at
   `https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/noaatidepredictions.html?id=<ID>`.
   For Dyes Inlet that is ID **9445901**. You can set the date range,
   time zone, units and datum, and download the table.
3. **Read the current prediction separately**, from
   https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/noaacurrents/ . For this area the
   station is **PUG1510**. The tide page will not tell you when slack
   water is.
4. **Check the datums** at
   `https://tidesandcurrents.noaa.gov/datums.html?id=<ID>`. If the page
   is full of nulls, you are at a subordinate station and its numbers
   come from somewhere else. That is worth knowing before you rely on
   them.

**What the station list actually means.** A station is not a station.
NOAA runs a small number of **reference** or **harmonic** stations,
which have long instrument records, published datums, and predictions
computed from their own measured harmonic constituents. Everything else
is a **subordinate** station, whose predictions are the reference
station's predictions with a time offset and a height ratio applied.
Both appear on the map and both give you a number. Only one of them
measured anything where you are standing.

The nearest few here, by distance from Silverdale: Tracyton 4.7 km (S),
Brownsville 6.0 km (S), Poulsbo 9.9 km (S), Seabeck 10.1 km (S),
Bremerton 10.7 km (R), Bangor Wharf 11.9 km (R). R is a reference
station. S is subordinate.

## Shellfish, and the two separate questions

A minus tide is what makes shellfish digging possible. The lowest water
of the month uncovers ground that is otherwise always submerged, which
is exactly where the good beds are. So the tide table is genuinely the
first thing you check, and the biggest spring tides are the days people
go out.

**The tide has nothing to do with whether the shellfish are safe to
eat.** These are two independent questions and it is dangerous to let
the first one answer the second.

Marine biotoxins accumulate in shellfish from algae. Washington State
Department of Health is direct about all three parts of this:

- "Toxic shellfish look, taste, and smell the same as safe shellfish.
  The only way to know that shellfish are safe from toxins is to test
  them."
- "Cooking or freezing doesn't kill or remove toxins. The toxins are
  part of the shellfish meat."
- "Check the Shellfish Safety Map for biotoxin closures and advisories
  on the day you plan to harvest shellfish."

Cooking does not help. Freezing does not help. Paralytic shellfish
poison is not a germ you can kill, it is a chemical inside the meat, and
no kitchen technique touches it. This is the same shape of problem as
chemical contamination in
[Making Water Safe to Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md): a hazard
that the obvious treatment does not reach.

**The recorded biotoxin closure hotline for Washington is
1-800-562-5632.** Call it or check the Shellfish Safety Map on the day
you intend to harvest, every time. A closure that was not in place last
weekend can be in place now.

## Tsunami, briefly, because it lives here too

The tide and the tsunami share a shoreline, and on this one the right
response depends entirely on which kind you have. Both are in
[the locale hazard list](../locale/silverdale_wa.md), with the modelled
numbers.

**Near field, the Seattle Fault case: minutes.** The Washington
Geological Survey models a roughly 5 ft wave at the Silverdale point
about **5 minutes** after the shaking stops, with less than 3 minutes at
Bremerton, Point White and the Bainbridge ferry terminal. There is no
time for an official warning to reach you. The earthquake IS the
warning.

**Far field, the Cascadia case: hours.** The same modelling gives about
**2 hours 40 minutes** to Bremerton. Inside Puget Sound this is the one
tsunami case where an official warning and an organised evacuation are
realistic, because the wave has to refract in through the Strait of Juan
de Fuca and Admiralty Inlet first.

The instruction that covers both comes from the National Weather
Service, and it is short. If you "feel a strong or long earthquake, the
ocean acts strange (e.g., it looks like a fast-rising flood or a wall of
water or it drains away suddenly, showing the ocean floor like a very
low tide) OR there is a loud roar coming from the ocean," then "do not
wait for an official tsunami warning or for instructions from local
officials." Go "as high or as far inland (away from the water) as
possible." And stay there: "The first wave may not be the last or the
largest and the danger may last for hours or days."

Note the second natural sign. Water draining away suddenly and exposing
ground that should be underwater looks exactly like a very good minus
tide. If the water leaves faster than a tide can leave, or leaves at a
time the table did not predict, that is not your lucky day. Go uphill.

One more thing the modelling makes clear, and it is the reason this
section belongs in a tide guide at all: the wave arrives **on top of
whatever the tide is doing.** With a great diurnal range of 11.7 ft
here, the stage of the tide matters as much as the wave. The same 5 ft
wave at mean lower low water and at a king high tide are two different
events.

## What can go wrong

- **You checked the tide and got cut off anyway.** Almost always a
  current-versus-height confusion, or the rule of twelfths biting: you
  budgeted your walk back against the average rate and it happened in
  the middle of the tide, at three times that.
- **Yesterday's timing did not work today.** Mixed semidiurnal. The two
  lows differ by an average of 2.9 ft here and the whole pattern slides
  about 50 minutes later each day. There is no "usual time."
- **The water was higher than the table said.** Onshore wind, low
  pressure, or both. Bremerton's record high beat the astronomical
  maximum by 1.5 ft. The table predicts astronomy, not weather.
- **You read a depth off a chart and touched bottom.** Charted depths
  are from MLLW, and roughly half of all lower low waters fall BELOW
  MLLW. A predicted minus tide subtracts from every charted number on
  the page.
- **You used a number from the nearest station without checking what
  kind of station it was.** Subordinate stations have no datums of their
  own. Ask whether the number was measured or derived.
- **You crossed at high water because you thought that was slack.**
  Slack water generally falls near the turn of the tide, but "generally"
  is not a schedule. Look up the current station.
- **Someone went in and a second person followed.** The most common way
  one casualty becomes two. Throw, reach, row, call. In 45 to 60 F water
  a strong swimmer has about ten minutes of useful muscle control and
  loses the first minute to uncontrolled breathing.
- **You cooked the clams thoroughly and someone got sick.** Heat does
  not touch marine biotoxin. Call 1-800-562-5632 before harvesting, not
  after.

## You own this when

- You can say, right now, whether the tide outside is rising or falling
  and when the next high water is.
- You know what datum your number is measured from, and that MLLW is an
  average of the lower lows rather than the lowest low.
- You expect the two daily lows to differ, and you check today's table
  rather than reusing yesterday's timing.
- You look up tidal CURRENT separately from tidal HEIGHT when a narrow
  passage is involved, and you can name the current station.
- You treat every printed height as a prediction of astronomy, and you
  leave margin for wind and pressure.
- You ask of any number whether it was measured at that place or derived
  from somewhere else, and you can tell which the local numbers are.
- You have a route back that does not depend on the water staying where
  it is.
- You would not go in after anything, and you know why in minutes.

The last one is the only item on the list that is about you rather than
about the water. Everything else here is lookup and arithmetic, and it
is genuinely easy once you have done it twice. The decision at the
water's edge, made in two seconds with a dog in the current, is the one
that has to be made before you get there.

### Not sourced to any authority

- **The rule of twelfths.** A mariner's rule of thumb. No federal or
  other authoritative publication of it was found while writing this
  guide, so it is presented as a convention for understanding the SHAPE
  of the curve and explicitly not as a substitute for the published
  prediction. Its two assumptions, a six-hour rise and a symmetric
  curve, are both imperfect here.
