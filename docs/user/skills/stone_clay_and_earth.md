# Stone, Clay and Earth

The oldest building materials are the ones already under your feet.
Stone from a field or a creek bed, clay from a bank, sand and gravel
from a pit, and the earth itself have built walls, ovens, floors and
whole towns for thousands of years, and they cost nothing but the work
of moving them.

They are also heavy, dusty, and in the case of clay and earth,
unforgiving of water. This guide is about what each one is good for,
how to tell what you have, how to build small things with them that
last, and where your own work should stop and a professional's begin.

It takes its building methods from the Army's field manual on concrete
and masonry (FM 5-428) and the National Park Service's guides to
preserving historic adobe and masonry buildings, its safety rules from
the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the National
Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), the armed
services' hand tool manual, the Consumer Product Safety Commission
(CPSC) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC), and its
warnings about earthquakes from the Federal Emergency Management Agency
(FEMA). All are United States
government publications, in the public domain. Where something is
general practice rather than a published rule, the text says so.

Several other guides cover the neighbours of this one. [What Soil
Is](what_soil_is.md) explains sand, silt and clay as sizes of the same
ground-up rock, and gives the ribbon and jar tests for telling them
apart. [Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) shows how to
look up what is under a particular piece of ground. [Ratios and
Mixing](ratios_and_mixing.md) gives the mixes for concrete and mortar,
and the full advice on cement burns. [Square, Level and
Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md) covers laying out a wall and digging
safely. [Choosing Where to Build](choosing_where_to_build.md) covers the
ground under a building.

## First: how stone and earth hurt people

### Digging

Clay and sand come out of holes, and holes collapse. OSHA's alert on
trench collapses (2022) says cave-ins are more likely than other
excavation accidents to kill workers, and "It only takes seconds to
become buried in thousands of pounds of soil." OSHA's rule (29 CFR
1926.652) requires protection from cave-ins in any excavation, with
two exceptions: one made entirely in stable rock, or one less than 5
feet deep where a competent person has examined the ground and found no
sign of a possible cave-in. So less than 5 feet is not automatically
safe. A clay pit with steep walls you are standing in, at any depth, is
a trench (our reading).

Two more rules from OSHA and the Department of Transportation, both
covered in [Square, Level and Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md): call 811
before you dig, because cables and gas lines are buried where you would
not expect them, and keep the dug soil, and your tools and stones, at
least 2 feet back from the edge of a hole (29 CFR 1926.651), so nothing
rolls in on whoever is in it.

### Lifting and moving stone

A stone does not look heavy until you try to move it. OSHA's guidance on
heavy lifting says that loads heavier than about 50 pounds increase the
risk of injury, and to use two or more people above that weight. Its
other rules: keep the load as close to your body as you can, keep your
spine straight, usually by bending at the knees rather than the waist,
lift from mid-thigh to mid-chest height where you can, and turn with
your feet rather than twisting. The Army's masonry manual makes the same
point for building with field stone: "No stones should be larger than
what two persons can handle easily."

Two habits (general practice): wear sturdy boots and gloves, because a
dropped or shifting stone crushes toes and fingers; and never get your hand or foot under a stone that is being levered or
rolled. Rolling a large stone, or lifting one with a bar, is covered in
[Force, Levers and Mechanical
Advantage](force_levers_and_mechanical_advantage.md).

### Chips and dust

Splitting and dressing stone throws sharp chips. The armed services'
hand tool manual (TM 9-243) says eye protection is necessary for flying
objects such as "chipped concrete and wood", during chipping, grinding
and similar work. Wear safety glasses or goggles every time you strike
stone, brick or concrete.

Cutting, grinding or drilling stone, brick, concrete and mortar makes a
finer, worse hazard. OSHA explains that sand, stone, concrete and mortar
contain crystalline silica, and that the tiny particles you breathe in
are created "when cutting, sawing, grinding, drilling, and crushing
stone, rock, concrete, brick, block, and mortar." Breathing them puts
people at risk of silicosis, which OSHA calls "an incurable lung disease
that can lead to disability and death", and of lung cancer, chronic
obstructive pulmonary disease and kidney disease. OSHA's construction
rule (29 CFR 1926.1153) lists, for power saws cutting masonry, a saw
"equipped with integrated water delivery system that continuously feeds
water to the blade."

At home, following that rule (general practice, because the rule is
written for workplaces):

- **Cut wet, with a saw built for it.** Use a saw made to feed water to
  the blade. Never pour water onto a saw or grinder made for dry
  cutting.
- **Keep the electricity safe around the water.** Plug a wet saw into an
  outlet protected by a ground fault circuit interrupter (GFCI), or
  through a portable GFCI. The CPSC's fact sheet on GFCIs names places
  "where electrical equipment is near water" as particularly
  vulnerable, and says to consider portable GFCI protection with
  electric tools, saws among them, for work in and around the house.
- **Cut outdoors**, keep other people away, and never dry-cut stone or
  concrete indoors.
- **Wear a respirator: a NIOSH-approved N95 or better, not a nuisance
  dust mask.** Water does not make it optional. OSHA's Table 1 calls
  for a respirator with an assigned protection factor of 10 for a
  handheld saw "When used indoors or in an enclosed area" even with
  water feeding the blade, and outdoors once the cutting passes 4 hours
  in a shift. OSHA's respirator rule (29 CFR 1910.134) gives half-mask
  respirators that factor of 10, a category it says includes filtering
  facepieces, which is what an N95 is.

Cleaning up is in the rule too. 29 CFR 1926.1153(f) does not allow dry
sweeping or dry brushing where it could raise silica dust, unless wet
sweeping, a vacuum with a HEPA filter or another low-dust method is not
feasible, and it limits using compressed air to clean clothing or
surfaces. So wet dust before you sweep it, or vacuum it with a HEPA
filter, and never blow it off with compressed air.

### Lime and cement

Quicklime, the burnt lime used to make lime mortar and plaster, reacts
with water. NIOSH's Pocket Guide lists water among its incompatibilities
and notes that it "liberates heat". The effects it lists go beyond
irritation of the eyes, skin and breathing passages: "ulcer, perforation
nasal septum; pneumonitis; dermatitis", which means sores that can wear
a hole through the wall between the nostrils, inflamed lungs, and
inflamed skin. Its protective advice is "Prevent skin contact" and
"Prevent eye contact", and its first aid for the eyes is "Irrigate
immediately" and for the skin "Water flush immediately".

So at home, buy hydrated lime or ready-made lime putty rather than
slaking quicklime yourself (general practice). If you do slake it, wear
goggles, gloves, long sleeves and an N95 respirator; add the quicklime a
little at a time to plenty of water, never water onto a heap of lime;
and stand back, because the mix can boil and spit (general practice).

Wet portland cement can cause caustic burns which, OSHA
says, may not hurt at first, can keep getting worse after the cement is
off the skin, and are a reason to see a health care professional
immediately. The full advice on
gloves, boots, eye protection and first aid for cement and lime is in
[Ratios and Mixing](ratios_and_mixing.md); read it before you mix
either.

### Fire, ovens and hot stone

An earth oven or a fire pit is a fire, with a fire's rules (general
practice): build it and use it outdoors only, well clear of buildings,
fences, overhanging branches and anything else that burns; follow your
local burn rules and any burn ban; and never fire one, or carry its
coals, into a garage, a shed or any other enclosed space. Burning fuel
makes carbon monoxide, a gas you cannot see or smell. The CDC says it is
in the fumes produced "any time you burn fuel", in grills, stoves and
fireplaces among other things, and tells people: "Never burn charcoal
indoors." [Firewood](firewood.md) covers burn bans and carbon monoxide
in full, and [Ventilation, Damp and
Mould](ventilation_damp_and_mould.md) covers why it kills.

Stone can break in a fire. Stones from a creek bed, and porous or
layered stones, can hold water, and when they are heated they can crack
or burst and throw hot pieces (general practice). Keep them away from
the flame: line a fire pit, or the floor of an oven, with fire brick
made for the purpose, and keep field and creek stones for the outside
of the ring (general practice).

## What is under your feet depends on where you are

This guide cannot tell you what stone or clay you have, because the
answer changes from place to place. Two free sources can:

- **Soil.** [Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) walks
  through the USDA's Web Soil Survey, which maps the soil series of a
  piece of ground with their texture, depth and drainage.
- **Rock.** The US Geological Survey (USGS) says geologic maps at many
  scales are listed in its National Geologic Map Database, and that
  many state geological surveys have detailed maps to download or buy.
  It notes that detailed geologic mapping has not been completed for
  the whole country, but that maps are available for most locations.

Taking stone is not always yours to do, either. You need the
landowner's permission on private land (general practice). In national
parks, the federal rule (36 CFR 2.1) prohibits possessing, removing,
digging or disturbing "A mineral resource or cave formation or the parts
thereof"; treat stone and clay in a park as not yours to take (our
reading). Other public lands have their own rules, so ask the agency
before you collect (general practice).

## Stone

### Which stone

The Army's masonry manual lists limestone, sandstone, granite and
slate among the common stones suitable for rough walls, and says to
use stones that are "strong, durable, and cheap", meaning unsquared
stones or field stones from nearby ledges or quarries. It adds that the
larger the variety of sizes you collect, the less mortar you need.

A stone that crumbles when you scrape it with a steel tool, or flakes
in layers after a frost, will not last in a wall (general practice).

### Laying a rubble wall

The manual calls a wall of rough, uncut stones **rubble masonry**, and
says it can be laid with or without mortar, "but for strength and
stability use mortar." Its rules for laying it are worth learning
whether you use mortar or not:

1. **Footing first, with the biggest stones.** A footing is wider than
   the wall above it; use the largest stones there, ideally each as long
   as the footing is wide, to give it strength and lessen the risk of
   uneven settling.
2. **Lay each stone on its broadest face.** A stone standing on its
   edge or end is waiting to fall over.
3. **Keep the bed joints horizontal.** The bed joints, the ones a stone
   sits on, should be horizontal for stability; the joints between
   stones side by side can run any way.
4. **Tie the wall through.** In every layer, include bonding stones
   that reach right through the wall to tie its two faces together: the
   manual says one for every 6 to 10 square feet of wall.
5. **Big at the bottom.** If appearance matters, lay the larger stones
   in the lower courses, and smaller ones as you go up.
6. **Pack tight.** Fit neighbouring stones as close as you can, and fill
   the gaps with smaller stones and mortar.
7. **Reset, do not shove.** If you need to move a stone after it is on
   the mortar bed, lift it clear and set it again.
8. **Wet porous stones** before they go into mortar, so they do not suck
   the water out of it and weaken the bond.

The manual says workmanship matters more than any other single thing
to the strength and life of a rubble wall.

### Worked example: a knee-high garden wall

A low wall along the edge of a raised bed, or around a fire pit, is a
good first project, because if it goes wrong little is at stake (our
example, using the manual's rules). Around a fire pit, keep the field
and creek stones on the outside and line the inside with fire brick, as
the section on fire above explains.

1. Mark the line, and dig a shallow trench down to firm subsoil, wider
   than the wall. Keep the spoil back from the edge.
2. Set the biggest, flattest stones in the trench as the footing, broad
   face down, packed tight.
3. Build up in layers, each stone resting on two below it where you can,
   the way bricks overlap, so no joint runs straight up the wall: the
   manual says to offset each head joint, the joint between stones side
   by side, from the head joints above and below it "as much as
   possible". In every layer, lay a long stone across the full width as
   a bonding stone.
4. Keep checking with a level and a string line, as in [Square, Level
   and Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md). Many builders lean each face
   slightly inward as it rises, so the wall is wider at the bottom
   (general practice).
5. Stop at knee height. A wall that is only there to edge a bed or sit
   on is yours to build. A wall that holds back earth is not, as the
   section on where your own work stops explains.

## Clay

### What clay is, and why it behaves the way it does

Clay is the finest part of soil: particles smaller than 0.002 mm,
which is why it feels sticky when wet and sets hard when dry ([What
Soil Is](what_soil_is.md) has the full table). Wet, it can be shaped.
Dry, it holds its shape. In between, it moves: the National Park
Service's guide to adobe buildings explains that because sun-dried
adobe bricks are not fired, "they do not permanently harden, but remain
unstable", shrinking and swelling with their water content, and that
"the higher the water content, the lower the strength."

That one fact explains most of what goes right and wrong with clay and
earth: keep it dry and it lasts; let it stay wet and it fails.

### Finding clay

Use the ribbon test in [What Soil Is](what_soil_is.md): a soil that
makes a long, strong ribbon has a lot of clay. Look below the dark
topsoil, which is full of roots and decaying plant matter that rot and
shrink in a wall (general practice). Creek banks and the subsoil of a
dug hole are the usual places to look (general practice), and the
digging rules above apply.

### Mud bricks, the adobe way

The Park Service's adobe guide describes the traditional brick: sand,
sometimes gravel, clay and water, often with straw or grass, mixed by
hand, formed in wooden moulds, and dried by the sun. Its other points:

- **Straw does not make the brick stronger.** The guide says straw and
  grass do not reinforce the brick or add long-term strength, but help
  the bricks shrink more evenly as they dry.
- **The clay-to-sand balance is what matters.** The guide says that
  more important for durability is the clay-to-sand ratio in the native
  soil. Too much clay and a brick cracks as it shrinks; too much sand
  and it crumbles (general practice).
- **Drying takes weeks.** The bricks are turned out of the mould onto a
  level surface covered with straw, dried for several days, then stood
  on end to air-cure for 4 weeks or longer.

**Worked example: finding your mix** (general practice). Make four or
five test bricks from your clay, each with a different share of sand
added, and mark which is which. Dry and cure them the way the adobe
guide describes, flat for several days and then standing on end, out of
the rain, and wait the full 4 weeks or longer of curing before you judge
them; a brick tested half-cured tells you nothing about the finished
one. Throw out any that cracked badly or crumble when you rub them.
Drop each of the rest from waist height onto grass: the ones that stay
in one piece tell you the mix to use. Write the result down, as in
[Keeping Records](keeping_records.md).

### Keeping earth walls dry

The adobe guide is blunt: adobe buildings generally deteriorate because
of moisture, from rain or from the ground, and their survival depends on
how well the building sheds water. Its remedies, which apply to a
garden wall or an earth oven as much as to a house (our reading):

- **A sound roof or cap, with drainage.** The guide calls a water-tight
  roof with proper drainage the best protection against rain erosion.
- **Ground sloping away.** Regrade so the ground slopes away from the
  wall and rain does not pool at its foot. Splashing rain and water from
  the ground hollow out the base of an earth wall, which the guide calls
  "coving".
- **Plants back from the wall.** Roots damage the wall and hold moisture
  against it.
- **Mud plaster, renewed.** The traditional coating is mud plaster, made
  of clay, sand, water and straw or grass, which bonds to the adobe
  because it is made of the same things. The guide says it is always
  better to cover adobe with mud plaster even though it must be renewed
  more often.

### Never seal earth in cement, or mortar it with lime

It seems sensible to protect a mud wall with cement. The Park Service
says it does the opposite. Cement stucco does not bond to unfired adobe;
its thermal expansion is 3 to 10 times greater than adobe's, so it
cracks; the cracks let water in, and the stucco stops the wall drying
out, so the damage goes on unseen. Cement mortar between adobe bricks
is stronger than the bricks, so the bricks crumble first.

Lime is no better here. The guide's rule for repairing mud mortar is to
"never replace adobe mud mortar with lime mortar or portland cement
mortar", because neither moves with the adobe as it warms and cools,
and so, it says, they make the bricks, the weaker material, crack,
crumble and eventually fall apart. Earth walls get mud mortar and mud
plaster.

That is the rule for traditional, unstabilized adobe. The guide notes
that cement and lime mortars are commonly used with stabilized adobe
bricks, the kind made with cement, lime or asphalt mixed in, but that
cement mortars are incompatible with unstabilized adobe. Mud bricks you make
yourself from clay, sand and straw are unstabilized.

## Mortar, lime and cement

### Mortar should be the weak part

The Park Service's guide to repointing masonry (1998) says new mortar must be softer and
more permeable to water vapour than the bricks or stones it joins, and
that "It is a common error to assume that hardness or high strength is a
measure of appropriateness". The reason: walls move, with heat, cold,
damp and settling. A softer mortar gives, and cracks in the joint, which
is easy to repoint. A mortar harder than the bricks makes the bricks
take the strain instead, and they crack and spall, which cannot easily
be repaired. Moisture should leave a wall through its joints, not
through its bricks. The guide calls mortar joints "intended to be
sacrificial".

In practice: for soft old brick and soft stone, a soft, high-lime
mortar; for adobe and other earth walls, mud mortar only, never lime or
cement (the adobe guide's rule, above); and strong portland cement
mortars only for hard stone and modern concrete block (general practice
following the two guides). The repointing guide adds that more lime
makes a softer, more workable mortar, and more portland cement a harder
one.

### Lime

Lime is made by burning limestone; quicklime then slakes with water into
lime putty or hydrated lime (general knowledge, and see NIOSH on
quicklime above). Lime mortar hardens slowly: the repointing guide says
that once the lime begins to carbonate, it continues for many years and
the lime gains strength as it turns back into calcium carbonate within
the wall. High-lime mortar left to dry out too fast cures badly, the
guide warns; it describes misting the joints with a fine spray for a day
or two, and covering the wall with burlap for the first three days.

### Portland cement and concrete

The Army's manual says portland cement's raw materials, lime and clay
minerals among them, are finely ground, proportioned, and heated to
between 2,600 and 3,000 degrees Fahrenheit to form clinker, which is
ground to a fine powder. At temperatures like those, it is not something
to make at home (our reading).

It hardens by a chemical reaction with water, not by drying. The manual
says the reaction, hydration, continues as long as the mix holds water
and the temperature is favourable, and "Once the water is removed,
hydration ceases and cannot be restarted." That is why new concrete is
kept moist while it cures: for most structural work, the manual says,
usually for 3 days to 2 weeks. The mixes, and why extra water weakens
concrete, are in [Ratios and Mixing](ratios_and_mixing.md).

Mixed mortar has a working life too. The manual says to use it within 2
1/2 hours of mixing when the air is 80 F or hotter, and within 3 1/2
hours below that, discarding any not used in time, and never to use
mortar that has frozen. The Park Service's repointing guide is stricter
for the mortar it describes for repointing old joints: use it within
about 30 minutes of the final mixing, and do not add more water to it
later.

## Sand and gravel

Sand and gravel are the bulk of concrete: the Army's manual says sand
and stone or gravel make up 60 to 80 percent of the volume of normal
concrete. They are also the base of paths and drains. The manual says
aggregates must be clean, hard,
strong and durable, and that the water should be free of organic matter
and other harmful substances. Dirty sand, with clay or soil in it, makes
weak mortar; rinse a handful in a jar and see what settles (general
practice; the jar test in [What Soil Is](what_soil_is.md) is the same
idea).

Gravel also helps keep earth walls and foundations dry. The adobe
guide describes footing drains around a building: a trench about 2 to
2 1/2 feet wide and several feet deep, dug at the base of the walls,
lined, with a pipe in the bottom and filled with gravel to within 6
inches of the surface. It warns that in weak soil the sides of such a
trench may need sloping to prevent a cave-in.

A trench several feet deep beside a wall is exactly what the digging
rules at the top of this guide are about: OSHA's protection rule
applies to it, and the wall's own weight bears on the trench side next
to it (our reading). Dig it only with the protection those rules
require, or have it dug by someone equipped to.

## Know where your own work stops

Low garden walls, edging, paths, steps of a stone or two, mud bricks and
mud plaster, small earth ovens built and fired outdoors (see the fire
section above) and repointing a few joints are yours to do. These are
not:

- **Anything that holds back earth.** A retaining wall carries the
  weight of the ground behind it, and when one fails it can fall on
  people. Have one designed, and check your local building code before
  you build one of any size (general practice).
- **Foundations and load-bearing walls for a building people live in.**
  These belong to a building permit, the code, and often an engineer
  (general practice).
- **Masonry or earth buildings in earthquake country.** FEMA's catalogue
  of its building science publications describes unreinforced masonry
  buildings as "typically the most vulnerable to earthquake damage"
  among buildings in use in US communities. Unreinforced means brick,
  block or stone with no reinforcing steel in it (general knowledge),
  which describes almost everything in this guide. Earth buildings
  carry the same risk (our reading). Check your area's earthquake
  hazard in [The Hazards Where You Live](the_hazards_where_you_live.md).
- **Cracks, bulges and leaning in an existing masonry or adobe
  building.** The adobe guide says structural problems are easy to see
  and hard to diagnose, and advises professional help in repairing them;
  it notes irreversible damage can be done by well-meaning but
  inexperienced restorers.
- **Chimneys and fireplaces.** Masonry that carries fire and smoke is a
  fire safety job (general practice).
- **Any trench you would stand in where the walls could cave in**, and
  any of 5 feet or more, without a competent person and the protection
  OSHA requires.

## Traps

- **Digging into a bank you are standing under.** It takes seconds.
- **The heavy stone you lift "just this once" alone.** Two people, or a
  bar and rollers.
- **Dry-cutting a paver indoors.** The dust you cannot see is the
  dangerous part.
- **Blowing the dust off with an air line.** It puts it back in the air
  you breathe.
- **Creek stones in the fire.** Wet stone can burst.
- **Slaking quicklime by pouring water on it.** It boils and spits.
- **Cement stucco on mud, or lime or cement mortar between mud bricks.**
  Both break the wall from inside.
- **Mortar stronger than the brick.** The brick loses.
- **Topsoil in a wall.** It rots and shrinks.
- **Letting concrete dry out in its first days.** It stops getting
  stronger and cannot restart.

## How the game models it

The game has the materials, and simple versions of what you make from
them.

- **The materials.** Raw Stone (granite), Raw Clay, Sand and Gravel are
  items in `data/items.csv`, along with Stone Brick, Cement and Concrete
  Block.
- **Where to find them.** Outside the home, beyond the outdoor fields,
  the game places stone outcrops, clay deposits and sand drifts. Walk
  up to one and press E to collect from it; it gives you more after a
  while (`data/entities/wild_spawns.ron`, `data/creatures.csv`).
  Collecting from these, like collecting eggs or berries, counts toward
  the Farming skill. The Mining skill grows only from the mining drones
  that fly to asteroids, and they bring back metal ores and graphite,
  not stone or clay (`src/systems/mining.rs`).
- **What you can make.** Cut Stone at a workbench turns one Raw Stone
  into two Stone Bricks and trains Woodworking; Fire Brick in a kiln
  turns 8 Raw Clay and 1 coal into 4 Stone Bricks and trains
  Metalworking; Mix Cement, by hand, makes Cement from Raw Stone, Raw
  Clay and Sand; Pour Concrete makes Concrete Blocks from Cement, Gravel
  and Purified Water; and a kiln is built from 12 Stone Bricks and 4 Raw
  Clay, once your Shelter Building skill reaches level 3
  (`data/recipes.csv`).
- **What you can build.** From the Crafting page, a Stone Foundation
  takes 12 Stone Bricks and a Stone Wall 10 (`data/blueprints/basic.ron`).

**Two settings change this.**

- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play mode
  (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode). In Dev, as in Creative, crafting
  takes no materials, no station and no tools, so you can make Stone
  Bricks and Cement without collecting anything. Building a wall or a
  foundation from the Crafting page still takes its bricks from your
  pack or the home's storage. To make collecting matter for crafting
  too, switch Play mode to Normal, or, in Dev, turn off the Creative
  mode switch on the Inventory page.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is also the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches, so
  what you collected starts the next session as it was in the default
  home.

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: cement
is mixed by hand from stone, clay and sand, with no kiln, where real
portland cement is burned at 2,600 degrees Fahrenheit or more; concrete
needs no curing; a fired clay brick and a cut stone are the same item;
and there is no dry-stone or mud building, no mortar, and no water
damage to earth walls. Nothing in the game's stonework makes dust, caves
in, or injures you by its weight, and in real life those three are
where the injuries come from.

## You own this when

- You call 811 before you dig, keep spoil back from the edge, and never
  stand in a trench whose walls could fall on you.
- You lift heavy stones with two people or with a bar, keep the load
  close, and wear boots, gloves and eye protection.
- You cut stone and concrete wet, with a saw built for it on a GFCI,
  outdoors, in an N95 or better, never dry-cut indoors, and clean up wet
  or with a HEPA vacuum, never with compressed air.
- You fire earth ovens and fire pits outdoors only, with fire brick
  next to the flame, never in a garage or any enclosed space.
- You buy hydrated lime or lime putty rather than slaking quicklime, or
  slake it with goggles and gloves, lime into water.
- You know where to look up the soil and rock under your own ground, and
  you have permission before you take stone.
- You can lay a low rubble wall on a footing of big stones, broad faces
  down, bed joints level, with bonding stones through it.
- You can find clay, make test bricks, cure them fully, and choose a mix
  from them.
- You keep earth walls capped, drained and plastered with mud, and never
  seal them in cement.
- You choose a mortar softer than the stone or brick it joins, and mud
  for earth walls.
- You keep new concrete moist while it cures.
- You know that walls holding back earth, building foundations, and
  masonry in earthquake country belong to a professional.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- That a clay pit with steep walls is a trench is our reading of OSHA's
  rule.
- Boots and gloves against crushed toes and pinched fingers, and keeping
  hands and feet from under a levered stone, are general practice.
- Cutting wet with a saw built for it, never adding water to a dry-cut
  tool, a GFCI on a wet saw, cutting outdoors, keeping people away, and
  an N95 or better for home cutting are general practice following
  OSHA's silica rule, which is written for workplaces, and the CPSC's
  GFCI sheet; that an N95 is a filtering facepiece is general
  knowledge. Wet sweeping, HEPA vacuuming and not cleaning with
  compressed air are the rule's own housekeeping paragraph.
- Buying hydrated lime or putty rather than slaking quicklime, and how
  to slake it if you do (protective gear, lime into plenty of water,
  boiling and spitting), are general practice beside NIOSH's entry; the
  plain-words meaning of NIOSH's symptom list is general knowledge.
- Building and firing earth ovens and fire pits outdoors only, clear of
  anything that burns, under local burn rules, and never in an enclosed
  space, are general practice beside the CDC's carbon monoxide advice;
  wet or porous stones cracking or bursting in a fire, and lining a pit
  with fire brick, are general practice.
- Asking permission on private land, and asking the agency on other
  public lands, are general practice; treating stone and clay in a
  national park as not yours to take is our reading of 36 CFR 2.1.
- What "unreinforced" means is general knowledge; FEMA's catalogue
  entry does not define it.
- Testing stone by scraping and watching for frost flaking, each stone
  resting on two below, leaning the faces inward and the knee-high
  garden wall example are general practice and our example.
- Avoiding topsoil, where to look for clay, the effect of too much clay
  or too much sand, and the test-brick method are general practice; the
  curing time before testing is the adobe guide's.
- Choosing soft lime mortar for soft brick and soft stone, and strong
  cement mortar for hard stone and block, is general practice following
  the Park Service's repointing guide; mud only for earth walls is the
  adobe guide's own rule.
- That the wall's weight bears on a footing-drain trench beside it is
  our reading.
- How lime is made and slaked is general knowledge; that portland
  cement is not something to make at home is our reading of the kiln
  temperatures.
- That the adobe guide's remedies apply to garden walls and earth ovens
  is our reading.
- Rinsing sand in a jar is general practice.
- Retaining walls, building foundations, chimneys and fireplaces
  needing a professional and the local code are general practice; earth
  buildings sharing the earthquake risk of unreinforced masonry is our
  reading of FEMA's description.
