# What to Do When You Are Lost

Getting lost happens to careful people. A trail fork in fading light, a
fog that comes down on a ridge, a detour round a fallen tree that never
quite finds the path again. What decides how it ends is rarely the
mistake that got you there. It is what you do in the next hour.

The National Park Service puts the hard part plainly: "Help can take
hours or days depending on your location, weather, and resources." This
guide is about using those hours well: what to do in the first minutes,
why staying where you are usually works better than walking, how to call
for help and be seen and heard, how to stay warm and dry while you wait,
what to do if it is someone else who is missing, and how to plan a trip
so that someone will come looking. The worked example is Kitsap County,
Washington, where the simulation is set.

It sits after the navigation guides: [Knowing Which Way Is
North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md), [Reading a Map](reading_a_map.md),
[Coordinates](coordinates.md) and [Navigating Without
Instruments](navigating_without_instruments.md). Those are about staying
found. This one is about what to do when that has failed. [Cold and
Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) covers what cold does to a body,
and [Light, Lenses and Mirrors](light_lenses_and_mirrors.md) covers
signal mirrors.

Its sources are the US Forest Service, the National Park Service, the
National 911 Program, NOAA's satellite rescue programme, the Federal
Aviation Administration, the Coast Guard, the National Weather Service,
the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention and the Army's survival
manual, all US federal and public domain; one peer-reviewed study; and,
for the worked example, Washington State law and the Washington Military
Department, restated in our own words. Where something is general
practice or our reading, the text says so.

## If you think you are lost, right now

The US Forest Service teaches the first minutes as four words that spell
STOP: Stop, Think, Observe, Plan.

1. **Stop.** "As soon as you realize you may be lost: stop, stay calm,
   stay put. Panic is your greatest enemy." Sit down if it helps. Eat
   something, drink something (general practice).
2. **Think.** Go back over how you got here and what landmarks you
   should be able to see. The same page: "Do not move at all until you
   have a specific reason to take a step."
3. **Observe.** Get out your compass and work out the directions from
   where you stand. "Do not walk aimlessly." If you are on a trail, stay
   on it.
4. **Plan.** Think of a few possible plans and choose one. Unless you are
   very, very confident of the route, the plan is to stay where you are,
   and if night is falling, you are hurt or you are near exhaustion, you
   stay even if you think you know the way (the Forest Service's rules).
   The one exception is the next step's: staying where you are is itself
   the danger.

Then, at once:

5. **Call 911 if you have any signal,** or text it if you cannot call.
   Tell them where you started, where you were last sure of, what you
   can see, what you are wearing and carrying, and any medical
   condition. Then stay on the line until they say otherwise (details
   below).
6. **Stay put** unless something is about to hurt you where you are. The
   National Park Service: "Changing location will make it difficult for
   authorities to find you. Move only if there is an immediate threat to
   your life."
7. **Get out of the wind and the wet, and make yourself easy to find.**
   Put on every layer you have. Get your whistle and your light out.

Everything below is the reasoning behind those seven, and the detail.

## Why staying put works

It feels wrong to sit still when you are lost. Here is why it is usually
right.

**Searchers start from where you were last known to be.** Rocky Mountain
National Park's page on search and rescue explains how a search begins:
rangers find the last known location and surround the area the person is
likely to be in. "For every hour that passes from the time a person is
lost, the search area must increase dramatically because the person
could have traveled farther in an unknown direction." Every hour you walk
is another hour of area somebody has to cover. The park's advice is the
same as the Forest Service's: "If you get lost, stay put so you can be
easily found."

**You probably cannot walk straight.** A 2009 study tracked people by GPS
walking as straight as they could through a large forest and across part
of the Sahara. When they could not see the sun they repeatedly walked in
circles; blindfolded, they walked in circles often less than 20 metres
across (Souman and others, 2009, restated). A lost
person walking to "get somewhere" may be getting nowhere, and tiring
themselves out doing it. That is the reason behind the Forest Service's
"Do not walk aimlessly." (our reading).

**A trail is a place people come.** "If you are on a trail, stay on it."

**When moving is right.** The Park Service's plan says to try retracing
your steps first, if it is safe to do so, using your map, compass, GPS
or the landmarks you can see; if you cannot, call for help and stay put.
The Forest Service's rule for deciding is the one in the Plan step
above: if you are not very, very confident in the route, it is always
better to stay put. The one strong reason to move anyway is the Park
Service's: an immediate threat to your life where you are, such as
rising water, fire or falling rock (our examples).

**Following a stream downhill is not a plan.** The Forest Service lists
it only "As a very last resort", and adds: "Again, this could be very
dangerous." Streams in steep country run into gorges, waterfalls and
thickets that are harder to get out of than to get into (general
practice).

## Calling for help

### 911 from a mobile phone

The National 911 Program, part of the National Highway Traffic Safety
Administration, says: "All wireless phones, even those that are not
subscribed to or supported by a specific carrier, can call 911." So it
is worth trying 911 even when your own network shows no service: another
company's network may carry the call (our reading). Its warning comes
with it: a phone without active service does not send your location to the
911 centre, and the centre cannot call you back if you are cut off.

If you cannot speak, text. The program's advice is to call 911 if you
can and text if you cannot; a voice call is still the best way to reach
911. Text-to-911 is not available everywhere, and if it is not where you
are, the program says you should get an immediate message back from
your phone company telling you the text was not delivered. Washington's
911 office adds two cautions (restated), which this guide takes to hold
elsewhere too (our reading): a text does not necessarily tell the
dispatcher where you are, so type your location into it, and texting
911 does not work while your phone is roaming.

When you get through, the Park Service's plan lists what to tell them:

- GPS coordinates from your phone, if it has them;
- the name of the trail or area where you started;
- your last known location before you became lost;
- what you can see around you, including landmarks;
- the types and colours of clothing you are wearing;
- the equipment you are carrying;
- any medical conditions you have.

[Coordinates](coordinates.md) shows how to read a position off a phone
so that someone else can find it. The 911 program's last instruction is
the one people get wrong: "Do not hang up until the call-taker instructs
you to do so."

### Saving the battery

The phone is now your radio. The Army's survival manual gives this advice
for survival radios, and it fits a phone just as well (our reading):
"Conserve battery power. Turn the radio off when you are not using it."
It also says that cold drains a battery quickly, so keep it inside your
clothing in cold weather. Agree with the dispatcher when you will turn
the phone back on, then keep to it (general practice). The Park
Service's list of ten essentials includes an extra battery for your
phone; carry one.

### A satellite beacon

A personal locator beacon (PLB) calls for help through satellites where
there is no phone signal at all. NOAA's satellite rescue programme
(SARSAT), which keeps the US register of these beacons, describes a PLB
as a beacon designed to be carried by one person, for use by people in
remote areas: "They can only be activated manually." Beacons fitted with
GPS send their position with the alert. NOAA says that in the US, beacon
registration is
required by federal regulations and free: "Registration is free and can
result in a more efficient search and rescue (SAR) effort." Register it
when you buy it, and renew the registration every two years as NOAA asks.

### On the water

On the water, the Coast Guard is the agency that coordinates maritime
search and rescue, as the FAA's Aeronautical Information Manual explains
(the Air Force coordinates inland). Its guide to the federal requirements
for recreational boats names VHF channel 16 as the primary marine radio
calling and distress channel. Call 911 too if you have a phone.
[Floating and Boats](floating_and_boats.md) covers the rest.

## Helping them find you

Searchers can only find what they can see or hear. Rocky Mountain
National Park says searchers often spread out and yell or blow whistles,
and assume the person they are looking for is alert, wants to be found
and will answer. Make that assumption true.

- **Whistle.** The Park Service's plan: "Blow your whistle three times,
  take a break, and blow three times again." Its reason: "Blowing a
  whistle helps people find you and saves your energy from yelling." The
  Army's survival manual says whistles have been heard up to 1.6
  kilometres away in some documented cases, and a manufactured whistle
  carries farther than a human one.
- **Light, at night.** The Park Service: "Flash the SOS pattern: three
  times quickly, three times slowly, three times quickly." Use the light
  sparingly; it will drain the batteries.
- **A mirror, by day.** The Park Service lists flashing a mirror to alert
  planes and helicopters, and notes that mirrors do not run out of
  battery. [Light, Lenses and Mirrors](light_lenses_and_mirrors.md)
  explains aiming one, and the survival manual's warning not to hold the
  flash on an aircraft's cockpit for more than a few seconds.
- **Colour and shape.** Wear or lay out something bright. The Park
  Service suggests laying out your emergency shelter, or making a shape
  with rocks, that can be seen from aircraft.
- **Symbols for aircraft.** The FAA's Aeronautical Information Manual
  prints a ground-to-air code for survivors: V means require assistance,
  X means require medical assistance, N means no, Y means yes, and an
  arrow means proceeding in this direction. If in doubt, it says, use
  SOS. Its instructions: lay the symbols out with strips of fabric,
  pieces of wood, stones or anything available, with as much colour
  contrast as possible against the ground, at least 10 feet high. To
  signal to a pilot with your body, the manual says: "Stand in the open
  when you make the signals."
- **Three of anything.** The survival manual calls three fires in a
  triangle the international distress signal, and three columns of smoke
  the same by day. But the Park Service's plan says: "Do not build a fire
  if there is a high fire risk at the park or if it is wildland fire
  season. It could cause a wildfire and put you and others at great
  risk." A signal fire that sets the forest alight is not a rescue.
  Check for burn bans and fire restrictions before any trip
  ([Firewood](firewood.md) explains the burn bans where the simulation is
  set), and do not light one in dry weather (general practice).
- **Answer.** When you hear searchers calling or whistling, whistle back
  and call out, and keep doing it until they reach you (general
  practice).

Choose where you wait with signalling in mind. The survival manual lists
being suitable for signalling among the things a shelter site should
offer. Its other rules for soldiers, to blend in and keep a low
silhouette, are for hiding from an enemy, and a lost person wants the
opposite (our reading).

## Staying well while you wait

You may be out overnight. The Park Service's list for waiting: find
shelter from sun, rain or snow; check what you carry and make it last;
put on extra layers as soon as you begin to feel cold.

**Cold and wet together are the danger.** The CDC says hypothermia often
happens in very cold weather but can happen at cool temperatures, above
40 F, if a person gets wet from rain, sweat or cold water and becomes
chilled. Being lost in a wet autumn is enough. [Cold and
Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md) has the signs and what to do about
them.

**A small shelter, off the cold ground.** The survival manual: "The most
common error in making a shelter is to make it too large." A shelter has
to be small enough to hold your body's heat. Get something between you
and the ground: it advises a layer of insulating material such as leaves
or pine needles inside a shelter to cut heat lost to the ground. Sit on
your pack if you have nothing else (general practice).

**Where to wait.** The survival manual's site rules that apply to anyone:

- stay off low ground such as ravines, narrow valleys and creek beds,
  which collect cold air at night;
- look up for dead branches and dead trees that could fall;
- keep out of flash flood areas, and on a shore, above the high water
  mark ([Reading the Tide](reading_the_tide.md) explains why that line
  moves);
- keep away from avalanche and rockslide slopes.

**Water, food and rest.** Drink and eat a little at a time from what you
carry (general practice; the Park Service's plan says to inventory and
conserve). Do not start eating wild plants or mushrooms to stretch your
food. The Army's survival manual: "Although you can live several weeks
without food, you need an adequate amount to stay healthy." A day or two
of hunger is safer than one wrong plant ([Identifying Wild
Plants](identifying_wild_plants.md); our reading). Stay where you are through the night: the Forest Service's
rule is to stay in place at nightfall. Keep a positive frame of mind;
the Forest Service calls it your most important tool once you are lost.

**Do not cross fast water to get out.** The National Weather Service:
"A mere 6 inches of fast-moving flood water can knock over an adult."

## If you are with others

- **Stay together.** One lost person is easier to find than two lost
  people in two places (general practice).
- **If someone is hurt,** the Park Service's plan: "Send two or more
  people to alert authorities while the rest of the group stays with the
  injured person." If there are only two of you, it says to help
  stabilise the injured person first, then go for help. Leave them warm,
  with water, light and a whistle, and mark the place well enough to lead
  rescuers back to it (general practice).

## If someone else is missing

- **Call early.** The Rocky Mountain National Park page: "Quick and
  accurate reporting of a lost person is extremely helpful because it
  keeps the search area small." The FAA's manual quotes an Air Force
  review of rescue missions that found that, for people with no filed
  plan, "36 hours normally pass before family concern initiates an
  (alert)." Do not wait for morning to see if they turn up.
- **Give the searchers everything:** the trip plan if there is one, when
  and where they were last seen, what they were wearing and carrying,
  their health, experience and phone number.
- **Do not go out to search alone,** in the dark, or into the ground that
  defeated them. Two missing people are worse than one (general
  practice).
- **Let the search be run.** In Washington, the law makes the chief law
  enforcement officer of each local government responsible for search
  and rescue, and forbids unauthorised people from interfering with a
  search (RCW 38.52.400, restated). If you want to help, ask the people
  running the search what they need (our reading).

## Children

A lost child does not think like a lost adult. Rocky Mountain National
Park describes searchers who assume the lost person wants to be found
and will answer, and says: "This is not always the case with children
who fear they may be in trouble for getting lost, and who hear the
searchers yelling."

So teach children, before any trip (general practice, built on the Park
Service's advice):

- to stay in one place if they are lost, and the park's reason: so they
  can be found;
- that the people calling their name are there to help, and that nobody
  will be angry;
- to blow their whistle, three blasts and a pause. The Park Service's
  trip planning guide says: "Pack a whistle, especially if you are
  traveling with kids."
- to wear something bright.

The Park Service's emergency plan points families with children to its
Junior Ranger Park Explorer, where children can learn about Hug-A-Tree,
a children's programme whose name gives away its main rule: pick one
tree, and stay with it (our reading of the name; this guide has not read
the programme itself).

## Before you go: the plan that gets you found

Almost everything in this guide works better if somebody knows where you
went. The National Park Service's trip planning guide says to tell a
trusted contact, someone who is not going on the trip with you, where
you are going, what you are doing and when you will be back, and: "Ask
them to call authorities and share your Trip Plan if you are not back
from your trip on time."

- **Write it down.** The Forest Service's list of details: where you are
  going, the trail you plan to follow, when you will return, the vehicle
  you are driving and where you will park it, and how many people are
  going. Its last word on the list: "do not go alone."
- **Keep to it.** The Park Service: "If you change your itinerary or
  route from the one noted on your Trip Plan, your Trusted Contact will
  not know where you are." If you must change, tell them.
- **Check in when you get back,** so that nobody starts a search for
  someone already home. The Park Service's trip planning guide reminds
  you to.
- **The trusted contact's job,** in the Park Service's form: if you are
  not back by the time on the plan, try to reach you, and if they
  cannot, contact the authorities and give them the plan.

Why it matters, from the FAA's manual, about pilots who fly without
telling anyone: "Search efforts are often wasted and rescue is often
delayed because of pilots who thoughtlessly takeoff without telling
anyone where they are going." Walkers are no different.

**Pack for a night out.** The Park Service's ten essentials: navigation
(map, compass and GPS), sun protection, extra clothing, a light, first
aid supplies, a way to make fire, a repair kit and tools, extra food,
extra water and emergency shelter. Its emergency plan adds a whistle, a
mirror and bright clothing.

**Turn round early.** The trip planning guide: "If a safety concern
impacts your activity, it may be better to turn around." Know when the
sun sets and start back with daylight to spare (general practice).

## Worked example: an afternoon walk near Silverdale

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on.

It is a November afternoon in the second-growth forest west of
Silverdale. It is raining lightly and about 6 C. At 3:30 you reach a
junction you do not recognise, and you realise you have not been sure
of the trail for twenty minutes. There is about an hour of daylight
left.

1. **Stop.** You sit down on your pack, put on your rain shell and the
   fleece from the bottom of the pack, and eat a bar. Wet, chilly and
   6 C is exactly the combination the CDC warns about.
2. **Think.** The last place you were sure of was a footbridge about
   twenty minutes back. You are not very, very sure of the way to it, and
   dark is coming. You stay.
3. **Call.** You have one bar. You call 911 and tell the dispatcher the
   trailhead, the footbridge, the junction, the coordinates on your
   phone, what you are wearing and what you are carrying, and that you
   are not hurt. You agree to keep the phone off and check it every half
   hour.
4. **Who comes.** Silverdale is a census-designated place, not an
   incorporated city (US Census Bureau), so the local government is
   Kitsap County. Washington law makes the chief law enforcement officer
   of each local government responsible for search and rescue (RCW
   38.52.400), and makes the sheriff the county's chief executive
   officer and conservator of the peace (RCW 36.28.010). So a search out
   here is the Kitsap County Sheriff's to run (our reading of the three
   together). Had you only been able to text, Washington's Military
   Department says every 911 centre in the state can now receive texts;
   it also warns that a text does not necessarily tell the dispatcher
   where you are, so you would type the location yourself.
5. **Wait well.** You move ten paces off the low, wet ground by the
   junction to a flatter spot under a big cedar, clear of dead branches
   overhead, still in sight of the junction, pile fallen fir boughs to
   sit on, and pull the space blanket over your knees. Every few minutes
   you blow three blasts on the whistle.
6. **Be found.** Your trusted contact had your trip plan and the time
   you expected to be back, and the dispatcher already has your
   coordinates. When you hear voices, you whistle and shout back.

On the water around Silverdale the order is the same, with the Coast
Guard, which coordinates search and rescue on the water, and VHF channel
16 beside 911 (our reading of the FAA manual and the Coast Guard's
guide).

## Know where your own effort stops

- **Once you have called, the search is not yours to run.** Do what the
  dispatcher asks. In Washington the chief law enforcement officer runs
  search and rescue and can close the search area to anyone not
  authorised (RCW 38.52.400, restated).
- **Do not rescue yourself across danger:** fast water, cliffs,
  snowfields, tide flats on a rising tide ([Reading the
  Tide](reading_the_tide.md)). Waiting is slower and safer (general
  practice).
- **Injuries** need first aid now and a professional soon: [Bleeding and
  Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md) and [Cold and
  Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md).

## Traps

- **"I'll just try one more direction."** That is how a small search
  becomes a large one.
- **"It's only a short walk; I don't need a plan."** Short walks are
  where people are least prepared.
- **"Follow water downhill and you'll come out."** The Forest Service's
  very last resort, and possibly very dangerous.
- **"My phone will save me."** The Forest Service expects it to have no
  signal. Carry the map, compass, whistle and light anyway.
- **Yelling until you are hoarse.** A whistle carries farther and costs
  less.
- **Waiting until morning to report someone missing.** Every hour grows
  the search area.
- **A child who hides.** Tell children, before the trip, that searchers
  are friends and nobody will be angry.

## How the game models it

The game cannot teach the most important thing in this guide, and it is
worth saying why.

- **Nothing is lost when you die.** If your health runs out, the game
  shows the cause and a Respawn button with the words "You wake in the
  respawner. Nothing was lost, but the body remembers: keep fed,
  hydrated, warm, and breathing." Real life has no respawner, which is
  the whole reason this guide exists.
- **Home is always marked.** The Maps page has a Track switch for the
  Home Station in orbit, on by default; while it is on and you are more
  than a kilometre from the station, a ring and a label with its distance
  mark it on screen whenever it is in view. You can always find which way
  home is. No real walk has that.
- **The default play mode can teleport you.** The game starts in the Dev
  play mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), which includes the Dev
  page and its travel buttons, among them one to return to where you were
  before you first teleported.
- **Waiting does cost something.** Hunger, thirst and energy fall over
  time (Settings > Gameplay > Vitals drain, where 1.0 means real time:
  thirst in about two days and hunger in about a week), and low energy
  makes you fatigued and slower. Your body heat is modelled from the
  weather, your clothes and any shelter you have built: wind, rain and
  wet clothes pull heat out, a roof you built keeps the rain off, and
  under a roof, walls on the windward side keep the wind off. Natural
  cover such as a tree or a hollow does not count. Under the default
  Body heat setting, Forgiving, your temperature swings half as far and
  cold harms you half as fast as under Realistic. Hypothermia halves your
  movement.
- **The rescue gear does nothing yet.** A Flare, a Radio, a Walkie Talkie,
  a Flashlight, a Compass, a Paper Map and a GPS Device are all in the
  item list, and some of them are parts in other recipes, but none of
  them does anything when you use it: nothing signals, nobody searches,
  and there is no 911.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay > "Start
  every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
  during development, only your character carries between launches.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game:
searchers, phones, whistles, darkness that stops you moving, and the
plain fact that a person cannot be put back where they started.

## You own this when

- You can say the four words of STOP and what each one means.
- You would stay put when lost, and you can name the reasons to move
  (an immediate threat to life, or a route you are very, very sure of).
- You know that any mobile phone can try 911, what to tell the call
  taker, and not to hang up until told.
- You carry a whistle and a light, and know three blasts and a pause, and
  the SOS flash.
- You know the ground-to-air V and X.
- You would not light a signal fire in fire season.
- You can make a small shelter off the ground, out of the wind and away
  from low ground, dead branches and flood lines.
- You leave a trip plan with a trusted contact every time, keep to it,
  and check in when you get back.
- You would call 911 quickly if someone else were missing, and not
  search alone.
- Your children know to stay in one place, blow their whistle and answer
  the people calling them.
