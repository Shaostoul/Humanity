# Fermenting Vegetables

Sauerkraut and old-fashioned dill pickles are not made with vinegar. They are
made with salt, time and the bacteria that already live on cabbage and
cucumbers. Given the right amount of salt, no air and a steady room
temperature, those bacteria turn the vegetables' own sugars into acid, and the
acid is what preserves the food and gives it its sour taste. It is one of the
oldest ways of keeping vegetables, it needs no canner and no power while it
works, and done by a tested recipe it is safe.

It is also a process that can fail, and a failed ferment is not merely a
disappointing one. This guide is about doing it so it is both safe and good:

- what lactic acid fermentation is, in plain words;
- salt by weight, and why the proportion is a safety rule, not a matter of
  taste;
- sauerkraut and fermented dill pickles, step by step, from the tested USDA
  recipes;
- keeping the vegetables under the brine;
- temperature and time;
- telling a good ferment from a spoiled one, what surface yeast is, and what
  to throw out;
- why tested recipes matter, and why a finished ferment is not a canned food;
- storing what you made.

It covers vegetables only. Fermented fish, meat and marine-mammal foods are a
different practice with a documented botulism record: the CDC reports that in
Alaska most cases of foodborne botulism have come from fermented fish and
other aquatic animals, and [Why Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md)
lists the outbreaks.

Temperatures are in degrees Fahrenheit with Celsius in brackets, and amounts
are in US measures with metric alongside, because the tested recipes are
written that way.

## Two rules before you start

**Use a tested recipe, and keep its salt exactly.** The USDA's Complete Guide
to Home Canning is blunt about the salt: "In fermented foods, salt favors the
growth of desirable bacteria while inhibiting the growth of others. Caution:
Do not attempt to make sauerkraut or fermented pickles by cutting back on the
salt required." Penn State Extension adds the other half: the only
research-based recipes for fermented vegetables are the ones for sauerkraut
and fermented pickles. Those are the two this guide teaches.

**Keep the vegetables under the brine the whole time.** The USDA guide:
"Cabbage and cucumbers must be kept 1 to 2 inches under brine while
fermenting." That is 2.5 to 5 cm. Air at the surface is where yeasts and
moulds grow; the vegetables below it, cut off from air, are where the right
bacteria do their work.

## What fermentation does, in plain words

The USDA guide's glossary defines fermentation as changes in food caused by
the deliberate growth of bacteria, yeast or mould, and describes the kind this
guide is about: "Native bacteria ferment natural sugars to lactic acid, a
major flavoring and preservative in sauerkraut and in naturally fermented
dills."

Step by step, as the sources describe it:

1. **The bacteria are already there.** Colorado State University Extension
   (CSU) notes that the bacteria needed to ferment cabbage live on the cabbage
   itself, so no starter culture is needed.
2. **Salt makes the brine and picks the winners.** Salt pulls juice out of the
   shredded cabbage; the USDA recipe has you pack the cabbage "until salt
   draws juices from cabbage," and CSU explains that salt makes the cabbage
   cells release the sugars the bacteria feed on. It also holds back the
   yeasts, moulds and bacteria you do not want, while the acid-making
   bacteria put up with it (CSU, and the USDA quotation above).
3. **Without air, the bacteria make acid.** Penn State describes fermentation
   as useful bacteria breaking down the food without air to produce acids.
   CSU names them: lactic acid, which makes the sour taste, and carbon
   dioxide, which makes the bubbles you see in the first days.
4. **The acid preserves the food.** As the ferment cures, the USDA guide says,
   its colour and flavour change and its acidity rises. The same guide lists
   sauerkraut, and fermented and pickled vegetables, among the acid foods,
   those with a pH of 4.6 or lower. (pH is the measure of acidity: the lower
   the number, the more acid. 4.6 is where the USDA draws the line between
   acid foods, which hold back the botulism organism, and low-acid foods,
   which do not; [Why Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md) explains
   it.)
5. **It stops by itself.** Penn State explains that fermentation ends
   naturally once the acid has built up so far that the bacteria cannot grow
   any more. That is when the bubbling stops.

The safety of a ferment is therefore something the process makes, not
something you add. Why Canning Has Rules puts the consequence plainly: a
ferment that stalls, or where the wrong organisms won, is not a slightly worse
ferment but a jar of low-acid vegetables in brine. That is why the salt and
the temperature in the tested recipes are not suggestions.

**Fermented and quick pickles are different things.** The USDA guide separates
them: fermented dill pickles and sauerkraut cure for about three weeks, while
fresh-pack or quick-process pickles are not fermented at all, but covered with
vinegar, which does the acid's job from the start. Quick pickles can be made
safely with less salt or none, because the vinegar is doing the safety work;
fermented ones cannot. Know which of the two you are making.

## Salt: by weight, and why the proportion matters

**The tested amount.** The USDA sauerkraut recipe has you mix 3 tablespoons of
salt into every 5 pounds (2.3 kg) of shredded cabbage as you go (its
ingredient list gives 3/4 cup of salt for 25 pounds of cabbage). Penn State
explains why that is not a matter of taste: the exact ratio of 3 tablespoons
of canning or pickling salt to 5 pounds of shredded cabbage is what keeps
disease-causing organisms from growing, and changing the proportions could
make the sauerkraut unsafe. For small batches in jars, Penn State uses 3 1/2
tablespoons per 5 pounds.

**Weighing is better than measuring.** CSU recommends weighing the shredded
cabbage and adding 2 to 2.5 percent of its weight in salt, which it puts at
about 3 tablespoons per 5 pounds. In metric that is 20 to 25 grams of salt for
every kilogram of shredded cabbage, or about 45 to 57 grams for 5 pounds. A
kitchen scale is the most useful tool in fermenting, because a spoonful of one
salt does not weigh what a spoonful of another does. The USDA guide rules out
flake salt because it "varies in density," and University of Illinois
Extension warns that natural salts such as sea salt weigh differently too,
which changes the fermentation.

**What goes wrong when the salt is off.**

- **Too little:** the ferment goes soft, or slimy. Oregon State University
  Extension traces soft sauerkraut to salt below 1.7 percent or salt spread
  unevenly, and slimy sauerkraut, which means spoilage, to too little salt or
  too much heat. For pickles, NCHFP (the National Center for Home Food
  Preservation) lists a brine that was too weak as the first cause of soft or
  slippery pickles.
- **Too much:** University of Minnesota Extension warns that with too much
  salt the fermentation does not start and the acidity never rises. NCHFP
  lists brine that is too strong as a cause of shrivelled pickles, and Oregon
  State lists excess or uneven salt as one cause of pink sauerkraut.
- **Unevenly mixed:** the same problems in patches. Mix the salt through
  thoroughly before packing (Oregon State, Penn State).

**Which salt.** Canning or pickling salt, which the USDA guide describes as
plain salt without the anti-caking or iodine additives. The same guide says
iodized or plain table salt can be used safely but that the anti-caking agent
may cloud the brine; Illinois adds that iodized salt may slow the ferment and
brown the vegetables, and NCHFP lists iodized salt as a cause of dark pickles.
Do not use reduced-sodium salt: the USDA guide says plainly that it is "not
recommended" in fermented pickle recipes, and NCHFP notes that potassium
chloride, the usual salt substitute, makes pickles bitter.

**Brine for pickles and for topping up.** Whole cucumbers do not make their
own brine, so the pickle recipe below uses a made brine: for each gallon of
container, 1/2 cup of salt dissolved in 8 cups of water with 1/4 cup of 5
percent vinegar. If shredded cabbage does not make enough juice to cover
itself, the USDA recipe tops it up with a brine of 1 1/2 tablespoons of salt
per quart (about a litre) of water, boiled and then cooled. Never top up with
plain water.

## What you need

- **A container.** The USDA guide allows 1 gallon (3.8 litres) of container
  for each 5 pounds of vegetables, so a 5-gallon crock holds about 25 pounds
  of cabbage or cucumbers. Food-grade plastic and glass work as well as
  stoneware. A 1- to 3-gallon plastic container that is not food grade can be
  used only if it is lined with a clean food-grade plastic bag, and never a
  garbage bag or bin liner. Mason jars work for sauerkraut, the USDA guide
  says, but may lose more batches to spoilage. Penn State rules out copper, iron and galvanised metal
  containers and lead-glazed crocks, and CSU any container that is cracked or
  chipped.
- **A plate and weights.** A dinner plate or glass pie plate just smaller than
  the opening, weighed down with two or three sealed quart jars of water; or,
  instead, a large sealed food-grade plastic bag holding 3 quarts of water
  with 4 1/2 tablespoons of salt dissolved in it (USDA guide; CSU uses the
  same idea). The bag holds brine rather than plain water because, as
  University of Minnesota Extension points out, a leaking bag then only adds
  more brine to the vegetables.
- **A cloth cover.** A clean, heavy towel over the top helps keep insects and
  mould spores out (USDA guide).
- **Clean equipment.** Wash the container, plate and jars in hot, soapy water
  and rinse them with very hot water (USDA guide). Use utensils of glass,
  stainless steel, food-grade enamel without chips, or stoneware; NCHFP warns
  that brass, iron, copper or zinc darken pickles.
- **A thermometer** in the room where the crock will sit, because temperature
  decides how the ferment turns out.
- **A kitchen scale**, for the salt.

## Sauerkraut, step by step

The tested USDA recipe, with detail from Penn State. For 25 pounds (11.3 kg)
of cabbage it makes about 9 quarts.

1. **Start with good cabbage, soon after picking.** Firm, fresh heads, shredded
   and started between 24 and 48 hours after harvest (USDA guide). Penn State
   recommends disease-free, firm, sweet, mature heads from mid and late season
   crops.
2. **Work 5 pounds at a time.** Remove the outer leaves, rinse the heads
   under cold running water and drain them, cut them in quarters and cut out
   the cores. Shred or slice the cabbage about as thick as a quarter, the coin
   (USDA guide); Penn State puts that at 1/16 inch, 1.5 mm.
3. **Salt and pack.** Put the 5 pounds of shredded cabbage in the container,
   add 3 tablespoons of salt, and mix it through thoroughly with clean hands.
   Pack it down firmly until the salt has drawn out enough juice to cover the
   surface.
4. **Repeat** with the next 5 pounds and the next 3 tablespoons, until all the
   cabbage is in. Leave the rim of the container at least 4 or 5 inches (10 to
   13 cm) above the cabbage.
5. **Cover it with brine.** If the juice does not cover the cabbage, add the
   boiled and cooled top-up brine.
6. **Weigh it down and cover it.** Plate and weights, or the brine bag, then a
   clean towel over the top.
7. **Ferment it at 70 to 75 F (21 to 24 C).** See the next section for other
   temperatures.
8. **Tend it.** With a brine bag as the weight, leave the crock alone until
   the fermentation is finished, which is when the bubbling stops. With jars as
   weights, check it two or three times a week and skim off any scum that
   forms (USDA guide).
9. **Judge it.** Finished sauerkraut, in Penn State's description, is as tart
   as you like it, firm, with brine that is not cloudy and no sign of mould
   or yeast.

**In jars.** Penn State gives a small-batch method: five pounds of cabbage
fills about two quart jars or one half-gallon jar. Mix in 3 1/2 tablespoons
of canning salt, let the cabbage wilt for 10 minutes, then squeeze and pound
it until it is soft and juice has collected. Pack it into sterilised jars,
pressing out air pockets, leaving about 2 inches (5 cm) of space at the top,
and add top-up brine if the juice does not cover it. Tuck a square of
cheesecloth over the cabbage, weigh it down with a small jelly jar filled with
brine or a sealed bag of brine, and cover the jar loosely so the gas can
escape. Stand it on a tray to catch overflow, check it two or three times a
week, and skim off any scum or mould promptly. At room temperature it takes
about three weeks.

**A note on shorter recipes.** CSU publishes a shorter schedule, about 7 to
14 days at 68 to 72 F (20 to 22 C), tasting from the seventh day, with the
jars then kept in the refrigerator. Our reading: that is a younger sauerkraut
than the one the USDA calls "fully fermented" after three to four weeks, and
it belongs in the refrigerator. If you want sauerkraut to can, or to keep for
months, use the USDA timing.

## Fermented dill pickles, step by step

The tested USDA recipe. The amounts are for each gallon of container, so a
5-gallon crock takes five times as much of everything.

- 4 pounds (1.8 kg) of 4-inch (10 cm) pickling cucumbers
- 2 tablespoons of dill seed, or 4 to 5 heads of fresh or dry dill weed
- 1/2 cup of salt
- 1/4 cup of vinegar (5 percent acidity)
- 8 cups of water
- and, if you like, one or more of: 2 cloves of garlic, 2 dried red peppers,
  2 teaspoons of whole mixed pickling spices

1. **Pick the right cucumbers, and brine them the same day.** Use a pickling
   variety; Penn State says slicing cucumbers are not suitable. They should be
   firm and about 4 inches long (USDA guide). NCHFP advises starting the
   ferment within 24 hours of harvest, because a long wait gives hollow or
   shrivelled pickles.
2. **Trim the blossom end.** Wash the cucumbers and cut a 1/16-inch (1.5 mm)
   slice off the blossom end and throw it away, leaving 1/4 inch (6 mm) of
   stem on. The USDA guide explains why: the blossom may contain an enzyme
   that makes pickles go too soft.
3. **Layer.** Put half the dill and spices in the bottom of the container,
   then the cucumbers, then the rest of the dill and spices.
4. **Pour on the brine.** Dissolve the salt in the vinegar and water and pour
   it over the cucumbers.
5. **Cover and weigh down** as for sauerkraut.
6. **Ferment at 70 to 75 F (21 to 24 C)** for about 3 to 4 weeks.
7. **Check it several times a week** and promptly remove any scum or mould
   from the surface. The USDA guide's warning: "Caution: If the pickles become
   soft, slimy, or develop a disagreeable odor, discard them."

## Temperature and time

| Ferment | Best | Also workable | Avoid | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sauerkraut | 70 to 75 F (21 to 24 C): fully fermented in about 3 to 4 weeks | 60 to 65 F (16 to 18 C): 5 to 6 weeks | Below 60 F (16 C) it may not ferment. Above 75 F (24 C) it may go soft | USDA guide |
| Fermented dill pickles | 70 to 75 F (21 to 24 C): about 3 to 4 weeks | 55 to 65 F (13 to 18 C): 5 to 6 weeks | Above 80 F (27 C) the pickles go too soft | USDA guide |

Penn State's figures for sauerkraut differ slightly: about six weeks at 60 to
65 F, and above 80 F sauerkraut may go soft and spoil. Where the sources
differ, the USDA range is the tested one and the safer to aim for. CSU also
asks for a relatively constant temperature, not one that swings.

## Keeping the vegetables under the brine

This is the part of fermenting most first attempts get wrong, and most of the
problems in the next section come from it.

Penn State's rule: to avoid mould growing on the surface, keep the cabbage or
cucumbers under the brine at all times. Oregon State traces rotten sauerkraut
at the surface to cabbage that was not covered enough to keep air out, and a
white scum on top to a weight that was not heavy enough.

How to keep them down:

- **Pack shredded cabbage tightly**, pressing out air pockets as you go (Penn
  State, CSU); Oregon State lists air pockets as a cause of soft sauerkraut.
- **Leave the brine deep enough.** The USDA figure is 1 to 2 inches (2.5 to 5
  cm) over the vegetables; CSU asks for at least an inch.
- **Weight it properly**, with a plate and water jars or a brine bag, as
  above. With a brine bag, the USDA guide lets you leave the crock alone until
  it has finished; with jars as the weight, it has you check two or three
  times a week.
- **Top it up** with the boiled and cooled brine if the level drops.
- **Push it back down.** CSU's advice once the first bubbling has settled is
  to check that the cabbage is still covered and press it down with clean hands
  to draw out more brine; if there is still not enough to cover it, stop
  fermenting at room temperature and move it to the refrigerator.

## Telling a good ferment from a spoiled one

### What is normal

- **Bubbles**, in the first days: carbon dioxide from the bacteria (CSU).
- **A sour smell.** CSU suggests a well-ventilated spot for the crock for
  this reason.
- **A white sediment** in the jar, which NCHFP describes as caused by the
  bacteria during fermentation, needing no action. (Its other cause is salt
  with an anti-caking agent; canning salt avoids it.)
- **Finished sauerkraut** that is tart, firm, with clear brine and no mould or
  yeast growth (Penn State).

### Surface yeast, and why you skim it

A white film on the surface of the brine is usually yeast growing on the top,
where it meets the air. University of Illinois Extension describes it: a white
film that is not fuzzy and not in round patches is kahm yeast, very common on
fermenting vegetables, and safe. CSU says the same of a white or pink yeast
film on sauerkraut: remove it and throw it away, and the sauerkraut below is
still fine to eat.

Safe does not mean harmless to the ferment. NCHFP explains that scum on a
fermenting brine is wild yeasts and bacteria that feed on the acid, lowering
the acidity if they are allowed to build up. So skim it off whenever it forms,
which is why the USDA recipes have you check the crock several times a week,
and take it as a sign that air is reaching the surface: more weight, more brine,
or a better cover.

### Mould

Illinois's description of surface yeast implies the difference: mould is
fuzzy, or grows in round patches. The sources treat it by how much there is:

- The USDA pickle recipe says to remove surface scum or mould promptly.
- Oregon State allows small patches of mould on sauerkraut to be skimmed off,
  but says to throw the whole batch away if there is a large amount.
- Penn State adds a rule for your own safety: do not taste a ferment that has
  mould on the surface, a slimy texture or a bad smell.

### Throw it out

| What you see | What to do | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Pickles that have gone soft, slimy, or smell disagreeable | Discard them | USDA guide |
| Slimy sauerkraut | Discard it; slime means spoilage | Oregon State |
| Rotten sauerkraut at the surface, where air reached it | Discard the rotten part; the cabbage below it is safe | Oregon State |
| A large amount of mould | Discard the batch | Oregon State |
| Dark pickles made with brass, copper or zinc utensils or equipment | Do not use them | NCHFP |
| A ferment you cannot vouch for, because you do not know how it was made | Do not eat it | CDC |
| Anything you are unsure of | Throw it out | Illinois |

The CDC's wording on the last-but-one point: "Do not eat food if you do not
know whether it was safely canned, preserved, or fermented." The same CDC page
names "homemade foods that have been improperly canned, preserved, or
fermented" among the common sources of botulism.

### Ugly but safe

Two faults spoil the quality without making a ferment unsafe, in Oregon
State's table:

- **Soft sauerkraut**, from too little salt, uneven salt, too much heat or air
  pockets, is poor quality but safe if it has a clean, acid smell and taste.
  Do not taste it if it is also slimy or smells bad (Penn State).
- **Pink sauerkraut** is safe unless it is slimy. It comes from yeast growing
  where the salt was too much, uneven or badly covered, or from natural
  pigment in the cabbage brought out by the acid.

None of these signs proves a ferment is safe. What makes it safe is the
process: a tested recipe, the right salt, the right temperature, the
vegetables under the brine, and a ferment that ran to the end.
[Telling Spoiled From Safe](telling_spoiled_from_safe.md) explains why your
senses can catch spoilage but cannot confirm safety.

## Why tested recipes matter, and why a ferment is not a canned food

**Only two recipes are tested.** Penn State's caution is worth repeating: the
only research-based procedures for fermented vegetables are for sauerkraut and
fermented pickles. Kimchi, fermented carrots, hot sauces and the rest have no
research-based procedure behind them in that account. Every added
ingredient changes the mixture, and the USDA guide's rule for pickled foods
applies to the principle: "There must be a minimum, uniform level of acid
throughout the mixed product to prevent the growth of botulinum bacteria."

**Outside a tested recipe, measure.** Utah State University Extension says a
ferment is safe only if its acidity drops to a pH of 4.6 or lower, and that
you cannot be sure it has without checking with a pH meter or test strips
that read to at least one decimal place; food that looks fine can still carry
harmful organisms. University of Minnesota Extension likewise treats a ferment
as ready only when the pH has reached 4.6 or lower and the bubbling has
stopped. A meter tells you the acid is there; it does not turn an untested
recipe into a tested one.

**Fermenting is not always acid.** University of Minnesota Extension points
out that some fermentations, such as fish sauce and natto (fermented
soybeans), break down protein instead and make ammonia, leaving a product
that is low in acid. The word "fermented" says nothing on its own about
whether a food is acid.

**A finished ferment is not shelf-stable.** Utah State: in most cases,
fermentation alone cannot make a food that keeps at room temperature, and
nearly all fermented foods need either canning or refrigeration. The USDA
guide gives the same choice for both of its recipes (see "Storing" below). A
jar of sauerkraut left in a warm cupboard is not a canned food, and it is not
safe to treat it as one. And while a ferment is still working, do not seal it
airtight: Penn State covers fermenting jars loosely so that the gas can
escape.

## Storing what you made

| What | How long | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Fully fermented sauerkraut, tightly covered in the refrigerator | Several months | USDA guide |
| Small-batch sauerkraut | Several months in the refrigerator, or frozen, or water-bath canned | Penn State |
| Fully fermented pickles in their own container, refrigerated, with scum and mould removed regularly | About 4 to 6 months | USDA guide |
| Canned sauerkraut or pickles | Best within a year; safe as long as the lids stay sealed | Penn State |

For pickles, the USDA guide adds: "Canning fully fermented pickles is a better
way to store them."

CSU also allows sauerkraut to stay in its crock in a cool store, as long as
the surface is kept from the air, kept covered and weighed down after each
portion is taken out; a little spoilage at the surface may happen and can be
skimmed off.

### Canning them

Sauerkraut and fermented pickles are acid foods, so they are canned in a
boiling-water canner (USDA guide). Read [Keeping What You Grew](keeping_what_you_grew.md)
for how to run one, then use these times from the USDA guide. Altitude is not
optional: water boils cooler higher up, so the times go up with it.

**Sauerkraut.** Hot pack: bring the kraut and its liquid slowly to a boil,
stirring often, then fill the jars firmly with kraut and juice, leaving 1/2
inch (1.3 cm) of headspace. Raw pack: fill the jars firmly with unheated kraut
and cover it with juice, leaving the same headspace.

| Pack | Jar | 0 to 1,000 ft | 1,001 to 3,000 ft | 3,001 to 6,000 ft | Above 6,000 ft |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Hot | Pints | 10 min | 15 min | 15 min | 20 min |
| Hot | Quarts | 15 min | 20 min | 20 min | 25 min |
| Raw | Pints | 20 min | 25 min | 30 min | 35 min |
| Raw | Quarts | 25 min | 30 min | 35 min | 40 min |

**Fermented dill pickles.** Pour off the brine, heat it slowly to a boil and
simmer it for 5 minutes (filter it through paper coffee filters if you want it
less cloudy). Fill the jars with pickles and hot brine, leaving 1/2 inch of
headspace, then process:

| Pack | Jar | 0 to 1,000 ft | 1,001 to 6,000 ft | Above 6,000 ft |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Raw | Pints | 10 min | 15 min | 20 min |
| Raw | Quarts | 15 min | 20 min | 25 min |

Or, for firmer pickles, the USDA guide's low-temperature pasteurisation: set
the jars in a canner half full of warm water, 120 to 140 F (49 to 60 C), add
hot water to an inch above the jars, and heat the water to 180 to 185 F (82 to
85 C) and hold it there for 30 minutes. Check it with a candy or jelly
thermometer the whole time: it must stay at 180 F or above for the full 30
minutes, and above 185 F the pickles soften. The guide warns that this method
must be managed carefully to avoid spoilage, and is to be used only where a
recipe calls for it.

## How the game models it

The game does not model fermenting yet.

- **The crops are there.** Cabbage and cucumbers grow in the garden and are
  food items (`data/items.csv`), and both spoil on the fresh-vegetable clock
  of about a week at room temperature (`data/food/item_profiles.ron`, the
  `raw_vegetables` row of `data/food_system.ron`).
- **Salt is there.** It is sold by the food vendor and used in several cooking
  recipes (`data/recipes.csv`).
- **Fermenting is only written down.** `data/food_system.ron` defines a
  "Fermented" cooking method (a preservation factor of five, 48 times the
  ordinary cooking time, a small food-safety risk, and the cooking skill at
  level 4), but the file notes that no code acts on its cooking methods yet,
  and no recipe turns cabbage into sauerkraut or cucumbers into pickles.
- **The nearest thing that works** is cheese: the "Make Cheese" recipe turns
  milk and salt into cheese and whey in one step, with no culture, time or
  temperature to manage.

What this means, so you do not learn it from the game: there is no crock,
brine, salt ratio, temperature, fermenting time, surface yeast or pH in the
simulation, and nothing in it can go right or wrong the way a real ferment
does. Everything in this guide is real-world only for now.
