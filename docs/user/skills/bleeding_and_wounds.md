# Bleeding and Wounds

Somebody is bleeding. Before you read another word, answer one question,
because everything else in this guide forks on it.

**Is the blood going somewhere?**

Blood that is spurting, pooling on the floor, soaking through a shirt or
running off a limb is a problem measured in minutes. Blood that is
welling up in a cut and stopping when you press on it is a problem
measured in days, and the thing that will hurt the person is infection,
not blood loss.

Those two problems have almost nothing in common. They want opposite
things from you. The first wants speed, force, and a very short list of
actions done immediately and without second-guessing. The second wants
patience, clean water, and a willingness to leave things alone.

If it is the first one, do this now and read the rest later:

1. **Call for help, or send somebody to call.** Then keep working.
2. **Press on it. Hard.** One spot, your whole weight if that is what it
   takes, and do not lift your hands to look.
3. **If pressure alone does not stop it and it is an arm or a leg, use a
   tourniquet**, high above the wound, tightened until the bleeding
   stops. Note the time.
4. **Keep the person flat and keep them warm** once the bleeding is
   controlled.

Everything after this is the explanation.

## What this guide is, and what it is not

This guide covers two things: stopping bleeding that will kill somebody,
and caring for a wound that will not.

**It does not qualify you to do either.** Bleeding control is a physical
skill, and the gap between having read about direct pressure and actually
leaning on a stranger's femoral artery with both hands while they scream
is not a gap that any amount of reading closes. There is free, in-person
bystander training for exactly this, and a few hours of it is worth more
than this entire document. The US Department of Homeland Security lists
the three things that training teaches as recognising and stopping
life-threatening bleeding, applying direct pressure and tourniquets, and
safely positioning the injured person. If you can take that course, take
it.

What reading can do is three things. It can tell you which of the two
problems you are in, which is the single decision everything else hangs
on. It can stop you doing the specific wrong things that people reach for
by instinct, several of which make bleeding worse and several of which
turn a clean wound into an infected one. And it can tell you where the
edge of home care is, so you hand the problem over before it becomes
somebody's permanent injury.

A note on scope that matters throughout. Much of the best free guidance
on serious bleeding comes from military trauma medicine, because that is
where the problem was studied hardest. Military guidance is written for
trained medics carrying purpose-built equipment, sometimes under fire.
Some of it transfers to a kitchen or a workshop exactly, and some of it
does not transfer at all. Wherever this guide uses a military source, it
says who that source was written for, because a number lifted out of its
setting is how a correct fact becomes a wrong instruction.

# Part one: bleeding that kills

## Recognising it

The instinct is to estimate how much blood there is. Do not. People are
extremely bad at this. Spilled blood spreads, soaks, and looks like far
more than it is, and the amount on the floor tells you nothing about the
amount still leaving. Meanwhile a wound in a thick thigh can put most of
its output into the leg itself and show you almost nothing.

Use what you can actually see instead.

**The wound.** Federal bystander guidance keys on things you can observe
without measuring anything. The Uniformed Services University's public
bleeding-control material describes serious or life-threatening bleeding
as red and continuously flowing, says it may be squirting, and says it
may be pooling on the surface. The DHS Stop the Bleed poster puts the
trigger for escalating plainly: if direct pressure does not stop the
bleeding and the dressing becomes soaked with blood, you need a
tourniquet. A limb that has been partly or completely severed is in this
category automatically and does not need a trial of pressure first.

The one volume benchmark a federal source does offer is deliberately
crude, and its crudeness is the point: the Uniformed Services University
page describes about half a can of soda's worth of blood on the ground.
That is a recognisable object, not a measurement, which is the only form
in which a volume estimate is any use to you.

**The person.** This is the more reliable signal, and it is the one
people ignore because they are staring at the wound. Military trauma
guidance reduces the assessment for haemorrhagic shock to two findings:
altered mental status, in somebody who has not had a head injury, and a
weak or absent pulse at the wrist. That is the whole test. Somebody who
is confused, vague, combative, or drifting, or whose radial pulse you
cannot feel, is in shock from blood loss until proven otherwise, and that
is true even if the wound in front of you looks unimpressive.

The plain-language version of the same thing, from the National Library
of Medicine's first aid material, lists confusion or decreasing
alertness, clammy skin, dizziness after an injury, paleness, a rapid
pulse, shortness of breath and weakness. Note how many of those are about
the person's head and skin rather than the wound.

**Two more things worth knowing.** Someone taking a blood thinner, or
with a bleeding disorder such as haemophilia, can bleed dangerously from
an injury that would be trivial in anybody else, and that is a reason to
escalate early rather than wait and see. And some small wounds bleed
alarmingly without being dangerous: the same first aid guidance gives a
scalp wound as its example of a minor injury that can bleed a lot.
Military wound guidance separately notes that the face and scalp have
very high blood flow, which is consistent with that, though neither
source explains the scalp's bleeding in those terms and this guide will
not invent a mechanism for it.

**The number you will hear, and what it does and does not mean.** The DHS
Stop the Bleed material states that a person who is bleeding can die from
blood loss within five minutes. Treat that as a statement about urgency,
not a countdown you can plan around. It does not mean you have five
minutes; it means the interval in which you can change the outcome is
short enough that there is no version of this where you go and look
something up first.

## Before your hands: about ten seconds, not more

You can catch a bloodborne infection through a break in your own skin,
including a small one. Gloves belong in every first aid kit for this
reason, and washing your hands before and after, when there is time, is
ordinary good practice.

**Say the trade-off honestly, because a guide that only says "wear
gloves" will get somebody killed.** If gloves are within arm's reach, use
them. If finding them means leaving a person who is bleeding arterially,
do not go and find them. Put a barrier between your hands and the blood
if one is already in your hand, and otherwise press with your bare hands
and wash thoroughly afterwards. The risk to you from a few minutes of
blood contact on intact skin is real but small. The risk to them from a
few minutes without pressure is neither.

## Direct pressure, which is most of the answer

Direct pressure stops most external bleeding, including a lot of bleeding
that looks unstoppable. It is not a holding action until the real
treatment arrives. It usually is the treatment.

The word doing the work is **direct**. Not near, not around, not on the
general area. On the bleeding point, through whatever you have.

**What it actually looks like.** Expose the wound first, tearing clothing
away if you have to, because you cannot press on a bleeding point you
cannot find and blood tracking under a sleeve will fool you about where
it is coming from. Then put something over it, gauze or a folded cloth or
a shirt, and push. The federal poster is explicit that what you push with
is whatever works, listing gauze, clean cloth, a hand, an elbow, or a
knee. That list is not there for variety. It is there because on a large
wound in the groin or the shoulder, a hand does not generate enough
force, and the correct answer is to get your body weight over the spot
through your knee or your elbow and stay there.

Press harder than feels polite. It is also worth thinking about where the
force goes: pressure is force divided by the area you spread it over, so
a fingertip or two driven into a small wound delivers far more pressure
than a palm spread across the whole area with the same effort. That is
arithmetic rather than medical guidance, but it is the reason a
determined fingertip often works where a flat hand does not.

**Then do not lift it to check. This is the commonest error and it is
worth understanding why it costs.** Bleeding stops because a clot forms
where the vessel is torn and because your pressure is holding the torn
edges shut. That clot is fragile, and it is stuck to the dressing as much
as to the wound, so lifting the dressing to see how it is going tears the
clot off and puts you back at the beginning. The person is a few more
minutes of blood down for the information. The first aid guidance says it
as flatly as guidance ever says anything: do not peek, because the less a
wound is disturbed, the more likely you are to control the bleeding.

The corollary catches people too. **If blood soaks through the dressing,
do not remove it.** Put another dressing on top of the first and keep
pressing. Peeling off a soaked dressing removes the clot with it.

**How long.** Until it stops, and longer than you think. Ten minutes of
genuinely uninterrupted pressure is the figure the first aid guidance
uses as a decision point: bleeding that is still going after about ten
minutes of pressure is a call-emergency-services problem. Note the trap
in that number. Ten minutes of pressure means ten minutes, not four
checks spread over ten minutes, and a person who has peeked three times
has not done the ten minutes and does not yet know whether pressure was
working.

**Holding pressure is physically hard, and this is a documented failure
mode rather than a character flaw.** A simulation study of 33 laypeople
compressing a bleeding-control simulator found that the primary cause of
failure was the inability to maintain adequate direct pressure for three
minutes. Three minutes. Plan for that: get your body weight over the
wound rather than relying on arm strength, kneel so your skeleton is
doing the work instead of your muscles, and if there is a second person,
swap before you fade rather than after. Handing over cleanly, with the
new person's hands going on before yours come off, is not an
interruption. Letting go because your arms gave out is.

**The one exception: an eye injury.** Direct pressure is the answer for
external bleeding everywhere except an injured eye. Do not press on an
eye. Cover it and get help.

## Packing a wound

Packing means pushing gauze or cloth down into a wound cavity, tightly,
until the cavity is full, and then pressing on top of it. It exists for
wounds where surface pressure cannot reach the bleeding vessel because
the vessel is at the bottom of a deep track.

**Where it applies.** The category that matters is the one military
guidance defines by exclusion: bleeding that is not amenable to a limb
tourniquet, which it directs be handled with a haemostatic dressing under
direct pressure, or with a purpose-built junctional device where one
exists. These are the junctional areas, and a federal clinical reference
does enumerate them: the places where an extremity meets the trunk,
namely the groin above the inguinal ligament, the buttock and pelvis, the
perineum, the armpit, and the base of the neck. What they have in common
is that a big vessel runs there and there is no limb above the wound to
squeeze, so where a tourniquet cannot go, packing and pressure are what
is left.

**It also applies on a limb**, as an adjunct, in two situations: when a
wound is deep and pressure alone is not controlling it, and as part of
how a trained provider later gets a tourniquet off. For a bystander with
a limb wound that pressure has not controlled, the tourniquet is the
simpler and more reliable answer and it is the one to reach for.

**Where it does not apply.** Do not pack a wound of the chest or the
abdomen. The reasoning is structural rather than a quoted prohibition,
and it is worth seeing: the same federal reference classifies chest,
abdominal and pelvic bleeding as non-compressible haemorrhage, as opposed
to the compressible bleeding that packing and pressure are for. The
bleeding you are worried about in a torso wound is inside a cavity, where
nothing you push into the hole reaches it. That reference does note that
abdominal bleeding can be packed, and says in the same breath that doing
so requires surgical capability, which tells you whose job it is.
Abdominal, pelvic, groin, neck and chest wounds are separately flagged in
the first aid guidance as wounds that can be far more serious than they
look because of internal bleeding, and as wounds needing medical care
immediately rather than home management. If organs or tissue are visible
through the wound, do not push them back in. Cover them with a clean
moist dressing and leave them.

**The neck deserves its own sentence.** Press on the bleeding point with
your fingers, do not wrap anything all the way around a neck, and get
emergency help now. Be clear that the second half of that is reasoning
rather than a quotation: no source opened for this guide states the
no-circumferential-wrap rule, and the reason it is here is that a
bandage taken right around a neck necessarily compresses the airway and
the uninjured side's blood supply as well as the wound. What the military
guidance does show is that head and neck bleeding is treated as its own
problem, reaching for a device that holds the wound edges together rather
than for a wrap. A bleeding neck is beyond what reading can teach.

**How to pack, if you must.** Find the bleeding point inside the wound
with your fingers. Pack gauze or cloth into the wound firmly, one
handful at a time, pressing each into the cavity and aiming at that
point, until the cavity will take no more. Then hold firm pressure on top
of the packing. Military guidance for haemostatic dressings specifies at
least three minutes of direct pressure after the dressing goes on, which
is a useful floor for ordinary gauze too: it is longer than people
naturally hold, and letting go early wastes the packing.

Haemostatic gauze, which is ordinary-looking gauze impregnated with a
clotting agent, is what military guidance uses here and it is available
to civilians. It is a genuine improvement over plain gauze and it is a
reasonable thing to keep in a kit. It is not a substitute for the
pressure. The guidance that recommends it also specifies that it be held
down hard for minutes.

## Tourniquets

This is the section most likely to contain something you have heard that
is wrong, because the guidance changed and the old version is still in
circulation.

**What changed, stated only as far as the sources support it.** The old
teaching treated a tourniquet as a last resort that would probably cost
the limb, and taught pressure points and elevation as the things to try
first. The current position is visible in what the US government now
publishes: a public campaign, originating in a federal interagency
workgroup convened by the White House National Security Council staff,
whose poster teaches ordinary bystanders to put a tourniquet on. A
systematic review of civilian tourniquet use frames the same shift from
the other side, describing a call to put tourniquets into civilian first
aid because of mass-casualty incidents with penetrating injuries. This
guide is not going to give you a date or a decade for the change, because
none of the pages opened for it does. If you are reading advice that
treats a tourniquet as a limb-sacrificing act of desperation, you are
reading the older position.

**When to apply one.** For life-threatening bleeding from an arm or a
leg. The federal poster's framing is that a wound on the leg or arm that
will not stop bleeding is a good candidate for a tourniquet, and its
trigger for going to one is that direct pressure has not stopped the
bleeding and the dressing has soaked through. Military guidance also
applies one immediately, without a trial of pressure, for a traumatic
amputation.

A tourniquet only works where there is a limb: one bone or two, with the
vessels running alongside them and a place above the wound to squeeze. It
does nothing for the torso, the neck, the head, or a wound in the groin
or armpit above where a strap can sit.

**Where to put it.** Two to three inches above the wound, which is about
five to eight centimetres, on the side toward the heart. The federal
poster words it as at least two to three inches from the wound and adds
the useful plain-language check that the tourniquet goes between the
wound and the heart. Military guidance words it as directly two to three
inches above the bleeding site.

If you cannot tell where the bleeding is coming from, military guidance
says to put it high and tight, meaning as far up the limb as it will go.
That instruction exists for the situation where finding the exact source
would cost more time than it is worth.

**Do not put it over a joint.** Not over an elbow, knee, wrist or ankle.
The federal poster gives the reason: the joint's structure shields the
blood vessels, so you cannot generate the pressure needed to shut an
artery there. If the wound is at or just above a joint, move the
tourniquet up past the joint, toward the torso, and apply it on the
solid limb above.

**How tight.** Tight enough that the bleeding stops, and the instruction
for getting there is uncompromising: tighten it as much as you can. With
a windlass tourniquet, the strap is pulled down snug by hand first, then
the rod is twisted to generate the real pressure, and it is twisted until
the bleeding stops. Then the rod is locked so it cannot unwind. In the
clinical version of this instruction, the endpoint is stricter still: the
pulse beyond the tourniquet should be gone, and if bleeding continues or
that pulse is still there, the answer is to tighten further or to put a
second tourniquet immediately alongside the first.

**Expect it to hurt, and do not treat that as a signal.** A tourniquet
tight enough to stop arterial bleeding is painful, and a conscious person
will tell you so, forcefully. None of the federal materials opened for
this guide says "it will hurt" in as many words, so treat that sentence
as an observation rather than a quotation. What they do say is the part
that matters: tighten it as much as you can, and if bleeding persists,
tighten it more. Pain is not an endpoint anywhere in that guidance.
Bleeding stopping is.

**Note the time.** Write it on the tourniquet, on tape, on the person's
skin, on your hand. The federal poster gives two reasons: leaving a
tourniquet on too long can damage tissue, and the timestamp tells the
people who take over which patients to treat first. If you cannot write
it, remember it and say it out loud to whoever takes over.

**Should a bystander ever loosen or remove it? No.** Be clear about where
that answer comes from. Military guidance does describe converting a
tourniquet to a dressing, and it is worth seeing the conditions attached,
because they are the reason the answer for you is different. That
guidance permits conversion only when three things are all true: the
casualty is not in shock, the wound can be watched closely for rebleeding
afterwards, and the tourniquet is not controlling an amputation. It adds
that every effort should be made to convert within two hours if the
bleeding can be controlled another way, and that a tourniquet in place
for more than six hours should not be removed unless close monitoring and
laboratory capability are available.

Read what those conditions assume: a trained provider who can assess
shock, watch a wound continuously, and undo the decision if it goes
wrong. A bystander waiting for an ambulance has none of that. The
Uniformed Services University's public material gives the bystander
version of the instruction directly: call 911 and leave the tourniquet in
place until medical professionals arrive. The old folk instruction to
loosen it periodically to "let blood through" appears in none of the
sources opened here. Put it on, note the time, leave it alone, hand it
over.

**What the two-hour and six-hour figures actually describe, and why they
should not make you hesitate.** A systematic review of 55 studies of
prehospital tourniquet use in civilians and military casualties found
that complications seemed tightly related to application time, and that
application times of less than two hours seemed reasonably safe in
previously healthy patients, while times beyond two hours seemed to
increase the rate of fasciotomy. A federal clinical reference goes
further on the limb question, reporting a series in which 308 limbs had
tourniquets applied with ten nerve palsies noted, 3.2 percent, and
stating that not a single amputation was performed because of tourniquet
use. It adds that other reports describe tourniquets left in place for up
to six hours without loss of the limb.

Read that against the fear the old teaching installed. The thing people
dread, that a tourniquet on for the twenty or forty minutes before an
ambulance arrives will cost the limb, is not what any of those numbers
describe.

**The number that should actually drive your behaviour is the one about
timing.** The same systematic review reports a study in which 96 percent
of patients survived when the tourniquet was placed before the onset of
shock, against 4 percent of those who received one later. Treat that
comparison with the caution its authors do, since the review graded all
its evidence as very low certainty and a patient who deteriorated before
the tourniquet went on was probably worse hurt to begin with. But the
direction of it is not in doubt, and it points the same way as everything
else here: the cost of applying a tourniquet a few minutes early is small
and reversible, and the cost of applying it late is not.

**Clothing, where the sources genuinely disagree.** The federal poster
says a tourniquet may be applied and secured over clothing. Military
guidance applies it over the uniform while under fire, then replaces it
with one applied directly to the skin once there is time and cover. The
National Library of Medicine's first aid material goes the other way and
advises against putting it directly on skin if you can avoid it, because
the skin and tissue can be twisted or pinched, suggesting padding or the
sleeve or trouser leg instead.

That is a real disagreement between three federal sources and this guide
is not going to pretend it away or invent a reason for it. What all three
agree on is more important than what they differ on: two to three inches
above the wound, not over a joint, tightened until the bleeding stops,
time noted. Do that with clothing or without it, whichever is faster, and
do not spend any of your minutes on the question.

## Improvised tourniquets, honestly

A belt, a strap, a torn shirt. The honest answer here is neither "never"
nor "sure, use a belt", and the evidence is specific enough to be useful.

**The single most important finding is about the windlass.** A windlass
is a rigid rod you slide under the band and twist, turning your limited
grip strength into a great deal of tension. A manikin study of improvised
tourniquets made from a tee shirt on a thigh, with two users performing
forty tests each, found that without a windlass improvised tourniquets
failed to stop the bleeding in 99 percent of tests, 79 out of 80. With a
windlass, the failure rate was 32 percent. The same pattern held for
abolishing the pulse: without a windlass, every single one of the 80
attempts failed; with one, 25 of 80 failed.

Read both halves of that. A strap you simply pull tight and knot, which
is what most people picture when they picture an improvised tourniquet,
essentially never worked in that model. Adding a rigid rod and twisting
it turned a technique that failed almost always into one that worked
about two times in three. But two in three is also the ceiling: even done
properly, it failed a third of the time on a manikin thigh, which is a
forgiving target compared to a real leg with muscle, fat and a person
attached.

**The same pattern shows up in people, not just manikins.** A randomised
crossover trial put 102 members of the public through five tourniquet
types and measured whether each was applied correctly, defined as
reaching at least 250 mm Hg within a two-minute cap on a simulator. The
purpose-built windlass tourniquet the course had taught was applied
correctly 92.2 percent of the time; the improvised tourniquet, 32.4
percent. Note the scope carefully, because it cuts the comforting way and
the uncomfortable way at once: these people were tested immediately after
completing a full bleeding-control course, so 32.4 percent is close to a
best case for an improvised tourniquet, not a worst one. It is also worth
seeing that two of the commercial designs in that trial scored 11.8
percent, well below the improvised one, which says the lesson is about
design and training rather than about commercial versus homemade as
categories.

**One systematic review reads oddly, and the honest thing is to say so
rather than pick the half that suits.** A review of twenty studies on
improvised tourniquets states in its results that in both simulated
experiments and real-life situations improvised tourniquets outperformed
commercial ones on success rate, and that the band-and-windlass design
performed most consistently. Its conclusion nonetheless recommends
against the use of and training in improvised tourniquets by civilian
immediate responders, citing limited efficacy and safety concerns, while
noting they may save lives when applied correctly. Those two statements
sit uneasily together, and this guide could not reconcile them: the full
text is behind a subscription and only the abstract could be read, so
whatever data lies behind that results sentence was not available to
check. Where the abstract and the trial above disagree, the trial is the
one you can inspect.

**And there is a real-world safety signal attached specifically to
improvised tourniquets.** The systematic review of civilian and military
prehospital tourniquet use found that very few amputations were caused by
tourniquets themselves, and that the few that were traced mainly to
improvised tourniquets and to prolonged application time. That is the
clearest statement available on why the improvised version is the
fallback rather than the plan.

What falls out of all of this in practice:

- **A commercial tourniquet is inexpensive and removes this entire
  question.** Put one in the kit in the workshop, one in the vehicle, one
  wherever a chainsaw or a table saw lives. That is the actual conclusion
  of this section.
- **If you have no tourniquet and you are facing a limb bleed that
  pressure has not stopped**, an improvised one with a proper windlass is
  worth attempting, because the alternative is not a better tourniquet,
  it is no tourniquet.
- **If you improvise, it must have a windlass**, it must use a broad band
  rather than a cord or wire, and the rod must be secured so it cannot
  unwind. The first aid guidance describes the construction: a band about
  two to four inches wide, five to ten centimetres, wrapped around the
  limb several times and tied with a half or square knot, a stiff rod
  placed between that knot and a second one, and the rod twisted until
  the bleeding stops and then secured.
- **Do not abandon pressure to go and build one.** If a second person is
  present, they build while you press.

A narrow cord, wire, or bootlace is the wrong material. It concentrates
force into a thin line that damages what is under it before it generates
the broad compression an artery needs.

## What not to do, and why

**Pressure points have been dropped, and for a stated reason.** The
technique of pressing on a proximal artery, in the groin or the upper
arm, to reduce flow to a limb was once standard first aid teaching. A
federal clinical reference now says plainly that using specific pressure
points to control bleeding is no longer recommended, on the basis of
scientific literature demonstrating that collateral circulation defeats
it. That is the mechanism worth understanding: limbs are not plumbed as a
single pipe, so shutting one artery upstream leaves other routes open and
the wound keeps bleeding. On top of that, the technique asks you to
compress a deep artery against a bone through soft tissue, with a
fingertip, accurately, for many minutes, while the person moves. Every
second spent on it is a second not spent on the two things that work. If
you know the technique it is not forbidden, but never let it replace
pressure on the wound or delay a tourniquet.

**Elevation, where two sources in date disagree.** A European
resuscitation guideline from 2015 says directly not to try to control
major external bleeding by using proximal pressure points or elevation of
an extremity. The National Library of Medicine's first aid material,
reviewed in October 2025, still tells the public to raise the bleeding
part of the body when possible. Both were opened for this guide and they
do not agree, so here is the reconciliation this guide will offer and
label as its own: nobody claims elevation stops serious bleeding, and the
disagreement is about whether a weak adjunct is worth mentioning at all.
Treat it accordingly. Raising a limb does not close a torn artery. If you
have a spare hand, it costs nothing. If holding a limb up is costing you
pressure on the wound, stop holding it up.

**Do not remove an impaled object.** A knife, a stake, a length of rebar,
a large splinter of wood. The object may be the only thing plugging the
vessel it cut, and removing it converts a blocked hole into an open one,
often somewhere you cannot reach. The instruction in the first aid
guidance is to leave it, build up padding and dressings around it to
stabilise it, and tape it in place so it cannot move in transit. Then
press around it, not on it. The same guidance says not to probe a wound
or pull out embedded material, for the same reason, and adds that a long
or deeply stuck object is a get-medical-help problem rather than a
tweezers problem. Military wound guidance draws the same line even for
trained providers: debris can be removed, but foreign bodies penetrating
vital structures, the eye included, are left in place until the patient
reaches surgical care.

There is a narrow exception people ask about: an object obstructing the
airway. That is a different emergency and not this guide's subject.

**Do not put a tourniquet over a joint.** Covered above, and repeated
here because it is a specific thing people do when the wound is on a
forearm or a shin and the obvious spot happens to be the elbow or knee.
Go above the joint.

**Do not wash out or scrub a major wound.** This surprises people who
have absorbed "clean the wound" as a universal rule. The first aid
guidance says plainly not to try to clean a major wound, and specifically
not after the bleeding is under control, because cleaning it restarts the
bleeding you just spent ten minutes stopping. Large wound cleaning is a
job for the hospital, under conditions where restarting the bleeding is
recoverable. Part two of this guide is about wounds where cleaning is the
main event, and the distinction between the two is the whole reason this
document is split.

**Do not use a tourniquet as the first move for bleeding that pressure
would have handled**, and equally, do not spend five minutes proving that
pressure will not work on an arterial limb bleed that is obviously
winning. The sequence is pressure, and escalate when pressure is losing.

## After the bleeding stops

It is not over. A person who has bled enough to need any of the above is
still in trouble, and this stage is where an otherwise good rescue gets
undone.

**It is still an emergency-services problem.** Bleeding that could not be
controlled, that required a tourniquet, or that came from a serious
injury is on the first aid guidance's list of reasons to seek medical
help immediately, and so is any suspicion of internal bleeding or shock.
Controlled bleeding is a pause, not a resolution. The person still needs
to be seen, the wound still needs proper cleaning and closure decisions,
and blood already lost still has to be made up.

**Lie them down.** Laying the person flat reduces the chance of fainting,
because it improves blood flow to the brain. Where the bleeding is
severe, the first aid guidance adds raising the feet about twelve inches,
thirty centimetres, and keeping the injured part still. There is an
important exception in the same guidance: if there has been a head, neck,
back or leg injury, do not move the person, because moving them can make
that injury worse. When in doubt between positioning them nicely and not
moving a possible spinal injury, do not move them.

**Keep them warm, and take this far more seriously than it sounds.** This
is the part of after-care that reads like a comfort measure and is not.

Military guidance on hypothermia in trauma states that hypothermia,
clotting failure and acidosis are the three derangements that make up
what it calls the triad of death in trauma patients, and that the
mortality of hypothermic trauma patients is approximately twice that of
similarly injured patients who are not cold. It reports that in civilian
trauma more than 80 percent of patients who did not survive arrived with
a core temperature below 34 degrees C, about 93 degrees F, and that in
both civilian and military trauma, 100 percent mortality has been
demonstrated once core temperature reached below 32 degrees C, about 90
degrees F. Those are figures about how cold an injured person already is
on arrival, not a prediction about the person in front of you, but they
tell you which direction the risk runs.

The mechanism closes the loop: cold blood clots badly, so a cold bleeding
patient bleeds more, gets colder, and clots worse still. The same
guidance notes that below roughly 30 degrees C a person stops shivering
altogether and so loses the ability to generate heat at all, and will
keep cooling unless warmed from outside.

What to do about it. The resuscitation guidance is explicit that this
hypothermia is multifactorial, with causes including cold exposure,
significant blood loss and shock itself, and that it occurs even when
ambient temperatures are elevated and the people treating the casualty
are uncomfortably warm. A warm day is not protection. Its instruction is
to cover the wounds, cover the casualty with a blanket or a space
blanket, and avoid or treat shock.

The hypothermia guideline adds the details that matter outdoors: minimise
exposure to cold ground, wind and air, and put insulation material
between the person and any cold surface as soon as possible. Replace wet
clothing with dry clothing or another thermal barrier such as a sleeping
bag, and if you cannot, wrap an impermeable vapour layer around them
instead and leave it until they are somewhere warm. Do not leave somebody
lying on a cold concrete floor in a cut-open shirt because it is July.

That same guidance contains one counterintuitive detail worth knowing if
a tourniquet is on: it directs that the limb below the tourniquet be left
exposed and allowed to stay cooler than the body core, because that
increases the likelihood of preserving the limb while its blood supply is
cut off. Warm the person; do not warm the tourniquetted limb.

**Stay with them and keep watching.** Shock develops. Somebody who was
lucid five minutes ago and is now vague and grey has deteriorated, and
whoever you are handing over to needs to know that they changed, not just
how they are now.

For the related problem of a person who has become dangerously cold,
whether from exposure or from an injury like this, see
[Cold and Hypothermia](cold_and_hypothermia.md).

# Part two: the wound that will not kill you today

Different problem, different clock. The bleeding has stopped, or never
seriously started. Nothing here is urgent in the sense that part one was
urgent. What is at stake now is whether this heals in a week and is
forgotten, or turns into a spreading infection over the next few days.

Almost everything that decides that is settled in the first hour, by how
well the wound gets cleaned and by whether it gets closed when it should
have been left open.

## Cleaning: irrigation is the whole job

If you take one thing from part two: **the intervention that prevents
wound infection is irrigation.** Not antiseptic, not ointment, not a
dressing. Flushing the wound out with a lot of liquid, with enough force
to actually move debris.

Military wound care guidance summarises the mechanism in a phrase worth
remembering: the solution to pollution is dilution. The point of
irrigation is mechanical. You are physically washing bacteria and dirt
out of the wound, and the reason volume matters is that bacterial load
drops as the volume of fluid going through goes up.

### What liquid

This is the question people agonise over, and it is the one with the most
evidence behind it. The short answer is that clean tap water is fine.

- **Emergency department trials found no meaningful difference.** A
  multicentre trial of adults with simple lacerations requiring closure
  found wound infection in 4 percent of the tap water group and 3.3
  percent of the sterile saline group, and concluded that the rates were
  equivalent. A double-blind randomised trial of lacerations needing
  repair found 3.5 percent infection with tap water against 6.4 percent
  with sterile saline, and concluded there was no difference between
  them, with a trend favouring tap water. A trial in children found 2.9
  percent against 2.8 percent.
- **Military surgical guidance says the same thing for war wounds.**
  Normal saline, sterile water and potable tap water all have comparable
  efficacy and safety as irrigation solutions.
- **The Cochrane review is more cautious, and its caution is the honest
  headline.** Its conclusion is that the effects of cleansing with tap
  water compared with saline are uncertain, and that all the evidence
  identified was low or very low certainty. Uncertain is not the same as
  "no difference proven"; it means the trials are not strong enough to
  settle it.

Put those together and the practical answer is stable even though the
evidence is weak: **if the water is clean enough to drink, it is clean
enough to irrigate an ordinary wound, and using it is much better than
not irrigating because you have no saline.** Note what the trials studied,
because it bounds the claim: simple lacerations in emergency departments.
The best of them specifically excluded puncture wounds, bite wounds,
wounds involving tendon or bone, wounds more than nine hours old, and
people who were immunocompromised or already on antibiotics. Those
exclusions are a fair list of the wounds this guide tells you to take to
somebody else.

If your water is not drinkable, military guidance for austere settings
gives a ladder: sterile isotonic solution is best, clean potable tap
water is next, and the minimum is the cleanest water available, with
non-potable water boiled for at least three minutes and then cooled to
body temperature before it goes near a wound. That is a longer boil than
is usually specified for making water safe to drink, so follow the wound
figure for this purpose; for the general problem of making water safe,
see
[Making Water Safe to Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md).

### How much

Here the useful number is not the military one. Guidance for ordinary
lacerations scales the volume to the wound: roughly 50 to 100 millilitres
of irrigation fluid per centimetre of wound length, which one reference
expresses as 250 millilitres per 5 centimetres of wound. For a
three-centimetre cut that is something between 150 and 300 millilitres, a
glass or two of water, deliberately directed into the wound rather than
run over the top of it.

The military volume bands exist for a different animal: one to three
litres for a small wound, four to eight for an intermediate or dirty one,
and nine or more for large or heavily contaminated wounds. **Do not
transplant those to a kitchen cut.** They are for war wounds under
surgical cleaning, where the wound is open, anaesthetised and being
actively explored. What they are good for is calibration, because almost
everybody under-irrigates: if a contaminated wound in an operating
theatre needs litres, the ten-second rinse a gravel-filled knee scrape
usually gets is not cleaning it.

### How hard

Pressure matters as well as volume, because you are trying to dislodge
things, and there is a band rather than a "more is better" rule.

Military austere-care guidance puts the ideal at 6 to 15 psi for smaller
wounds and states that very high pressure, above 15 psi, is not
associated with reduced infection and has been associated with tissue
damage and with driving bacteria deeper into the tissue. For moderate
wounds it suggests low pressure, under 6 psi, from a bulb syringe or a
bottle with holes punched in the cap. For large wounds it says bluntly
that any large container may be used to pour water onto the wound while
gently scrubbing it.

**A conflict between two sources, reported rather than resolved.** That
same military guidance says a 19-gauge catheter on a 35 millilitre
syringe delivers about 8 psi. A clinical reference on wound irrigation
says the same hardware, a 19-gauge tip on a 35 to 50 millilitre syringe,
produces 25 to 40 psi, and puts the threshold for tissue injury at 70
psi. Those cannot both be right, and this guide cannot tell you which is.
What survives the disagreement is the practical shape: a syringe with a
narrow tip is the high-pressure option, a bulb syringe or a punctured
bottle cap is the low-pressure option, and both are used.

The largest trial on the question suggests the anxiety is misplaced
anyway. A trial of over 2,400 patients with open fractures of the limbs
compared high pressure, above 20 psi, with low pressure, 5 to 10 psi, and
very low pressure, 1 to 2 psi. Reoperation rates were 13.2, 12.7 and 13.7
percent respectively, and the authors concluded that very low pressure is
an acceptable, low-cost alternative. Scope matters: those were open
fractures irrigated in an operating theatre. But if pressure barely moved
the needle there, it is not the thing to agonise over at a sink.

### In practice, at home

Wash your hands. Irrigate the wound generously with clean running water,
aimed into it, for long enough that nothing more comes out. Wash the
surrounding skin with mild soap and water. For a puncture, the first aid
guidance specifies rinsing for five minutes under running water and then
washing with soap, and that is a good floor for anything deeper than a
scratch. For a bite or scratch from an animal, federal travel-health
guidance says to wash with soap and running water for at least twenty
minutes, which is a much longer time than people expect and is worth
taking seriously. Then dress it with something clean that will not stick.

**Two warnings that pull in opposite directions, both of which matter.**
Do not assume a wound is clean because you cannot see dirt in it: the
first aid guidance says so explicitly, and grit ground into a wound is
often invisible under blood. But equally, do not go digging. Look for
objects without poking around, and if something is in there, do not
remove it and do not pick at it. Gravel or dirt that will not come out
with gentle cleaning is itself a listed reason to be seen, precisely
because the alternative is a wound healing over embedded material.

And one hard boundary carried over from part one: **none of this applies
to a major wound.** Do not irrigate or scrub a large or heavily bleeding
wound, and particularly not after you have got the bleeding under
control.

## What does not belong in a wound

The clearest statement comes from the National Library of Medicine's
home wound care instructions, which tell you not to use skin cleansers,
alcohol, peroxide, iodine, or soap containing antibacterial chemicals,
because these can damage the wound tissue and slow healing. The same page
tells you to clean the skin with normal saline or mild soapy water, so
read the ban precisely: it is aimed at antiseptics and antibacterial
additives, not at ordinary soap on the surrounding skin.

**Hydrogen peroxide.** The bottle in the bathroom cupboard is three
percent, and three percent is the concentration with a problem. A review
of hydrogen peroxide in wound healing reports a dose-dependent reversal:
very dilute concentrations promoted wound closure in animal studies,
while three percent delayed healing, and no beneficial effect of three
percent on wound healing has been found in the literature. An older
laboratory study that applied antiseptics directly to cultured human
fibroblasts found three percent hydrogen peroxide cytotoxic, and found
its cellular toxicity exceeded its bactericidal potency, concluding it
was unsuitable for wound care.

Be precise about what that does and does not establish, because the
picture is more mixed than the confident folk version that peroxide
"kills the new tissue". That same laboratory study reported that of the
agents it found cytotoxic, all except hydrogen peroxide went on to
adversely affect healing in an animal model. So the in-vitro case against
peroxide is stronger than the in-vivo case. What is not in dispute is
that household-strength peroxide has no demonstrated healing benefit,
which is a poor trade for something being used because it fizzes. The
fizzing is oxygen coming off as the peroxide breaks down; it is not a
measure of anything being cleaned. Use water.

**Full-strength antiseptics.** An in vitro study of common antiseptics on
human skin cells found povidone iodine, chlorhexidine and ethanol the
most cytotoxic of those tested, with povidone iodine reducing fibroblast
viability to zero and blocking the cells' migration across a scratch,
which is the laboratory stand-in for a wound closing. Dilute sodium
hypochlorite was the least damaging of that set. State the limits
honestly: cultured cell lines, not people, and hydrogen peroxide was not
among the agents tested. A clinical reference makes the same point
practically for povidone iodine, saying it should be used on the wound
edges and not poured profusely inside the wound.

Fibroblasts are the cells that rebuild the tissue. The general principle
is that anything strong enough to kill bacteria indiscriminately in a
wound is usually strong enough to damage the cells doing the repair, and
the wound's own blood supply plus your irrigation are better at the
bacteria problem than a chemical is.

**One real exception, from a federal source, because it matters.** CDC
guidance on rabies exposure directs immediate and thorough cleansing of
all wounds with soap and water and, where available, irrigating the wound
with a virucidal agent such as a povidone-iodine solution. It also states
that thorough wound cleansing alone, without vaccine or other treatment,
markedly reduces the likelihood of developing rabies. So for a possible
rabies exposure the calculus reverses, and the washing itself is one of
the most valuable things you will ever do with a tap.

**Antibacterial ointment is a different thing, and the distinction is
easy to lose.** The first aid guidance for minor cuts and punctures ends
with applying antibacterial ointment and a clean bandage that will not
stick, and military dressing guidance does the same for abrasions,
putting antibiotic ointment on the skin under a non-adherent dressing.
None of that conflicts with the paragraphs above. What the sources warn
against is pouring antiseptics and antibacterial cleansers into an open
wound, and mixing antibiotics into irrigation fluid. A thin layer of
ointment on a cleaned minor wound, under a dressing, is what the guidance
actually describes. The ointment is also not what prevents the infection.
The irrigation you did before it is.

**Additives in the irrigation fluid.** Military guidance states that
additives such as iodine, bacitracin or alcohol are not associated with
reduced infection, and its surgical counterpart says including iodine,
bacitracin or antibiotics in irrigation fluid is without proven benefit.
There is also a trial result worth knowing: in that open-fracture trial,
patients irrigated with a castile soap solution had a higher reoperation
rate than those irrigated with saline, 14.8 percent against 11.6 percent.
That is about putting a soap solution through a surgical wound, not about
washing your skin, but it is a reason not to improvise additives into a
wound flush. Plain water does the work.

**Things people put on wounds that are not treatments.** Butter, oil,
toothpaste, ash, cobwebs, chewing tobacco, raw flour. None of these
belong in a wound. The home wound care guidance says not to put lotion,
cream, or herbal remedies on or around a wound unless a provider has
approved it.

There is one exception worth reporting honestly, because it sounds like
folk medicine and is not. Military guidance for infected wounds in very
austere settings, where evacuation is impossible, describes irrigating
the wound and then packing it with sugar or honey under a dry dressing,
changed every 24 hours. Note every part of that scope: an
already-infected wound, no evacuation, a trained provider, alongside
antibiotics and telemedicine advice, as one option among several. It is
not first aid for a fresh cut, and it is not a reason to reach past clean
water.

For the adjacent question of keeping yourself and your kit clean in the
first place, see
[Handwashing and Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md).

## Closing a wound, and when closing it is dangerous

Closing a wound, with stitches, staples, glue or tape, makes it heal
faster and with a better scar. It also traps whatever is inside it. That
is the entire trade, and getting it wrong is where a confident home
reader does real and lasting harm.

Military prolonged-care guidance states the trade explicitly: closure may
speed healing, but it also greatly increases the risk of infection, and
only wounds that are very clean and have been well cleaned out should be
closed. Its rule for uncertainty is worth adopting wholesale: when in
doubt, it is better to leave a wound open.

**What "left open" actually means**, because people hear it as neglect.
An open wound is irrigated, dressed with something clean and
non-adherent, and redressed regularly while it fills in from the bottom.
It heals more slowly and scars more. It is a worse cosmetic result and a
much better infection result, and for a contaminated wound it is the
correct answer rather than the lazy one.

**The golden period is weaker than its reputation.** The six-hour rule
that circulates in first aid folklore is not supported by the studies
opened for this guide. A prospective study following 2,663 patients with
traumatic lacerations found an overall infection rate of 2.6 percent and
no difference in infection rates between lacerations closed before and
after 12 hours, concluding that time from injury to closure is not as
important as previously thought and crediting improvements in irrigation
and decontamination. A systematic review of four trials covering 3,724
patients concluded that the existing evidence supports neither a golden
period nor a role for wound age in infection of simple lacerations. A
family medicine reference states that studies have been unable to define
a golden period, and gives up to 18 hours for clean wounds and up to 24
hours for head wounds.

Not everything points one way, and the honest report includes the
dissent: a smaller single-centre study of 297 patients found the median
time to closure significantly longer in the wounds that became infected,
867 minutes against 330, while explicitly stating it did not control for
confounding factors.

**What the same evidence does identify as risk factors is more useful
than the clock.** In that 2,663-patient study the independent predictors
of infection were diabetes, a laceration longer than 5 centimetres, a
location other than the head and neck, and moderate or heavy
contamination. Note that three of the four are properties of the wound
and the person, not of the time elapsed. Military guidance makes the same
ordering: its window for closing a laceration is under 12 hours, extended
to 24 for the face or scalp, but the conditions attached to it are that
the wound is clean, has minimal tissue loss, needs minimal cleaning out,
and closes without tension. A wound that fails those does not become
closable by being fresh.

Two further things about that. First, the 2,663-patient study excluded
human and animal bites, so none of its reassurance extends to them.
Second, the surgical guideline for war wounds goes hard the other way for
its own population, directing that closure be avoided after the first
washout and within the first 48 hours, and stating that premature closure
causes severe illness from tissue-destroying infection that is often
invisible from outside until late. Those guidelines are not in conflict;
they are about different wounds. Clean-edged lacerations are closable
early. Wounds with tissue destruction and heavy contamination are not.

**The wounds that must not be closed at home, which is the point of the
section:**

- **Puncture wounds.** Military guidance states that puncture wounds in
  general should not be primarily closed, and that small ones should be
  allowed to drain freely and heal from the bottom. A puncture is a deep,
  narrow track that carried surface contamination inside and that you
  cannot irrigate properly because you cannot reach the bottom of it.
  Sealing the entrance traps that. This is the wound type that most often
  gets closed by somebody being helpful.
- **Anything that may have entered a joint.** A clinical reference on
  animal bites states that deep puncture wounds and those involving
  joint spaces should not be closed.
- **Bite wounds.** The first aid guidance lists any human or animal bite
  as a reason to contact a healthcare provider right away, and military
  guidance says most animal bites should heal by secondary intent,
  especially puncture wounds. A clinical reference reports the
  Infectious Disease Society of America's position as leaving bite wounds
  open unless there is a cosmetic concern such as the face. Report the
  uncertainty too: a Cochrane review of primary closure for mammalian
  bites found the evidence very low certainty and concluded that the
  benefits and harms of closing them remain uncertain, and every study it
  found concerned dog bites only. Uncertain evidence about what a surgeon
  should do is not a licence to close a bite at home.
- **Wounds with tissue loss or crushing.** Dead tissue inside a closed
  wound is the ideal environment for exactly the infections you are
  trying to avoid.
- **Heavily contaminated wounds**, and anything where you are not
  confident you got the contamination out.
- **Anything with retained foreign material.** Closing over a retained
  fragment guarantees the problem.
- **Any wound already showing signs of infection.** If a closed wound
  becomes infected, the guidance is that it has to be reopened, which
  tells you what closing an infected wound achieves.

**A note on bite infection rates, because they explain the caution.** A
clinical reference puts infection after a dog bite at between 2 and 25
percent and after a cat bite at about 30 percent, attributing the
difference to how deeply cat teeth inoculate pathogens, and cites a study
finding 14 percent of dog bites infected against 58 percent of cat bites.
It also reports that nearly a third of bites to the hand become infected.
A cat bite is the worst combination this guide describes: a puncture,
carrying saliva, often on a hand, and looking like nothing.

**Wound closure strips and glue at home.** For a clean, shallow, straight
cut with edges that fall together on their own, in a low-tension area,
adhesive strips are a reasonable home measure. Two cautions. First, they
follow the same logic as stitches, so strips over a puncture or a bite
are the same mistake as sutures over one. Second, do not pull the edges
together under tension. Absence of tension is one of the conditions the
guidance names for a wound being closable, and a closure fighting to stay
shut will fail and will scar worse than leaving it alone.

**Stitching a wound yourself is not covered here, deliberately.** It is a
procedure with a skill floor and a judgement floor, and the judgement
floor is higher: the hard part is not the stitch, it is correctly
deciding that this wound should be closed at all. A person who can suture
neatly and decides wrongly has sealed an infection inside somebody.

One geographic note that explains something people notice: military
guidance says wounds of the face and scalp rarely become infected,
because of the high blood flow there, and that most can be closed after
irrigation. That is why the closure window for facial lacerations is
longer. It is not a reason to manage a facial wound at home. Facial
wounds are on the list of wounds to have seen, for cosmetic reasons and
because what is under them matters.

## Tetanus

Tetanus is caused by a bacterium that lives in soil and manure, grows
where there is no oxygen, and produces a toxin that causes severe muscle
spasm. It is rare where vaccination is common and it is vicious when it
happens, and prevention is entirely about the vaccination interval.

**The intervals, from the CDC's wound management guidance.** For somebody
who has had a complete primary series of three or more doses:

- **Clean, minor wound:** a booster is indicated if the last dose was
  **10 or more years** ago.
- **All other wounds:** a booster is indicated if the last dose was
  **5 or more years** ago.

That is the whole rule for the vaccinated majority, and it is why the
routine advice is a booster every ten years: a dirty wound halves the
interval, so a person who is current at five years is covered either way.

**A tetanus-prone wound is defined by more than dirt, and this is the
part people get wrong.** The CDC's examples of wounds that are not "clean
and minor" include penetrating and puncture wounds; wounds containing
dirt, soil, faeces or saliva, which is why animal and human bites are in
this category; and wounds containing dead tissue, which brings in burns,
compound fractures, crush injuries, frostbite, and necrotic or gangrenous
wounds. A clean-looking nail puncture with no visible dirt at all is
tetanus-prone because it is deep and airless, and a burn is tetanus-prone
because of the dead tissue, not because of contamination.

**If you cannot remember when your last tetanus shot was, that is the
answer.** People with an unknown or incomplete vaccination history, and
those never vaccinated, are in the group the CDC says needs tetanus
immune globulin in addition to the vaccine for a dirty or major wound,
which is a stronger intervention and a good reason not to guess about
your own history. Immune globulin is never indicated for a clean minor
wound.

**Two things tetanus prevention is not.** It is not a reason to delay
cleaning the wound, and the CDC's guidance puts cleaning first: clean all
wounds, remove dirt and foreign material, remove dead tissue. And it is
not something antibiotics substitute for. The CDC states that
antibiotics, topical or systemic, are not recommended for tetanus
prevention.

This same interval logic appears in [Burns](treating_burns.md), because a
burn is a tetanus-prone wound for the dead-tissue reason.

## Recognising infection

Most wounds that go wrong go wrong over two to five days, and the
person's own account is a good instrument. A wound that hurt on day one,
less on day two, and more on day three is telling you something.

**The local findings**, from the first aid guidance and from military
wound care guidance, which agree closely: increasing pain rather than
decreasing, warmth and redness spreading out from the wound edges,
swelling, throbbing, and pus or cloudy tan, green or yellow drainage that
may smell bad. A wound producing more discomfort each day rather than
less is infected until shown otherwise. Home wound care guidance gives a
temperature threshold for calling somebody: 100.5 degrees F, 38 degrees
C, or higher.

**The findings that mean go now, not tomorrow:**

- **A red streak running away from the wound**, usually toward the trunk,
  following the line of a limb. It means the infection is travelling
  rather than sitting still. The first aid guidance lists it among the
  reasons to seek help, and this repo's burns guide flags it in the same
  terms. Do not wait overnight on a red streak.
- **Fever, chills, or feeling systemically unwell.** A wound infection
  that has made your whole body sick has stopped being a local problem.
- **Swollen, tender lymph nodes** in the groin, armpit or neck draining
  the area.
- **Pain out of proportion to what you can see, with redness spreading
  fast.** This is the one to take most seriously. The CDC's guidance on
  necrotising fasciitis lists the early symptoms as a red, warm or
  swollen area of skin that spreads quickly, fever, and severe pain
  including beyond the area that is red, warm or swollen, and states that
  the infection spreads very quickly and that even with treatment up to 1
  in 5 people died from it. It names cuts and scrapes, puncture wounds,
  burns, insect bites and surgical wounds among the ways the bacteria get
  in, and says to see a healthcare provider right away, particularly
  after an injury or surgery. If the pain and the appearance disagree,
  believe the pain.
- **Confusion, a racing heart, breathlessness, clammy skin, or extreme
  pain or discomfort.** The CDC lists these among the signs of sepsis,
  which it describes as the body's extreme response to an infection and a
  life-threatening medical emergency, warning that without fast treatment
  it leads rapidly to tissue damage, organ failure and death. The skin is
  one of the places it says these infections commonly start. Somebody
  with an infected wound who becomes confused or short of breath is an
  ambulance call, not a clinic appointment.

Military guidance uses the same two-tier split: local signs of increasing
pain, redness and pus, and then systemic illness meaning fever, a fast
heart rate, low blood pressure, lethargy or reduced alertness. The second
tier is the emergency.

## What you cannot manage at home

Hand this over. Not eventually, now.

- **Bleeding you could not control, or that needed a tourniquet.** On the
  first aid guidance's list of reasons to seek help immediately.
- **Any suspicion of arterial injury:** bright red blood that pulsed or
  spurted, a limb that is cold, pale, or numb below the wound, or a pulse
  you can no longer feel past it.
- **Loss of feeling or function.** The first aid guidance makes this an
  emergency call: the person cannot feel the injured area, or it does not
  work right. Numbness means a nerve, and an inability to bend or
  straighten something means a tendon. Both need repair, both are
  time-sensitive, and neither heals by being bandaged. Test it
  deliberately: ask the person to move each joint beyond the wound
  against gentle resistance, and ask whether the skin beyond it feels
  normal.
- **Deep wounds of the hand, wrist, or foot.** The hand packs tendons,
  nerves, arteries and joints into almost no space, so a cut that looks
  minor on the surface can have divided something underneath, and the
  loss-of-function test above is the one that catches it. Hands are also
  where bite infections concentrate: nearly a third of bites to the hand
  become infected. A hand wound deeper than the skin should be seen.
- **Anything that penetrated a joint**, or that you cannot rule out has,
  and any wound that reaches bone.
- **Facial wounds**, for what is under them and for the scar.
- **Wounds more than about a quarter inch deep**, 0.64 centimetres, or
  large or deep wounds generally, even if the bleeding has stopped,
  because these are the ones that may need stitches and the closure
  window is finite.
- **Any bite, human or animal**, and any possible rabies exposure, where
  washing the wound thoroughly is itself one of the most effective things
  you can do while arranging care.
- **Punctures from a nail, a fishhook, or a rusty object**, and stepping
  on a nail specifically.
- **Anything with material stuck in it**, or where part of the object
  that caused the wound is unaccounted for. The first aid guidance makes
  the point that if a piece of whatever cut you is missing, that is
  itself a reason to be seen, even when you can see nothing in the wound.
- **Abdominal, pelvic, groin, neck and chest wounds**, which the first
  aid guidance flags as potentially far more serious than they look.
- **Any infected wound with a red streak, fever, or systemic illness.**
- **Anybody on blood thinners, with a bleeding disorder, with diabetes,
  or with a weakened immune system.** Diabetes was one of the strongest
  independent predictors of laceration infection in the study above, and
  it roughly tripled the odds.
- **An uncertain tetanus history with a tetanus-prone wound.**

## Antibiotics: why this guide will not give you one

This guide names no antibiotic, no dose, and no course length, and the
refusal is deliberate rather than squeamish.

The military wound guidance this document leans on elsewhere does give
specific regimens, and it would be easy to copy them. Look at what that
guidance assumes and it becomes obvious why they must not be copied. It
assumes a trained provider choosing between agents based on where the
wound is and what contaminated it, intravenous access, the ability to
change the drug when the wound declares itself, telemedicine advice, and
an evacuation plan. It prescribes different drugs for wet jungle terrain
than for elsewhere, which alone tells you the choice is not generic. Its
regimens are part of a package that includes surgical cleaning, and in
that package the antibiotic is explicitly called a complement to
debridement and irrigation, not a replacement for it.

The three practical reasons this matters to you:

- **An antibiotic does not fix a wound that needed cleaning or opening.**
  For an infected wound, the guidance's first instructions are
  mechanical: take the dressings off, inspect it, reopen it if it was
  closed, and get the pus and dead tissue out. Antibiotics come alongside
  that. Taking a tablet instead is treating the part that is easiest to
  treat.
- **The wrong antibiotic is worse than none**, because it delays. Days
  spent watching a spreading infection fail to respond to a leftover
  prescription are days the correct treatment did not happen.
- **Leftover and partial courses select for resistance**, which is
  everyone's problem and eventually yours.

The single exception this guide will name is the one the CDC names, and
it is a negative one: antibiotics are not tetanus prevention.

What this guide will say instead: the things you control are irrigation,
the decision not to close a wound that should stay open, and noticing
early. Those three are worth more than any antibiotic you could obtain
without a clinician, and unlike an antibiotic they are all available to
you right now.

## What the simulation models, and what it does not

The game's medical data lives in `data/medical.ron`, and the mapping to
this document is partial in ways worth knowing, because the simulation
teaches a shape and the shape is not yet complete.

What it represents well:

- **`laceration`** ("Deep Laceration") carries the `bleeding` symptom,
  offers `suturing` or `bandaging`, and progresses to `infected_wound` if
  untreated. That is the part two story in miniature: a wound whose
  danger is infection, not blood loss.
- **`puncture_wound`** is modelled as distinct and worse, listing both
  `bleeding` and `infected_wound` from the outset, progressing to
  `infection_bacterial`, and carrying a longer recovery time than the
  laceration. A puncture starting out already carrying the infection flag
  matches the real guidance.
- **`infection_bacterial`** progressing to `sepsis`, which is marked
  `critical` and described as fatal within days without treatment,
  correctly represents the escalation the "go now" list is about.
- **`bandaging`** is a level 1 procedure taking 30 seconds; `suturing` is
  level 3, takes 300 seconds and carries four times the complication
  chance, 0.08 against 0.02. That ordering is right: closing a wound is a
  harder skill with more ways to go wrong than dressing one.
- **`blood_transfusion`** is a level 6 procedure and a treatment for
  `internal_bleeding`, which correctly puts replacing lost blood out of
  reach of a first responder.

What it does not yet cover is, almost exactly, part one. There is no
life-threatening external haemorrhage condition in the file:
`internal_bleeding` is the only `critical` bleeding entry, and its
treatments are `surgery_major` and `blood_transfusion`. There is no
tourniquet, as a procedure, an item, or a consequence; the word does not
appear. There is no wound packing, no irrigation step, and no tetanus or
immunisation state. Nothing distinguishes a dirty wound from a clean one,
and nothing expresses that a treatment can be contraindicated rather than
merely less effective, which is why `suturing` is currently offered for
`puncture_wound` even though closing a puncture is the error the real
guidance warns against.

None of that is a criticism of the file, which is a solid condition and
procedure catalogue. It is a note that the half of this subject that
saves lives is the half the simulation does not yet model, and that a
player could master every medical procedure in the game without
encountering the idea that you press on a wound and do not let go.

  free of infection compared with no closure or with delayed closure,
  both very low certainty, and the benefits and harms of primary closure
  for mammalian bites remain uncertain.
  https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6894945/
- Eliya-Masamba, M.C., and Banda, G.W., and others, as summarised in the
  systematic review of wound age and infection: Impact of wound age on
  infection rate of simple lacerations. *Injury*, 2012. Four trials,
  3,724 patients; the existing evidence supports neither a golden period
  nor a role for wound age in the infection rate of simple lacerations.
  Abstract read through the NLM service above; PMID 22424703.
- Forsch, R.T., Little, S.H., and Williams, C. Laceration Repair: A
  Practical Approach. *American Family Physician*, volume 95, issue 10,
  2017, pages 628 to 636. That studies have been unable to define a
  golden period; primary closure up to 18 hours for non-infected wounds
  caused by clean objects and up to 24 hours for head wounds; 50 to 100
  mL of irrigation solution per centimetre of wound length; and that
  potable tap water instead of sterile saline does not increase infection
  risk.
  https://www.aafp.org/pubs/afp/issues/2017/0515/p628.html

### What could not be sourced

- **That a correctly applied tourniquet is painful.** Stated in the text
  as an observation, because none of the federal materials opened here
  says it in those words. What they do say, and what the guide relies on
  instead, is that the tourniquet should be tightened as much as possible
  and tightened further if bleeding persists.
- **An instruction to bystanders to loosen a tourniquet.** This one
  resolved in the guide's favour: the Uniformed Services University's
  public page tells the bystander to leave the tourniquet in place until
  medical professionals arrive, so "leave it alone" is sourced and not
  merely inferred. What is still absent from every source opened here is
  any mention of the old folk practice of periodically loosening one, in
  either direction.
- **Why military guidance prefers a tourniquet on bare skin while other
  federal guidance prefers padding or clothing.** The disagreement is
  reported as it stands. No source opened here explains it, and none is
  invented.
- **Why a scalp wound bleeds so heavily.** The first aid guidance gives
  the scalp as its example of a minor injury that bleeds a lot but does
  not say why, and the military note about high blood flow in the face
  and scalp is made in the context of infection, not bleeding. No
  mechanism is asserted here.
- **The rule against wrapping a bandage right around a neck.** No source
  opened here states it. It is presented in the text as reasoning, with
  the reasoning shown, rather than as guidance.
- **A plain prohibition on packing the chest or abdomen.** No source
  opened here says it in those words. The torso is excluded structurally
  instead: packing is scoped to compressible bleeding and the torso is
  classified as non-compressible. The text shows that reasoning rather
  than presenting a quotation.
- **Wound packing technique taught to lay rescuers.** The widely taught
  civilian packing course material is published by a copyrighted
  professional body and was not used. What is here comes from the
  military junctional-haemorrhage guidance, the federal clinical
  reference on haemorrhage control, and the first aid guidance on torso
  wounds.
- **Numerical failure rates for improvised tourniquets applied by
  genuinely untrained people to real limbs.** The closest figures
  available are from a manikin model and from a randomised trial of
  people tested immediately after training, and both are reported with
  those limits attached.
- **When first aid teaching changed its position on tourniquets.** No
  page opened here gives a date or decade, so none is given. The change
  is evidenced only by what the sources currently say.
- **A resolution of the syringe pressure conflict.** Two sources opened
  here give figures roughly four times apart for the same hardware. The
  conflict is reported rather than resolved.
- **A resolution of the elevation disagreement.** A 2015 European
  guideline says not to use elevation for major bleeding; a federal
  consumer page reviewed in 2025 still tells the public to raise the
  bleeding part. Both are reported. The reconciliation offered in the
  text is labelled as this guide's own.
- **The current edition of the Tactical Combat Casualty Care
  Guidelines.** The 25 January 2024 edition was read in full and is what
  is cited. A later edition dated 1 May 2026 exists, but its PDF is
  encrypted and would not yield text here, so it was not read and nothing
  is attributed to it.
