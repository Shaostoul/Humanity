# Why Canning Has Rules

Take a jar of home-canned green beans off a shelf and look at it. The lid
is domed down. The liquid is clear. It does not smell of anything, because
it is sealed. Every single thing you can observe about that jar is
consistent with it being perfectly good food.

Every single thing you can observe about that jar is also consistent with
it containing enough botulinum toxin to kill the person who eats it.

That is the whole problem, and it is why canning is the one kitchen
activity where a published procedure is not a suggestion. In most cooking,
the feedback loop works: you taste, you adjust, you learn. Here the
feedback loop is broken on purpose by the organism itself. You cannot
taste your way to safety, and the one test everybody reaches for, opening
the jar and having a careful sniff, is not a test at all.

## What this document does, and what it refuses to do

This document explains **why** the rules exist and what each one is
protecting against. It is the reasoning layer. Once you understand what
the tested processes are actually doing, the parts that look excessive
stop looking arbitrary, and, more usefully, you stop being able to guess
which ones are safe to skip. The answer turns out to be none of them, but
for a reason you can follow rather than on somebody's authority.

**This document contains no canning process times, and you must not try to
can anything from it.** It pairs no food with a time and a jar size
anywhere, which is what a process actually is. It does contain some numbers
with minutes and pressures in them, because you cannot explain why the
rules exist without them: the range of temperatures a pressure canner
reaches, the published boil that destroys the toxin at serving time, and
USDA's own comparison of how long things take. Each of those is flagged
where it appears. None of them tells you how to process a jar of anything.

That distinction is deliberate. Process times are tied to one specific
food, prepared
one specific way, in one specific jar size, at one specific altitude, and
a time lifted out of that context is worse than no time at all because it
carries false confidence. The tested processes live in the **USDA Complete
Guide to Home Canning**, which is a free public-domain download, and that
is where you go when you want to actually do this. It is in the Sources
section at the bottom.

The practical companion inside this Library is
[Keeping What You Grew](keeping_what_you_grew.md), which walks through
freezing, drying, root cellaring and water-bath canning, and which gives
one complete tested procedure so you can see what "tested procedure"
means in practice. This document is the theory behind the fence. Read
both. Do neither instead of reading the USDA guide.

One more honest boundary. Reading cannot make you a competent pressure
canner. It can make you a person who understands why pressure canning
exists, which is a genuinely different thing and is what this document is
for. The mechanical skill, the gauge testing, the judgement about your own
equipment, those come from the tested manual and ideally from somebody
experienced standing next to you.

## The organism, and the four things it needs

*Clostridium botulinum* is a bacterium that exists in two states. As a
**spore** it is inert, tough, and effectively everywhere. FDA's Bad Bug
Book describes the organism and its spores as "widely distributed in
nature," found "in both cultivated and forest soils; bottom sediments of
streams, lakes, and coastal waters; in the intestinal tracts of fish and
mammals." The spores are on the carrot you just pulled up. They are on
your hands, in your garden, in the dust. This is normal and it is not a
hygiene failure.

That last point deserves emphasis, because it is where people's intuition
misfires hardest. Botulism is not a dirtiness problem. A spotless kitchen
does not protect you, scrubbing the vegetables does not remove the spores,
and no amount of care with
[Handwashing and Personal Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md) touches
this hazard. Hygiene is genuinely important and it prevents a long list of
other illnesses; it simply is not the control for this one.

USDA's own passage here is worth reading in full rather than in the one
line people usually pull from it, because the full version is more honest
and still makes the point. Writing about "most bacteria, yeasts, and
molds," USDA says they "are difficult to remove from food surfaces.
Washing fresh food reduces their numbers only slightly. Peeling root crops,
underground stem crops, and tomatoes reduces their numbers greatly.
Blanching also helps, but the vital controls are the method of canning and
making sure the recommended research-based process times, found in these
guides, are used."

So surface preparation is not useless, and peeling in particular does a
lot. What USDA will not let you conclude is that any of it substitutes for
the process. The phrase to hold onto is the last one: **the vital controls
are the method of canning and the tested process times.** Cleanliness is
worth doing and is not the control here.

Spores are harmless to eat. You have eaten many. The danger begins only
when a spore finds conditions that let it wake up into an actively growing
cell, because the growing cell is what makes the toxin. USDA's Complete
Guide states the conditions precisely. When conditions suit them, USDA
says, the spores produce vegetative cells which multiply rapidly and "may
produce a deadly toxin within 3 to 4 days of growth in an environment
consisting of:"

- "a moist, low-acid food"
- "a temperature between 40 and 120 degrees F"
- "less than 2 percent oxygen"

Take those three lines seriously, because they are the master key to this
entire subject. Everything else in this document is a consequence of them.

Notice what USDA says next: "Botulinum spores are on most fresh food
surfaces. Because they grow only in the absence of air, they are harmless
on fresh foods." A fresh carrot on the counter has spores on it and is
safe, because it is sitting in air. The spores are not the hazard. **The
container is the hazard.** A sealed jar is an oxygen-free box, and if you
put a moist low-acid food in it and store it at room temperature, you have
deliberately built all three conditions at once and then put a lid on them.

This is the thing that makes canning different from every other
preservation method. Canning does not avoid the dangerous conditions. It
creates them on purpose, because an airtight oxygen-free jar is exactly
what keeps food edible for years. Then it relies entirely on having killed
everything inside first. There is no second line of defence. That is why
the process has to be right.

## The number that explains most of the rulebook: pH 4.6

Of the three conditions, acidity is the one you can control by choosing
what to put in the jar, and it is the one the whole rulebook is organised
around.

USDA draws the line at **pH 4.6**, and draws it in both directions:

- "Low-acid foods have pH values higher than 4.6. They include red meats,
  seafood, poultry, milk, and all fresh vegetables except for most
  tomatoes."
- "Acid foods have a pH of 4.6 or lower. They include fruits, pickles,
  sauerkraut, jams, jellies, marmalades, and fruit butters."

**What 4.6 means.** Below it, the food is acidic enough that *C.
botulinum* cannot grow, so the jar cannot generate toxin no matter how
long it sits. USDA: acid foods "contain enough acid to block their growth,
or destroy them more rapidly when heated." Above it, the food will support
growth, and the only thing standing between you and toxin is having killed
every spore during processing.

**What 4.6 does not mean.** It is not a flavour threshold and you cannot
taste it. Plenty of things that taste sharp are above 4.6 and plenty of
things that taste bland are below it. It is also not a property of a food
category you can reason about from first principles; it is a measured
number for a specific product, and mixtures behave in ways that surprise
people. USDA is explicit that combining foods does not average out in your
favour: "Most mixtures of low-acid and acid foods also have pH values
above 4.6 unless their recipes include enough lemon juice, citric acid, or
vinegar to make them acid foods." Adding a lot of tomatoes to a vegetable
soup does not make the soup an acid food. A jar of mostly-fruit chutney
with onion in it is not automatically safe. The tested recipe is what tells
you where the mixture landed.

Now read that low-acid list once more, slowly. **All fresh vegetables.**
Green beans. Corn. Carrots. Potatoes. Peas. Beets. Asparagus. Greens.
Squash. Every vegetable in your garden is a low-acid food except tomatoes,
which USDA writes into the sentence as the exception and which this guide
gives a section of its own, and so is every piece of meat, poultry and
fish. The set of things that can go in a
boiling-water canner is much smaller than most people assume, and it is
almost entirely fruit, pickles and jam.

**A note on measuring it yourself.** If you have a home water test kit
from [Testing Water](testing_water.md), you own pH strips, and it is
natural to think you could just test the jar and settle the question. Do
not do this. Household pH strips and inexpensive meters are made for a
different job at a different precision, the reading you need is the
*finished equilibrium* pH of the whole sealed product rather than the pH
of the liquid you dipped into, and being slightly wrong here is not a
slightly wrong answer, it is a lethal one. The reason the tested recipes
specify measured amounts of a standardised acid is precisely so that
nobody has to measure pH at home. Follow the recipe instead of testing.

## Tomatoes, the classic trap

Tomatoes are where this goes wrong most often, because they sit almost
exactly on the line and because everybody's grandmother canned them in a
water bath without incident.

USDA: "Although tomatoes usually are considered an acid food, some are now
known to have pH values slightly above 4.6. Figs also have pH values
slightly above 4.6. Therefore, if they are to be canned as acid foods,
these products must be acidified to a pH of 4.6 or lower with lemon juice
or citric acid. Properly acidified tomatoes and figs are acid foods and
can be safely processed in a boiling-water canner."

Read the structure of that carefully, because it is doing something
specific. It does not say tomatoes are acid foods. It says tomatoes are
acid foods **if you make them so**, and that the acidification is what
moves them across the line into the category where a boiling-water bath is
the correct tool. The acid is not an ingredient in the recipe sense. It is
part of the safety process, in the same way the processing time is. A
tomato recipe without added acid is not a variant, it is an incomplete
process.

**"Acidified" means the acid went in, in the measured amount, before
processing.** Not stirred in afterwards, not approximated, not adjusted
down because the result tasted too sharp. NCHFP's tomato acidification
directions anticipate exactly that temptation and answer it: sugar may be
added to balance the acidic taste if you want, but the acid is not to be
left out. Sugar changes how it tastes without undoing the chemistry.
Reducing the lemon juice undoes the chemistry.

This is also the single most common place where a reader half-remembers a
rule and applies it backwards, so here it is at the point of the mistake:
**choosing a pressure canner does not excuse you from acidifying
tomatoes.** The acidification and the heat process are two separate
controls doing two separate jobs. NCHFP's acidification directions are
explicit that the specified acid is added to ensure safe acidity for both
pressure and boiling water bath processing, so the step belongs to the
tomatoes, not to the choice of canner. The measured amounts per jar size
are in those directions and in
[Keeping What You Grew](keeping_what_you_grew.md); they are deliberately
not reproduced here.

Why did this change? Because the reference point moved. The varieties in
cultivation now are not the varieties the old family method was worked out
on, and USDA's current position is the one quoted above: some are now
known to sit above the line. Your grandmother was not being reckless with
the tomatoes she had. She was working with a different input and, in all
likelihood, getting lucky some of the time as well. There is more on
picking tomatoes at a sensible stage in
[Your First Tomato](your_first_tomato.md), and on planning a harvest that
does not force you into preserving a glut of over-ripe fruit in a panic in
[The Growing Calendar Where You Live](the_growing_calendar.md).

There is a second, stranger number in this area, and it is worth knowing
about specifically so that you do not get caught out by it. The federal
regulation covering **commercially** canned foods, 21 CFR 113.3, defines
low-acid foods as "any foods, other than alcoholic beverages, with a
finished equilibrium pH greater than 4.6 and a water activity (aw) greater
than 0.85," and then adds: "Tomatoes and tomato products having a finished
equilibrium pH less than 4.7 are not classed as low-acid foods."

So there is a real, published, federal **4.7** for tomatoes. If you meet it
in isolation it looks like permission to relax. It is not.

**That number is scoped to commercial processors** operating under a
scheduled process, with laboratory pH measurement, process authority
oversight and batch records, inside a regulation about hermetically sealed
containers in a factory. It is a regulatory classification boundary that
decides which rulebook a cannery falls under. It is not home-canning
guidance, it does not appear in the home-canning guidance, and there is no
version of your kitchen in which it applies to you. For home canning the
number is 4.6 and the instruction is to acidify. If you ever see somebody
online cite 4.7 to argue that tomatoes are fine unacidified, they have
taken a factory regulation and pointed it at a kitchen.

## Why a boiling-water bath is not enough for low-acid food

Here are two facts. They are about two different things at two different
temperatures, and mixing them up is the mistake that puts people in
intensive care. Read them as a pair and keep them apart.

**Fact one: boiling water does not reliably destroy the spores.** USDA:
"Botulinum spores are very hard to destroy at boiling-water temperatures;
the higher the canner temperature, the more easily they are destroyed.
Therefore, all low-acid foods should be sterilized at temperatures of 240
to 250 degrees F, attainable with pressure canners operated at 10 to 15
PSIG." A boiling-water canner cannot get there. Water boils at a fixed
temperature for a given pressure, and open to the air that is a ceiling,
not a target you can push past by boiling harder or longer. The jars sit
at the temperature of boiling water and no higher, however vigorous the
bubbling looks.

**Fact two: boiling does destroy the toxin, once it has been made.** The
toxin is a protein and it is far less heat-tolerant than the spore that
produced it. CDC's instruction is to "Boil some foods for 10 minutes before
eating them," and it carries the altitude correction in the same breath:
"Add 1 minute for each additional 1,000 feet of elevation." NCHFP, which
publishes USDA's tested procedures, gives the same figures and is explicit
that the target is the toxin: 10 minutes of boiling below 1,000 feet should
destroy it where it is present.

Now the part that matters. These two facts are about **different targets**,
and the gap between them is exactly where people hurt themselves.

The spore is the seed and the toxin is the poison the sprouted seed makes.
Boiling handles the poison. Boiling does not handle the seed. So a
boiling-water bath applied to low-acid food leaves surviving spores in a
sealed, oxygen-free, moist, low-acid jar held at room temperature, which
is the complete list of conditions the organism needs, and then you put it
on a shelf and wait months. USDA says toxin can appear within 3 to 4 days
of growth. You have given it a year.

If you want a sense of how far a water bath falls short, USDA quantifies
it, and the numbers are startling: "At temperatures of 240 to 250 degrees
F, the time needed to destroy bacteria in low-acid canned food ranges from
20 to 100 minutes... The time needed to safely process low-acid foods in a
boiling-water canner ranges from 7 to 11 hours; the time needed to process
acid foods in boiling water varies from 5 to 85 minutes."

Sit with that. **Seven to eleven hours.** The gap between a water bath and
a pressure canner for low-acid food is not a twenty percent safety margin
that a careful person might shave. It is most of a working day of boiling,
against a pressure process measured in tens of minutes. When somebody says
they water-bathed their green beans and everyone was fine, they did not
find a shortcut through that gap. They rolled dice that mostly come up
blank, because most jars do not happen to contain a spore that germinates.

Those ranges are given here to show the size of the gap, and for no other
purpose. They are not process times. Do not use them as one.

CDC compresses all of the above into one sentence, and it is the single
line to remember out of this whole section: **"Pressure canning is the only
recommended method for canning low-acid foods."** Not the preferred method,
not the method for the cautious. The only one.

This is also why the spores, not the bacteria, are what the process is
aimed at. FDA's Bad Bug Book puts the durability plainly: "The spores are
heat-resistant and can survive in foods that are incorrectly or minimally
processed." A process that kills every living cell in the jar and leaves
the spores has not made the food safe, it has only reset the clock.

**And the 10-minute boil is not a rescue.** This is the second half of the
trap, so here it is at the point of the mistake: the fact that boiling
destroys the toxin does not mean you can water-bath low-acid food and then
boil it before serving.

The two bodies scope the pre-serving boil slightly differently, and you
should follow the broader one. NCHFP aims it at low-acid and tomato foods
that were not canned according to the tested recommendations, and is
emphatic that it applies even when you can detect no signs of spoilage at
all. CDC recommends it more generally, listing home-canned tomatoes,
low-acid home-canned foods and fermented Alaska Native foods among the
things to "Boil some foods for 10 minutes before eating them." Treating it
as a standing habit for any home-canned low-acid food, however confident
you are in the batch, is the safer reading and costs nothing.

NCHFP also notes that CDC's own position since 2013 has been to discard
any home-canned food that might contain the toxin, and is explicit that the
boil is not a guarantee against every defect in a badly made jar. Both
points cut the same way as the paragraph below.

What it is not is permission, and it is worth being exact about where even
damage limitation stops. NCHFP says in the sentence immediately after the
recommendation that this is not intended to serve as a recommendation for
consuming foods known to be significantly underprocessed, and CDC's position
since 2013 is to discard home-canned food that might carry the toxin. So for
a batch you already know was canned the wrong way, low-acid food put through
a boiling-water canner say, the instruction is not to boil it thoroughly. It
is to throw it out. The ten-minute boil is a habit for correctly canned
low-acid food, not a way to rescue a bad one. It also depends on
you boiling every portion thoroughly, every time, for the full time, with
the altitude correction, and on nobody ever tasting the jar while cooking.
A safety margin that a distracted person can skip in one second is not a
process. Use it as the habit it is meant to be, and can the food correctly
in the first place.

## Why you cannot judge a jar by looking

This is the core of the guide, and it is the part people resist most,
because refusing to trust your own senses feels like superstition.

There are real published warning signs, and you should know them. USDA
describes what to check: examine the lid for tightness and vacuum, because
"lids with concave centers have good seals"; then, "while holding the jar
upright at eye level, rotate the jar and examine its outside surface for
streaks of dried food originating at the top of the jar. Look at the
contents for rising air bubbles and unnatural color." Then, "while opening
the jar, smell for unnatural odors and look for spurting liquid and
cotton-like mold growth (white, blue, black, or green) on the top food
surface and underside of lid."

Learn those. A swollen lid, a broken seal, streaks down the outside,
bubbles, odd colour, mould, a bad smell, liquid that spurts when the seal
breaks. Any one of them means the jar is finished and you do not taste it
to check. USDA: "Do not taste food from a jar with an unsealed lid or food
that shows signs of spoilage."

**And now the sentence this whole document is built around.** USDA, on
low-acid foods: "Spoiled low-acid foods, including tomatoes, may exhibit
different kinds of spoilage evidence or very little evidence. Therefore,
all suspect containers of spoiled low-acid foods, including tomatoes,
should be treated as having produced botulinum toxin and handled carefully."

**Very little evidence.** The warning signs are useful in one direction
only. Their presence tells you a jar is bad. **Their absence tells you
nothing at all.** A jar can be sealed, clear, correctly coloured,
pleasant-smelling and lethal, because botulinum toxin is not a spoilage
product in the sense your nose understands. Your nose evolved to detect
the bacteria that rot food and make it smell of rot. This organism is not
one of those. It can grow in a jar and leave the contents looking like
dinner.

CDC puts the same fact in one line, and then closes the obvious loophole in
the next: **"You cannot see, smell, or taste the toxin that causes
botulism. But taking even a small taste of food containing the toxin can be
deadly."** That second sentence is there because the natural response to
"you cannot taste it" is to try a cautious little taste anyway. A cautious
little taste is not a smaller version of the test. It is the exposure.

This is why the inspection ritual, however carefully performed, is not a
safety system. It is a filter that catches the obvious failures. The
process is the safety system. The tested time and pressure are the only
thing that ever protected you, and if the process was wrong, no amount of
looking at the jar afterwards recovers the situation. That is the entire
argument for following the published procedure exactly: it is the only
control in the chain, and you get no feedback on whether it worked.

It also explains the rule that frustrates people most, which is that "we
have always done it this way and nobody ever got sick" carries no
information. Botulism is rare per jar. A method with a small failure rate
per jar produces a long run of uneventful years and then one event, and
the uneventful years are not evidence the method works. They are what a
small failure rate looks like from the inside.

**If you do find a suspect jar, do not improvise the disposal either.** The
toxin is dangerous by contact as well as by mouth, which most people do
not expect. USDA: "Contact with botulinum toxin can be fatal whether it is
ingested or enters through the skin. Take care to avoid contact with
suspect foods or liquids. Wear rubber or heavy plastic gloves when
handling suspect foods or cleaning up contaminated work surfaces and
equipment."

**The published procedure splits on whether the jar is still sealed, and
the two branches are opposite. Do not mix them up.** USDA sends a suspect
container that is "still sealed" into "a heavy garbage bag," closed and put
in the trash or taken to a landfill. It is only a container that is
"unsealed, open, or leaking" that gets the detoxification boil, laid on its
side in a large pot and boiled for 30 minutes. Boiling a still-sealed jar
is not the instruction, and treating it as one risks bursting a sealed jar
of toxin-bearing food in an open pot, which is exactly the contact exposure
the glove warning above exists to prevent.

There is also a specified bleach solution for the surfaces afterwards. The
whole procedure, both branches and the cleanup, is written out step by step
in [Keeping What You Grew](keeping_what_you_grew.md) and in the USDA guide;
read it there rather than working from this summary. Do not pour a suspect
jar down the sink and rinse it.

## Why altitude changes the numbers

Water boils at a lower temperature as you go up, because there is less
atmosphere pressing on it. The bubbles look identical. The thermometer
does not agree.

USDA: "Water boils at lower temperatures as altitude increases. Lower
boiling temperatures are less effective for killing bacteria. Increasing
the process time or canner pressure compensates for lower boiling
temperatures. Therefore, when you use the guides, select the proper
processing time or canner pressure for the altitude where you live."

So the published times and pressures are not one number, they are a small
table, and the row you need depends on where you are standing. Using the
sea-level row in the mountains means your jars were held at a lower
temperature than the process assumed, for the length of time the process
assumed. You under-processed them, and, because of everything in the
previous section, you have no way to tell from the outside. USDA names
1,000 feet as the point where this starts to matter: failing to adjust can
"result in spoilage if you live at altitudes of 1,000 feet or more."

Two practical consequences. First, the correction is not always a smooth
dial. A dial-gauge canner can be set to an intermediate pressure, but a
weighted-gauge canner only has fixed steps, so above 1,000 feet USDA has it
jump to the next weight up rather than trim the pressure finely. The actual
weights are in the process tables, paired with the food and jar size they
belong to, and that pairing is the reason they are not reproduced here.
Second, the altitude correction also applies to the pre-serving boil
described earlier, at one extra minute per 1,000 feet.

Look up your elevation once, from a mapping service or your local
extension office, and write it somewhere permanent near where you can. It
is a single number you will use every season, and it is the easiest of all
these rules to get right.

The same physics shows up elsewhere in this Library, which is a useful
cross-check on whether you have understood it:
[Making Water Safe to Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md) carries CDC's
instruction to boil water for 1 minute, and for 3 minutes above 6,500
feet, for exactly the same reason. If the altitude adjustment there makes
sense to you, the one here is the same idea applied to a jar.

**For Silverdale, Kitsap County, Washington**, the local case is
comfortable: the town sits on Dyes Inlet at close to sea level, so the
sea-level row is the right row for most of the settled area. That is a
local fact and not a general one. It is also exactly the kind of fact that
travels badly, because a person who learned to can at sea level and then
moves inland and uphill carries a set of habits that are now quietly
wrong. The habit to build is not "use the sea-level row," it is "look up
the row for where I am."

## Which methods cannot produce this problem at all

Canning is the risky one. It is worth being clear about why the others are
not, because the reasoning is more useful than the list.

Go back to USDA's three conditions: a moist low-acid food, a temperature
between 40 and 120 degrees F, and less than 2 percent oxygen. All three
must hold at once. **Remove any single one RELIABLY and the organism cannot
grow, so no toxin can be produced, so the hazard does not exist.** Every
safe preservation method below is a different way of removing one of them.

That word "reliably" is carrying weight, and the temperature condition is
where it matters. USDA's 40 degrees F is the floor for the shelf-stable
canning case it is describing. It is not a universal floor for the organism:
FDA puts the minimum growth temperature for type E and the non-proteolytic
B and F types at 38 degrees F. That is why CDC's refrigeration instructions
always pair the cold with a second barrier rather than resting on the cold
alone, and you will see both of them below: air for the foil-wrapped potato,
a four-day clock for the garlic oil. Freezing removes the temperature
condition outright. Refrigeration only slows it, which is a different claim
and buys a different amount of time.

**Freezing removes the temperature.** A freezer is far below the bottom of
the growth range, so the spores simply sit there inert. Note what freezing
does not do: it does not kill the spores, and USDA notes that accidental
freezing of canned food "will not cause spoilage unless jars become
unsealed and recontaminated." Freezing is a pause, not a sterilisation.
When frozen food thaws it re-enters the growth range, which is why thawed
food goes in the refrigerator and not back on the counter. Refrigeration
works the same way with a much smaller margin.

CDC's two everyday examples are worth knowing precisely, because both are
the canning hazard rebuilt without a canner. For homemade flavoured oils:
"Refrigerate homemade oils made with garlic or herbs. Throw the oils away
after 4 days." Garlic is a low-acid food and a layer of oil is an air
barrier, so a bottle of garlic in oil on a warm counter has assembled all
three conditions on your kitchen shelf.

The second is more specific than people remember, so note its exact scope.
CDC's guidance here is about **potatoes that were baked in foil**, not about
leftovers in general. Under the heading "For potatoes that were baked in
foil," CDC says to either "Keep them at 140 degrees F or hotter until
they're served" or "Refrigerate them at 40 degrees F or colder with the
foil loosened so they get air." That last clause is not housekeeping
advice, it is the whole mechanism: the foil is the anaerobic seal, and
loosening it readmits the oxygen that keeps the organism dormant. A
foil-wrapped baked potato left on a counter to cool is a moist, low-acid
food wrapped away from air at room temperature, which is the same three
conditions again in a form nobody thinks of as canning.

**Drying removes the moisture.** The organism needs liquid water available
to it, and the measure of that is water activity, written aw. The
regulation defines it precisely; 21 CFR 113.3 calls it "a measure of the
free moisture in a product and is the quotient of the water vapor pressure
of the substance divided by the vapor pressure of pure water at the same
temperature." FDA's technical guidance then gives the biological threshold:
"The minimum aw level for the growth of *C. botulinum* is approximately
0.93. Depending on various product characteristics this minimum level can
be as high as 0.96." Below that, it cannot grow whatever else you do to it.

Two cautions on that number, because it is easy to misuse. **0.93 is a
minimum for growth, not a home target**, and it is a laboratory measure of
free water, not a percentage of moisture you can estimate by feel; there
is no way to check it in a kitchen. There is a second published figure,
0.85, which comes from the commercial regulation quoted earlier and is the
level at which a product falls outside the low-acid canned food rules
entirely; that one is a regulatory boundary for factories, not a home
drying instruction. What you should take from both is the mechanism, which
is that properly dried food is safe from this particular organism because
there is not enough available water in it, and that "properly dried" is
defined by the tested drying guidance rather than by your judgement of
when something feels crisp. Under-dried food that gets sealed in a
container is the failure mode, and it usually announces itself as mould
rather than as botulism.

**Acid removes the low-acid condition**, which is the pH 4.6 line already
covered. This is why jam, pickles and fruit are the easy cases.

**Salt and sugar work mainly by taking the water away.** They dissolve into
the water that is present and hold onto it, lowering water activity, which
is why FDA notes that water activity "is usually controlled by the use of
salt or sugar." That is the same mechanism as drying, reached by a
different route.

**Now a distinction that most writing on this gets wrong, including an
earlier draft of this document: whether the salt is doing a safety job
depends entirely on the method, and USDA splits it cleanly.**

In ordinary canning, salt is seasoning and you may leave it out. USDA:
"To can tomatoes, vegetables, meats, poultry, and seafood, use the
procedures given in Guides 3 through 5, but omit the salt. In these
products, salt seasons the food but is not necessary to ensure its safety."
Fresh-pack pickles are the same: they "may be prepared safely with reduced
or no salt," at some cost to quality, because there the vinegar is doing
the safety work.

In **fermented** products it is the opposite, and USDA says so in the very
next breath: the salt in fermented sauerkraut and brined pickles "not only
provides characteristic flavor but also is vital to safety and texture. In
fermented foods, salt favors the growth of desirable bacteria while
inhibiting the growth of others." Hence: "Caution: Do not attempt to make
sauerkraut or fermented pickles by cutting back on the salt required."

Note that this second case is not purely a water-activity effect. USDA
describes it as selective inhibition, salt tilting the competition toward
the organisms you want, which is a different mechanism from simply drying
the food out. So the rule to carry is not "never reduce salt." It is:
**reducing salt is a quality decision in canning and a safety decision in
fermenting**, and you need to know which one you are doing.

**Fermentation deserves its own paragraph, because it is safe for a reason
that can fail.** A successful ferment is safe because the lactic acid
bacteria you are cultivating drop the pH below the line. The acid is the
safety, and the acid is a *product* of the process rather than an
ingredient you added. That means the safety is contingent on the ferment
actually working. A ferment that stalls, that was made with too little
salt so the wrong organisms won, or that was diluted away from the tested
proportions, is not a slightly worse ferment. It is a jar of low-acid
vegetables in brine with no acid in it, which is a different and dangerous
object wearing the same clothes. USDA states the requirement plainly:
"There must be a minimum, uniform level of acid throughout the mixed
product to prevent the growth of botulinum bacteria."

USDA's matching caution is written for pickled products, where the acid
arrives in a bottle: "Do not alter vinegar, food, or water proportions in a
recipe or use a vinegar with unknown acidity. Use only recipes with tested
proportions of ingredients." A sauerkraut ferment contains no vinegar, so
read that as the general principle rather than the literal instruction: in
a ferment the proportion you must not alter is the salt, which is what
steers the acid-producing organisms, and the tested recipe is still the
thing you follow rather than adjust.

Note the phrase **minimum, uniform**. Uniform is doing work there. An
average pH of 4.4 across a jar that has an un-acidified pocket in the
middle is not a safe jar, which is also why tested procedures care so much
about how food is packed. One scope note before going further: that
uniform-acid caution is written for pickled products, like the first
bullet of the same block, so read it as a principle this document is
borrowing rather than as a general USDA statement. Now, pureeing. Be
precise about
that restriction, because it is not a blanket ban. USDA permits pureed
*fruit*, which is acid. What it forbids is the low-acid case: "Do not
attempt to can pureed vegetables, red meats, or poultry meats, because
proper processing times for pureed foods have not been determined for home
use." Its alternative is to can those foods by the standard procedure and
puree them at serving time instead.

Note what USDA's reason actually is, because it is simpler than the one
you might reach for. It is not acid uniformity: the foods in that
sentence are low-acid and contain no added acid at all. USDA gives one
reason and it is in the quote, that proper processing times for pureed
foods have not been determined for home use. A dense puree heats
differently from the pack the process was tested on, nobody has measured
it for home equipment, and no tested process means do not do it rather
than do it carefully. That last step is the general rule of this whole
document.

**Now the general tool.** If you meet a preservation method this document
did not list, you do not need somebody to rule on it. Ask which of the
three conditions it removes, and ask whether it removes it reliably or
only usually.

- A method that keeps the food cold enough, dry enough, or acid enough,
  and does so dependably, is working on the mechanism.
- A method that leaves a moist, low-acid food sealed away from air at room
  temperature is building the hazard, whatever it is called and however
  traditional it is. Oil is an air barrier. Wax is an air barrier. A tight
  lid is an air barrier. Vacuum sealing removes oxygen deliberately.
- A method whose safety depends on something *happening* rather than
  something *being*, like fermentation, is only as safe as the thing that
  has to happen.

That test will not give you a process time and it is not a substitute for
a tested procedure. It will reliably tell you when something sounds wrong,
which is most of the value.

## Honey and infants

This is a separate mechanism from everything above and it needs its own
section so that the two do not blur together.

In ordinary foodborne botulism, which is what the rest of this document is
about, the bacteria grow in the food and you swallow toxin that was
already made. **Infant botulism is different: the infant swallows spores,
and the spores grow inside the intestine and make the toxin there.** CDC
describes it as infants getting "botulism when the spores get into their
intestines, grow, and produce the toxin." The food does not have to be
spoiled, contaminated in any visible way, or badly handled. It only has to
contain spores, which honey sometimes does.

The published threshold is an age, and it is 12 months. CDC: **"Do not feed
honey to a child who is younger than 1 year old."** CDC's infant nutrition
guidance states the reason and closes the obvious loopholes: "Honey given
to children younger than 12 months may cause a severe food poisoning called
botulism," and "Do not add honey to your baby's food, water, infant
formula, or pacifier."

NIH's MedlinePlus encyclopedia states the same rule with the same force and
extends it in two ways worth carrying: it applies the under-one-year rule
to corn syrup as well as honey, and it is explicit that even a small taste
on a pacifier counts. (That page is licensed medical content rather than a
federal work, so its facts are restated here rather than quoted. See
Sources.)

Three things that are commonly assumed and that I will not tell you,
because the pages I opened do not support them. **Cooking or baking the
honey is not addressed** on those pages as a way to make it safe, so do not
treat heat as a solution. **Pasteurised honey is not distinguished from
raw** on CDC's page; the guidance is written about honey, without that
carve-out, so applying it to all honey is the reading the source supports.
And while it is clear that this affects infants and not older children or
adults, **the reason is not settled**, and CDC says so itself rather than
leaving it to be inferred. Its sentence on infant botulism opens: "For
reasons we do not understand, some infants get botulism when the spores get
into their intestines, grow, and produce the toxin." That is a federal
health agency stating plainly that the mechanism is not understood. Treat
any confident explanation you meet elsewhere with that in mind, and take
the rule regardless: the rule is well established even though the
explanation behind it is not.

Worth knowing: the age threshold is not the only route. CDC lists **adult
intestinal toxemia**, which "can happen if the spores of the bacteria get
into an adult's intestines, grow, and produce the toxin." It is rare. It is
mentioned here only so that "infants get it from spores, adults cannot" is
not filed away as a law of nature.

## What the outbreak record actually shows

CDC publishes an annual National Botulism Surveillance Summary. Reading
three consecutive years is more informative than any general claim about
how dangerous canning is, and it corrects two opposite errors at once.

Here is what CDC reported, quoted with its own framing:

- **2021:** "health departments reported 273 cases of botulism to CDC. Of
  those, 243 cases were laboratory-confirmed and 30 were probable. The
  cases were of the following types: 181 (66%) infant, 66 (24%) wound, 22
  (8%) foodborne, and 4 (1%) other."
- **2020:** "health departments reported 226 cases of botulism to CDC. Of
  those, 202 cases were laboratory-confirmed and 24 were probable. The
  cases were of the following types: 159 (70%) infant, 55 (24%) wound, 9
  (4%) foodborne, and 3 (1%) other."
- **2019:** "health departments reported 215 cases of botulism to CDC.
  Among those cases, 201 were laboratory confirmed and 14 were probable."
  The types were "152 (71%) infant," "41 (19%) wound," "21 (10%)
  foodborne," and "1 (<1%) other."

**The scope of those figures matters more than the figures.** These are
cases reported to CDC by health departments in the United States, for one
calendar year each, counting all routes of botulism and not only food.
Each year's counts and percentages are given as shares of the **total
reported** cases, confirmed and probable together, which is why the
laboratory-confirmed subtotal is always smaller. If you see a foodborne
figure quoted somewhere as 18 for 2021 rather than 22, or 19 for 2019
rather than 21, that is the laboratory-confirmed count rather than the
reported count, and the two are easy to mix up because the same page
carries both. Quote whichever you like, but say which one you are quoting.

The four type counts sum to the reported total in each year (181 + 66 + 22
+ 4 = 273; 159 + 55 + 9 + 3 = 226; 152 + 41 + 21 + 1 = 215). Note that this
sum re-adds numbers printed in the same sentence, so it cannot fail and
proves very little; the check worth doing is against the per-category
tables, and there 2021 and 2019 reconcile and **2020 does not**.

CDC's 2020 page gives the confirmed and probable split three incompatible
ways. Its narrative says 202 laboratory-confirmed and 24 probable. Its
Table 1 rows give 159 + 32 + 9 + 3 = 203 confirmed and 23 probable. Its
Table 1 header reads 227 confirmed and 23 probable, which matches neither.
The 226 total is stable across all three. The body sections agree with the
rows. I have not resolved which is right, and I am flagging it rather than
picking one, because a surveillance summary is exactly the kind of document
people quote without opening.

The percentages behave differently between years, and it is worth saying so
rather than waving at rounding. For 2021 and 2020 the four rounded
percentages sum to 99 (66 + 24 + 8 + 1, and 70 + 24 + 4 + 1). For 2019 the
three stated percentages already sum to 100 on their own (71 + 19 + 10),
because the fourth category is given as "<1%" rather than as a rounded 1
percent. Both are ordinary rounding behaviour and neither is an error, but
they are not the same pattern, and a single sentence claiming they always
sum to 99 would have been wrong for one year in three.

Across those three years, **52 foodborne cases were reported out of 714
botulism cases in total**, which is about 7 percent. Infant botulism is the
large majority at 492 cases, about 69 percent.

**Now the part that matters for this document: which foods.** Not
speculation, the actual named items.

In 2021, the foodborne outbreaks were traced to seal oil, fermented beluga
whale, an unknown source, and **home-canned potatoes and sausages**. The
single cases included **home-canned beans**, **home-canned meat**,
**home-canned potatoes**, improperly stored clam chowder, and a
commercially canned soup.

In 2020, the named items were **home-canned asparagus**, **home-canned
green chili peppers with onions**, **home-canned peaches**, an unspecified
home-canned food, and an expired canned food.

In 2019, they included **home-canned green beans**, **homemade pickled
eggs**, **home-canned mushrooms and apples**, **home-canned salmon**, an
unidentified home-canned food, an unidentified homemade food, and
**fermented beluga flipper**. That year also had two commercial outbreaks,
from commercially pre-packaged roasted potatoes and from commercially
prepared preserved peppers. And it had one case that belongs to none of
those categories: Maryland, June 2019, "Inadequately refrigerated macaroni
& cheese and green beans from a fast-food restaurant."

Look at that list against the pH rule. Potatoes, asparagus, green beans,
mushrooms, peppers with onions, meat, sausages, salmon. **Low-acid foods,
every one.** The outbreak record is not a random scatter across the whole
of home preserving. It lands, year after year, mostly where the theory says
it will: low-acid vegetables and meats in jars.

Several items on those lists are not that, and they are worth keeping in
view rather than trimming away, because each teaches something the tidy
version would hide.

**Peaches, pickled eggs, and mushrooms with apples** all sit at least
partly on the acid side of the line, and all appear in the record anyway.
Peaches are fruit; pickled eggs are pickled precisely to acidify them; the
mushrooms were canned with apples. So being in the easy category, or
intending to be, is not the same as being exempt. Acidity protects you only
when the finished product really is below the line, uniformly, and the rest
of the process was done properly. A jar whose acid was assumed rather than
measured into it by a tested recipe is not an acid food just because its
ingredients sound like one.

**The 2019 macaroni and cheese, and the clam chowder in 2021**, were never
canned at all. Both were low-acid foods held at the wrong temperature,
one from a restaurant and one simply stored wrong; CDC does not say where. They are a reminder
that the three conditions do not care whether a jar was involved. Canning
is the activity that most reliably assembles all three on purpose, which is
why this document is about canning, but "I do not can" is not the same as
"this cannot happen to my food."

**Two conclusions, and they point in opposite directions. Hold both.**

Botulism is genuinely rare. Foodborne cases reported nationally ran to 22,
9 and 21 in those three years, in a country of hundreds of millions and
against a very large number of jars canned every autumn. That is a small
number, and it is worth stating it as the actual figure rather than as a
vague "dozens," because the vague version drifts upward every time it is
repeated. You should not be frightened of a jar of jam,
and you should not take from this document that home preserving is
reckless. It is not.

And the rarity is not reassurance about your method, because the cases that
do occur land mostly on home-preserved food. Across those three years, 25
of the 46 confirmed foodborne cases had a home-preserved vehicle, against
9 commercial, 2 that were never canned at all, and 10 whose source was
never identified. Mostly, not overwhelmingly, and the commercial column is
not empty. Rare events
concentrated in one specific practice tell you that the practice is the
risk factor. The correct reading is not "this almost never happens so I can
relax," it is "this almost never happens, and when it does, it happens to
people doing the exact thing the rules tell you not to do."

## Symptoms, and what to do

If you ever need this section, you need it quickly, so it is short and
specific.

**When.** FDA's Bad Bug Book gives both a typical window and an outer
range: "Onset: Adult: Usually 18 to 36 hours after ingesting food
containing the toxin, although times have varied from 4 hours to 8 days."
NIH's MedlinePlus encyclopedia gives a narrower typical window, of roughly
8 to 36 hours, which is consistent with the same picture; the numbers below
follow FDA because it states the outer bound as well.

Take the wider figure seriously, because it is the one that prevents a
specific mistake. **Feeling fine the next morning is not the all-clear.**
Onset has been recorded as late as 8 days, so a person who ate from a
suspect jar three days ago and has no symptoms has not proved anything,
and should still be told to watch for the pattern below. Equally, the
typical window is long enough that the meal will not be an obvious suspect
by the time symptoms start, and long enough that somebody may have eaten
from the same jar more than once before the first one made them ill. If a
jar is implicated, think about everyone who ate from it, not just the
person who is currently unwell.

**What.** CDC's list: "Difficulty swallowing, Muscle weakness, Double
vision, Drooping eyelids, Blurry vision, Slurred speech, Difficulty
breathing, Difficulty moving the eyes." With foodborne botulism CDC also
lists "Vomiting, Nausea, Stomach pain, Diarrhea."

**The pattern to recognise.** The distinctive thing is where it starts and
how it spreads. It starts in the head, with the eyes, the eyelids, speech
and swallowing, and moves downward into the body. FDA's Bad Bug Book
describes the result as "flaccid paralysis of muscles, including those of
the respiratory tract," and MedlinePlus describes the weakness as affecting
both sides of the body equally. So: symmetrical, both sides together,
starting
with the face and eyes, descending. A person with double vision and
drooping eyelids and slurred speech who is not drunk, not feverish and
still mentally clear is the picture to hold in mind. Do not wait for the
whole set, though: CDC says plainly that people with botulism might not
have all of these signs at the same time. Any one of them, in a person who
is not drunk, not feverish and still mentally clear, is enough to act on.

In an infant, the presentation is different and quieter, which is why it
gets missed. CDC lists "Constipation, Poor feeding, Drooping eyelids,
Pupils that are slow to react to light, Face showing less expression than
usual, Weak cry that sounds different than usual." A floppy, poorly feeding
baby with a weak cry is an emergency even though nothing about it looks
dramatic.

**What to do.** This is not a wait-and-see illness, and the reason is the
treatment. The treatment is an antitoxin, and an antitoxin can only
neutralise toxin that has not yet bound to the nerve endings. Every hour of
delay is more toxin bound and more paralysis that the drug can no longer
prevent. That is why the Bad Bug Book ties the outcome directly to the
delay: "The mortality rate is high if treatment is not immediately
administered. The disease is generally fatal in 5% to 10% of cases."
MedlinePlus makes the same point in plainer terms, that prompt treatment
greatly reduces the risk of death.

Read those two sentences together rather than separately, and note what the
source does not say. It gives one overall figure, 5 to 10 percent, without
splitting it into treated and untreated cases, so do not read the 5 to 10
percent as "the odds if you get to hospital" or as "the odds if you stay
home"; the page I took it from does not break it down that way. What it
does say is that mortality is high specifically when treatment is not
immediate, which is the half of the statement that should govern what you
do in the next hour. And a non-fatal case is not a mild one: the Bad Bug
Book notes that with antitoxin, other treatment "and the help of a
breathing machine, the paralysis usually goes away within weeks or, in
severe cases, months."

So the instruction is the emergency one. MedlinePlus tells you to go to the
emergency room or call 911, or your local emergency number, if you suspect
botulism.
CDC: "If you or your child has signs and symptoms of botulism, immediately
see your doctor or go to the emergency room. Do not wait."

Do not phone a friend who cans. Do not wait until morning to see whether it
settles. Do not let the fact that the person can still walk about reassure
you: the paralysis descends from the eyes and face to the throat, then the
chest, then the limbs. The chest comes BEFORE the limbs, so someone still
on their feet can already have the muscles of breathing involved. Do not
read steady breathing or the ability to walk as evidence that this is early.
Note that speech and swallowing are not a late sign but an early one, which
is why slurring in a person who has not been drinking belongs on the list
of things that get a phone call rather than a night's sleep. If you suspect
it, call emergency services, say the
word botulism, and say what was eaten and when. If the suspect jar still
exists, keep it, sealed and out of reach of everyone including pets, since
it may be needed to identify the toxin type. Do not clean it up and do not
throw it away.

## The data layer behind this topic

Inside the simulation, this hazard is backed by a record in
`data/chemistry/toxins.csv` with the id **`botulinum_toxin`**. Reading it
against the sources is a good illustration of what a data layer is and is
not.

The record classifies the toxin as biological, names its source as
*Clostridium botulinum*, gives its antidote as heptavalent antitoxin serum,
lists its effects as muscle paralysis, blurred vision, difficulty
swallowing and respiratory failure, and describes it as the most toxic
substance known, acting by blocking acetylcholine release at the
neuromuscular junction.

Two things to say plainly about that record, in the spirit of the rest of
this document.

The symptom list and the antitoxin match the federal sources well; CDC's
symptom list and the heptavalent antitoxin are both real and consistent
with what is written above. CDC describes botulinum toxin as "one of the
most lethal toxins known," which is slightly more measured than the
record's "most toxic substance known," and CDC's phrasing is the one to use
in anything you write.

The record's `onset_time_minutes` field holds 720, which is 12 hours. That
is a single number where the published figure is a range, and it sits
outside the part of the range that matters most. FDA gives the usual window
as 18 to 36 hours, with recorded times from 4 hours to 8 days. Twelve hours
is inside the outer range but below the usual one, so a simulation that
fires symptoms at exactly 12 hours every time is modelling an early case as
if it were the typical case, and it cannot represent the late-onset
possibility at all. If you are reasoning about real food, use the published
range and not the record's single value.

The record's lethal-dose field holds 0.000001 mg per kg, which is 1
nanogram per kilogram of body weight. I could not find a federal page
stating a dose in those units, so I cannot confirm the figure as written.
What I can say is that the order of magnitude is not invented: the Bad Bug
Book gives the infective dose as "An extremely small amount, a few
nanograms, of the toxin can cause illness." Nanograms is the right scale.
Whether the specific per-kilogram value is right, and what route of
exposure it was derived for, I could not verify, so treat that field as a
simulation parameter rather than as a sourced medical fact.

## Where the real instructions live

Everything above is reasoning. None of it is a process. When you want to
actually preserve food:

**The USDA Complete Guide to Home Canning** is the authority for every
tested process time, pressure, jar size and altitude adjustment. It is a US
government work, it is free, and it is a download rather than a paywall.
That single document is what all the trustworthy advice you will encounter
is ultimately quoting. It is `usda-canning-guide` in this project's source
registry, and the link is in Sources below.

Alongside it, **the National Center for Home Food Preservation** hosts the
guide and publishes current procedures and updates. Your **local extension
office** is the other good route, and has the advantage of being able to
test your pressure canner's dial gauge, which is a real annual maintenance
task that nothing in a book can do for you.

Inside this Library, [Keeping What You Grew](keeping_what_you_grew.md) is
the practical guide: four methods, their trade-offs, and one complete
tested canning procedure written out so you can see the shape of one.

Three rules to carry out of here, which between them prevent almost every
way this goes wrong:

1. **Know whether the food is above or below pH 4.6**, and if it is above,
   it needs a pressure canner. All fresh vegetables EXCEPT MOST TOMATOES
   are above, and so are all meats, poultry and seafood. Tomatoes are the
   special case this guide spends a section on: most sit below the line,
   some do not, which is why the tested recipes make you acidify rather
   than guess.
2. **Use a tested process, unaltered**, including the altitude row, the
   jar size, the headspace, and the acid where acid is called for. Do not
   scale, halve, double or substitute.
3. **Never use your senses as the safety check.** They catch obvious
   failures and miss the one that matters.

The rules are not there because somebody wanted the hobby to be
bureaucratic. They are there because this organism produces no warning, the
container you preserve in is the container it prefers, and the only moment
you have any control over the outcome is the moment you are processing the
jar. Get that moment right and home canning is a safe, ordinary, immensely
useful skill that will feed you in February from a garden that stopped
producing in September.

Supplied, as restated facts: a typical onset window of roughly 8 to 36

### What I could not source

- **Why infants specifically are susceptible** to intestinal colonisation
  when older children and adults generally are not. This turned out not to
  be a gap in my reading: CDC states outright that the reason is not
  understood ("For reasons we do not understand"). The document now says
  that the cause is unsettled and attributes that to CDC, rather than
  hedging about what I personally could find. It is a genuinely open
  question, not a missing citation.
- **Whether cooking or baking honey makes it safe for an infant**, and
  **whether pasteurised honey is treated differently from raw.** Neither
  distinction appears on the CDC pages I opened. The document declines to
  infer either way and applies the guidance as written.
- **The lethal dose figure** in the `botulinum_toxin` record, as a
  per-kilogram value. FDA's Bad Bug Book confirms the scale ("a few
  nanograms") but states an infective dose in absolute terms, not per
  kilogram of body weight, and does not name a route of exposure for it.
  The document reports what is corroborated, says what is not, and flags
  the field as a simulation parameter.
- **The exact boiling point of water at sea level (212 degrees F)** does
  not appear in the extracted text of USDA Guide 1. It is ordinary physics
  and is not controversial, but since it could not be quoted from a source
  opened this session, the document describes the ceiling qualitatively
  instead of asserting the number.
