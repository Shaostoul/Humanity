# When Heat Becomes Dangerous

Heat does not look dangerous. There is no wind tearing at the roof, no
water rising, nothing falling. A hot week is just uncomfortable, until
for one person in one room it is not. FEMA's Ready.gov puts the scale of
it in one line: "Extreme heat is responsible for the highest number of
annual deaths among all weather-related hazards." The CDC counted 2,391
heat-related deaths in the United States in 2024, with the largest share,
21.1 percent, among people aged 65 to 74.

The skill this guide teaches is recognising when heat has stopped being
uncomfortable and started being an emergency, and knowing what to do on
each side of that line: how to keep a household cool and watered through
a hot spell, how to spot heat illness early, and what to do in the
minutes after someone stops making sense in the heat.

It matters most where heat is rare. The place this simulation is set is
mild: at the Bremerton weather station, the nearest long temperature
record to Silverdale, the normal June high is about 21 C (70 F). In late
June 2021 that station read 100, 102 and 101 F on three days running, and
the CDC counted 171 heat-related deaths in Washington that year, the second
highest number of any state. A place that is rarely hot is a place where
bodies are not used to heat, and that, as the sections below explain, is
one of the things that makes heat dangerous (our reading of NIOSH's advice on
getting used to heat and of the way HeatRisk weighs early-season heat).

The advice here comes from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
(CDC), its National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH),
the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the National
Weather Service and FEMA's Ready.gov, all United States government works in
the public domain, and from NOAA's daily weather records for the worked
example. Washington's Department of Health is cited for its count of the
2021 deaths, restated in our own words. Where something is general
practice, arithmetic or our own reading, the text says so.

Several neighbours carry parts of this subject. [Heat and How It
Moves](heat_and_how_it_moves.md) explains how a body sheds heat and why a
humid day is harder than a dry one. [How Your Body

## If someone is ill in the heat, right now

1. **Confused, slurring, collapsed or having a seizure? Call 911.** OSHA:
   confusion, slurred speech or unconsciousness are signs of heat stroke,
   and when they are present you call 911 immediately. The CDC's sheet
   says it plainly: "heat stroke is a medical emergency". If they do not
   respond and are not breathing normally, their heart may have stopped:
   the CDC's advice for a cardiac arrest is to call 911, get an AED and
   give CPR until medical help arrives. So start CPR and send for an AED
   first ([First Aid Until Help Arrives](first_aid_until_help_arrives.md)),
   and let anyone else there cool them while you push.
2. **Cool them, starting now, before help arrives.** Get them into shade or
   air conditioning, take off outer layers, and cool them hard: in cold
   water or an ice bath if you can, otherwise soak their skin and clothes
   with cool water and move air over them, with ice or cold wet cloths on
   the head, neck, armpits and groin (OSHA and NIOSH).
3. **Nothing to drink for someone confused or not fully awake.** The CDC
   says not to give a person with heat stroke anything to drink.
4. **Stay with them.** OSHA: "Never leave a worker with heat-related
   illness alone."
5. **Awake, sweating heavily, weak or dizzy, but thinking clearly?** That
   is heat exhaustion. Move them somewhere cool, loosen their clothes, put
   cool wet cloths on them or have them take a cool bath, and give sips of
   water. The CDC says to get medical help right away if they are throwing
   up, if the symptoms get worse, or if they last longer than an hour.

If you cannot tell which it is, do not try. OSHA: "Do not try to diagnose
which illness is occurring." And: "When in doubt, cool the worker and call
911." OSHA writes for workplaces; a body in a kitchen is the same body
(our reading).

Everything below is the reasoning behind those five steps, and how to stop
it getting that far.

## What heat does to a body

A body makes heat all the time and has to lose it. In hot weather it does
that mainly by sending more blood to the skin and by sweating, so that the
sweat evaporates and carries heat away; [Heat and How It
Moves](heat_and_how_it_moves.md) explains the physics, and why humid air,
which slows evaporation, cools a person less. The National Weather Service
describes what goes wrong: when the body heats too fast to cool itself, or
loses too much fluid or salt through sweating, its temperature rises.

NIOSH describes the end of that road. Heat stroke is the most serious
heat-related illness: the body can no longer control its temperature, its
temperature rises fast and it cannot cool down. NIOSH says the body
temperature can climb to 106 F (41 C) or higher within 10 to 15 minutes,
and that heat stroke can cause permanent disability or death without
emergency treatment. The National Weather Service: "Delay can be fatal."

## The ladder of heat illness

Heat illness can climb from one rung to the next, which is why catching
it on a low rung matters so much; but it can also climb fast, as NIOSH's
10 to 15 minutes above shows (our reading of the National Weather
Service's warning, below, that heat cramps may lead to heat exhaustion or
heat stroke). These come from the CDC's heat sheet, NIOSH and the
National Weather Service, lowest first.

**Heat rash.** Red clusters of small blisters that look like pimples,
usually on the neck, chest, groin or in the elbow creases. The CDC: stay
somewhere cool and dry, keep the rash dry, and use a powder such as baby
powder to soothe it. NIOSH adds: no ointments or creams.

**Sunburn.** Painful, red, warm skin, perhaps with blisters. The CDC: stay
out of the sun until it heals, cool it with cool cloths or a cool bath,
use a moisturising lotion, and do not break the blisters. Full sun also
adds to the heat a body has to lose: the Weather Service says it can raise
the heat index by up to 15 F (next section).

**Heat cramps.** Heavy sweating during hard exercise, then painful muscle
cramps or spasms. The National Weather Service says heat cramps may be the
first sign of heat illness and can lead on to heat exhaustion or heat
stroke. The CDC's steps: stop, move somewhere cool, drink water or a sports
drink, and wait for the cramps to pass before doing anything strenuous
again. The Weather Service adds firm pressure on the cramping muscle or
gentle massage, and sips of water unless the person feels sick. Get
medical help right away, the CDC says, if the cramps last longer than an
hour, or if the person is on a low-sodium diet or has heart problems.

**Fainting or dizziness (heat syncope).** NIOSH: fainting or dizziness,
usually from standing too long or standing up suddenly, made more likely by
dehydration and by not being used to the heat. Sit or lie down somewhere
cool and slowly drink water, clear juice or a sports drink. The general
first aid for fainting is in [First Aid Until Help
Arrives](first_aid_until_help_arrives.md).

**Heat exhaustion.** The CDC's signs: heavy sweating; cold, pale and
clammy skin; a fast, weak pulse; nausea or vomiting; muscle cramps;
tiredness or weakness; dizziness; headache; and fainting. OSHA adds
irritability and thirst. What to do is step 5 above. NIOSH's advice for
workplaces goes further and says to take a worker with heat exhaustion to a
clinic or emergency room to be checked, and to call 911 if medical care is
not available.

**Heat stroke.** The CDC's signs: a body temperature of 103 F (39.4 C) or
higher; hot, red, dry or damp skin; a fast, strong pulse; headache,
dizziness, nausea and confusion; and losing consciousness. OSHA's list
adds slurred speech and seizures. What to do is steps 1 to 4 above, and the
next two sections.

**Muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis).** NIOSH warns of this after heavy
work in the heat: muscle cramps or pain, abnormally dark urine, the colour
of tea or cola, weakness, and an inability to keep exercising, though it
can also show no symptoms. Stop the activity, drink more (water is
best), get medical care at once, and ask to be checked for it.

## The sign that changes everything is the brain

The line between heat exhaustion and heat stroke is not the temperature on
a thermometer you probably do not have. It is the person's mind. OSHA:
confusion, slurred speech or unconsciousness mean heat stroke. Odd
behaviour, irritability that is not like them, not making sense, stumbling:
treat any change in how someone thinks or acts in the heat as the top rung
(our reading of OSHA's lists and its warning that, during physical work in
a warm place, any unusual symptom can be a sign of overheating).

**Sweating does not rule it out.** Among the signs of heat stroke, the
CDC's sheet lists "Hot, red, dry, or damp skin". NIOSH lists either hot,
dry skin or profuse sweating, and OSHA heavy sweating or hot, dry skin. Some public sheets
describe heat stroke as red, hot, dry skin with no sweat, and the belief
that a sweating person cannot have heat stroke is how a person who is still
sweating gets left to sit it out (our reading). A confused person in the
heat is a 911 call, wet skin or dry.

## Cooling someone down

OSHA's first aid for heat illness, for any of the rungs:

- **Get them somewhere cooler,** such as shade or air conditioning.
- **Cool them immediately.** Put them in cold water or an ice bath if you
  can: OSHA says "This is the best method to cool workers rapidly in an
  emergency." Make the bath by tipping all the ice you have into a large
  container of water. Anything that holds water and a person will do: a
  bath, a stock tank, a paddling pool (general practice). Someone holds
  the person's head and shoulders above the water the whole time, because
  a confused person can slip under; and a person who is unconscious or
  having a seizure is cooled where they lie, with the steps in the next
  bullet, rather than lifted into water (general practice).
- **Otherwise:** take off outer layers of clothing, put ice or cold wet
  towels on the head, neck, trunk, armpits and groin, and use fans to move
  air around them. NIOSH's list is the same, with soaking the clothes and
  wetting the skin.
- **Stay with them,** and keep cooling until help arrives.

**Fans need wet skin.** The National Weather Service warns that when the
heat index is above the high 90s Fahrenheit, a fan blowing on someone can
make them hotter. NIOSH's cooling steps use moving air on skin that has
been wetted, which is a different thing: the air speeds the evaporation of
the water (our reading of the two together, and of the physics in [Heat and
How It Moves](heat_and_how_it_moves.md)). So wet them first, then fan.

**Drinks.** Someone with heat exhaustion who is fully awake can sip water
(the CDC). Someone with heat stroke gets nothing to drink (the CDC), and
nobody who is confused or not fully awake should be given a drink, because
they can choke ([Heat and How It Moves](heat_and_how_it_moves.md), as
general first aid practice).

## Reading the forecast

**The heat index.** The National Weather Service defines it as how hot it
really feels when the relative humidity is added to the air temperature.
Its own example: 96 F air at 65 percent humidity has a heat index of 121 F.
Two things about it are worth knowing. The published values are for shade
and a light wind, and the Weather Service says full sunshine can add up to
15 F. And humidity moves it a long way. Worked through the Weather
Service's own heat index formula (our arithmetic), a 90 F afternoon reads
about 95 F at 50 percent humidity and about 106 F at 70 percent.

The Weather Service sorts heat index values into four bands:

| Heat index | Band | What it says about the body |
|---|---|---|
| 80 to 90 F | Caution | fatigue possible with long exposure or activity |
| 90 to 103 F | Extreme Caution | heat stroke, heat cramps or heat exhaustion possible with long exposure or activity |
| 103 to 124 F | Danger | heat cramps or heat exhaustion likely, heat stroke possible with long exposure or activity |
| 125 F or more | Extreme Danger | heat stroke highly likely |

On that scale, the 90 F afternoon above is Extreme Caution at 50 percent
humidity and Danger at 70 (our reading of the table).

**HeatRisk.** The National Weather Service and the CDC built a seven-day
heat forecast, HeatRisk, that rates each day from 0 to 4: green, little or
no risk; yellow, minor, affecting mainly people who are extremely sensitive
to heat; orange, moderate; red, major, affecting anyone without cooling or
water, and health systems; and magenta, extreme, rare or long heat with no
relief at night. It weighs how far above normal the heat is for that
place, how early in the season it comes (before people are used to it),
how long it lasts, and whether nights stay warm. The Weather Service still
calls it experimental and says it supplements, and does not replace, its
official alerts. You can look up your own area by zip code on the CDC's
HeatRisk dashboard.

**The alerts.** The National Weather Service issues three kinds. A Heat
Advisory is for dangerous heat that will not reach warning level. An
Extreme Heat Watch means conditions are favourable for extreme heat but the
timing is uncertain; it says to plan to suspend major outdoor activities
if a warning comes, and, if you have no air conditioning, to find the
nearest cooling shelter or arrange to stay with someone who has it. An
Extreme Heat Warning means extremely dangerous heat is expected or
happening: avoid outdoor activities, especially in the heat of the day,
stay in air conditioning as much as possible, including overnight, and
check on family and neighbours. FEMA's Ready.gov describes extreme heat as
a period of high heat and humidity above 90 F lasting at least two to three
days.

For people working or training outdoors, the Weather Service also
forecasts the wet bulb globe temperature, which adds wind and sunshine to
temperature and humidity. It calls it a particularly good measure of heat
stress for outdoor workers and athletes, and notes that the American
College of Sports Medicine bases its guidelines for sports practice on it.

## Who is at greatest risk

The CDC's list of people who may need to do more on hot days: people with
asthma, especially children; people with heart disease; pregnant women;
people aged 65 or older; people working or exercising outside in the heat;
and infants and young children. Its guidance for clinicians adds people
with disabilities, with mental health conditions, with chronic illness or
substance use disorders, people without good housing or without access to
cooling, people who are socially isolated, and people who breathe polluted
air. It notes that people in the countryside face heat too, with less
access to cooling and farther to travel for care.

Why some bodies struggle, in the CDC's words and the Weather Service's:

- **Older adults** do not adjust as well as young people to sudden changes
  in temperature, are more likely to have a condition that changes the
  body's response to heat, and are more likely to take medicines that
  affect temperature control or sweating (the CDC).
- **Babies and young children** rely on others to keep them cool and give
  them drinks (the CDC), and their bodies are less able to adapt to heat
  (the National Weather Service).
- **People with chronic conditions** may be less able to sense and respond
  to a change in temperature, and may take medicines that make heat worse;
  heart disease, mental illness, poor circulation and obesity are risk
  factors in themselves (the CDC).

**Medicines.** The CDC's guidance for clinicians says commonly prescribed
medicines that raise the risk from heat include diuretics (water pills),
anticholinergic drugs (among them some allergy and sleep medicines, such
as diphenhydramine) and some psychiatric medicines, that some blood
pressure medicines taken together with a diuretic may raise it
significantly, and that over-the-counter medicines can play a part too.
Medicines can blunt thirst, interfere with sweating or temperature
control, lower blood pressure, or build up to harmful levels in someone
who is dehydrated. Heat also harms some medicines and devices: insulin
belongs in a refrigerator, adrenaline auto-injectors may deliver less when
they get hot, and inhalers can burst in a hot car. The CDC's advice to
patients: do not stop or change a medicine without talking to your doctor,
and have a plan for refrigerated medicines and electrical medical devices
if the power goes out.

## Keeping a household cool

From the CDC and FEMA's Ready.gov, with the source named for each:

- **Air conditioning is the thing that works.** If your home has none, the
  CDC says to find somewhere that does: dial 2-1-1, or contact your local
  health department or emergency management office, and spend time in a
  shopping mall or public library, because "even a few hours spent in air
  conditioning can help".
- **Fans have a limit.** The CDC says to use a fan only when the indoor
  temperature is below 90 F, because above that a fan can raise body
  temperature, and its page for older adults says: "Do not rely on a fan as
  your main cooling source when it's really hot outside." Ready.gov:
  "Fans create air flow and a false sense of comfort, but do not reduce body
  temperature or prevent heat-related illnesses."
- **Cool the body directly.** Cool showers or baths (the CDC, Ready.gov).
- **Keep the heat out of the house.** Cover windows with drapes or shades,
  use the oven less, and over the longer run weather-strip doors and
  windows and add insulation (Ready.gov); the CDC adds using the stove
  less too.
- **Dress for it.** Loose, lightweight, light-coloured clothing and a
  wide-brimmed hat outside, and sunscreen reapplied as the package says
  (the CDC, Ready.gov).
- **Move the work.** Do outdoor jobs and exercise in the coolest part of
  the day, start slowly and build up, and if you feel faint or weak, stop
  everything and get somewhere cool (the CDC).
- **Check on people.** The CDC asks caretakers to visit an older or
  chronically ill person at least twice a day and ask four questions: are
  they drinking enough water, do they have air conditioning, do they know
  how to keep cool, and do they show signs of heat stress? Use a buddy
  system, and have someone check on you.
- **Pets.** Ready.gov: plenty of cool water and comfortable shade, and
  remember that asphalt and dark pavement can burn a pet's feet.

**Never leave anyone in a parked car.** The CDC says the temperature inside
a car can rise almost 20 F within the first 10 minutes, "even with a window
cracked open". The National Weather Service says it is never safe to leave
a child, a disabled person or a pet locked in a car, that children can die
in 10 minutes, and that 31 children were reported to have died in hot cars
in 2025. The CDC's trick for not forgetting a sleeping child: keep a stuffed
animal in the car seat, and move it to the front seat beside you whenever
the child is buckled in. [Heat and How It Moves](heat_and_how_it_moves.md)
has the Weather Service's advice to lock your cars if you have a toddler.

**When the power goes out in the heat,** the dangers of an outage and of
the heat arrive together. The rules for generators, refrigerated food and
carbon monoxide are in [The Hazards Your Place Actually
Has](the_hazards_where_you_live.md); the CDC's rule is never to run a
generator inside a home or garage, even with the doors and windows open,
and only outside, more than 20 feet from windows, doors and vents. Ready.gov
suggests that people who use powered medical equipment ask their power
company to put them on its list for priority restoration.

## Drinking enough, and not too much

- **Drink before you are thirsty.** The CDC: "Drink more water than usual
  and don't wait until you're thirsty to drink."
- **Check the colour.** The CDC: light yellow or clear urine usually means
  you are drinking enough.
- **Go easy on some drinks.** The CDC suggests limiting drinks high in
  sugar, sodium or caffeine, and alcohol.
- **If your fluids are limited by a doctor,** the CDC's advice is specific:
  "If your doctor limits the amount of fluids you drink or has you on water
  pills, ask them how much you should drink during hot weather."

**For work in the heat,** NIOSH's guidance for workers: for moderate work
lasting less than two hours, drink a cup (8 ounces) of water every 15 to 20
minutes; if the sweating goes on for several hours, drink sports drinks
with balanced electrolytes; avoid alcohol and drinks high in caffeine or
sugar. And there is a ceiling: "Generally, fluid intake should not exceed 6
cups per hour." OSHA likewise says that for jobs lasting more than two
hours, workers need electrolyte drinks as well as water, because water
cannot replace the salts lost in sweat.

**Salt.** NIOSH says to avoid salt tablets. The CDC's advice for people
with chronic conditions: "Talk to your doctor about how to safely replace
salt and minerals lost through sweating."

## Getting used to the heat

A body adapts to heat over days, among other things by sweating more
efficiently (NIOSH). NIOSH says the adaptation holds through a few days
away from the heat; OSHA says workers can lose it over the winter or during
an extended absence, such as a week or more away, and that almost half of
heat-related deaths at work happen on a worker's first day on the job or
first day back after an extended absence ([Organising Shared
Work](organising_shared_work.md) applies this to a work day). That is why
the first hot days of the year, and the first day of hot work, are the
dangerous ones. NIOSH's schedule for workers:

- build up time in the heat gradually over 7 to 14 days;
- someone new to the heat does no more than 20 percent of a usual day's
  heat exposure on the first day, and adds no more than 20 percent a day
  after that;
- someone who has worked in heat before does no more than 50 percent on
  day one, 60 percent on day two, 80 percent on day three and the full
  amount on day four;
- people who are not physically fit need longer;
- breaks in air conditioning do not undo it.

[Organising Shared Work](organising_shared_work.md) applies OSHA's version
of the same rule to a group work day, where the danger is a volunteer who
turns up for one hot day and does not want to look weak.

## Worked example: Silverdale, June 2021

What happened, from the records:

- **The heat.** The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration's daily
  record for the Bremerton station shows 37.8, 38.9 and 38.3 C (100, 102 and
  101 F) on 27, 28 and 29 June 2021. The middle reading is the highest in
  that station's record, which runs, with gaps, back to 1899 (our reading
  of the daily file). The normal June high there is about 21 C (70 F)
  ([The Climate Where You Live](the_climate_where_you_live.md) walks
  through these numbers).
- **The warnings.** The CDC reported that from 25 to 30 June 2021 most of
  Oregon and Washington were under a National Weather Service excessive
  heat warning. That is the old name: the Weather Service renamed its
  Excessive Heat Warning the Extreme Heat Warning on 4 March 2025.
- **The harm.** In the CDC's figures for Alaska, Idaho, Oregon and
  Washington, emergency departments saw 1,038 visits for heat-related
  illness on 28 June 2021 against 9 on the same date in 2019, and the
  groups most affected were men and people aged 75 or older. The CDC
  counted 171 heat-related deaths in Washington in 2021. The state's
  Department of Health, counting the deaths it tied to the heat between
  26 June and 31 August 2021, reported 157, 100 of them in the week of the
  heat wave itself, 26 June to 2 July (restated). The two counts cover
  different spans of time: the CDC's the whole of 2021, the state's 26 June
  to 31 August.

Now a made-up household in a heat wave like that one, today, to show the
steps.

**Monday, the forecast.** An Extreme Heat Watch is out, and HeatRisk for
the weekend is red going to magenta. You think of the 81-year-old across
the road who lives alone in an upstairs flat with no air conditioning and
takes a water pill. You ask her two things: whether her doctor has told her
how much to drink in hot weather, and whether she would spend the hot
afternoons at the library or with you. She says she will call the
surgery.

**Saturday afternoon.** You visit, as the CDC asks, at least twice a day.
The flat is 31 C (88 F). The fan is running; at that temperature the CDC
says a fan is still worth using, but it is not enough on its own. She has
been drinking, her urine is pale, and she is clear-headed. You drive her
to the library for the afternoon (general practice, following the CDC's
advice that a few hours of air conditioning help) and bring her home in
the cool of the evening, with the blinds drawn and a cool shower before
bed.

**Monday, the third hot day.** Her neighbour rings: she is muddled,
cannot find words, and her skin is hot and damp. That is heat stroke,
sweat or no sweat. You call 911 on speaker, then cool her where she sits,
because the bath is down a staircase she cannot manage: outer clothes off,
cold wet towels on her neck, armpits and groin, the towels re-soaked and
the fan pointed at her wet skin, ice from both freezers in a bag. You give
her nothing to drink. You stay with her and keep cooling until the crew
takes over, and you tell them when you found her and what you have done.

**Meanwhile, next door.** A fifteen-year-old has been mowing at two in
the afternoon. He comes in pale, sweating hard, dizzy and sick, but making
sense. That is heat exhaustion. He lies down in the coolest room, with
clothes loosened, a cool wet cloth on him and sips of water. He does not
vomit, and after forty minutes he is better. Had he been throwing up, got
worse or still been ill after an hour, the CDC says get medical help right
away; had he become confused, it would have been the 911 call above.

## Where your own help stops

- **Anyone confused, slurring, collapsed, fitting or unconscious in the
  heat** is a 911 call: the CDC calls heat stroke a medical emergency.
  Start cooling at once and keep cooling until the crew takes over (OSHA),
  unless they are not breathing normally: then CPR comes first.
- **Heat exhaustion that does not improve within an hour,** or with
  vomiting, or getting worse, needs medical help that day (the CDC).
- **Medicines.** Do not stop or change one because of the heat without a
  doctor (the CDC); ask what to do with fluid limits.
- **Dark urine, muscle pain and weakness after hard work in the heat** need
  a medical check for muscle breakdown (NIOSH).
- **The infant, the frail elderly person, the person with heart disease:**
  their margin is smaller, so call earlier for them, not later (our reading
  of the risk groups above).

## Traps

- **"She's sweating, so it isn't heat stroke."** The CDC's sheet says the
  skin can be dry or damp.
- **"The fan's on, he's fine."** Not above 90 F indoors, and not as the
  main cooling when it is really hot (the CDC).
- **"I'll drink when I'm thirsty."** The CDC says not to wait.
- **"Drink as much as you can."** NIOSH's ceiling is 6 cups an hour.
- **"It's only 90 degrees."** At 70 percent humidity that feels like 106 F,
  in the Danger band.
- **"I'm fit, I'll be fine on the first hot day."** The first days are the
  dangerous ones, for the fit too, until the body has adapted.
- **"Give her a cold drink to bring her round."** Not to anyone confused or
  not fully awake.
- **"I'll just pop into the shop."** Never with a child or a pet in the car.

## How the game models it

The game models your body's heat in real detail and leaves out most of the
rest of this guide.

- **A real heat balance.** Your core temperature comes from a published
  model of human heat regulation: you make heat, more when you walk or
  sprint, and lose it to the air, to your surroundings and by sweating, and
  humid air slows the evaporation of sweat in the model as it does in life
  (`src/systems/body_heat.rs`). In the open, the sun's heat counts and a
  roof you have built shades you. [Heat and How It
  Moves](heat_and_how_it_moves.md) describes the model and the HUD's
  "feels" line.
- **Sweat costs water.** The water you sweat out comes off your Hydration
  on top of the ordinary daily drain, at about 20 points for every litre
  (`src/systems/food.rs`), and the Vitals drain slider (Settings >
  Gameplay) scales it like your other needs.
- **The conditions.** The game names two heat conditions, from the core
  temperature it shows you: Heat Exhaustion above 39 C, which slows you to
  80 percent of your speed, and Heatstroke above 40 C, which halves your
  speed and drains your health (`data/status_effects.csv`,
  `src/systems/body_heat.rs`). Under Realistic, a core of 41 C would take
  your health from full to nothing in an hour, and 42 C in half an hour.
- **The default is gentler.** Settings > Gameplay > Body heat starts on
  Forgiving, which uses the same weather, clothes and shelter but shows
  your temperature moving only half as far from normal, and harms you at
  half the Realistic rate for each degree that shown temperature reads past
  40 C. So under Forgiving, Heat Exhaustion appears only once the model's
  real core passes about 41 C, and Heatstroke, with its harm, only once it
  passes about 43 C; the harm then comes far more slowly than under
  Realistic. A real core of 44 C, which empties a full health bar in about
  15 minutes under Realistic, reads 40.4 C under Forgiving and takes about
  5 hours (`src/systems/body_heat.rs`, and the rule as the setting explains
  it in the game; the conversions and times are our arithmetic). Realistic
  shows the real balance.
- **Body heat runs in every play mode,** including the default Dev mode;
  only the developer flying mode switches the weather off your body.
- **Nothing carries over, by default.** Your health, your vitals, your
  core temperature among them, and the conditions on you are saved with
  your game, so quitting neither cools nor heals you (`src/save_load.rs`).
  But while Settings > Gameplay > "Start every session from the default
  home" is on, which it is by default during development, every session
  starts with a new body, and only your character's name, look and
  clothes carry over.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no heat index or HeatRisk forecast and no heat alert; no weather event in
the game is a heat wave (`data/weather/events.ron`), and the game's
disaster system, which lists one, is not registered, so it never runs
(`src/systems/disasters.rs`); there is no heat rash, cramp, fainting or
sunburn (the Sunburn row in `data/status_effects.csv` is applied by
nothing); there are no medicines that change your response to heat, no
cars that heat up, and nothing you can do for anyone else's heat illness.
In the game, a person in the heat slows down and then loses health. A real
person in the heat can become confused, and confusion is the sign this
whole guide is built around.

## You own this when

- You know the line between heat exhaustion and heat stroke is the
  person's mind, and you call 911 for confusion, slurring, collapse or a
  seizure in the heat, sweating or not.
- You check that someone who has collapsed in the heat is breathing
  normally, and start CPR and send for an AED if they are not.
- You start cooling at once and keep going, with cold water if you can and
  wet skin and moving air if you cannot, and you stay with the person.
- You give sips of water to someone with heat exhaustion and nothing to
  someone who is confused.
- You know heat exhaustion needs medical help if it brings vomiting, gets
  worse or lasts more than an hour.
- You can read a heat index, know that sun can add 15 F to it, and know the
  four bands.
- You know what HeatRisk's colours mean and what an Extreme Heat Watch and
  Warning ask of you.
- You know who in your street is at most risk, and you check on them at
  least twice a day in a heat wave, with the CDC's four questions.
- You know where you would go for air conditioning, and you do not trust a
  fan above 90 F.
- You drink before you are thirsty, keep to NIOSH's ceiling of 6 cups an
  hour at work, and ask a doctor about fluid limits and medicines.
- You build up to hot work over days, not hours.
- You never leave a child, a disabled person or a pet in a parked car.
