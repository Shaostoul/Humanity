# Navigating Without Instruments

Sooner or later the thing that tells you where you are stops telling
you. The phone has no signal, the battery is flat, the fog has come
down, the trail signs were never put up. The US Forest Service lists a
compass you know how to use among the essentials for anyone going
outdoors, and says plainly why the electronics are not enough: GPS
devices sometimes do not receive a signal or the battery fails, and
"Cell phones also likely will not work because of a lack of signal."

You can still know where you are. If you know where you started, which
way you went and how far, you know where you are now. Sailors call this
dead reckoning, and the US government's own navigation manual, *The
American Practical Navigator*, defines it like this: "Dead reckoning (DR) is a method for determining the estimated
position of a vessel by advancing from a known fix of position along the
vessel's ordered course and speed." On land it works the same way, with
paces and minutes instead of knots.

"Without instruments" in this guide means without anything that tells
you your position: no GPS, no phone, no map app. A compass and a watch
help a great deal, and the guide uses them where you have them, but the
method also works with the sun, the stars and your own feet. What it
cannot do is be exact. The same manual says of a dead reckoned position:
"This is an approximate position; it does not allow for the many errors
that can cause a vessel to veer off course such as helmsmen error,
compass error, or current and wind." Most of the skill is knowing how
wrong you might be, and arranging things so that it does not matter.

Several guides sit beside this one. [Knowing Which Way Is
North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md) teaches the compass, the sun and
the stars as ways of finding a direction. [Estimating](estimating.md)
teaches the pace count. [Reading a Map](reading_a_map.md) teaches
matching a map to the ground, and [Coordinates](coordinates.md) teaches
passing a position to someone else. [What to Do When You Are
Lost](what_to_do_when_you_are_lost.md) is for when all of this has
failed. This guide is the part in between: keeping track of a position,
leg by leg, when nothing does it for you.

Its sources are US Army field manuals approved for public release
(*Map Reading and Land Navigation*, FM 3-25.26, 2001; *Foot Marches*,
FM 21-18, 1990), the US Forest Service, the National Park Service and the
National Weather Service, all public domain; the National
Geospatial-Intelligence Agency's *American Practical Navigator* (2024
edition), which claims no domestic copyright but says some of its
sections belong to others, so only its own text is quoted; and one
peer-reviewed study, restated in our own words. Where something is
general practice, our reading or plain arithmetic, the text says so.

## First: how dead reckoning gets people hurt

Dead reckoning is calm arithmetic. The ways it turns into an emergency
are few, and all of them come from trusting the arithmetic more than the
ground.

- **A line on paper does not see the ground.** A compass bearing runs
  straight through cliffs, rivers, bogs and thickets. The Army's map
  reading manual warns: "It is dangerous to select a single steering
  mark, such as a distant mountaintop, and then move blindly toward it."
  Before you walk a bearing, look along the whole line on the map, and
  along the strip either side of it that your errors could put you in
  (the section on the growing circle, below, says how wide).
- **The errors pile up.** The same manual says compasses and pace counts
  are imprecise, and: "Error from them compounds over distance;
  therefore, you could soon be far afield from your intended route even
  if you performed the procedures correctly." Confirm your position
  against something real at every chance.
- **Never let a straight line take you across fast water.** The
  National Weather Service: "A mere 6 inches of fast-moving flood water
  can knock over an adult." Go round, or turn back (general practice).
- **Darkness and fog shorten everything.** The manual says that in fog,
  in dense vegetation or in darkness your steering marks must be close
  together, which introduces more chance for error. Carry a light, which
  the National Park Service lists among its ten essentials, and plan to
  be off difficult ground before dark (general practice).
- **Tell someone where you are going.** The Forest Service asks you to
  give someone the exact details of your trip, and ends its list with
  "do not go alone." A trip plan is what makes it possible for anyone to
  come looking; [What to Do When You Are
  Lost](what_to_do_when_you_are_lost.md) explains why it matters so much.
- **Carry enough to spend a night out.** The National Park Service's ten
  essentials are navigation, sun protection, extra clothing, a light,
  first aid supplies, a way to make fire, a repair kit and tools, extra
  food, extra water and emergency shelter. A reckoning that goes wrong at
  dusk is an inconvenience with them and an emergency without them (our
  reading).
- **When the ground stops matching what you expect, stop.** The Forest
  Service's advice for anyone who may be lost begins "stop, stay calm,
  stay put." A dead reckoning that has gone wrong is not repaired by
  walking faster.

## The idea: where you started, which way, how far

Every position in dead reckoning is the same kind of statement: so far
east (or west) and so far north (or south) of a place you know. Each leg
you walk adds to it.

Walk 300 metres due east, then 400 metres due north. You are now 300
metres east and 400 metres north of where you started. The straight line
back is 500 metres long (a 3-4-5 triangle), on a bearing of about 37
degrees from the start, so the bearing home is about 217 degrees
(arithmetic). You walked 700 metres to get somewhere 500 metres away,
and you could walk home on a single bearing without retracing either
leg.

The Army manual describes dead reckoning as two steps: "Dead reckoning
consists of two fundamental steps." The first is working out direction
and distance on a map with a protractor and a scale; the second is
applying them on the ground with a compass and some way of measuring
distance. You can add legs together the same two ways.

- **Draw them.** On squared paper, choose a scale (1 centimetre for 100
  metres is easy), draw each leg at its bearing with a protractor and at
  its length with a ruler, and measure the line from the start to the
  end. This is the method to use in the field, and it is good enough
  (our reading of the manual's protractor-and-scale step).
- **Calculate them.** For each leg, the distance east is the leg's
  length times the sine of its bearing, and the distance north is its
  length times the cosine. Add up all the easts and all the norths. A few
  values cover most needs: at 0 degrees the leg is all north; at 90 all
  east; at 30 degrees, 0.5 of it goes east and 0.87 north; at 45, 0.71
  each way; at 60, 0.87 east and 0.5 north. Between 90 and 270 degrees
  the north part is negative (the leg goes south), and between 180 and
  360 the east part is negative (the leg goes west) (trigonometry).

The comparison is where the learning happens. *The American Practical
Navigator* says navigators compare the dead reckoned position with a
known fix to find the other forces acting on the vessel, such as wind and
current. On foot, the gap between where your arithmetic put you and
where the map shows you actually are is a measurement of your own
errors, and the same lesson applies.

## How far: paces, time and speed

The manual lists the ways of measuring distance as you go: "You might
use pacing, a vehicle odometer, or the application of elapsed time for
this purpose, depending upon the situation."

### Paces

The pace count is the main tool on foot, and [Estimating](estimating.md)
shows how to calibrate yours on a measured course and why you never keep
the count in your head. Three rules from the map reading manual matter
for dead reckoning in particular:

- **Start again from every confirmed position.** "Pace counts or any
  type of distance measurement should begin anew each time your position
  is confirmed on the map." An error carried from an old count is an error
  you no longer need.
- **On a detour, count only progress.** When you step off your line to
  get round an obstacle, the manual says to "be certain that only paces
  taken toward the final destination are counted as part of your forward
  progress."
- **Night is a different count.** "Smaller steps are taken during night
  navigation, so remember, the pace count is different." The manual
  recommends a separate night count from a 100 metre course.

### Time and speed

Distance is speed times time. The Army's foot march manual (FM 21-18)
says it in three lines: rate is distance over time, distance is rate
times time, time is distance over rate. The map reading manual's own
example: a unit marching at an average of 4 kilometres an hour takes
about 3 hours to cover 12 kilometres.

What rate? FM 21-18 gives the pace of a marching soldier as 30 inches at
a cadence of 106 steps a minute, and the sample unit procedure printed
in its appendix sets these rates:

| | On roads | Cross-country |
|---|---|---|
| By day | 4.0 km/h | 2.4 km/h |
| By night | 3.2 km/h | 1.6 km/h |

The manual notes that rates like these include time for short halts and
other minor delays. They are for soldiers in formation carrying loads
over normal terrain, so treat them as a starting point and measure your
own: time yourself on your pace course and on a trail you know, on flat
ground and up a hill, and write the results in the same notebook as your
pace count (general practice).

Time is most useful as a check on the pace count. If your count says you
have come 2 kilometres through brush but your watch says 20 minutes, one
of them is wrong: at the manual's 2.4 km/h cross-country rate, 20 minutes
is about 800 metres (arithmetic). Stop and work out which.

Time is also the only measure you have when you are not walking: in a
canoe, on a bicycle, on a horse. The section on water, below, says what
that adds.

## Which way: holding a direction

### Why you cannot walk straight by feel

People believe they walk in a straight line. A study published in 2009
by researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Biological Cybernetics
tested it: they tracked people by GPS for hours, walking as straight as
they could through a large forest and across part of the Sahara. When
they could not see the sun, people repeatedly walked in circles. When the
sun was visible, they sometimes drifted off a straight course but did not
circle. Blindfolded, people walked in circles often less than 20 metres
across, rarely turning the same way consistently. The researchers'
explanation is small errors in the body's sense of straight ahead that
add up when nothing outside corrects them (Souman and others, 2009,
restated). Every method in this section is a way of supplying that
outside correction.

### With a compass

[Knowing Which Way Is North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md) teaches
taking and following a bearing. The parts that make dead reckoning work:

- **Walk to steering marks, not with the compass held out.** The manual
  says never to walk with the compass open in front of you, because it
  will not stay steady or level; sight a landmark on your bearing, close
  the compass, walk to the landmark, and repeat.
- **No mark ahead? Use one behind.** The manual's advice is to take a
  back bearing to a feature behind you until a good steering mark
  appears in front.
- **No marks at all?** In featureless ground or at night, the manual
  describes sending one member of the group out ahead, placing them on
  the bearing by signals, walking to them, and repeating.

### Without a compass

- **The sun.** The shadow-tip method gives east and west within a
  quarter of an hour ([Knowing Which Way Is
  North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md)). If you then steer by keeping
  the sun at a fixed angle, you will turn with it. The map reading
  manual: "On average, the amount of variation in shadow fall per hour is
  15 degrees." (It gives the figure for reading shadows on aerial
  photographs, and says it needs correcting for the season and the
  latitude more than three hours from noon.) Fifteen degrees is an
  average, not a steady rate: the sun swings fastest around midday, and
  fastest of all around a summer midday. At Silverdale's latitude, in the
  hour either side of noon, it swings about 31 degrees in June, 20 at the
  equinoxes and 14 in December, and early and late on a summer day
  nearer 10 (our arithmetic from the standard formulas for the sun's
  position). Kept for an hour unchecked, that swing turns your course by
  about 15 degrees on average, and at the manual's 18 metres off line per
  degree per 1,000 metres that puts you about 270 metres off line for
  every kilometre; around a summer midday it is about twice that
  (arithmetic). Take a fresh direction from a shadow often,
  and use the sun to pick a distant landmark, then walk to the landmark
  (general practice, the steering mark method again).
- **The stars.** The map reading manual puts Polaris less than 1 degree
  off true north, and [The Sky as an
  Instrument](the_sky_as_an_instrument.md) explains finding it. A star
  low on the horizon makes a steering mark, but it moves: the same manual
  says a star near the northern horizon serves for about half an hour,
  and [Knowing Which Way Is North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md) gives
  the rest of its advice on steering by stars.
- **The land.** A road, a ridge or a stream that runs the way you are
  going can replace precise direction keeping altogether. [Reading a
  Map](reading_a_map.md) calls these handrails.

However you hold your direction, the cost of an error is the same: about
18 metres sideways for every degree, for every 1,000 metres walked. Ten
degrees of carelessness over two kilometres is 360 metres (arithmetic).

## Keep a written log

A dead reckoning lives on paper or it does not live at all. Memory loses
counts and bearings the moment something else needs your attention, which
is exactly when you will need them.

For each leg, write down:

- the time you start it;
- the bearing, and whether it is a compass (magnetic) or a map (true)
  bearing: treating one as the other puts you off by the whole
  declination, and correcting in the wrong direction doubles it ([Knowing
  Which Way Is North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md));
- the distance, as a pace count or a time, and what it works out to in
  metres;
- the time you finish it, and anything you passed that might be on the
  map: a stream, a fence, a change in slope.

*The American Practical Navigator* tells a ship's navigator when to plot
a new dead reckoned position: "1. At least every hour on the hour. 2.
After every change of course or speed. 3. After every fix or running fix.
4. After plotting a single line of position." The same four rules make a
good habit on foot (our adaptation): write an entry at least every hour;
at every change of direction, and at every change of pace, such as the
start of a climb; every time you confirm where you are; and whenever you
learn part of your position, for example that you have reached a
particular stream but not where along it.

## How wrong you might be: the growing circle

Ships' navigators draw their uncertainty. *The American Practical
Navigator* calls the method fix expansion: start with a circle around
the last known position as big as the error of whatever gave you that
position, and let it grow by your estimated error rate every hour. Its
example: a 0.5 nautical mile error at the start and errors that can add
up to 2 nautical miles an hour make circles of 2.5 nautical miles after
one hour and 4.5 after two. Its warning about the errors: "While the
errors may in fact cancel each other out, the worst case is that they
will all be additive, and this is what the navigator must anticipate."
Lines drawn tangent to the circles mark the band the ship could be in,
and the navigator searches that band on the chart for dangers.

The same thing on foot, as an illustration (our arithmetic). Suppose your
compass may be 3 degrees out (the map reading manual's limit for a
compass you still use) and your pace count 10 percent out. After 2
kilometres in one direction, the sideways error could be 3 x 18 x 2 = 108
metres, and the error along your line 200 metres either way. You are
somewhere in a box about 220 metres wide and 400 metres long, not on a
dot.

That box is what you look at on the map before you go on. *The American
Practical Navigator* says the most important use of dead reckoning is
"in projecting the ship's position in the immediate future and avoiding
hazards to navigation." On foot the same holds (our reading): if a cliff
edge or a river runs through the box ahead of you, it is in your path
whatever the dot says.

And it tells you what to aim at. A target smaller than your box can be
missed without your knowing which way it lies. A target bigger than your
box is hard to miss, if your errors are no worse than you assumed.
[Reading a Map](reading_a_map.md) covers the tools
the map reading manual gives for this: aim deliberately to one side of a
point that sits on a long feature, so that you know which way to turn
when you reach the feature; follow handrails; use a catching feature
that tells you when you have gone too far; and do the last, precise
stretch from an attack point no more than about 500 metres away.

## Fixing your position, and starting again

Every confirmed position shrinks the box back to the size of the
confirmation. Take one whenever you can.

- **At a feature you can find on the map,** such as a stream junction,
  a road crossing or a summit. Restart the pace count there.
- **By resection,** with a compass and a map. The map reading manual's
  method: identify two or three distant landmarks you can see and can
  find on the map, take a compass bearing to each, convert each to a map
  bearing ([Knowing Which Way Is North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md)),
  take the back bearing (add or subtract 180), and draw it on the map
  from the landmark back toward you. Where the lines cross is your
  position. Three landmarks are better than two.
- **On a linear feature,** the manual's modified resection needs only
  one landmark: if you know you are on a particular road or stream, one
  back bearing drawn from one landmark crosses it at your position.

The map reading manual is firm that neither method should stand alone:
"One should not depend entirely on compass navigation or map navigation;
either or both could be lost or destroyed." Dead reckoning tells you
where to look on the map; the map tells you whether the dead reckoning is
still right.

## Detours

Something will be in the way. The method for going round it on a box
course, with right-angle legs and the side legs cancelling out, is in
[Knowing Which Way Is North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md). Two rules
from the map reading manual keep the log honest: count only the paces
that take you toward the destination, and "always return to the original
line as soon as the terrain or situation permits." If you can see a mark
on your line across the obstacle, such as a tree beyond a swamp, the
manual's simpler method is to walk round to it and start again from
there; if the mark is behind you, sight back to it once you are past.

## At night and in fog

The manual calls dead reckoning the basic technique for navigating at
night, and the reasons it gives are the reasons to be careful:

- **Your pace shortens,** so use a night count measured on a 100 metre
  course in the dark.
- **Colours vanish** and objects turn to grey silhouettes that change
  shape as you move, so steering marks must be more distinctive than by
  day, and closer together.
- **The manual recommends several compasses** in a group, and someone a
  few steps ahead on the bearing as a moving steering mark.

The same applies in fog. Our advice is shorter: go slower, keep legs
short, write more often, and turn back earlier than you would by day
(general practice).

## On water

Dead reckoning began at sea, and the sea adds a problem the land does
not have: the surface you are on is moving. *The American Practical
Navigator* names the parts. "Set refers to the current's direction, and
drift refers to the current's speed." Leeway is the sideways push of the
wind, which it says especially affects sailing vessels and high-sided
vessels. A dead reckoning from heading and speed through the water leaves
all three out, and they all add up with time.

An illustration (our arithmetic): paddling across a bay with a current
of 1 kilometre an hour running across your course, every 15 minutes on
the water moves you 250 metres sideways, however straight you keep the
bow. The navigator's cure is the same as on land: confirm your position
often, aim off so that you meet a long shoreline on the side you
intended, and treat every dead reckoned position as a box rather than a
dot. The navigator's manual also measures what the water did: the
direction from the dead reckoned position to the real one is the set,
and the distance between them divided by the hours since the reckoning
was last restarted is the drift.

Water is also where mistakes are least forgiving. The Coast Guard's
guide to the federal requirements for recreational boats advises
carrying a device such as a GPS to determine your position, so that you
can give rescuers an accurate location. Small boats, cold water and
currents are covered in [Floating and Boats](floating_and_boats.md) and
[Reading the Tide](reading_the_tide.md). Do not attempt a crossing in fog
by dead reckoning unless you are trained, equipped and wearing a life
jacket (general practice).

## Worked example: two legs into the woods and the way back

An illustration with made-up numbers, to show the steps in order.

You leave your car at a gate on a forest road that runs north and south.
Your pace count is 120 paces per 100 metres on this ground, and your
compass is set to read true bearings.

| Time | Bearing | Paces | Metres | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 09:00 | Start at the gate | | | Road runs north-south |
| 09:00 to 09:07 | 90 (east) | 360 | 300 | Crossed a small dry gully at about 200 m |
| 09:07 to 09:17 | 0 (north) | 480 | 400 | Gentle climb; reached a clearing with a big stump |

1. **Where you are.** 300 metres east and 400 metres north of the gate:
   500 metres from it, on a bearing of about 37 degrees from the gate
   (arithmetic).
2. **Time check.** 700 metres in 17 minutes is about 2.5 km/h, close to
   the manual's 2.4 km/h cross-country rate, so the pace count is
   plausible.
3. **How wrong you might be.** With a 10 percent pace error and a 3
   degree compass error, the worst case puts you up to about 50 metres
   east or west and 55 metres north or south of the logged position, 75
   metres if both happen at once (our arithmetic, adding the errors of
   both legs).
4. **The way back, the wrong way.** The gate lies at 217 degrees. Walk
   straight at it and an error that size can put you on the road out of
   sight of the gate, not knowing whether to turn left or right.
5. **The way back, the right way.** Aim off. The map reading manual
   calls an offset of 10 degrees adequate for most tactical uses, but
   here you start with up to 50 or 55 metres of error and add more on the way
   back, so give yourself a wider margin: walk on 237 degrees, 20
   degrees to the right of the gate. You meet the road about 200 metres
   north of the gate, after about 360 metres of walking, and even with
   every error adding up in the worst case you are still about 90 metres
   north of it (arithmetic). Turn left, south, and walk down the road to
   the gate.
6. **If the road does not appear** when your pace count passes 360
   metres plus a margin, stop. Do not walk on in hope. Check the log and
   the compass, then follow [What to Do When You Are
   Lost](what_to_do_when_you_are_lost.md).

## Know where your own navigation stops

- **Someone is hurt, missing or overdue.** Call 911 and let searchers
  search. Dead reckoning is for staying found, not for leading a rescue
  (general practice; [What to Do When You Are
  Lost](what_to_do_when_you_are_lost.md)).
- **Glaciers, avalanche slopes, open water, fog at sea and flying** need
  training, charts and instruments that this guide does not give
  (general practice).
- **Property lines.** A paced bearing is not a boundary. That is a job for
  a licensed land surveyor (general practice).

## Traps

- **"I'll remember the count."** You will not, and the manual says
  never to try. Pebbles, knots or a notebook.
- **"The sun was on my left all morning."** It moved about 15 degrees
  an hour on average, about twice that around a summer midday, and you
  moved with it.
- **"It's straight on the map."** The map line does not show the gully,
  the windfall or the cliff. Look along the whole corridor first.
- **"An hour at walking pace is 5 kilometres."** Through brush it may be
  nearer half that. Measure your own rates.
- **"I'll know the place when I get there."** Aim at something you
  cannot miss, then go the last stretch carefully.
- **Counting the detour.** Only paces toward the destination count.
- **Trusting an old reckoning.** The box grows every hour. A position
  from three hours ago with nothing confirmed since is a guess.

## How the game models it

The game has a real-scale Earth, and gives you one useful tool for
practising and several things that are not what they look like.

- **A real-scale planet.** A kilometre walked on the game's Earth is a
  kilometre of the planet, and the Silverdale and Seattle Center map
  regions are built in at their real latitude and longitude, as
  [Coordinates](coordinates.md) describes. A session starts in your home
  aboard the ship in orbit, not on the ground; in the default Dev play
  mode, the Dev page's Land on surface buttons put you on the ground,
  and its travel buttons take you to a planet's orbit. Both leave fly
  mode on, and the line under the compass says so: "FLY x1 - gravity on
  [F9 to hover]". Press F9 twice (the first press turns the hover on, the
  second turns both off and the line reads "WALK x1 [F9 to fly]"), or
  untick Fly mode on the Dev page, and you are really walking. Until you
  do, there are
  no footsteps, and the mouse wheel can raise your speed far past the
  fifty-times limit that holds while walking.
- **Footsteps you can count.** Walking on the ground of a planet, or on
  foot aboard the ship in first-person view, with fly mode off, the game
  plays a footstep sound about every 1.5 metres you move. So 100 metres
  is about 60 to 67 footsteps (arithmetic), and you can practise keeping
  a count by ear. It is steadier than yours but not perfect: a step is
  about 1.5 metres, a little more at low frame rates, because the
  distance left over at each step is dropped rather than carried on. It
  is the same uphill and on the flat, in mud and on a road, by day and by
  night, but at the normal speed gear, walking down a slope steeper than
  about 30 percent makes no footsteps at all, because the game counts
  you as in the air. Fly mode makes no footsteps, nor does time in the
  air on a planet (aboard the ship a jump still counts toward the next
  step), and any single frame that moves you 2 metres or more is
  ignored, so at high speed gears the steps go silent.
- **Walking speed is not yours.** On a planet's surface you walk at 5
  metres a second at the normal speed gear (the "Gear: x1" beside the
  altitude on screen). That is 18 km/h, four and a half times the Army's
  4 km/h marching rate (arithmetic). If you have raised the speed in dev
  fly mode, with the mouse wheel while flying or with the Dev page's
  slider, walking with fly mode off keeps that speed up to fifty times
  faster (the Dev page's 1x button sets it back). Hiking boots add 5
  percent and being well nourished 10 percent. Hunger (10 percent),
  thirst (20), fatigue (15), low blood oxygen (30) or none at all (half),
  heat exhaustion (20), hypothermia (half) and an unsanitary build-up of
  waste (5) slow you down. A heavy load slows you only when Settings >
  Gameplay > Carrying weight is set to Realistic; the default,
  Forgiving, only warns you. A time-and-speed estimate learned in the
  game is wrong for your own legs.
- **Direction.** The N, E, S and W letters at the top of the screen turn
  with your view, but [Knowing Which Way Is
  North](knowing_which_way_is_north.md) explains why they have not been
  checked against true north on a planet's surface. The Compass item
  shows no bearing. The night sky is drawn from a real star catalogue, so
  Polaris can be found and used.
- **No position fix.** Nothing shows your own position as you walk: the
  GPS Device in the item list does nothing yet, and the Maps page draws
  the real regions with north up and a bar scale but does not mark where
  you are. That makes the game a fair place to practise keeping a log,
  and an honest one: if you lose track, nothing on screen will rescue the
  arithmetic. The Maps page's Track switch for the Home Station, on by
  default, rings your home in orbit with its distance, from the ground
  too, and pins the ring to the edge of the screen on the side to turn
  toward when the station is out of view. That shows where home is in
  the sky, not where you are on the ground ([What to Do When You Are
  Lost](what_to_do_when_you_are_lost.md)).
- **The Navigation skill exists, but nothing in the game levels it
  yet.**

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: errors.
You walk exactly straight while you hold the key, your footsteps change
length only a little, with the frame rate, nothing drifts, and the sun,
the slope and the dark do not shorten your stride. Dead reckoning is
mostly the management of errors, and those you have to practise with
real feet.

## You own this when

- You can say, at any point on a walk, where you are relative to where
  you started, and how wrong that might be.
- You keep a written log of each leg: time, bearing (magnetic or true),
  distance and what you passed.
- You can add two or three legs by drawing them to scale, and find the
  bearing and distance home.
- You know your own pace count and your own walking speed on the kinds
  of ground you use, by day and by night.
- You restart the count at every confirmed position, and count only
  forward progress on a detour.
- You can hold a direction with a compass and steering marks, and
  roughly without one, and you know why the sun cannot be steered by for
  long.
- You look along the whole corridor your errors could cover before you
  walk a bearing, and you aim at targets bigger than your error.
- You can fix your position by resection from two or three landmarks.
- You stop when the ground stops matching your reckoning, instead of
  walking on.
