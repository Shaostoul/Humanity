# Your First Solar Power

If the power went out tonight, could you keep a phone alive, a light on,
and the internet running? A small portable solar setup can do exactly
that, and it is the safest way to learn how solar works. This guide covers
plug-and-play gear only: a portable power station (a battery box with
ordinary outlets) plus a folding solar panel, or an even smaller USB solar
charger. You plug things together; you never touch wiring.

One hard boundary before anything else: whole-house solar, rooftop panels,
and anything that touches your home's electrical wiring is work for a
licensed electrician, full stop. So is building your own battery bank from
loose cells or car batteries. House wiring can kill, roof and ladder falls
maim, and a badly built battery bank is a fire. This guide is the safe
first rung of the ladder.

Where a number matters, it comes from the US Department of Energy, the
EPA, the US Fire Administration, or a university extension service.

## Watts and watt-hours, in kitchen language

- A watt (W) is how hard electricity is flowing right now, like how far a
  faucet is open.
- A watt-hour (Wh) is how much electricity flowed over time, like how much
  water ended up in the pot.

A lamp makes it concrete. The Department of Energy's method: wattage
multiplied by hours of use gives watt-hours, and 1,000 watt-hours make one
kilowatt-hour (kWh), the unit on your electric bill. Their example is an
electric kettle rated at 1,500 watts: run it one hour and it has used 1,500
watt-hours, which is 1.5 kWh.

A power station's size is listed in watt-hours (how much the tank holds).
Its output rating is in watts (how far the faucet can open at once). You
need both numbers, and the troubleshooting section shows why.

## What you need (cheap first)

- Cheapest rung: a small folding USB solar charger. No battery; it charges
  a phone or power bank directly in sunlight. Good for learning how much
  sun actually matters, for less than most gadgets cost.
- The main event: a portable power station. Pick one with LiFePO4 (lithium
  iron phosphate) battery cells; the chemistry section explains why.
  Around 250 to 500 watt-hours is a sensible first size.
- A matching folding solar panel. Portable panels commonly range from 100
  to 400 watts (University of Colorado Denver's emergency preparedness
  guidance). Buy the panel made for your station so the plugs simply fit.
- The cords that came in the box. Use only those, nothing improvised.
- Free but essential: the labels on your devices, and ten minutes of
  arithmetic.

## Step 1: Read a device label

Every device has a nameplate: a sticker with its electrical ratings,
usually on the bottom or on the power brick. The Department of Energy's
rule for it (republished by Virginia Cooperative Extension):

1. If the label lists watts (W), that is the most the device draws.
2. If it only lists amps (A) and volts (V), multiply them: amps x volts =
   watts. A router brick marked 12V 1.5A can draw at most 12 x 1.5 = 18
   watts.
3. The label is the most the device draws, so budgeting the label
   number is a safe overestimate, the direction you want to err in.

## Step 2: Add up a day, one worked example

Say you want to run a phone, one LED lamp, and your internet router through
a one-day outage.

- Phone: the EPA's official figure is 0.019 kWh, about 19 watt-hours, for
  one full smartphone charge, counting charger losses. Two charges = 38 Wh.
- LED lamp: a roughly 10-watt LED bulb matches an old 75-watt bulb (the
  Department of Energy lists 9.5 to 14 watts as the equivalent range).
  Five evening hours: 10 W x 5 h = 50 Wh.
- Router: label says up to 18 watts, running all day: 18 W x 24 h = 432 Wh.

Total: 38 + 50 + 432 = 520 watt-hours.

Notice what happened: the always-on device ate the budget, not the phone.
Turn the router off for 8 sleeping hours and you save 18 x 8 = 144 Wh,
bringing the day to about 376 Wh. That is the most useful lesson in home
energy: small loads that never stop cost more than big loads used briefly.
For the trimmed 376 Wh day, a 500 watt-hour station covers you with
margin; the full 520 Wh day needs a bigger one. And know that a station
delivers roughly 10 to 15 percent less than its rated watt-hours through
the AC outlets (the conversion electronics take their cut), so buy above
your budget number, never right at it.

## Step 3: Why panels rarely give their rated watts

A "100-watt" panel almost never delivers 100 watts, and that is not a
scam; it is how the rating works. Panels are rated under a standard test
of 1,000 watts of sunlight per square meter: bright, direct, noon sun.
University of Maryland Extension calls one hour of that a "peak sun hour,"
and most places get only a few per day: Maryland averages about 3.92 peak
sun hours daily in its west and 5.06 near the Chesapeake. Clouds, haze,
season, and sun angle are already averaged into those numbers.

On top of that, Maryland Extension applies a standard derate factor of 86
percent (14 percent lost to wiring, dirt, aging, temperature, and similar
real-world friction). Colorado's emergency-preparedness guidance saw the
same thing at the product level: a 400-watt portable panel typically
produces 350 to 360 watts even in optimal conditions. Shade is worse
still: full shade produces nothing, and even a small shadow across the
panel causes a significant loss.

So the honest recharge math for a 100-watt panel in a 4-peak-sun-hour
location is: 100 W x 4 h x 0.86 = about 344 watt-hours per day. That does
not fully refill the 515 Wh day above; a 200-watt panel (about 688 Wh per
day) does. Size the panel to the day, not to the brochure.

## Step 4: One honest paragraph about battery chemistry

Portable stations use two main lithium chemistries. Older-style lithium-ion
(NMC, the laptop kind) stores more energy per pound. LiFePO4 (lithium iron
phosphate, LFP) is heavier but lasts far longer and resists overheating.
The Department of Energy's grid-storage cost assessments have used about
2,000 full charge cycles for LFP versus about 1,200 for NMC at 80 percent
depth of discharge (the 2022 update raised both); makers now commonly
advertise 3,000-plus cycles for LFP. Even at the conservative 2,000, that
is a full drain and refill every day for over five years. For a box that
will be cycled for years, LiFePO4 is worth its extra weight and cost.

## Step 5: Place and angle the panel

1. Ground level only. Never put a portable panel on a roof; roof and
   ladder work is where this hobby injures people. Nothing up there is
   worth it at this scale.
2. Face it south (in the northern hemisphere). Maryland Extension: due
   south is best; southeast or southwest costs about 5 percent.
3. Tilt it toward the sun instead of laying it flat. The extension rule
   of thumb is a tilt near your latitude (30 to 45 degrees across the mid
   US); Colorado's guidance suggests about 30 degrees if you want one
   number. Folding panels have kickstand legs for exactly this.
4. Chase the shade away. Recheck every hour or two; a shadow creeping
   over one corner quietly ruins the whole panel's output.
5. Keep the station itself out of the sun while charging, in shade or
   indoors with the cable running out. Heat is the battery's enemy.

## Step 6: Charging discipline and storage

The US Fire Administration and university safety offices agree on these:

- Charge only between 32 F and 105 F (0 C to 40 C). Charging a lithium
  battery below freezing damages it; charging it in high heat is a fire
  risk.
- Never leave it in direct sunlight or a hot car; both are a fire risk.
- When wall-charging, plug the station straight into a wall outlet, never
  into a power strip, and use only the cord that came with it.
- Do not keep charging after it is full, and do not charge while asleep.
- Store it at room temperature, away from anything that can burn.
- Stop using any battery that gives off an odor, changes color or shape,
  swells, leaks, gets unusually hot, or makes odd noises. If you safely
  can, move it away from anything that can catch fire (outdoors on bare
  ground is ideal), and do not charge it again.

If a battery ever smokes or flames, get out and call 911. There is no
beginner-level response to a lithium fire.

## What a realistic budget buys (2026 prices, will drift)

Ballpark only, and prices move constantly: in 2026, small portable
stations (a few hundred watt-hours) start around $150 to $300, mid-size
units around 1,000 watt-hours commonly sell in the $400 to $700 range,
and the 1,500 to 2,500 Wh class runs roughly $700 to $2,000. Folding
panels add money on top, from well under $100 for small ones upward.
The honest way to compare is dollars per watt-hour of capacity: divide
the price by the rated Wh and prefer the lower number at a given size,
favoring LiFePO4 chemistry for its longer life. A $200 station you
understand beats a $2,000 one bought blind.

## What can go wrong (honest troubleshooting)

- It charges much slower than the panel rating. Normal; reread Step 3. A
  100-watt panel giving 50 to 70 watts under a hazy sky at a lazy angle
  is physics, not a defect. Fix the angle, chase the shade, and expect
  the rating only in cloudless noon sun. Near zero in full sun? Check
  every cable connector first.
- It will not run my heater, kettle, or toaster. Also physics. Heating
  appliances draw 1,000 to 1,500 watts continuously (the Department of
  Energy's example kettle is 1,500 W), and a small station's output may
  top out at 300 to 600 watts, so it shuts off to protect itself. Motors
  (fridges, pumps) are sneakier: they briefly surge far above their
  running watts at startup, so a station can fail to start a fridge it
  could otherwise run. Match appliance labels to the station's continuous
  and surge ratings before the outage. Heat is the job small solar does
  worst; plan blankets, not electric heaters.
- Winter output collapses. Expected. Peak sun hours are annual averages;
  winter days sit well below them, and the effect grows with latitude:
  the University of Alaska Fairbanks extension's solar manual puts
  Fairbanks' maximum midwinter sunlight near 100 W per square meter
  versus about 970 in June, roughly a tenth. Plan winter around the wall
  charger, top up before storms, and treat winter solar as a bonus.
- It sat in the closet a year and the level dropped. Batteries self-drain
  slowly. Top it up on a calendar reminder every few months, the same
  habit as rotating stored water.

Your first afternoon will probably include a shaded panel, a surprise
shutdown, and slow charging. That is cheap tuition; every failure here
teaches you before the day it matters.

## How you know it worked

- You can read any device label and say what it draws, in watts.
- You have a written watt-hour budget for your outage day, and your
  station's capacity covers it.
- Your panel has real sun on it, angled and unshaded, and you have watched
  the input watts change as you moved it.
- The station lives indoors at room temperature, charged, away from
  anything that burns, and you know the temperature rules by heart.
- You know exactly where this rung ends: the day you want solar on a roof
  or in your house wiring, you call a licensed electrician.

When all five are true, you own working backup power, and you understand
it. That understanding is what scales: sizing loads in watt-hours,
derating panels, respecting battery chemistry, and knowing your limits are
exactly the skills a professional whole-house install is built on.
