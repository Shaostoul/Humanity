# Salting, Curing and Smoking

Salt, cure and smoke kept meat and fish through the winter long before
anyone owned a refrigerator; one extension guide puts curing's history at
thousands of years. Most home curing today makes something that still
lives in the refrigerator or the freezer, such as bacon, smoked salmon or
a ham, and the old methods are only as safe as their numbers: how much
salt, how much cure, how hot, for how long, and how cold afterwards. Each
of those numbers is there because something can grow in meat and fish
that a cook cannot see, smell or taste.

This guide is about how salt, curing and smoking keep meat and fish safe
to eat: what salt does, what the nitrite in curing salt adds and why it
must be measured exactly, how hot smoking differs from cold smoking, the
steps a beginner must not skip, and where tested methods come from. The
worked example is hot-smoked salmon in Silverdale, Washington, where the
simulation is set.

It leans on other guides rather than repeating them. [Drying
Food](drying_food.md) covers jerky, whose one rule is to heat the meat to
160 F before you dry it. [Why Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md)
explains water activity, which is the reason salt preserves at all, and
the organism behind botulism. [Fermenting
Vegetables](fermenting_vegetables.md) is salt's other job, steering a
ferment. [Fishing](fishing.md) covers keeping fish cold from the moment
it is caught, and the parasites in fish eaten raw or cold-smoked.
[Pressure Canning](pressure_canning.md) is how smoked fish can be kept on
a shelf. [Cold Storage Without a Fridge](cold_storage_without_a_fridge.md)
has the rules for keeping perishable food at 40 F or below, and [Telling
Spoiled From Safe](telling_spoiled_from_safe.md) covers mould on country
hams and salami.

Its sources are the US Department of Agriculture's Food Safety and
Inspection Service (FSIS) and Complete Guide to Home Canning, the Centers
for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) and the Health Resources and
Services Administration (HRSA), all US federal and public domain, and,
restated in our own words, Pacific Northwest Extension publications from
Oregon State University, Washington State University and the University
of Idaho. Where something is general practice or our reading, the text
says so.

## First, where curing and smoking can hurt you

### Curing salt is not table salt

Curing salt is salt with sodium nitrite mixed into it. The kind sold for
curing bacon at home, Prague Powder #1 (also sold as Curing Salt #1 or
Insta Cure #1), is 6.25 percent sodium nitrite and 93.75 percent salt,
and most curing salts are dyed pink so that nobody mistakes them for
table salt. That pink is not the pink of Himalayan salt, which comes from
trace minerals (PNW 784, the Pacific Northwest guide to curing bacon).
Nitrite is toxic enough that, in that guide's words restated, curing
salts must be used in exactly the quantities a researched recipe sets.

What happens when the two are mixed up is on record. The CDC investigated
a household in Yonkers, New York, where in 2002 five adults fell ill
almost as soon as they had eaten a meal seasoned from a bag labelled
"Refined Iodized Table Salt". The bag held pure sodium nitrite, put there
by a man who had cured meat in the house where the family lived before;
two of the five needed help to breathe, and all of them recovered. The CDC report's conclusion was "the
need to store and label potentially hazardous materials properly to avoid
unintentional ingestion".

So:

- **Keep curing salt in its own labelled container**, never in a salt
  shaker, a sugar bowl or a food jar, and out of the reach of children.
  HRSA's advice for anything poisonous at home is to keep it in its
  original container, out of children's reach, and never in containers
  meant for food; applying that to curing salt is our reading.
- **Measure it for every batch**, with proper measuring spoons or a
  scale, exactly as the recipe says, and never add extra "to be safe"
  (general practice, following the Pacific Northwest guide's rule).
- **If someone may have eaten curing salt,** call Poison Help at
  1-800-222-1222 straight away, as HRSA advises for any suspected
  poisoning, and call 911 if they are short of breath, turning bluish,
  confused or collapsing (our reading of the CDC report).

### Smoke does not make raw meat safe

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service puts it in one line:
"Smoking is slowly cooking food indirectly over a fire." Smoked meat is
safe because it was cooked through, not because it was smoky, and hot
smoking has to bring meat to the same safe internal temperature as any
other cooking, checked with a thermometer (FSIS). For fish, the Pacific
Northwest guide to smoking fish (PNW 238) says that smoke itself is not
an effective preservative under most conditions.

### Curing, smoking and drying do not kill every parasite

Pork and wild game can carry *Trichinella*, a worm that causes
trichinellosis. The CDC: "Curing (salting), drying, smoking, or
microwaving meat alone does not consistently kill the worms." Freezing
pork less than 6 inches thick for 20 days at 5 F kills them, the CDC
says, but not reliably in wild game: "Freezing wild game meats may not
effectively kill all worms because some worms that infect wild game are
freeze-resistant." It names bear, wild boar, wildcat, fox, wolf, seal and
walrus among the meats at risk. So cured or smoked pork and wild game get
cooked to a safe temperature, or made only by a tested method with a step
against the parasite, and wild game simply gets cooked (our reading of
the CDC page). Fish has parasites of its own, and salting, brining and
cold smoking do not deal with them ([Fishing](fishing.md)).

### Cold-smoked fish and dry sausages are raw food

Cold-smoked fish is cured and smoked below about 80 to 90 F, so it is
never pasteurized and has to be handled carefully (PNW 238). Many dry
sausages, pepperoni among them, are fermented and dried rather than
cooked, and the USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service records that a
dangerous strain of *E. coli* can survive dry fermenting: in 1994
children fell ill from dry cured salami, after which FSIS wrote specific
processing rules for dry sausages. The CDC advises people at higher risk
from *Listeria* (pregnant women, newborns, people 65 or older and anyone
with a weakened immune system) to avoid refrigerated smoked fish and
unheated fermented or dry sausages, and to choose shelf-stable smoked
fish, smoked fish cooked in a dish, or sausages reheated to 165 F or
until steaming hot. This guide does not teach cold smoking, dry-cured
hams or dry sausages ("Know where your own work stops", below).

### A smoker is a fire

A smoker burns charcoal or wood for hours. The CDC: "Never burn charcoal
indoors." Burning fuel makes carbon monoxide, and the CDC lists its
commonest symptoms as headache, dizziness, weakness, upset stomach,
vomiting, chest pain and confusion, and warns that people who are asleep
can die of it before they have symptoms. Run a smoker outdoors only,
never in a house, garage, basement or shed, even with the door open
(general practice, following the CDC's rules for charcoal and
generators).

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service adds where outdoors, for a
charcoal smoker: a well-lit, well-ventilated place away from trees,
shrubbery and buildings, lit only with approved fire starters and never
with gasoline or paint thinner. And, for any smoker, what to smoke in:
"Don't smoke foods in makeshift containers such as galvanized steel cans
or other materials not intended for cooking. Chemical residue
contamination can result."

### Botulism, which salt, nitrite and cold hold back together

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service: "Nitrite and salt inhibit
the growth of Clostridium botulinum, a deadly microorganism which can
occur in foods under certain situations." Take either away and the cold
has to do all the work (our reading). The Pacific Northwest guide to curing bacon says
bacon made without nitrite must be kept constantly below 40 F, and the
guide to smoking fish warns that fish smoked without proper salting and
cooking can cause foodborne illness, even fatal illness, with botulism the
most notorious danger; it keeps smoked fish at 38 F or below, and frozen
if your refrigerator cannot reliably hold under 40 F (PNW 784, PNW 238).

## How salt keeps food

Salt draws water out of meat and fish and takes its own place in the
flesh, and the less free water there is, the less anything can grow.
That free water is called water activity; [Why Canning Has
Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md) explains it with the FDA's numbers. The
Pacific Northwest guide to curing bacon says high salt and the resulting
low water activity prevent the growth of pathogens, spoilage bacteria and
most fungi.

Two things make home salting less certain than it sounds.

- **You cannot tell how much salt went in.** The Pacific Northwest guide
  to smoking fish says that without chemical analysis it is hard to be
  certain a fish has absorbed enough salt, which is why cooking and
  refrigeration, not salt alone, are what make home-smoked fish safe. The
  minimum salt it reports the FDA recommends for commercial smoked fish is
  3.5 percent of the water left in the fish, a figure that, the guide
  notes, most home smokers do not measure (PNW 238).
- **Salt is uneven.** In a dry-cured ham, the USDA's Food Safety and
  Inspection Service explains, the high salt on the outside holds back
  *Staphylococcus aureus*, but the moister inside does not, so once the
  ham is cut the bacteria can grow on the slices: "Thus, sliced dry-cured
  hams must be refrigerated." A cut surface is a different food from the
  rind (our reading).

In fermented vegetables salt has a different job, tilting the
competition toward the bacteria you want ([Fermenting
Vegetables](fermenting_vegetables.md), [Why Canning Has
Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md)).

## What curing adds: nitrite

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service, in its page on hams,
defines curing as adding salt, nitrate or nitrite, and often sugar and
seasonings, to pork for preservation, colour and flavour. Nitrate and
nitrite give cured pork its pink colour and its cured taste, and nitrite,
with salt, holds back the organism that causes botulism (FSIS). The same
page lists saltpeter as another name for nitrate; nitrate works more
slowly than nitrite, as a time-release cure (PNW 784).

The Pacific Northwest guide to curing bacon sorts the curing salts sold
for home use:

- **Prague Powder #1** (Curing Salt #1, Insta Cure #1): 6.25 percent
  sodium nitrite, the rest salt. Recommended for curing bacon at home.
- **Prague Powder #2** (Curing Salt #2, Insta Cure #2): sodium nitrite
  plus sodium nitrate, for cured meats that are not cooked before eating,
  such as salami and prosciutto. Not recommended for bacon, because it is
  likely to make more nitrosamines when the bacon is fried.
- **Proprietary blends**, sold under brand names: follow their makers'
  own recipes, which may not suit other recipes.

It also explains the nitrosamines: compounds formed from nitrite during
curing and cooking, some of which cause cancer in test animals at high
doses. Federal rules cap the nitrite in commercial bacon, the guide's
recipes stay under that cap, and cooking bacon less (not well-done or
burnt, and in the microwave rather than the frying pan) forms fewer
nitrosamines (PNW 784).

### A tested home cure: bacon

The Pacific Northwest guide to curing bacon (PNW 784, August 2025) is a
short, modern, tested method, and a good first cure. Its standard dry
cure, for 5 pounds of pork belly:

- 5 tablespoons of salt and 1 teaspoon of Curing Salt #1, which is
  Prague Powder #1, with brown sugar, paprika and coffee grounds for
  flavour. The guide adds: check the curing salt maker's instructions to
  confirm the amount for 5 pounds of meat.
- Coat the belly, put it in a 2-gallon zipper bag, and cure it **in the
  refrigerator, at 40 F**, for 5 to 7 days, turning the bag every day.
  It is ready when it feels firm.
- Rinse it well under cold running water to take off the surface salt
  (rinsing does not take the cure out of the middle), and pat it dry. It
  can wait, uncovered in the refrigerator, for up to 24 hours.
- Smoke or cook it to an internal temperature of **160 F**. With the
  smoker or oven at 225 to 250 F, that takes about three hours for a
  whole belly.
- Let it cool for an hour, wrap it tightly and chill it overnight, then
  slice it. Keep it in the refrigerator for up to 7 days, or freeze it
  and use it within 4 months for best quality.
- Cook it before eating, as you would bacon from a shop.

The guide's own checklist is the short version: good-quality meat, the
proper amounts of salt and cure, cure in the refrigerator, cook to 160 F,
chill, store cold.

## Smoking: hot and cold

### Hot smoking meat and poultry

The USDA's Food Safety and Inspection Service's rules for smoking meat
and poultry:

- **Thaw it completely first,** in the refrigerator, never at room
  temperature. Smoking cooks at low temperatures, and meat that is still
  frozen lingers too long in the "Danger Zone" of 40 to 140 F where
  bacteria multiply.
- **Marinate in the refrigerator,** not on the counter, and never put
  marinade that held raw meat on cooked food unless it has been boiled
  first.
- **Do not part-cook ahead.** Partly cook meat beforehand only if it goes
  straight from the stove or microwave into the hot smoker.
- **Use two thermometers.** One shows the air in the smoker, which must
  stay between 225 and 300 F the whole time; the other shows the meat.
  Smoking can take 4 to 8 hours.
- **Cook to a safe internal temperature:** 145 F for beef, pork, lamb and
  veal steaks, chops and roasts, then rest at least three minutes; 160 F
  for ground beef, pork, lamb and veal; 165 F for all poultry.
- **Chill it promptly.** In the service's words: "Refrigerate meat and
  poultry within 2 hours of removing it from a smoker." Cut it into
  smaller pieces in shallow covered containers, and use it within 4 days
  or freeze it.

### Hot smoking fish

Fish has its own tested method, from the Pacific Northwest guide to
smoking fish (PNW 238), which rests on three rules that must all be met:

1. **Salt.** Brine or salt the fish long enough for adequate salt to
   reach all through it.
2. **Heat.** Heat the fish until its internal temperature reaches 150 F,
   preferably 160 F, and hold it there for at least 30 minutes.
3. **Cold.** Keep smoked fish refrigerated at 38 F or below.

The fish is smoked first and cooked second: up to 2 hours of smoke at
around 90 F, then more heat until the thickest piece reaches temperature.
The worked example below follows the whole method.

### Cold smoking

Cold smoking keeps the smoker cool enough that the food never cooks, so
it is raw food with a smoky taste, and it has to be handled carefully to
avoid illness (PNW 238; the CDC's *Listeria* advice above). It is not a
beginner's method, and this guide does not teach it (our reading).

## The steps a beginner must not skip

1. **Start with good, fresh, cold meat or fish.** Smoking does not improve
   a fish, and the Pacific Northwest guide warns it can hide conditions
   that cause trouble later; it allows no more than 2 hours at room
   temperature between cleaning fish and smoking it (PNW 238). Keep meat
   and fish at 40 F or below until you start ([Cold Storage Without a
   Fridge](cold_storage_without_a_fridge.md)).
2. **Use a tested method**, from a source that tested it (below), and
   change nothing that affects safety: the salt, the cure, the times, the
   temperatures.
3. **Measure the salt and the cure exactly**, for the weight of meat in
   the recipe, using the cure the recipe names. Check the cure maker's
   instructions too (PNW 784).
4. **Cure in the refrigerator**, at 40 F, for the tested time (PNW 784).
5. **Thaw completely, in the refrigerator, before smoking** (FSIS).
6. **Use thermometers**: one in the smoker and one in the food (FSIS).
7. **Cook to the safe internal temperature** for what you are smoking:
   145 F and a 3-minute rest for whole cuts, 160 F for ground meat, 165 F
   for poultry (FSIS), 160 F for bacon (PNW 784), and 160 F for 30
   minutes for fish, 150 F at the least (PNW 238).
8. **Chill promptly and store cold**: within 2 hours of leaving the
   smoker, and eaten within 4 days, for meat and poultry (FSIS); within 7
   days for home-cured bacon (PNW 784); within 2 weeks at 38 F or below
   for smoked fish (PNW 238). Freeze anything you will keep longer.
9. **Cook pork and wild game** that might carry *Trichinella* (CDC).
10. **Keep the cure labelled, separate and out of children's reach**
    (CDC, HRSA).
11. **Keep the smoker outside**, away from the house, and never use it in
    a closed space (FSIS, CDC).

## Worked example: hot-smoked salmon in Silverdale

An illustration with made-up details, set in the place the simulation is
built on, following the Pacific Northwest guide to smoking fish (PNW
238).

You have three pounds of fresh coho salmon fillets, about an inch thick,
bought this morning and kept in the refrigerator. You have a small
electric smoker, a long-stemmed thermometer, a bag of alder chips, and an
oven for backup.

1. **Plan the cold.** The fish stays in the refrigerator except while it
   is being worked on, and never sits more than 2 hours at room
   temperature between cleaning and smoking.
2. **Cut it evenly.** Cut the fillets into pieces of the same size and
   thickness, so that they all take up salt alike.
3. **Brine it.** Dissolve 1 cup of salt in 7 cups of water, which the
   guide says will salt 2 to 3 pounds of fish. Use salt without iodine or
   anti-caking agent. Lay the pieces in the brine without overlapping, so
   the salt reaches every surface, and keep them in the refrigerator, as
   the guide does with its herring. One hour works for most fish; thinner,
   leaner or skinned pieces need less.
4. **Rinse and dry.** Rinse the surface, set the pieces flesh side up on
   a greased rack in a cool place, and leave them until a pellicle forms,
   a shiny, slightly tacky skin on the cut surface: at least an hour.
5. **Smoke.** Smoke the fish for up to 2 hours at around 90 F, with alder
   chips. The guide names alder, maple, oak, hickory, birch and fruit
   woods, and rules out fir, spruce, pine and cedar, whose smoke leaves an
   unpleasant taste. Red alder is one of the easiest trees to find around
   Silverdale (the locale's `red_alder` record); its wood still has to be
   dry, clean and free of mould (general practice).
6. **Cook.** Turn up the heat until the thickest piece reaches 160 F on
   the thermometer, pushed into it through a hole in the smoker wall so
   the door stays shut, and hold it there for 30 minutes. The guide warns that small
   smokers often cannot get hot enough: if yours cannot reach an air
   temperature of 200 to 225 F, move the fish to the oven within 2 hours
   of smoking, and hold the thickest piece at 160 F for 30 minutes there.
7. **Cool and store.** Let it cool, wrap it in paper towel or cloth
   rather than plastic so it does not sweat and grow mould, and refrigerate
   it at 38 F or below. Eat it within 2 weeks, or wrap it tightly and
   freeze it, where the guide says it keeps up to a year.

Your refrigerator thermometer reads 37 F, so the fridge is cold enough.
Had it read above 40 F, the guide's rule is to keep the fish frozen
instead.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Dry-cured whole hams, dry fermented sausages, cold-smoked fish and
  gravlax** are raw-food processes that depend on controlled curing,
  drying and handling. A country ham is aged for months and loses at
  least 18 percent of its weight (FSIS), and after the 1994 salami
  outbreak FSIS wrote specific processing rules for dry sausages, which
  must be followed or the product heat-treated. A home cook has
  no way to check the result, so these are for a class and a tested
  method, not a first attempt (our reading).
- **Wild game for curing or smoking** is cooked to a safe temperature,
  because neither curing, smoking, drying nor freezing reliably deals with
  its *Trichinella* (CDC).
- **Selling cured or smoked meat** is regulated. FSIS inspects sausages
  sold across state lines, and a state's health or agriculture department
  may govern sausage made in a shop; ask before you sell any (general
  practice for the asking).
- **Anyone at higher risk from *Listeria*** follows the CDC's advice on
  smoked fish and dry sausages above.

## Traps

- **"It's smoked, so it'll keep."** Smoke is flavour and a little
  protection, not preservation. Keep it cold.
- **"Pink salt from the spice aisle."** Himalayan pink salt is not curing
  salt, and curing salt is not table salt.
- **"A bit more cure for safety."** Nitrite is measured, never guessed.
- **"Pour the cure into the salt shaker."** That is how five people ended
  up in hospital in 2002.
- **"Curing kills the worms."** Not reliably, says the CDC. Cook pork and
  wild game.
- **"The bacon can cure in the garage; it's cold out."** Cure in the
  refrigerator, at 40 F.
- **"The smoker says 225, so the fish is done."** The food thermometer
  decides, in the thickest piece, held for 30 minutes.
- **"It's raining; I'll run the smoker in the garage."** Never in a
  closed space.
- **"A clean trash can makes a fine smoker."** Not a galvanized one.

## How the game models it

The game has salt-cured meat, but no curing salt and no smoking.

- **Two salt-cured meat recipes.** On the Crafting page, Make Jerky turns
  two Raw Mutton, one Salt and one Spice Mix into four Jerky, and Dry Meat
  turns two Raw Mutton and one Salt into four Dried Meat, an item the game
  describes as "Salt-cured preserved meat" (`data/recipes.csv`,
  `data/items.csv`). Both are made at the home's Stove, which stands in
  the default home's kitchen (`data/machines/home.ron`), and both are
  level-1 Cooking recipes, which anyone can make. Make Jerky needs a
  Utility Knife in your backpack (the Crafting page lists it as Knife
  Utility); Dry Meat needs no tool (`data/crafting/tools.ron`). Make
  Jerky's description quotes the Food Safety and Inspection Service's
  rule to heat the meat to 160 F before drying it, but the craft itself
  has no heating step, thickness, temperature or drying time to get right.
- **Under the default settings.** In the default Dev play mode (Settings >
  Gameplay > Play mode) a craft uses up none of its parts and wears no
  tool, but the Craft button still waits until the parts are in your
  backpack, or in your home's storage while you are at home, and any tool
  it needs is in your backpack (`src/gui/pages/crafting.rs`,
  `src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`). The page's "Dev: stock all materials"
  button fills your backpack with every recipe's parts, Raw Mutton and
  Salt included, but no tools; a new character's starting kit holds the
  Utility Knife (`data/world/player.ron`).
- **No curing salt.** No item contains nitrite. The item list has
  Saltpeter, which it describes as, among other things, "a meat cure",
  but no recipe uses it (`data/items.csv`, `data/recipes.csv`).
- **No smoking.** There is no smoker or smokehouse, no recipe smokes meat
  or fish, and no recipe uses Raw Fish at all (`data/recipes.csv`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: curing
salt and how carefully it must be measured, smoke and fire, thermometers
and safe internal temperatures, parasites, and the organisms that salt,
nitrite and cold hold back. Botulism is not in the game: a record for its
toxin sits in `data/chemistry/toxins.csv`, but no game code reads it.

## You own this when

- You can say how salt preserves, and why home salting alone is not
  enough to make meat or fish safe.
- You keep curing salt in its own labelled container, out of children's
  reach, and measure it exactly by a tested recipe.
- You know Prague Powder #1 from #2, and either from Himalayan salt.
- You cure in the refrigerator, at 40 F, for the tested time.
- You can say why smoke is not a preservative, and you use two
  thermometers every time you smoke.
- You know the safe internal temperatures: 145 F and a rest, 160 F, 165
  F, and 160 F for 30 minutes for fish.
- You refrigerate smoked food within 2 hours and know how long each kind
  keeps.
- You cook pork and wild game, and you know why cold-smoked fish and dry
  sausages are not beginner's food.
- You run a smoker only outdoors, away from buildings.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- Keeping curing salt out of shakers and food jars and out of children's
  reach, read from HRSA's general poison advice; measuring it for every
  batch and never adding extra; calling 911 for someone short of breath,
  turning bluish, confused or collapsing, read from the CDC report.
- That wild game for curing or smoking simply gets cooked, read from the
  CDC page.
- Running a smoker only outdoors, never in a garage, basement or shed,
  read from the CDC's rules for charcoal and generators.
- That a cut surface of a dry-cured ham is a different food from the
  rind; that without salt or nitrite the cold has to do all the work.
- That cold smoking is not a beginner's method.
- That dry-cured hams, dry sausages, cold-smoked fish and gravlax are for
  a class and a tested method, not a first attempt; asking before selling
  cured or smoked meat.
- That smoking wood must be dry, clean and free of mould.
