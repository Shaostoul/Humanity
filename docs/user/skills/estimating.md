# Estimating

An estimate is a measurement made with whatever you happen to be
carrying, which is usually your body, your eyes, your ears and some
arithmetic. Done well, it gets you close enough to decide: whether the
fence wire on the spool will reach the corner, whether the storm is
coming this way, whether the trailer can take the load, whether the
number your calculator just printed is believable.

The goal this guide sets is to get within ten percent with no
instrument. That is a target, not a promise. Some methods here can beat
it on a good day and some can miss it by a factor of three, and the only
way to know which you are doing is to check your estimates against real
measurements and keep score. That habit, more than any trick, is what
makes a person good at estimating.

Most of the numbers here come from United States government
publications, which are in the public domain: the Army's map reading
and land navigation manual, the National Weather Service, the US
Geological Survey, the National Institute of Standards and Technology
(NIST), the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) and the US Department
of Agriculture (USDA). Where something is a working rule or plain
arithmetic rather than a published figure, the text says so.

A note on how things are written. This file stays plain ASCII, so
temperatures are written "4 C" rather than with a degree symbol.

## First: where you do not estimate

Estimating is for decisions where being ten or twenty percent out costs
you a second trip or a bit of waste. It is not for decisions where being
out hurts somebody.

- **Medicine doses, for people or animals.** Measure, and copy the unit
  exactly. [Units and Converting Them](units_and_converting_them.md)
  explains why a single wrong letter is a thousandfold error.
- **Canning times, temperatures and acidity.** These come from tested
  processes and are followed as written; see
  [Why Canning Has Rules](why_canning_has_rules.md).
- **Anything structural or electrical where a rating exists.** A rope,
  a jack, a ladder, a beam and a cable each have a rated load. Read the
  rating; do not guess the load and hope.
- **Distances to danger.** How close you can stand to a power line, a
  falling tree or a fire is not something to judge by eye on the day.

The Army manual this guide leans on says it plainly about one of its
own methods, the artillery's quick angle-based range rule: it involves
only estimations and is not accurate enough for survey purposes.
Estimating is the first answer, and it tells you when you need a better
one.

## Why estimate at all, if you could measure?

Three reasons, and the third is the one people forget.

1. **You have no instrument.** No tape, no scale, no clock. This is the
   obvious case.
2. **Measuring costs more than the decision is worth.** You do not need
   a surveyor to tell you whether a wheelbarrow of compost will cover a
   bed.
3. **An estimate is how you catch a wrong measurement.** A calculator,
   a tape read from the wrong end, a scale left in pounds when you
   wanted kilograms: each produces a confident, precise, wrong number.
   If you guessed "about 30" before you measured and the tape says 300,
   one of you is wrong and you look again. Without the guess you would
   have believed the tape. [Units and Converting
   Them](units_and_converting_them.md) is a whole guide about errors
   that look like ordinary numbers; a rough estimate made first is the
   cheapest defence against them.

So the habit worth building is: **guess first, then measure, then
compare.** The guess costs nothing and the comparison teaches you
something every time.

## Your body is the instrument, so calibrate it

Every estimating method starts from something you know the size of.
The thing you always have with you is yourself, but the textbook figures
for a "typical" stride or hand are somebody else's. Measure your own,
once, and write the numbers down somewhere you will not lose them.

### Your pace

The US Army's Map Reading and Land Navigation manual (FM 3-25.26, 2001
edition) teaches distance by pace count, and its method is worth copying
exactly. That edition has since been superseded, most recently by TC
3-25.26 (November 2013), which carries the same pace count, range
estimation and Table 5-1 text; this guide cites the 2001 edition because
it is approved for public release and the 2013 one is not.

1. **Count paces, where a pace is one natural step.** The manual puts
   a pace at about 30 inches. You do not need that number; you need your
   own count.
2. **Walk a course whose length you know.** The manual's pace courses
   run from 100 to 600 metres. Count the paces it takes you, then divide
   by the number of hundreds: on a 600 metre course, divide the total by
   6. The answer is your paces per 100 metres.
3. **Calibrate on ground like the ground you will use it on.** The
   manual says it does no good to walk a course on flat ground and then
   use that count on hills.

Walk the course more than once and average the counts. One walk tells
you a number; three walks tell you how much that number wobbles, which
is the size of your error.

If you cannot lay out 100 metres, a 100 foot tape walked out and back a
few times will do, as long as you add up the total distance and the
total paces. That is our suggestion rather than the manual's, and the
reason it works is simple: a miscount of one pace matters less over a
long walk than a short one.

**Do not keep the count in your head.** The manual is firm about this:
put a pebble in your pocket every time you have walked 100 metres, tie
knots in a string, or put marks in a notebook, and never try to remember
the count. A count kept in your head is lost the moment your attention
goes to something else, and a long walk gives it plenty of chances.

**Know what changes your pace.** The manual lists the conditions:

- **Slopes.** Your pace lengthens going downhill and shortens going up.
  Its example is a person who takes 120 paces for 100 metres on the flat
  needing 130 or more on an upslope.
- **Wind.** A head wind shortens the pace and a tail wind lengthens it.
- **Surfaces.** Sand, gravel, mud and snow shorten it.
- **Weather.** Falling rain, snow or ice shortens it.
- **Clothing.** Heavy clothing and boots with poor grip change it.
- **Visibility.** Fog, rain and darkness shorten it. The manual
  recommends a separate night pace count from a measured 100 metre
  course, because people take smaller steps in the dark.

For ground you walk often, the manual's suggestion for jungle
navigators works anywhere: a personal pace table, with your paces per
100 metres on each kind of ground you meet. Write it on the inside cover
of your notebook.

**Worked example.** Suppose your count is 120 paces per 100 metres on
the flat. You pace along the edge of a field and count 186. The edge is
about 186 / 120 x 100 = 155 metres. If the field ran uphill, the real
distance is a little less than that, because your steps were short.

### Your hand, your height, your reach

The same idea works for everything else about you. Measure once:

- your height, and the height of your eyes above the ground;
- your arm span, fingertip to fingertip;
- the span of your open hand, thumb tip to little finger tip;
- the width of your thumb, and the length from your elbow to your
  fingertips.

None of these needs a published figure, because the published figure
would be for somebody else. For angles, which is what your hand at
arm's length really measures, the section "Measuring an angle with your
hand" in [The Sky as an Instrument](the_sky_as_an_instrument.md) shows
how to calibrate your fingers and fist against stars whose spacing is
known.

## Two kinds of error, and why it matters which you have

NIST, the US national measurement institute, separates measurement
error into two parts, and the distinction is the most useful idea in
this guide. The definitions below are from its Technical Note 1297,
which reproduces them from the international vocabulary of metrology
(the VIM).

- **Random error** is the scatter. Pace the same field three times and
  get 184, 189 and 186: that spread is random error. NIST defines it as
  the result of one measurement minus the average you would get from a
  great many repeats.
- **Systematic error** is the lean. If every one of your estimates of
  distance by eye comes out 15 percent short, that is systematic error.
  NIST defines it as the average of all those repeats minus the true
  value.

The two behave completely differently:

- **Averaging shrinks random error and does nothing for systematic
  error.** TN 1297 does not say this in so many words; it follows from
  its definitions, and it is our reading. Pace the field ten times and
  the average settles down. If your pace count was calibrated on flat
  ground and the field is a hill, the average settles down on the wrong
  number.
- **Systematic error can be corrected, but only once you have measured
  it.** NIST calls this a correction: a value added to compensate for
  systematic error, equal to the negative of the error you estimated.
  NIST also notes, fairly, that because the systematic error is never
  known perfectly, the correction can never be complete either.

In practice: keep a small log of your estimates beside the measured
answers. After a dozen entries, look at whether your misses scatter on
both sides (random) or lean one way (systematic). If you always
underestimate distance across open water by a third, you now know to add
half again to your first impression over water. That log is a record of
the kind described in [Keeping Records](keeping_records.md), and it is
the only way your estimating gets better rather than just more
confident.

## Distance by eye

The Army manual gives two methods for estimating range when pacing is
not possible.

### The 100 metre unit

Learn what 100 metres looks like on the ground: pace it out on a road or
a field edge until you can picture it. A US football field is 100 yards
between the goal lines, a little over 91 metres, which is close enough
to serve as the picture if you have stood on one.

Then, for distances up to 500 metres, count how many of those 100 metre
units would fit between you and the object. Beyond 500 metres, the
manual's rule is to pick a point halfway, count the units to the
halfway point, and double it. The manual gives the rule without the
reason. Ours: the units shrink with distance until they are too small to
count, and the halfway point is near enough that they can still be
pictured.

### What fools the eye

The manual includes a table of conditions that push estimates one way
or the other. In short:

**You will tend to underestimate the distance (think things are
closer than they are) when:**

- most of the object is visible and its outline is clear;
- you are looking across a dip in the ground that is mostly hidden, or
  looking down from high ground;
- you are looking down a straight, open road or along a railway;
- you are looking over a uniform surface such as water, snow, desert or
  a grain field;
- the light is bright or the sun is behind you;
- the object stands out sharply against its background;
- you are looking through the clear air of high altitudes.

**You will tend to overestimate the distance (think things are further
than they are) when:**

- only a small part of the object can be seen, or it is small compared
  with its surroundings;
- you are looking across a dip in the ground that you can see all of;
- your view is hemmed in, as along a street, a gully or a forest trail;
- you are looking up from low ground toward high ground;
- the light is poor, at dawn or dusk, or in rain, snow or fog;
- the sun is in your eyes;
- the object blends into the background or the terrain.

How big can the error get? The manual, describing desert conditions
with clear air and strong light, says they often lead to gross
underestimates of range, and that errors of up to 200 or 300 percent are
not uncommon. The error runs one way there: things look much closer than
they are. That is not ten percent. It is the reason to check by pacing
whenever you can.

### How to get better at it

The manual's training advice is the best advice in this guide: estimate
the distance, then pace it. It notes that finding out the real distance
for yourself makes a bigger impression than being told it. Do it on
walks you take anyway. Estimate the distance to the next gate, then
count. It takes no extra time, and your eye gets corrected every day
instead of once a year.

## Distance by sound

Light arrives almost instantly. Sound does not. So the gap between a
flash and its sound measures distance.

- The **National Weather Service** puts it at about 5 seconds for sound
  to travel a mile: count the seconds from the lightning flash to the
  thunder, and divide by 5 for the distance in miles. 15 seconds is
  about 3 miles.
- The **Army manual** uses 330 metres per second: multiply the seconds
  by 330 for the distance in metres.

The two rules agree to within about 3 percent: a mile in 5 seconds is
about 322 metres a second, against the manual's 330. To count without a
watch, the manual suggests a steady "one-thousand-one,
one-thousand-two", and if the count passes ten, starting over at one,
which means you also have to keep track of how many tens you have
counted.

The Weather Service adds the part that matters: count from somewhere
safe, and if you can hear thunder at all, you are probably within
striking distance of the storm. It says thunder can be heard only about
10 miles from the strike, and that the sound of thunder is a warning to
anyone outside to get to a safe place immediately. So the count is not a
reason to stay out and watch. Go in first, then count if you want to.
The flash-to-bang count tells you how far away the last strike was. It
does not tell you that you are safe.

## Height, with a stick or a shadow

Both methods below are geometry, not rules of thumb. They work exactly,
to the limit of how carefully you sight and measure, because they rest
on similar triangles: two triangles with the same angles have their
sides in the same proportion.

### The stick method

1. Find a straight stick. Hold it upright in your fist at arm's length
   and cut or mark it so that the part above your fist is as long as
   the distance from your eye to your fist.
2. Hold it out vertically at arm's length and walk toward or away from
   the tree until the top of the stick lines up with the top of the tree
   and the top of your fist lines up with its base.
3. Your distance from the base of the tree is now its height. Pace it.

Why it works: the stick and the tree make two similar triangles with
your eye at the shared corner. The stick's height divided by its
distance from your eye equals the tree's height divided by its distance
from your eye. You made the stick's height equal to its distance, so the
tree's height equals its distance.

It assumes level ground and an upright tree. On a slope, or with a
leaning tree, the answer is off, and you should say so when you write
it down.

### The shadow method

On a sunny day, measure your own height and the length of your shadow,
then pace or measure the length of the tree's shadow. The tree's height
is its shadow length times your height divided by your shadow length.
Measure both shadows within a few minutes of each other, because shadows
change length as the sun moves, and on level ground, because a slope
stretches or shrinks a shadow.

**Worked example.** You are 1.7 m tall and your shadow is 2.0 m. The
tree's shadow paces out at 18 m. The tree is about 18 x 1.7 / 2.0 =
15.3 m tall.

## Weight, from volume

Many of the heavy things on a homestead are water, or mostly water, and
water is the one material whose weight you can estimate from its volume
almost exactly.

The US Geological Survey gives the density of water as roughly 1 gram
per millilitre, changing a little with temperature, and tabulates it at
62.424 pounds per cubic foot at 4 C. From there:

- **1 litre of water is about 1 kilogram.** It is worth knowing by
  heart.
- **A US gallon of water is about 8.3 pounds.** NIST defines the US
  gallon as 231 cubic inches, and a cubic foot is 1,728 cubic inches, so
  a cubic foot holds 1,728 / 231 = 7.48 gallons. Divide 62.4 pounds by
  7.48 and you get 8.3 pounds a gallon. That is arithmetic on the two
  published figures.
- **A cubic metre of water is about a tonne.**

**Worked examples.**

- A 55 gallon drum, full of water: 55 x 8.3 = about 460 pounds, or
  210 kg. This is why you move drums empty and fill them where they will
  stand.
- A 1,000 gallon tank: about 8,300 pounds, close to four tonnes, before
  you count the tank. Whatever it stands on carries that. Check what the
  pad, the deck or the floor is rated for before you fill it.
- A 5 gallon bucket of water: about 42 pounds, which is a useful thing
  to know before you promise to carry two of them up a hill.

This also gives you a way to check a kitchen scale with no reference
weight: weigh a litre of water from a measuring jug. Your answer will
only be as good as the jug's marking, so treat it as a check for a scale
that is badly wrong, not as a calibration.

## Counting many things without counting them all

When there are too many to count, count a part and scale up.

- **Seeds.** Count out 100 seeds and weigh them, then weigh the whole
  lot. Number of seeds = 100 x total weight / weight of the hundred.
  A kitchen scale is too coarse for small seeds unless you count a
  larger sample; the larger the sample, the smaller the error.
- **Plants in a field or bed.** Count the plants in one metre of row,
  in two or three places, average, and multiply by the total length of
  row. Do not count only the best-looking stretch, which is an easy way
  to get this wrong: the sample has to look like the whole.
- **A flock, a crowd, a pile of firewood.** Count a group of ten, see
  how much room ten takes up, and count how many of those groups fit.

The principle in each is the same: the sample must be typical, and
more than one sample tells you how much the parts differ.

## Breaking a big unknown into small knowns

Some quantities cannot be seen at all, such as how much water a house
uses or how much food a family eats in a year. The method is to break
the unknown into pieces you can each estimate, then multiply. These are
often called Fermi problems, after the physicist Enrico Fermi.

### A worked example from the USDA

The USDA's Complete Guide to Home Canning includes a worksheet for
deciding how much food to put up for a year, and it is this method
exactly. Its example, for a family of four eating fruit:

| Step | Arithmetic | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Servings a week, per person | (a family decision) | 12 |
| Servings a week, family | 12 x 4 people | 48 |
| Cups a week | 48 x 1/2 cup serving | 24 |
| Quarts a week | 24 cups / 4 | 6 |
| Quarts a year | 6 x 36 weeks the fruit is served | 216 |

The guide's example then splits those 216 quarts into 72 canned and 144
frozen. Its general advice is to can no more than you will use within a
year.

Notice what makes this work. Every line is something you can picture or
look up, and the hard question ("how much fruit do we eat?") never has
to be answered in one go.

Notice also how errors travel through it. Each line multiplies the
previous one, so if four of the numbers were each 10 percent too high,
the answer is 1.1 x 1.1 x 1.1 x 1.1 = 1.46 times too high, nearly half
again. In practice the errors are rarely all in the same direction and
partly cancel, but the lesson holds: a long chain needs a check against
something independent. For the canning plan, the independent check is
how many jars you actually opened last year, which is a reason to keep
the record (see [Keeping Records](keeping_records.md)).

### A worked example you can check against a published figure

The EPA says a faucet dripping once a second can waste more than 3,000
gallons a year. You can estimate your own drip and see how close you
come.

1. Count the drips in one minute.
2. Catch drips in a teaspoon and count how many fill it. NIST gives the
   practical metric equivalent of a US teaspoon as 5 mL.
3. Multiply out to a year. There are 60 x 60 x 24 x 365 = 31,536,000
   seconds in a year.

Suppose your tap drips once a second and 15 drips fill the teaspoon.
Each drip is 5 / 15 = 1/3 mL. A year of drips is 31,536,000 x 1/3 mL =
about 10,500 litres, and at 3.785 litres to the US gallon (NIST) that is
about 2,780 gallons. The EPA's figure for a drip a second is "more than
3,000 gallons", so this estimate lands within about ten percent of it.
The difference is the size of the drip, which the EPA page does not
state, and which is the one number in the chain that you measured
rather than assumed.

### Checking an estimate against a published average

The EPA gives the average American family's water use at home as more
than 300 gallons a day, with roughly 70 percent of it used indoors.
Estimate your own household's daily use the same way, piece by piece:
how long the showers run and how much a shower gives in a minute (catch
it in a bucket for ten seconds), how many loads of laundry, how much
watering. Then compare.

If you land far from the average, that is not proof you are wrong.
Households differ, and the EPA notes that outdoor use is much higher in
dry places. It is a prompt to look at which piece of your chain is
carrying the difference. If you have a water meter, read it at the same
time on two days and you have the real answer to check against.

## Speed of wind, from what it moves

Some estimates are made by matching what you see to a described scale.
The Beaufort wind scale is the classic one. The National Weather
Service's version gives, for each force, what it looks like on land and
at sea and the range of speeds it corresponds to. Some of its land
descriptions:

| Force | Speed (mph) | What you see on land |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 to 1 | Calm; smoke rises vertically |
| 2 | 4 to 7 | Wind felt on face; leaves rustle |
| 3 | 8 to 12 | Leaves and small twigs in constant motion; a light flag extends |
| 4 | 13 to 18 | Dust and loose paper raised; small branches move |
| 5 | 19 to 24 | Small trees in leaf begin to sway |
| 6 | 25 to 31 | Large branches in motion; umbrellas used with difficulty |
| 7 | 32 to 38 | Whole trees in motion; inconvenient to walk against the wind |
| 8 | 39 to 46 | Twigs broken off trees; progress generally impeded |

The same Weather Service table describes the sea at each force, for
anyone estimating wind from a boat or a shore. The metres-per-second
column is our conversion of its mph figures (1 mph is exactly 0.44704
metres a second, so 1 metre a second is about 2.24 mph):

| Force | Speed (m/s) | What you see at sea |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 0 to 0.4 | Sea like a mirror |
| 1 | 0.4 to 1.3 | Ripples like scales, but no foam crests |
| 2 | 1.8 to 3.1 | Small wavelets; crests look glassy and do not break |
| 3 | 3.6 to 5.4 | Large wavelets; crests begin to break; perhaps scattered white horses |
| 4 | 5.8 to 8.0 | Small waves, becoming larger; fairly frequent white horses |
| 5 | 8.5 to 10.7 | Moderate waves, longer in form; many white horses |
| 6 | 11.2 to 13.9 | Large waves begin to form; white foam crests more extensive everywhere |
| 7 | 14.3 to 17.0 | Sea heaps up; foam from breaking waves blown in streaks along the wind |
| 8 | 17.4 to 20.6 | Moderately high, longer waves; crest edges break into spindrift; foam in well-marked streaks |

The scale teaches a habit worth copying everywhere: **the answer is a
range, not a number.** "Force 4, somewhere between 13 and 18 mph" is an
honest estimate. "15 mph" from looking at a tree is a guess pretending
to be a measurement.

## Say it as a range

When you estimate, say how sure you are. "About 150 metres, give or take
20" is more useful than "150 metres", because whoever acts on it knows
how much margin to leave. It is also checkable: when you measure, either
the true value landed inside your range or it did not. Keep score of
that, and you learn whether your ranges are honest. If the truth lands
outside your stated range more than now and then, your ranges are too
narrow.

Writing an estimate with too many digits is a related mistake. "153.7
metres" from a pace count claims a precision the method does not have.
[Units and Converting Them](units_and_converting_them.md) covers why the
number of digits you write is a claim about how well you know the
number.

## Estimate twice, two ways

The best check on an estimate is a second estimate made by a different
method, because two different methods are less likely to share the
same error.

- The height of a tree by the stick method and by its shadow.
- The length of a field by pace count and by the 100 metre unit.
- A household's water use by adding up its pieces and by reading the
  meter.

If the two agree, you can trust either one more. If they disagree by
more than your error on each, one of them has a systematic error, and
finding out which teaches you more than either answer would.

## Area and volume make small errors bigger

A 10 percent error on a length becomes a bigger error on anything
calculated from it.

- **Area** multiplies two lengths. If both sides of a garden are paced
  10 percent long, the area comes out 1.1 x 1.1 = 1.21 times too big,
  about 21 percent.
- **Volume** multiplies three. Ten percent on each of three sides is
  1.1 x 1.1 x 1.1 = 1.33, a third too big.

So when you are estimating how much compost fills a bed, or how much
water a pond holds, the ten percent you got on each side is not ten
percent on the answer. Be more careful with the lengths, or leave more
margin in the order. [Units and Converting
Them](units_and_converting_them.md) has a whole section on why area and
volume do not scale the way length does.

**Worked example.** You pace a bed at 40 paces by 25 paces, with your
count of 120 paces per 100 metres. That is 33.3 m by 20.8 m, about 690
square metres. If each length could be 10 percent out either way, the
area could be anywhere from about 560 to 840 square metres. That range
is the honest estimate.

## How the game models it

The game does not test your eye. It does do one kind of estimating in
front of you, and doing it yourself beside it is good practice.

- **The power meters on the Construction page are a rate-times-time
  estimate.** Under "Usage + self-sufficiency" the page says each machine
  is counted at its average draw over a day, and the energy for the day
  is that average multiplied by 24 hours. A solar panel whose data gives
  no yearly average output is credited with its peak output for a
  representative day of 4.5 hours of full sun. The machines, their
  draws and their averages come from `data/machines/home.ron`. Before you read the meter,
  add up a few machines yourself and see how close you get.
- **The Buildability checks on the same page do the comparison for
  you.** The "Energy balance" check sets what the home makes in a day
  against what it uses, which is the comparison you would make on paper
  with your own estimates.
- **The sea is built from the wind.** The live weather panel (F11) sets
  a wind speed, shown in metres per second, and the game builds its
  ocean waves from that speed. So the sea table in the Beaufort section
  above, which is given in metres per second for this reason, is
  something you can look for there: set a speed without looking at the
  number, judge the force from the waves, then check. How closely its
  waves match a real sea at each force is not something this guide has
  checked, so treat it as practice in looking, not as a reference.

What the game does not model: pacing, distance by eye, or the
optical effects that fool the eye in real terrain. Those you have to
practise outside.

## You own this when

- You know your own paces per 100 metres on the ground you walk, and
  you never keep the count in your head.
- You guess before you measure, every time, and you notice when the
  measurement surprises you.
- You can say whether your misses are scatter or lean, because you have
  a log of estimates and measurements to look at.
- You can find the height of a tree two ways and say why both work.
- You know that a litre of water is about a kilogram and a US gallon
  about 8.3 pounds, and you estimate the weight of a full tank before
  you fill it.
- You break a large unknown into pieces you can picture, and you check
  the result against something independent.
- You give an estimate as a range, and you can say how often the truth
  lands inside your ranges.
- You know when not to estimate at all.
