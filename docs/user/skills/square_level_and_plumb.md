# Square, Level and Plumb

Almost everything you build has to be three things. **Level**: flat
in the horizontal, so a floor does not tilt and water does what you
meant it to. **Plumb**: truly vertical, so a wall or a post carries
its load straight down instead of leaning. **Square**: corners at
right angles, so the next piece fits, the roof sits on the walls, and
the door closes.

None of this needs more than school geometry and a few cheap tools: a
level, a plumb bob, a tape, string and stakes. What it does need is
the habit of checking, because the errors are small at the corner and
large at the far end. A wall one degree out of square at one corner is
70 millimetres out at the end of a 4 metre run, and every piece built
on top inherits it.

The methods in this guide come from the US Army's carpentry manual (FM
5-426) and the armed services' joint hand tool manual (TM 9-243), both
approved for public release. The safety rules come from the
Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA) and the Pipeline
and Hazardous Materials Safety Administration (PHMSA), and the drainage
advice from the Environmental
Protection Agency (EPA) and the federal rules for installing
manufactured homes. All are United States government publications, in
the public domain. Where something is arithmetic, general practice or
our own example, the text says so.

## Before you dig or climb

Laying out a building usually ends with digging for footings, and
building it usually means ladders. Both have rules that come before
any geometry.

**Call before you dig, every time.** PHMSA, the federal agency for
pipeline safety, says every digging project needs a call to the
one-call centre first, even small projects like planting trees or
shrubs. In the United States you dial 811, and all states have set up
one-call centres and require by law that you call 48 to 72 hours
before digging. The utility companies then mark where their buried
lines are with flags or paint. The call is free. PHMSA's four steps
are: call, wait the required time for the lines to be marked, respect
the marks, and dig with care. OSHA's excavation rule for workplaces
(29 CFR 1926.651) says the same in its own terms: the estimated
location of sewer, telephone, fuel, electric and water lines is to be
determined before an excavation is opened. A gas line or a buried
power cable is not something to find with a spade.

**The marks are an estimate, and they do not show everything.** OSHA's
word for what the locate gives is the "estimated location", and its
rule goes on: when digging approaches it, "the exact location of the
installations shall be determined by safe and acceptable means"
(29 CFR 1926.651(b)(3)). At home that means digging by hand, gently,
near the marks, not with a machine (general practice). And the utility
companies mark only their own lines. Lines that you or an earlier
owner put in, such as a cable to a shed or a pump, a pipe to a well, a
septic line, or a gas line to a propane tank, are generally not marked;
find them from your own records or with a private locating service
before you dig (general practice).

**Trenches collapse.** OSHA (29 CFR 1926.652) requires anyone working
in an excavation to be protected from cave-ins by a designed
protective system, such as shoring or sloped sides, unless the
excavation is entirely in stable rock, or is less than 5 feet (1.52 m)
deep and a "competent person" has examined the ground and found no
sign of a possible cave-in. That is a defined term, not a compliment:
OSHA means someone capable of identifying the hazards and with the
authority to put them right at once (29 CFR 1926.650), in practice a
person trained in excavation work. A homeowner reading this guide is
not one, so the 5 foot exception is not yours to use. A separate
section, 29 CFR 1926.651(j)(2), requires the dug soil, and materials
and equipment, to be kept at least 2 feet (0.61 m) back from the edge,
or held back by retaining devices, so they cannot fall or roll in.

A footing trench for a shed is usually shallow; a trench for a water
line below the frost depth may not be. **Do not get into any trench
deeper than your waist.** A deeper one is past this guide: it needs a
protective system and someone qualified to design it (the waist limit
is general practice, set well below OSHA's 5 feet because you are not
the competent person that rule depends on).

**Ladders.** Set a straight or extension ladder so its foot is out
from the wall by about one quarter of its working length, which is
OSHA's rule (29 CFR 1926.1053) and works out at about 75 degrees. The
geometry is in the worked examples below; the rest of the ladder rules
are in [Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md).

**Walls standing on their own fall over.** The Army's carpentry manual
has framed walls plumbed and straightened and held with temporary
braces before the permanent braces and rafters go on, and it says good
bracing keeps corners square and plumb. A wall frame that is standing
but not yet braced can be pushed over by wind or a knock (general
practice); brace it the moment it stands.

## Level: true horizontal

The Army's hand tool manual (TM 9-243) defines levels as tools for
proving whether a surface is truly horizontal or truly vertical. A
carpenter's level has three glass vials: one horizontal, one vertical,
and one at 45 degrees. With the level on a horizontal surface, the
bubble in the horizontal vial should sit between the two etched lines;
if it does not, the surface is not level.

### Check the level before you trust it

A level that has been dropped can lie. TM 9-243's check is simple:
place the level on a known level surface and note the position of the
bubble, then reverse the level end for end and look again. If the
relative position of the bubble is the same for both readings, the
level is accurate. If it is not, the level is not to be trusted for
anything that matters until it is adjusted or replaced (general
practice).

### Level over a distance

A carpenter's level is about as long as your arm. Two points across a
building site are further apart than that. Three ways to carry a level
line across a distance:

- **A line level.** TM 9-243 describes it as a single vial with a hook
  at each end, hung on a cord, which must be tightly stretched. The
  Army's carpentry manual adds that it gives the greatest accuracy when
  it is placed halfway between the two points being levelled. The
  reason (our explanation) is that a string sags under its own weight
  and the level's. Near one end the sag tilts the string, but at the
  middle of a gently sagging string, its slope matches the slope of a
  straight line between the two ends, which is what you want to know.
- **A straightedge and a level.** A long, straight board with the level
  on top extends the level's reach. Check the board is straight by
  sighting along it (general practice).
- **A water level.** A long clear tube, filled with water and free of
  air bubbles: the water at the two ends settles at the same height,
  however the tube runs between them. It needs no line of sight and
  works around corners. This is general practice and plain physics, not
  from the manuals read here.

## Plumb: true vertical

A plumb bob is a weight on a string. TM 9-243 calls it a precision
instrument for establishing a true vertical, and the reason it works
is that gravity pulls the hanging weight straight down. The vertical
vial of a carpenter's level does the same job over its own length.

Both Army manuals give the same way to plumb a post with a plumb bob:

1. Lay a rule on top of the post so that 2 inches of it stick out
   beyond the face you are checking.
2. Hang the plumb bob's string over the end of the rule, so it hangs 2
   inches from the post, with the bob near the bottom.
3. Measure from the post to the string at the bottom. If it is 2
   inches there too, the post is plumb on that face. If not, move the
   post until it is, then nail a temporary brace.
4. Repeat on the next face. A post is plumb only when it is plumb on
   two faces at right angles.

The carpentry manual adds that if you have no plumb bob or level, a
rock, half a brick or a small piece of metal on a string will do.

The plumb bob also carries a point straight down. When string lines
cross above a corner stake, the carpentry manual has you hold a plumb
bob over the stake and adjust the lines until they touch the plumb
line, so the crossing point in the air is exactly above the mark on the
ground.

## Square: the right angle

### The 3-4-5 triangle

Pythagoras's theorem says that in a right-angled triangle, the square
of the longest side equals the sum of the squares of the other two. The
smallest whole-number case is 3, 4 and 5: 3 x 3 + 4 x 4 = 9 + 16 = 25 =
5 x 5. It works backwards too, and that is what makes it useful: **if
you make a triangle with sides 3, 4 and 5, the angle between the 3 and
the 4 is a right angle.** No protractor needed, only a tape.

The Army's carpentry manual calls this the 6-8-10 method, which is the
same triangle doubled:

1. From the corner, measure 6 feet along one line and mark it.
2. Measure 8 feet along the other line and mark it.
3. Adjust the second line until the distance between the two marks is
   exactly 10 feet. The corner is now square.

Any multiple works: the manual gives 12-16-20 for a layout 50 feet by
100 feet. In metres the same triangle is 3, 4 and 5 m, or 6, 8 and 10 m
for something big. The manual's rule for accuracy is never to start
with a measurement of less than 6 feet (about 1.8 m). The reason, which
is our arithmetic: a tape can only be read and held so precisely, and
the same few millimetres of slack are a much bigger share of a short
side than of a long one. Use the biggest triangle the site allows.

For small work, a box or a bench, use a framing square or a try square
instead. Check the square first: a dropped square is no longer square,
and the test for one is in [Hand Tools](../making/hand_tools.md).

### Check the diagonals

The carpentry manual's second method is to measure the diagonals. In a
rectangle, the two diagonals, corner to opposite corner, are equal. If
they are not, the corners are not square, and you move the lines until
the diagonals match.

One condition, which is geometry rather than the manual's wording:
equal diagonals prove a rectangle only when the opposite sides are
already equal. So measure the sides first, then the diagonals. With
both, the shape is a true rectangle.

The diagonal check is sensitive. Worked out for a 4 metre square
(our arithmetic):

| Corner angle | Diagonal one | Diagonal two | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 90 degrees | 5.657 m | 5.657 m | 0 |
| 89.5 degrees | 5.682 m | 5.632 m | about 5 cm |
| 89 degrees | 5.706 m | 5.607 m | about 10 cm |

Half a degree shows up as 5 centimetres on the tape. That is why the diagonal check is the last
thing you do before you pour concrete or nail down a frame.

### How far one degree goes

The table below is plain trigonometry: how far a line drifts sideways
when it starts out of square by a given angle.

| Error at the corner | After 1 m | After 4 m | After 10 m |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.5 degrees | 9 mm | 35 mm | 87 mm |
| 1 degree | 17 mm | 70 mm | 175 mm |

The lesson is to square from the longest lines you can, and to check at
the far end, not only at the corner.

## Laying out a building on the ground

This is the Army carpentry manual's method, shortened. It is how the
corners of a shed, a cabin or a garden building get from a plan onto
the ground.

1. **Start from something fixed.** Work from an established line, such
   as a road or a property line, measuring off it to place the front
   of the building.
2. **Stake the corners.** Measure and drive a stake at each corner,
   squaring as you go with the 6-8-10 method.
3. **Set batter boards.** Digging will knock out the corner stakes, so
   the manual has you build batter boards: short horizontal boards
   nailed to posts set 3 or 4 feet outside each corner, out of the way
   of the digging. The boards are set level, using a carpenter's level,
   at the height of the top of the foundation, and right-angle batter
   boards are set close to perpendicular with a framing square.
4. **Run string lines.** Stretch a line from board to board over each
   pair of corner stakes. Saw cuts or nails in the tops of the boards
   hold each line in place, and separate cuts can mark the building
   line, the foundation line, the footing line and the edge of the
   excavation, so a line can be taken off for digging and put back in
   exactly the same place.
5. **Plumb down to the corners.** Where two lines cross, hang a plumb
   bob; adjust the lines until the plumb line touches both and hangs
   over the corner stake.
6. **Prove it.** Check every line with a line level or a carpenter's
   level, check the corners with 6-8-10, and check the diagonals.

For a building that is not a rectangle, the manual's advice is to lay
out one large rectangle that takes in all or most of it first, then
lay out the smaller rectangles that make up the rest, proving each one
separately. A small error in an irregular layout is harder to find, so
break it into shapes you can check.

## Slope: rise over run

Slope is how much something climbs (the rise) for how far it goes
across (the run). The same slope can be written several ways, and
mixing them up gives the wrong answer.

- **As a ratio of rise to run**, such as 4 in 12 for a roof, or one
  half inch per foot for drainage.
- **As a percentage**: the rise divided by the run, times 100. A 1 in
  10 slope is 10 percent.
- **As an angle in degrees**: the angle whose tangent is rise over run.

| Percent | Degrees | What it looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 2 | 1.1 | Barely visible; a gentle drainage fall |
| 4.2 | 2.4 | One half inch per foot |
| 10 | 5.7 | A noticeable slope |
| 33 | 18.4 | A 4 in 12 roof |
| 70 | 35 | A steep bank |
| 100 | 45 | Rise equals run |

The table is arithmetic. Note that 100 percent is 45 degrees, not
vertical: a slope described in percent can go far above 100. The
slopes in [Choosing Where to Build](choosing_where_to_build.md) are
given in percent.

### Ground that sheds water

The EPA's guide to mould and moisture says to make sure the ground
slopes away from the building's foundation, so that water does not
enter or collect around it. The federal rule for installing
manufactured homes (24 CFR 3285.203) gives a number: drainage must
slope a minimum of one half inch per foot away from the foundation for
the first ten feet. That is a fall of 5 inches over 10 feet, about 4
percent. Where property lines, walls or other conditions make that
impossible, the same rule calls for drains or swales instead. That
number is the rule for manufactured homes; your local building rules
for other buildings may set their own (general practice: ask before you
build).

To check a fall: stretch a string out from the wall, level it with a
line level, and measure down from the string to the ground at the
wall and at the far end. The difference is the fall.

### Roofs

Roof slopes are usually written as rise in 12 of run: a 4 in 12 roof
rises 4 inches for every 12 inches across. Older books and the Army's
manual also use "pitch", meaning the rise divided by the whole width of
the building, so a 4 in 12 roof is a 1/6 pitch, 6 in 12 is a 1/4 pitch
and 12 in 12 a 1/2 pitch. Because the two systems use the same words
for different numbers, always say which one you mean (our advice). Rise
in 12 is the less ambiguous.

The length of a rafter is the third side of a right triangle, which is
why Pythagoras turns up on every roof. For a building 24 feet wide
with a 4 in 12 roof, the run of each rafter is 12 feet and the rise is
4 feet. The rafter's length, before any overhang, is the square root of
12 x 12 + 4 x 4, which is the square root of 160, or 12.65 feet: 12
feet 7.8 inches. The rafter table on a framing square, which the Army's
manual explains, gives 12 feet 7 10/12 inches for the same roof: the
same answer to within a twentieth of an inch, read off in twelfths.

### Stairs

The Army's carpentry manual puts it directly: a flight of stairs forms
a right triangle, with the rise as its height, the run as its base, and
the stringer as its long side. Its method finds the number of steps
first: mark the total rise on a stick, step off about 7 inches at a
time with dividers, and adjust until the stick divides evenly. A total
rise of 102 inches (8 feet 6 inches) divided into steps near 7 inches
comes out at 15 risers of 6.8 inches each (our arithmetic). Equal steps
matter more than any one number, because people find stairs by the
rhythm of their feet (general practice). The legal limits on a
house's stairs are set by the building rules where you live.

## Worked examples

### Squaring a 4 metre shed base

You want a base 4 m by 4 m for a small shed.

1. Drive a stake for one corner. Run a string along where the front
   will go.
2. Measure 4 m along the string and drive the second corner stake.
3. At the first corner, measure 3 m along the front string and mark
   it. Run a second string off at roughly a right angle, mark 4 m along
   it, and swing it until the distance between the two marks is exactly
   5 m. That string is square to the front.
4. Measure 4 m along it for the third corner, and set the fourth the
   same way from the second corner.
5. Check: all four sides 4 m, and both diagonals 5.657 m. If the two
   diagonals differ by 5 cm (one about 2.5 cm long, the other about 2.5
   cm short), the base is about half a degree out of square. Move the
   two back corners sideways, both the same way and by the same amount,
   until the diagonals match, then check the sides again (general
   practice).

### How long a ladder for a 3 metre gutter

First, where the top goes. **Do not rest a ladder on the gutter
itself:** a gutter can bend or pull away under the load (general
practice), and OSHA's ladder rule (29 CFR 1926.1053(b)(10)) wants the
top placed "with the two rails supported equally". Rest both rails on
the wall, or fit a stand-off, a bracket that holds the top of the
ladder clear of the gutter and bears on the wall or roof (general
practice).

Then, how long. OSHA's one-quarter rule puts the foot of the ladder out
from the wall by a quarter of its working length. That makes the ladder
the long side of a right triangle whose base is a quarter of that side.
By Pythagoras, the height it reaches is the square root of (1 - 1/16),
about 0.968, times its working length (our arithmetic). For the top to
bear at 3 m up, the working length is 3 divided by 0.968, about 3.1 m,
with the foot about 0.78 m out from the wall. The angle is about 75.5
degrees. Climbing on to the roof from it needs the rails to reach at
least 3 feet (0.9 m) above the roof edge, which OSHA also requires, so
a ladder reaching about 4 m along its length.

When you buy or borrow an extension ladder, note that the size it is
sold as is the length of its sections added together, and because the
sections overlap when it is extended, it works at less than that. Check
the label for its maximum working length, and compare that with your
answer (general practice).

### Concrete for a slab

Area and volume are where measurement errors grow: see [Units and
Converting Them](units_and_converting_them.md) and
[Estimating](estimating.md). A slab 4 m by 4 m and 10 cm thick is 4 x 4
x 0.1 = 1.6 cubic metres. In feet, a slab 12 feet by 12 feet and 4
inches (one third of a foot) thick is 48 cubic feet; a cubic yard is 3
x 3 x 3 = 27 cubic feet, so that is about 1.8 cubic yards. Concrete is
ordered with extra for waste and uneven ground (general practice); how
much extra is for whoever sells it to you to advise.

### The height of a tree or a wall

Similar triangles measure things you cannot reach. The stick and
shadow methods are in [Estimating](estimating.md).

## Know where your work stops

Getting a shed base square and level is a skill you can learn from
this guide. Some things are not:

- **Footings, foundations and frames for anything people sleep in or
  that carries a load over people's heads.** Their sizes depend on the
  soil, the loads and the local rules, and are a question for your
  local building department or an engineer (general practice).
- **Any trench deeper than your waist.** See OSHA's rules above.
- **Anything near buried utilities that have not been marked.** Call
  811 and wait, and find your own private lines before you dig.
- **Work on a roof** if you are not sure of your footing or the ladder
  (general practice).

## How the game models it

The game has two ways to build, and they handle geometry differently.
Real ground and real lumber do none of it for you, so the difference is
worth noticing.

**Pieces you build from the Crafting page** (the blueprints in
`data/blueprints/basic.ron`, such as a wall, a foundation or a roof)
have the geometry done for you:

- **Square by construction.** A piece you place snaps to a 1 metre
  grid, and it turns only in quarter turns (R by default, while
  placing). So every corner between these pieces is exactly a right
  angle, and you never need a 3-4-5 triangle. A wall is 4 m long and 3
  m high and a foundation 4 m by 4 m, so a 4 m room lines up. In real
  life, squaring the corners is the job.
- **Plumb by construction.** Pieces stand along the vertical of the
  place you build: the ship's own up when aboard, and the local up of a
  build site on a planet.
- **Levelled for you, within reason.** On uneven ground, a wall
  standing on the ground that touches another wall of the same kind is
  stretched or shortened so the two tops meet, as long as they are
  within 0.3 m of each other; a wall or a machine built on a foundation
  rests on its top. In real life that job is done by getting the
  foundation level, which is why the batter boards are set at its
  height.
- **Building trains you.** Finishing one of these structures gives
  experience in the Shelter Building skill.

The rules for these pieces are in
`src/systems/construction/placement.rs`.

**The construction editor for your home** works differently, and is
closer to real layout:

- **Interior walls are drawn corner to corner.** Each click drops a
  corner, and the editor snaps it to a 0.25 m grid, to an existing
  corner or to the edge of the zone, and nothing more. So a wall can
  run at any angle, and two walls can meet at an angle that is not a
  right angle. The editor does not square a room for you.
- **The editor measures for you.** Its dimension overlay, on by
  default, shows each wall's length to the centimetre and the angle at
  each corner, so the job a tape and a 3-4-5 triangle do outside is done
  by reading a number. The angle is shown to the nearest whole degree,
  so a corner almost half a degree out can still read 90; on a 4 m room
  that is nearly the 5 cm of diagonal in the table above.
- **Stairs, a ramp, a ladder and an elevator** are on the Structure tab
  of the editor's palette, and while one is held for placing, [ and ]
  turn it in 15 degree steps. The stairs and the ramp climb; the ladder
  stands straight up.

The editor's corner snapping is in `src/engine/geom.rs` and
`src/engine/editor.rs`, its measurements in `src/gui/pages/hud.rs`
(`draw_construction_overlay`), and its structural pieces in
`data/blueprints/structure_types.ron`.

**One setting changes what you keep.** While Settings > Gameplay >
"Start every session from the default home" is on, which is the default
during development, only your character carries between launches, so
what you built and the experience it earned start over at the next
launch. Turn the setting off to keep them.

What the game leaves out: the roof is a flat slab, so there are no
rafters or roof pitch; a staircase is one ready-made piece, so there are
no risers to work out; the ladder piece stands straight up, so there is
no one-in-four angle to set; there is no digging and there are no buried
utilities; and you never hold a tape, a square or a level, because the
pieces are squared for you and the editor reads its own angles. Those
you practise outside.

## You own this when

- You call 811 before you dig, every time, find your own private lines,
  dig by hand near the marks, and never get into a trench deeper than
  your waist.
- You rest a ladder on both rails against something solid, never on a
  gutter.
- You check a level by reversing it end for end before you trust it.
- You can plumb a post with a plumb bob and a 2 inch offset, on two
  faces.
- You can square a corner with a 3-4-5 triangle, as big as the site
  allows, and you know the reason it works.
- You check rectangles by their sides and then their diagonals, and you
  can say how far out a corner is from the difference.
- You can set batter boards and string lines, and plumb down from the
  crossing to the corner.
- You can turn a slope from inches per foot into percent and degrees,
  and you know which way a slope is written before you build to it.
- Water runs away from everything you build.
- You can find a rafter's length or a ladder's angle with Pythagoras.
- You know which parts of a building are past you, and who to ask.

### Labelled in the text as arithmetic, general practice or our explanation, not sourced

- Pythagoras's theorem, the 3-4-5 arithmetic, the diagonal and drift
  tables, the slope conversions, the rafter, ladder, slab and stair
  figures, and the 75.5 degree ladder angle are arithmetic.
- That equal diagonals prove a rectangle only when opposite sides are
  equal is geometry, not the manual's wording.
- Why a line level is most accurate at the middle, and why short
  triangles square less accurately, are our explanations.
- The water level, sighting a straightedge, not trusting a level that
  fails the reversal check, bracing a wall the moment it stands,
  correcting a base by moving the back corners, equal stair risers, and
  ordering extra concrete are general practice.
- Stopping at waist-deep trenches, and leaving footings and load-bearing
  frames for occupied buildings to the local building department or an
  engineer, are general practice; local rules may set their own
  drainage and stair limits.
- Digging by hand near the locate marks, and finding your own private
  lines (to a shed, well, septic tank or propane tank) from records or a
  private locating service, are general practice, beside OSHA's rule
  that the exact location be found by safe means.
- Never resting a ladder on a gutter, using a stand-off, and the
  difference between an extension ladder's sold size and its working
  length are general practice, beside OSHA's rule that both rails be
  supported equally.
- Saying which slope notation you mean is our advice.
