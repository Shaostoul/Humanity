# Moving Water Without Power

Water is heavy, and it only ever wants to go one way: down. Everything
in this guide is a way of working with that instead of against it. A
tank set higher than the garden, a hose that keeps flowing over the
lip of a barrel, a pump handle that lets the air push water up a pipe
for you, a stream that lifts a little of its own water uphill: none of
them needs a wire, a battery or a drop of fuel.

That matters most on the day the power is out, which is usually the
day you need water most. It also matters every ordinary day, because a
gravity system has almost nothing in it to break.

This guide takes its physics from the Environmental Protection
Agency's (EPA) manual on cross-connections, its pump and pipeline rules
from the Army's well-drilling manual and the Natural Resources
Conservation Service (NRCS), and its safety rules from the National
Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH), the Occupational
Safety and Health Administration (OSHA), the Consumer Product Safety
Commission (CPSC) and the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention
(CDC). All but one are United States government publications, in the
public domain. The exception is Poison Control, the National Capital
Poison Center, which is not; its facts are restated in our own words.
Where something is general practice rather than a published rule, the
text says so.

It sits beside the other water guides. [Where Water Comes
From](where_water_comes_from.md) is about the source, and whether you
are allowed to take from it. [Collecting
Rainwater](collecting_rainwater.md) and [Storing Water
Safely](storing_water_safely.md) are about holding it. [Making Water Safe
to Drink](making_water_safe_to_drink.md) is about treating it. This
guide is about getting it from where it is to where you need it.

## First: how moving water hurts people

Moving water by hand is quiet, slow work, and the dangers in it are
quiet too. These are the ones a beginner most needs to know.

**Never climb into a tank, cistern or well.** A space like that can
hold air with too little oxygen, or a gas you cannot smell. OSHA's rule
on confined spaces (29 CFR 1910.146) names "tanks, vessels, silos,
storage bins, hoppers, vaults, and pits" as spaces that may have limited
ways in and out, and it defines a hazardous atmosphere as one that may
expose people to the risk of death, incapacitation, or being unable to
escape unaided. NIOSH's alert on deaths in confined spaces (1986) says the
deaths often happen because the air is short of oxygen or toxic, and
that "More than 60% of confined space fatalities occur among would-be
rescuers." If someone collapses inside a tank or a well, do not go in
after them. Call emergency services, and keep everyone else out. The
CDC says the same of the pit around a well head, in its advice on wells
after an emergency: "Do not enter the well pit. Gases and vapors can
build up in well pits, creating a hazardous environment." The OSHA and
NIOSH rules are written for workplaces; applying them to a farm cistern
or a dug well is our reading, and the safe one.

**Keep small children away from any container of water.** The CPSC
warns that 5-gallon buckets pose a serious threat to toddlers: the tall,
straight sides and the bucket's stability make it nearly impossible for
a top-heavy infant to get out after toppling in headfirst. It lists
pails, coolers, fish tanks and landscape ponds among the other
containers children have drowned in, and its advice is direct: "Be sure
all containers with liquids are emptied immediately after use." A
bucket brigade, a trough or an open tank is exactly this hazard. Empty
it, fence it, or cover it with a rigid lid that is fastened down and
that a child cannot lift or slide aside (general practice).

A loose or floating cover is not a barrier. The same CPSC sheet warns,
of spas and hot tubs: "A solar cover can allow children to slip into the
water while the cover appears to stay in place, hiding the child." It
tells owners to "Always secure the safety cover on your spa or hot
tub." A tarp stretched over a tank or a pit is the same trap
(our reading). The opening of a well or a cistern needs the same kind
of lid: rigid, fastened, and too heavy or too well locked for a child
to move (general practice).

**Never start a siphon with your mouth on fuel, chemicals or dirty
water.** Poison Control warns that siphoning petrol by mouth can let it
into the lungs, causing pneumonia, coma or death, and that its advice
is simply not to do it. Do not mouth-siphon a pesticide mix or dirty
water either (general practice). The safe ways to start a siphon are
below.

**Never let a hose join your drinking water to anything else.** This is
called a cross-connection, and it is the hidden danger of a garden
hose. When the pressure in a water main drops, water can be sucked
backwards up a hose, which the EPA calls backsiphonage. Its manual
records a case from 1979 where an exterminator left the end of a garden
hose, still connected to an outside tap, in a barrel of diluted
pesticide; when the water was shut off for a repair, the pesticide was
siphoned back through the house and into the water mains. Never leave a
hose connected to a tap with its end sitting in a tank, a trough, a
sprayer, a pond or a bucket. Fill from above the water, with a gap (the
air gap, below).

**Water is heavy, and lifting it can hurt your back.** A litre of water weighs
about a kilogram, and a US gallon about 8.3 pounds (general knowledge;
the EPA gives 62.4 pounds for a cubic foot). A full 20-litre can weighs
over 20 kg once the can itself is counted. NIOSH's lifting equation
starts from a load of 23 kg (51 lb), which it calls the maximum
recommended weight to lift under ideal conditions: the load held close,
about 25 cm (10 inches) out from the body, starting about 75 cm (30
inches) off the floor and moving less than 25 cm up or down, with no
twisting, good handholds, and lifted only occasionally. Its committee
estimated that a lift that heavy, even then, would be acceptable to 75
percent of women workers and about 90 percent of men. In NIOSH's
equation, each way a real lift falls short of those conditions, such as
lifting from the ground, reaching out or lifting often, lowers the
recommended weight. So carry two half-full cans rather than
one full one, use a wheelbarrow or cart, and lift with the load close
(general practice beside NIOSH's figure).

**Mosquitoes breed in standing water.** The CDC's advice: once a week,
empty and scrub, turn over, cover, or throw out anything that holds
water, and "Tightly cover water storage containers (buckets, cisterns,
rain barrels) so mosquitoes cannot get inside to lay eggs."

## Why water flows downhill, in numbers

Water pushes on whatever is below it, and the push depends only on how
deep the water is above that point. The EPA's manual gives the number:
"The rate of increase for water is 0.433 psi per foot of depth." It
calls a height of water **head**: one foot of head is 0.433 pounds per
square inch (psi), and one hundred feet of head is 43.3 psi.

In metric units (arithmetic from the same number): one metre of head is
about 1.42 psi, or about 9.8 kilopascals, and 10 metres is about one
atmosphere.

Three things follow, and between them they explain most of this guide.

1. **Height is what makes pressure, not the amount of water.** A
   narrow pipe full of water 10 feet tall pushes as hard at the bottom
   as a pond 10 feet deep. The width of the tank does not change the
   pressure; it changes how long the flow lasts.
2. **Pressure is measured from the water's surface, not the tank's
   top.** As a tank empties, its surface falls and the pressure at the
   tap falls with it. A rain barrel gives its best flow when full and a
   trickle at the end.
3. **Moving water loses some of its head to friction.** The pressure
   you have when nothing is flowing is the most you will ever get. Once
   water moves, the pipe, every bend and every valve take a share. The
   NRCS's Vermont standard for livestock pipelines (2021), cited
   throughout this guide, tells designers to count
   those losses, including the "minor losses" at valves, bends and
   changes of size. A longer, narrower or rougher pipe loses more, so a
   wider pipe delivers more water from the same height (general
   practice; the NRCS gives the formulas).

### Worked example: how much pressure does a raised tank give?

All arithmetic from the EPA's 0.433 psi per foot.

| Water surface above the outlet | Pressure with nothing flowing |
|---|---|
| 3 feet (a barrel on two courses of blocks) | about 1.3 psi |
| 10 feet (a tank on a stand) | about 4.3 psi |
| 40 feet (a tank up a hillside) | about 17 psi |
| 100 feet | 43.3 psi |

That first row is why a rain barrel will fill a watering can but will
not run a sprinkler, which the rainwater guide also says. A tank up a
slope, well above the garden, is a different thing entirely.

The last row matters for a different reason. Pressure can be too high as
well as too low: a long drop down a hillside can build more pressure at
the bottom than cheap hose fittings were made for. The NRCS standard,
as a safety factor against pressure surges, limits the working pressure
in plastic pipe to 72 percent of the pipe's pressure rating. A pipe
carrying 100 feet of head would need a rating of at least 43.3 / 0.72,
about 60 psi, by that rule (arithmetic). Check the rating printed on
the pipe before you trust it with a big drop.

## Gravity systems: tank above, tap below

The simplest system there is: get the water up high once, by rain off a
roof, a spring above the house, or a hand pump, and let gravity deliver
it after that.

### A tank up high is a heavy thing up high

Water is heavy, and a raised tank holds a lot of it. At about a kilogram
a litre, a 200-litre barrel holds over 200 kg of water and a 1,000-litre
tank over a tonne, before the tank itself is counted (arithmetic). A
stand that tips or gives way drops all of that at once. So (general
practice):

- Build or buy a stand rated for the full weight of the tank when it is
  full, not when it is empty.
- Set it on firm, level ground that will stay firm when it is soaked,
  and check it after heavy rain and after a frost.
- Keep children off it, and off the tank.
- Anything taller than a few courses of blocks, or for a large tank, is
  a designer's job, not a home project (see where your own work stops,
  below).

### The air in the pipe

A home-made gravity line can stop for no visible reason, and the
reason is often air (general practice).

The NRCS standard explains what a designer has to do: provide for air
to get into and out of the pipeline, "as needed to prevent air locking",
and "Design the pipeline to remain below the hydraulic grade line
during operation." The hydraulic grade line is easiest to picture like
this. With the outlet shut and nothing flowing, it is level with the
water surface in the tank. While water runs out of an open end, it falls
roughly in a straight line from the tank's water surface down to that
open end, because friction uses up the head along the way. The flowing
line is the lower of the two, so it is the one a pipe has to stay under.
Any part of the pipe that rises above it is in trouble: the pressure
there falls below the air's, air can collect at the high point, and the
flow slows or stops (all of this is our explanation of the standard's
rule). The standard says that where
parts of a pipeline sit above the grade line, an air pump may be needed
from time to time.

In practice (general practice, following the standard):

- Lay a gravity line so it falls all the way, or at least never climbs
  above a straight line from the tank's water level to the outlet.
- Where a line has to go over a rise, fit a vent or an air-release valve
  at the high point.
- Fill a new line slowly, from the bottom up where you can, so the air
  can get out ahead of the water. The standard says that filling a
  closed pipe faster than 1 foot per second needs special provisions to
  remove trapped air and prevent excessive pressure surges, and that
  where slow filling is not possible, the outlets should be opened
  before the line is pressurized.

### The dirt in the pipe, and the ice

The NRCS standard also says: where the water carries sediment, fit a
suitable valve at the far end or the low point of the pipeline to flush
it out; and make provision for draining the pipeline completely when
freezing temperatures are a hazard. Water expands as it freezes and
splits pipes and fittings. A drain valve at the lowest point, opened
before a hard frost, with a tap or vent opened at the top so air can
get in and the line can empty, is the usual answer (general practice).

### Keep the clean water clean

Two rules from the EPA's cross-connection manual apply to every tank:

- **An air gap.** Where water is fed into a tank, trough or barrel,
  the pipe should end above the highest level the water can reach,
  with open air between them. "In general, the air gap must be twice
  the supply pipe diameter but never less than one inch." A 3/4 inch
  pipe needs at least 1 1/2 inches of air (arithmetic). The NRCS
  standard uses the same rule for pipelines in two parts: a backflow
  prevention device or an air gap between a pump's outlet and the
  pipeline wherever harmful backflow may occur, the gap at least twice
  the supply pipe's inside diameter or 1 inch, whichever is greater; and
  "approved backflow prevention devices on all pipelines where back flow
  may contaminate the source water supply or ground water."
- **A vacuum breaker on hose taps.** The EPA describes hose bibb vacuum
  breakers: small devices screwed onto an outside tap, which let air in
  and stop backsiphonage when the supply is turned off. They are cheap
  and widely sold (general practice).

And the third rule, which is ours, following from both: **never join a
rain tank, a pond or a greywater line to your drinking water
plumbing.** If you want to use them together, use separate taps.
[Greywater](greywater.md) covers the rules for water that has already
been used once.

## Siphons: water over the top of the wall

A siphon is a hose or pipe that carries water up over the edge of a
container and down to a lower place, with no pump. It works because of
the difference in height between the two ends: the EPA's manual works
it through with pressures, for two open containers joined by a filled
tube:
when one water surface is higher, water flows from the higher to the
lower, and "This arrangement will be recognized as a siphon."

The rules:

1. **The outlet must be lower than the water surface you are drawing
   from.** The flow is driven by the difference between the two
   surfaces (or the surface and the open end of the hose). When they
   are level, it stops. This is also why a siphon will empty a barrel
   only down to the level of the hose's outlet end.
2. **The hose must be full of water to start.** A hose full of air
   does nothing.
3. **The top of the siphon cannot be too high.** The EPA's manual says
   the crest of a siphon cannot be higher than 33.9 feet above the upper
   water level at sea level, because the atmosphere cannot hold up a
   taller column of water. In practice, keep the top of the hose as low
   as you can; a siphon that only has to clear the rim of a barrel is
   far more reliable than one climbing a bank (general practice).
4. **Air leaks stop it.** A cracked hose or a loose joint above the
   water lets air in and the siphon dies (general practice).

### Worked example: emptying a rain barrel or a stock trough

A safe way to start a siphon on clean water, with no mouth involved
(general practice):

1. Push the whole hose under the water in the barrel until no more
   bubbles come out, so it is completely full.
2. Seal one end with your thumb, or a cap, and keep the other end
   under the water.
3. Lift the sealed end out over the rim and down to a point below the
   barrel's water level, keeping it sealed all the way.
4. Release it. Water should run out of the low end, and keep running
   until the barrel's water level falls to the level of that end, or
   the barrel end of the hose sucks air.

A hand squeeze-bulb or a pump-type siphon starter does the same job
for liquids you must not touch. Use a separate one for fuel, and never
use a fuel hose for water (general practice).

If you must move fuel at all, treat it as fuel first and a siphon
second (general practice): do it outdoors, never in a garage or a shed;
with every engine involved switched off and cold; with nothing nearby
that could flame or spark, and no smoking; and into an approved fuel
can standing on the ground.

The siphon is also the reason for the cross-connection rule above. A
hose dangling in a barrel, still attached to a tap, *is* a siphon, just
waiting for a pressure drop to start it backwards.

## Hand pumps: letting the air do the lifting

A gravity system needs the water to start high. When it starts low, in a
well or a cistern, somebody has to lift it. A hand pump turns arm work
into lift, and the kind of pump you need depends on how far down the
water is.

### Suction pumps, and their limit

The familiar cast-iron pitcher pump, and the simplest shallow-well
pumps, sit at the top and suck. They do not really pull water up: they
take away the air pressure inside the pipe, and the air pressing on the
water down in the well pushes it up to replace it. That sets a hard
limit. The EPA's manual says the weight of the atmosphere at sea level
balances a column of water 33.9 feet high, so no suction can ever lift
water further than that, and in practice far less.

The Army's well-drilling manual (TM 5-297, 1943) gives the working
figure for shallow wells raised by suction pumps: "the depth to water
must not exceed 20 to 25 feet." It describes the common pitcher pump
supplied for driven wells as being of the suction type. Its section on
pitcher pumps explains the margin: a well-made pump in good condition
may lift 28 feet or more, but "allowance must be made for elevation
above sea level, friction, presence of air in the water, and eventual
wear", and once the suction lift plus friction passes 22 feet for a
plunger pump, reduced capacity and more frequent failure may be
expected. The air presses less at altitude, which is why the limit
falls in the mountains (general knowledge).

The same section gives a warning worth knowing before you work a pump
whose lift is too great, or whose suction pipe is too small: "the
handle is likely to fly up when released during the downstroke." Keep
your face and head out of the handle's path, and do not let go of it
partway through a stroke (general practice).

Measure the depth to the water in the dry season, when it is lowest,
before you buy a suction pump (general practice). The depth that
matters is from the pump down to the water surface while you are
pumping, which can be lower than the resting level (general practice).

### Deep-well pumps

For water deeper than a suction pump can reach, the pumping part has to
go down the well, near the water, so it pushes water up rather than
sucking it. The same Army manual says that where 2-inch or larger pipes
are used, it is possible to lift water from a greater depth "by
installing a Eureka cylinder or similar type pump near the water
level." Modern deep-well
hand pumps work the same way: a cylinder down in the water, worked by a
rod from the handle at the top (general knowledge). They lift from much
deeper, at the cost of a long rod and harder pumping.

### Priming

A pitcher pump usually has to be primed: water poured into the top to
wet the leather or rubber seal so it can grip. Prime with clean water,
the same water you would drink, because priming water runs down into
the well (general practice).

A **foot valve** is a one-way valve at the bottom of the suction pipe.
It holds the water in the pipe, so a pump does not need priming each
time it stops; the Army manual recommends one for the pump that keeps a
dug well dry while it is being dug. But a pump that holds its water can
freeze. Most pitcher pumps, the manual says, have a tilting check valve
that makes them easy to drain: lifting the handle to its highest point
pushes the plunger down onto that valve, opens it, and lets the water in
the pump and the suction pipe run back down, after which the pump is
simple to prime again. On a pump like that, the manual says, a foot
valve "prevents convenient drainage. Foot valves, therefore, are not
installed where either the pump or the suction line is exposed to
freezing temperatures." If your pump or its pipe can freeze, leave the
foot valve out and drain the pump before a frost by lifting the handle
high (general practice following the manual).

A pump that has lost its prime overnight may have a leaking foot valve
or check valve, or someone may have lifted the handle high and drained
it (general practice).

A pump is part of the well. [Where Water Comes
From](where_water_comes_from.md) covers why a well's top has to stay
sealed against surface water, and [Testing Water](testing_water.md)
covers how you find out whether the water is safe.

## A stream that pumps itself: the hydraulic ram

Where a stream runs all year with some fall in it, a hydraulic ram pump
can lift part of the stream's water well above the stream, with no
fuel and no power, using only the energy of the falling water. The
NRCS's North Carolina office describes one in its cost scenarios for
pumping plants: the ram transfers water from a live stream to a
watering trough "utilizing the energy of moving water to transfer a
portion of that water to a higher elevation". Its typical example: a
2-inch ram, with 10 gallons a minute flowing in and 10 feet of drop,
can supply about 1 gallon a minute to a place about 50 feet higher than
the ram.

Two things in that example are worth understanding. First, most of the
water does not go uphill: the same document says the bypass water,
which could easily be 90 percent of what is diverted, goes back to the
stream. A ram trades a lot of water falling a little way for a little
water rising a long way. Second, it needs a stream that keeps flowing;
a ram on a seasonal ditch stops in August. The document also notes the
ram may need fencing to protect it from curious cattle.

Before you divert any stream, read the section on water rights in
[Where Water Comes From](where_water_comes_from.md): in many states
using surface water needs a water right, and the NRCS standard reminds
landowners to obtain every permission needed, or document that none is.

## Carrying: the oldest pump there is

Where water is not piped, it is carried. The rules for doing it
without injury are the lifting rules from the top of this
guide, and a few habits (general practice):

- Two smaller loads, one in each hand, balance your body better than
  one big one on one side.
- A wheelbarrow, a garden cart or a sled carries far more than arms
  do, for less strain. A 20-litre can on a cart is easy; in one hand it
  is hard work.
- Containers for drinking water are used for drinking water only.
  [Storing Water Safely](storing_water_safely.md) covers which containers
  are safe and how to clean them.
- Fill at the source and put the lid on before you lift. Water slopping
  out of an open bucket onto your feet and the path is a slip waiting to
  happen.

## Know where your own work stops

Plenty of water moving is yours to do. Some is not.

- **Never enter a tank, cistern or well.** Not to clean it, not to fetch
  a dropped bucket, not to help someone who has collapsed. Work inside a
  large tank is for people trained in confined-space entry, who test the
  air before going in, monitor it the whole time and have a rescue plan
  ready (NIOSH; OSHA).
- **Well work beyond the pump handle.** Pulling a deep-well cylinder,
  and deepening or drilling a well, belong to a licensed well contractor
  in most places (general practice; your state sets the rules). Even
  disinfecting a well, which means opening its vent cap, is a
  contractor's job in the CDC's advice on wells after an emergency:
  "Wells should be disinfected by a well or pump contractor, due to
  safety concerns." Where a well has an electric pump, the CDC's first
  precaution is to "Turn off all electricity to the well area". It
  adds: "Do not attempt to repair the water system unless you are
  experienced with this type of work; electrical shock can occur." If
  you are not experienced, it says to contact a qualified electrician,
  well contractor or pump contractor.
- **A tall tank stand, or any stand for a large tank.** A stand that
  fails drops the whole weight of the water at once. Have it designed,
  or buy one rated for the full tank (general practice).
- **Anything that connects to a public water supply or to your house
  plumbing.** The EPA's manual gives a whole chapter to testing backflow
  preventers, and its sample local ordinance has them tested by
  certified backflow device testers, when installed and periodically
  after that. Your local water utility and plumbing code decide what is
  needed.
- **Diverting a stream, building a pond or a dam.** These need permits
  in most places, and a dam that fails puts everyone downstream at risk
  (general practice; see the water rights section of Where Water Comes
  From).
- **Big drops in pressure pipe.** Where a pipeline falls far enough for
  the pressure at the bottom to approach the pipe's rating, the pipe
  rating, pressure-reducing and relief valves and surge allowances in
  the NRCS standard are a designer's job. Get it designed (general
  practice).
- **Any digging for a pipe.** Call 811 first, and know when a trench
  needs protection: see [Square, Level and
  Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md). The NRCS standard says "The
  landowner and/or contractor is responsible for locating all buried
  utilities", and lists addressing trench safety among the ways to
  ensure safe conditions.

## Traps

- **A hose left in a tank.** It is a siphon waiting to run the wrong
  way into your drinking water.
- **Mouth-siphoning "just this once".** Once is all it takes to
  breathe some in (our reading of Poison Control's warning).
- **A pipe that climbs.** Water will not carry air over a high point
  for you. Keep the line below the grade line or vent the high point.
- **Measuring the well at its spring level.** A suction pump that works
  in April can lose its water in September.
- **An open bucket in a yard with toddlers.** Empty it the moment you
  are done.
- **A tarp or a floating cover on a tank.** It looks closed and is not.
- **A foot valve on a pump that can freeze.** The water it holds splits
  the pump. Drain it instead.
- **Forgetting the pressure goes up as the pipe goes down.** The
  fitting at the bottom of a long drop carries all the head above it.

## How the game models it

The game's homes have plumbing, and it is honest about one thing this
guide is about: what happens when the power goes.

- **The well pump is electric.** The home's Water pump draws 10 watts
  for its motor, and the plumbing system only moves its water while that
  machine has power. Cut the power, or let the home shed the load in a
  shortage, and the pump stops and the cistern stops filling. The
  Home page's Live water card says it plainly: "powered pumps +
  purifiers fill the cistern; cut the power and it drains", and it shows
  production, demand, the balance and how many days the cistern would
  last. The pump is `water_pump` in `data/machines/home.ron`; the rule
  that its water needs power is in `src/engine/home_spawn.rs` and
  `src/systems/plumbing.rs`.
- **Rain needs no power.** The 8,000 litre cistern also gains a small,
  steady trickle the game counts as rain off the roof, with no power
  needed. It is a fixed amount: the same every day, whatever the
  weather is doing.
- **You can carry water.** A 20-litre jerrycan, filled at a water
  tank's card and poured back there, weighs 21.2 kg full in the game
  (`data/items.csv`, `data/containers/fluids.ron`), and that weight
  counts toward what you are carrying, as it would on your back.
- **The only pump you can build is electric.** The Water Pump item is
  described as an electric water extraction pump, and building one at a
  workbench trains Engineering (`data/recipes.csv`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no gravity flow, no siphon and no hand pump. Height does not matter to
the game's water: machines joined in the home's plumbing share their
water as soon as it is pumped, whatever their height or the length of
pipe between them, so there is no head, no friction, no air lock and no
frozen pipe. And nothing about the game's tanks and vessels can drown a
child, trap you in bad air, or siphon dirty water back into the drinking
water. In real life, every one of those is part of the job.

**Starting from the default home.** Settings > Gameplay > "Start every
session from the default home" is off by default, so the water in the
cistern and everything you carry are kept between launches. With the
setting on, only your character carries between launches, and they
start each session as they are in the default home.

## You own this when

- You never enter a tank, cistern or well, and you would not go in after
  someone who had collapsed there.
- You empty every bucket and container of water where small children
  could reach it, or close it with a rigid, fastened lid a child cannot
  move, and you cover stored water against mosquitoes.
- You set a raised tank on a stand rated for its full weight, on firm
  ground.
- You never start a siphon by mouth on anything you would not drink.
- You never leave a hose connected to a tap with its end in a tank,
  trough, sprayer, pond or bucket, and you fill tanks through an air gap.
- You can work out the pressure a tank will give from the height of its
  water above the outlet, at 0.433 psi per foot.
- You can lay a gravity line that stays below the grade line, with a
  vent at any high point, a flush at the low point and a drain for
  winter.
- You can start a siphon safely, and you know it stops when the levels
  meet.
- You know a suction pump will not lift water from more than about 20 to
  25 feet down, and what to use when the water is deeper.
- You drain a pitcher pump before a frost, and leave out the foot valve
  where the pump or its pipe can freeze.
- You carry water in loads you can lift close to your body, and use a
  cart for the rest.
- You check the water rights before you take water from a stream.

### Labelled in the text as general practice, arithmetic or our reading, not sourced

- Applying OSHA's and NIOSH's workplace confined-space rules to farm
  cisterns, tanks and dug wells is our reading; the CDC's warning covers
  well pits only.
- A rigid, fastened, child-proof lid on buckets, tanks, wells and
  cisterns is general practice; that a tarp over a tank is the same trap
  as a spa's solar cover is our reading of the CPSC's warning.
- The weight of a raised tank is arithmetic; a stand rated for the full
  tank, firm ground, keeping children off, and a designer for tall or
  large stands are general practice.
- The rules for moving fuel (outdoors, engines off and cold, no flame or
  spark, an approved can on the ground) are general practice.
- A litre of water weighing about a kilogram, and a US gallon about 8.3
  pounds, are general knowledge; the EPA's 62.4 pounds per cubic foot is
  the sourced figure.
- Carrying two half loads, using a cart, filling with the lid on and
  lifting close to the body are general practice beside NIOSH's lifting
  figures.
- Metric conversions of the EPA's head figure, the pressure table and
  the pipe-rating example are arithmetic.
- That a wider pipe delivers more water from the same height, laying a
  gravity line to fall all the way, venting high points, filling from the
  bottom, and a drain valve at the low point are general practice
  following the NRCS standard.
- Vacuum breakers being cheap and widely sold, and never joining a rain
  tank, pond or greywater line to drinking water plumbing, are general
  practice following the EPA's manual.
- The safe way to start a siphon, keeping the crest low, air leaks
  stopping a siphon, and keeping fuel and water siphons separate are
  general practice.
- Why the suction limit falls at altitude, and modern deep-well hand
  pumps working with a cylinder and rod, are general knowledge (the
  allowance for altitude itself is the Army manual's); measuring the
  depth to water in the dry season, pumping drawing the level down,
  priming with drinking water, keeping clear of a handle that can fly
  up, draining a pump before frost, and a leaking valve or a raised
  handle losing the prime are general practice.
- How the hydraulic grade line lies with the outlet shut and with water
  flowing, and why air collects above it, are our explanation of the
  NRCS rule; that a home-made line often stops because of air, and
  opening a tap at the top when draining, are general practice.
- That one mouth-siphon is enough to breathe fuel in is our reading of
  Poison Control's warning.
- That mouth-siphoning chemicals or dirty water is as bad as fuel is
  general practice beside Poison Control's warning about petrol.
- Pulling cylinders and deepening or drilling wells belonging to
  licensed contractors, and permits and dam risks for streams and ponds,
  are general practice; the rules are set by each state. Disinfecting a
  well, and the electricity warning, are the CDC's.
