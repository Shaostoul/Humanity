# Power Tools

A power tool puts more energy behind a blade, a bit or a wheel than your
arm ever could, and turns it faster than you can react. That is the whole
point of it, and the whole danger. Each kind of tool hurts in its own
way: a circular saw can kick back at you, a grinding wheel can burst, a
drill that jams twists your wrist instead of the bit, a chain saw climbs
towards your face, a nail gun fires through the board, and every tool
with a cord can shock. This guide covers the habits that hold for every
power tool, the kinds of power that drive them, and the particular
respect the common ones demand.

Two things come before anything in it. **Read the tool's own manual,**
which OSHA's booklet on hand and power tools makes one of its five basic
rules ("Operate tools according to the manufacturers' instructions").
And **get shown by someone who already uses the tool well,** before you
use it alone. NIOSH's manual for electrical trades students puts the test
in two questions worth asking before any job: "What could go wrong? Do I
have the knowledge, tools, and experience to do this work safely?"

Some tools have a guide of their own, and this one does not repeat them:
the table saw, the jointer and the portable saw's guards are in [Working
with Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md), the bench grinder in
[Sharpening](../making/sharpening.md), nail guns in [Nails, Screws and
Bolts](nails_screws_and_bolts.md) and [Framing a
Building](framing_a_building.md), the chain saw's protective gear in
[Firewood](firewood.md), and mowers, guards and fuel in [Keeping Things
Working](keeping_things_working.md). The electricity behind a corded tool
is explained in [Electricity and How It
Flows](electricity_and_how_it_flows.md), and the hand tools that came
first are in [Hand Tools](../making/hand_tools.md).

The sources are the Occupational Safety and Health Administration (OSHA),
its booklet *Hand and Power Tools* and its regulations; the National
Institute for Occupational Safety and Health (NIOSH); the Consumer
Product Safety Commission (CPSC); the USDA Forest Service's chain saw
course; and the Department of Labor's rules on young workers. All are
United States government publications, in the public domain. OSHA's
rules are written for employers and their workers; they are quoted here
because they set out in detail what keeps hands whole, not because they
bind a household (our reading). Where something is general practice or
our own reading rather than a published rule, the text says so.

## First: what every power tool asks of you

OSHA's booklet puts the warning in one sentence: "Power tools must be
fitted with guards and safety switches; they are extremely hazardous
when used improperly." Its five basic rules are short enough to learn:
keep every tool in good condition with regular maintenance; use the
right tool for the job; examine each tool for damage before use and do
not use a damaged one; operate it according to the manufacturer's
instructions; and use the right protective equipment.

Its list of general precautions for power tools, restated:

- **Never carry a tool by its cord or hose, and never yank the cord to
  unplug it.** Keep cords and hoses away from heat, oil and sharp edges.
- **Disconnect it before you touch the business end.** OSHA: "Disconnect
  tools when not using them, before servicing and cleaning them, and when
  changing accessories such as blades, bits, and cutters." For a battery
  tool, that means taking the battery out (our reading).
- **Keep everyone not involved in the work at a safe distance.**
- **Clamp the work.** "Secure work with clamps or a vise, freeing both
  hands to operate the tool." The hand that holds the work is the one
  nearest the blade (general practice).
- **Avoid accidental starting:** "Do not hold fingers on the switch
  button while carrying a plugged-in tool."
- **Keep tools sharp and clean,** and follow the manual for lubricating
  them and changing accessories. OSHA's woodworking guidance says
  sharpening blades prevents kickback.
- **Keep good footing and good balance.**
- **Dress for it.** "Loose clothing, ties, or jewelry can become caught
  in moving parts." NIOSH adds: keep long hair contained.
- **Take a damaged tool out of use and tag it** so nobody picks it up.

### Guards stay on

OSHA's booklet: "Safety guards must never be removed when a tool is being
used." Its construction rule says a power tool designed to take a guard
shall have that guard on when in use (29 CFR 1926.300(b)(1)), and its
general rule for machines requires guarding against the hazards of the
point of operation, ingoing nip points, rotating parts, and flying chips
and sparks (29 CFR 1910.212). A guard that has been taken off, wedged
open or tied back has been switched off by a person (our reading), and
the protection goes with it.

### Switches that stop when you let go

OSHA's rule for portable tools requires a hand-held circular saw with a
blade over 2 inches, a chain saw or some percussion tools to have a
constant-pressure switch, one that shuts the power off when you let go
(a petrol chain saw, a throttle that does the same). It requires such a
switch on drills, angle grinders with wheels over 2 inches, belt sanders,
disc sanders with discs over 2 inches, reciprocating saws, and jig,
saber and scroll saws whose blade shanks are wider than a nominal quarter
inch too, which may also have a lock-on button only if the same finger
can turn it off in a single motion (29 CFR 1910.243(a)(2)). Taping or
wedging a trigger down defeats the one device that stops the tool when
your hand leaves it (general practice).

### A blade keeps turning after you let go

Letting go of the switch does not stop the blade; it starts it slowing.
The CPSC's 2023 proposed rule on table saws, withdrawn on 29 September
2025 but still a record of how injuries happened, lists among the ways
people's hands meet a blade an operator who "brushes debris from the
table while the blade is still spinning after shutoff." OSHA's rule for
power mowers allows a mower's blade up to 15 seconds to stop after the
power is cut (29 CFR 1910.243(e)(2)(vii)). So: let go, and wait until
the blade, bit or wheel has stopped before you lift the tool, put it
down or reach near it (general practice).

### Eyes, ears, lungs, hands

- **Eyes.** OSHA's rule requires eye or face protection against flying
  particles, with side protection where objects can fly (29 CFR
  1910.133(a)(1) and (2)). NIOSH tells students to wear safety glasses
  with side shields or goggles marked "Z87", for the American National
  Standards Institute's standard. With a grinder, OSHA's booklet says:
  "Always use eye or face protection."
- **Ears.** Saws, grinders and routers are loud. [Sound and
  Hearing](sound_and_hearing.md) gives the limits and how to protect
  your hearing.
- **Lungs.** Cutting and sanding make dust. Wood dust is in [Working with
  Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md). OSHA says that respirable
  crystalline silica, a lung hazard behind silicosis, an incurable
  disease, is created when cutting, sawing, grinding, drilling and
  crushing stone, rock, concrete, brick, block and mortar; see [The
  Ground Under You](the_ground_under_you.md). Sanding, grinding or
  planing old paint can raise lead dust: [Repairing a
  Building](repairing_a_building.md).
- **Hands.** OSHA's booklet says to use gloves and suitable footwear with
  electric tools, and gloves protect against sparks, hot metal and
  splinters (general practice). But a glove can be caught: the CPSC
  lists among table saw injuries the case where "the blade catches the
  operator's glove and pulls the operator's hand into the blade." Keep
  gloves, sleeves and anything loose clear of a spinning blade, bit or
  wheel, and wear close fitting gloves, or none, near anything that can
  wind them in (general practice).

### Who should use them

OSHA's booklet: "Employees should be trained in the proper use of all
tools." The Department of Labor's child labour rules go further: they
declare operating power-driven woodworking machines, portable ones
included, "particularly hazardous for minors between 16 and 18 years of
age" at work, apart from apprentices and student-learners (29 CFR
570.55). That rule does not reach a family workshop, but it tells you how
the government rates the danger (our reading). Children belong outside
the work area, along with everyone else not involved.

## The power behind the tool

OSHA's booklet sorts power tools by what drives them: electric,
pneumatic (compressed air), liquid fuel, hydraulic and powder-actuated.
Battery tools, which the booklet does not treat separately, make a sixth.
Each brings hazards of its own on top of the blade.

### Electric tools with a cord

OSHA's booklet: "Among the most serious hazards are electrical burns and
shocks." It warns that "Under certain conditions, even a small amount of
electric current can result in fibrillation of the heart and death," and
that a shock can make you fall off a ladder. The physics of why is in
[Electricity and How It Flows](electricity_and_how_it_flows.md).

**Three ways a tool is protected.** OSHA's booklet: a three-wire cord
with a ground, plugged into a grounded outlet; or a double-insulated
tool, in which "an internal layer of protective insulation completely
isolates the external housing of the tool"; or a low-voltage isolation
transformer. And: "The third prong must never be removed from the plug."
The CPSC's home electrical checklist asks whether every corded tool has a
three-prong plug or is marked double insulated, and warns that
"Metal-cased electrical tools without proper grounding become more
dangerous as old internal insulation wears and cracks": replace such a
tool, or at the very least use it only on a working ground-fault circuit
interrupter (GFCI).

**Use a GFCI.** A GFCI cuts the power when current leaks out of the
circuit, for instance through you. OSHA requires either GFCIs on the
temporary outlets workers use on construction sites or a program of
regular grounding checks on every cord and tool (29 CFR 1926.404(b)(1)).
For home projects the CPSC says to consider a portable
GFCI with electric tools (drills, saws, sanders) and with electric garden
equipment, and that portable ones are as effective as installed ones.

**Keep it dry.** OSHA's booklet: "Do not use electric tools in damp or
wet locations unless they are approved for that purpose." The CPSC: "Even
double insulated tools can become dangerous if they get wet." If a tool
gets wet, unplug it before touching it and let it dry thoroughly; if it
was immersed, have it tested at a qualified repair centre before you use
it again (CPSC).

**Look after the cord.** Check the cord and plug before each use for
cuts, cracks, missing or bent pins, and a crushed or pinched jacket; OSHA
requires that inspection at work (29 CFR 1910.334(a)(2)). Use an
extension cord rated for the tool, marked for outdoor use if you are
outdoors, with three prongs for a three-prong tool. NIOSH warns that an
undersized cord can overheat and drop the voltage and the tool's power,
and says to check the tool maker's recommendations for the gauge and
length of cord.

**A tool that tingles is broken.** NIOSH's manual includes the case of a
carpenter who was mildly shocked all morning by his power saw, changed
his extension cord to stop it, and was killed when he shifted the saw
from one hand to the other on a wet site. The saw, not the cord, was the
fault. NIOSH's lesson: "Any and all electrical equipment involved in a
malfunction should be taken out of service immediately." Another case:
a worker climbing a metal ladder to pass up an electric drill was killed
because its extension cord's grounding prong was missing and its ground
wire was touching the live wire; the drill was not double insulated.

**Cutting or drilling into a wall is cutting near wiring.** OSHA's
construction rule requires finding out, by asking, by looking or with
instruments, whether any part of an energised circuit, "exposed or
concealed", could be touched by the work before it starts (29 CFR
1926.416(a)(3)). Before you saw or drill into a wall, floor or ceiling,
switch off the circuits in it and find out what is behind it (general
practice; the electrical side is in [Where Your Own Electrical Work
Stops](where_your_electrical_work_stops.md)). Before digging, call 811:
[Square, Level and Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md).

### Battery tools

A battery tool has no cord to trip on or cut, and no mains voltage to
shock you, but it has every mechanical hazard in this guide: the blade,
the kickback, the flying chips and the noise are the same (general
practice). Its battery is its plug, so take it out before changing a
blade or clearing a jam (our reading of OSHA's rule to disconnect). The
battery itself is a lithium-ion pack: [Batteries and
Storage](batteries_and_storage.md) gives the US Fire Administration's
warning signs (a smell, a change in colour or shape, heat, leaking or odd
noises) and the advice to buy products tested by a recognised
laboratory.

### Air tools

OSHA's booklet: the first danger of a pneumatic tool is "getting hit by
one of the tool's attachments or by some kind of fastener the worker is
using with the tool." Its rules: fasten the tool securely to the hose;
use the safety clip or retainer that stops a chisel or other attachment
from flying out; a hose over half an inch across needs a safety valve at
the supply that cuts the flow if the hose fails; eye protection is
required. And: "Compressed air guns should never be pointed toward
anyone." Using compressed air to blow off dust is limited at work to
under 30 psi, with chip guarding and protective equipment (29 CFR
1926.302(b)(4)); [Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md) has
more. Disconnect the air before changing an attachment or clearing a jam
(OSHA's rule to disconnect tools, above), and shut the air off at the
supply and let the pressure out of the hose before you uncouple it, so
that neither the hose nor the tool can whip or fire (general practice).
Nail guns, the most common air tool at home, have their own rules,
starting with the full sequential trigger, in [Nails, Screws and
Bolts](nails_screws_and_bolts.md).

### Fuel-powered tools

OSHA's booklet: "The most serious hazard associated with the use of
fuel-powered tools comes from fuel vapors that can burn or explode and
also give off dangerous exhaust fumes." Shut the engine off and let it
cool before refuelling, keep fuel in approved containers, keep a fire
extinguisher at hand, and remember the exhaust. The CPSC's safety alert
on portable generators calls carbon monoxide "a poison you cannot see or
smell", and its rule for generators is outdoors only, never in a home,
garage, basement, crawlspace or shed, even with fans running or doors and
windows open. The same rule fits any engine, a chain saw or a mower
included (our extension, as in [Keeping Things
Working](keeping_things_working.md)). The commonest fuel tool at home
after the mower is the chain saw, below.

### Powder-actuated tools

Powder-actuated tools fire a nail or stud into concrete or steel with a
gunpowder cartridge. OSHA's booklet: they "operate like a loaded gun and
must be treated with extreme caution. In fact, they are so dangerous that
they must be operated only by specially trained employees." OSHA's rule
allows only workers trained on the particular tool to use one (29 CFR
1926.302(e)(1)). NIOSH's manual records a carpenter's apprentice killed
by a nail that passed through a hollow concrete form and travelled 27
feet before it struck him, and a worker killed by a nail that shot
straight through the plywood and the stud it was meant to fasten. This
is not a household tool (our recommendation).

### Hydraulic tools

OSHA's booklet: never exceed the manufacturer's safe operating pressure
for hoses, valves and fittings. Jacks are the hydraulic tool most homes
own, and [Keeping Things Working](keeping_things_working.md) has the
rules: rated for the load, on firm ground, and the load held on stands,
never on the jack.

## The respect each tool demands

### Drills and drivers

- **When the bit jams, the drill turns instead.** A drill pushes hard
  against a bit that is spinning; when the bit catches, in a knot, a nail
  or the far side of a hole, all that twist goes into your wrist (general
  practice). Hold it with both hands, use the side handle if it has one,
  and on a driver set the clutch low enough to slip (general practice).
- **Clamp small work.** A small piece held in the hand spins with the bit
  (general practice; OSHA's clamp rule above).
- **Hair and sleeves.** A spinning bit winds in whatever touches it
  (OSHA's clothing rule above).
- **Know what is behind the wall** before drilling into it (above).

### Circular saws

- **The guards.** OSHA's rule for portable circular saws with blades over
  2 inches requires an upper guard covering the blade to the depth of
  the teeth, and a lower guard that does the same and, when the saw is
  withdrawn from the work, returns "automatically and instantly" to
  cover the blade (29 CFR 1910.243(a)(1)). Check that the lower guard
  snaps back before every cut; [Working with
  Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) calls a sticky lower guard what it
  is, a defect.
- **Kickback.** OSHA's woodworking guidance defines it for the hand-held
  saw: "when the blade catches the stock and throws it back toward the
  operator". It happens when the wood twists and binds against the blade
  or catches in the teeth, and OSHA lists the causes: a blade that is dull
  or set at the wrong height, ripping along the grain more than cutting
  across it, poor lumber (knots, nails, frozen wood), and missing
  safeguards. With a hand-held saw, it is often the saw that is thrown
  back, out of the cut and towards you (general practice).
- **The controls OSHA lists.** Use the right blade for the cut (not a
  crosscut blade for ripping), keep it sharp, run the saw at the speed
  the maker specifies, leave clearance for the stock, avoid deep cuts,
  and "Properly support all pieces of stock, including the cut and uncut
  ends, scrap, and finished product." Keep your hands out of the line of
  the cut. OSHA's general kickback advice is to stand to the side, and
  with a hand-held saw that means beside the line of the blade, never
  behind it (our reading).
- **Hands above the work, never under it.** Never reach under the work
  while the saw is cutting, where the blade comes through, and never
  hold the piece being cut off in your hand: support it instead, as
  OSHA's control above says (general practice).
- **Let it stop.** Release the trigger and wait for the blade to stop
  before you lift the saw out of the cut or put it down (general
  practice; see "A blade keeps turning" above).

### Table saws, jointers and bench grinders

These stationary tools are covered in [Working with
Wood](../making/working_with_wood.md) and
[Sharpening](../making/sharpening.md). One finding belongs here too,
because it applies to every blade. In the CPSC's 2023 proposal for a
table saw rule, its staff assessed that most blade-contact injuries were
not related to kickback, drawing on its 2017 special study of injuries,
figures from the Power Tool Institute (which the proposal describes as
the industry trade group for makers of consumer table saws) and one saw
maker's own data on its blade-stopping system. The proposal lists ways
contact happens without kickback: a hand too close while feeding a
small piece, a distracted operator, a glove caught by the blade,
reaching to regain control of a piece, and brushing debris away while
the blade is still turning. The Commission withdrew that proposal, with
the rest of its table saw rulemaking, on 29 September 2025, saying it no
longer intends to issue a final rule (90 FR 46541); the finding is cited
here for what it says about injuries, not as a rule.

### Angle grinders and other abrasive wheels

A grinding or cut-off wheel turns very fast and can fly apart. OSHA's
booklet and its rule for portable abrasive wheels set out the defences:

- **The guard,** which on a right-angle grinder stays between you and
  the wheel, adjusted so that pieces of a broken wheel are thrown away
  from you, with no more than half the wheel exposed (29 CFR
  1910.243(c)(3)).
- **The right wheel.** Inspect every wheel closely before mounting it,
  sound it with a ring test, and check that the grinder's spindle speed
  does not exceed the maximum operating speed marked on the wheel (29
  CFR 1910.243(c)(5)). A wheel rated for less than the grinder's speed
  can fly apart. Fit only wheels and discs made for that grinder (general
  practice).
- **Start-up.** OSHA's booklet: "An abrasive wheel may disintegrate or
  explode during start-up." Let it come up to speed before it touches the
  work, and "never stand in the plane of rotation of the wheel as it
  accelerates to full operating speed."
- **Eyes and face, always,** and turn it off when not in use. "Never
  clamp a hand-held grinder in a vise." (All from OSHA's booklet.)
- **Both hands, the side handle on.** A cut-off disc pinched in the cut
  can throw the grinder back at you, so keep the side handle fitted and
  both hands on the tool, and support the work so the cut cannot close
  on the disc. Never grind with the flat side of a thin cut-off disc,
  which is made to cut with its edge and can break if pushed sideways
  (all general practice).
- **Sparks.** A grinder throws a stream of hot sparks. Clear away
  anything that can burn from where they land, and never grind near fuel,
  solvents or sawdust (general practice).
- **Masonry.** Cutting stone, concrete or brick makes silica dust (above);
  OSHA's construction rule for silica sets out water and respirator
  controls for hand-held saws, given in [The Ground Under
  You](the_ground_under_you.md).

### Jigsaws and reciprocating saws

OSHA's switch rule puts every reciprocating saw, and any jig, saber or
scroll saw whose blade shank is wider than a nominal quarter inch, with
the drills: a constant-pressure switch, with a lock-on button allowed
only if the same finger can release it. A jig, saber or scroll saw whose
shank is a nominal quarter inch wide or less may have a plain on-off
switch, like a router (29 CFR 1910.243(a)(2)(ii) and (iii)). The rule
turns on the width of the blade shank, not on the size of the saw. The
blade sticks out below and behind the work, where you cannot see it:
know what is there, including wires and pipes in a wall, and keep the
saw's shoe pressed against the work so the blade cannot hammer it
(general practice).

### Sanders and routers

OSHA's rule requires a belt sander to be guarded at each nip point where
the belt runs onto a pulley, and the unused run of the belt guarded too
(29 CFR 1910.243(a)(3)). A sander's danger is mostly its dust: wood dust,
silica, and lead from old paint (all above). A router turns its cutter
very fast in the open: both hands on it, the work clamped, and the cutter
stopped before you set it down (general practice).

### Chain saws

The chain saw's protective gear and OSHA's logging rules (cut-resistant
leg protection, boots, eye and head protection, starting on the ground
with the chain brake engaged, both hands, the brake on to carry it) are
in [Firewood](firewood.md). Here is the mechanism they protect against,
from the USDA Forest Service's chain saw course: "Kickback is the most
powerful reactive force you will encounter while operating a chain saw."
It happens "when the upper quadrant of the bar nose contacts a solid
object or is pinched," and "During kickback, the bar is forced up and
back in an uncontrolled arc toward the sawyer."

The course's ways to avoid it: hold the saw with both hands, the thumb
wrapped round the handlebar; know where the bar's nose is at all times
and never let it touch another object; never cut with the saw higher
than your shoulder; never overreach; cut one log at a time; stand to the
side of the kickback arc; keep the chain sharp and correctly tensioned;
and use a low-kickback chain. OSHA's rule requires a chain brake on saws
placed in service since its logging standard took effect, and forbids
removing or disabling any kickback device (29 CFR 1910.266(e)(2)(i)).

Why the leg protection works, from the same course: when a running chain
strikes saw chaps, fibres from the chaps are pulled into the saw's drive
sprocket, slowing and quickly stopping the chain. And why the left leg
matters most: "Because chain saws require right-hand operation, the
majority of chain contact injuries occur on the left leg." The course
itself, for basic to intermediate sawyers, runs 16 to 32 hours.

## Worked example: cutting a sheet of plywood with a circular saw

A 4 by 8 foot sheet cut along its length, at home. Each step names its
source; the rest is general practice.

1. **Read the saw's manual,** and fit a sharp blade of the right type
   for plywood (OSHA: the right blade for the cut, kept sharp).
2. **Inspect.** The cord and plug, ground prong included (OSHA's
   booklet); the lower guard, which must snap back on its own (29 CFR
   1910.243(a)(1)).
3. **Power.** Plug into a GFCI outlet or a portable GFCI (CPSC), through
   an extension cord rated for the saw and laid where neither you nor the
   blade can catch it.
4. **Support the whole sheet.** Lay it across supports on both sides of
   the line, so that both pieces stay supported when the cut finishes
   (OSHA: support all pieces, including the cut and uncut ends). A sheet
   held only at its ends sags in the middle and closes on the blade
   (general practice).
5. **Clamp it,** so both hands are free for the saw (OSHA's booklet).
6. **Set the depth** so the blade reaches just through the sheet
   (general practice); OSHA says deep cuts increase the likelihood of
   kickback.
7. **Protect yourself.** Safety glasses with side shields, hearing
   protection, a dust mask for the sawdust, no loose sleeves (above).
8. **Stand to the side** of the line of the blade, not behind it, with the
   cord behind you (our reading of OSHA's advice, above).
9. **Start the saw clear of the wood,** let it reach full speed, and feed
   it steadily. If the blade binds or the saw labours, let go of the
   trigger and hold the saw still until the blade stops; do not pull it
   back out of the cut while it is turning.
10. **At the end of the cut, let go and wait** for the blade to stop
    before lifting the saw, and unplug it before changing the blade
    (OSHA's booklet).

## Worked example: cutting off a rusted bolt with an angle grinder

1. **The right disc.** A cut-off disc made for the grinder, inspected for
   cracks, with a maximum speed marked on it at least the grinder's speed
   (29 CFR 1910.243(c)(5)).
2. **The guard on,** turned so it sits between you and the disc (29 CFR
   1910.243(c)(3)).
3. **Clear the sparks' path** of anything that can burn: fuel, rags,
   sawdust, dry grass (general practice). Oily rags can catch fire by
   themselves even without sparks: [Keeping Things
   Working](keeping_things_working.md).
4. **Eye and face protection,** hearing protection, gloves for the hot
   metal, sleeves close (OSHA's booklet; the rest above).
5. **Clamp the work, never the grinder** (OSHA's booklet).
6. **Start it with the disc clear of the work,** stand out of the plane of
   the disc while it comes up to speed, then cut with both hands on the
   tool (OSHA's booklet; both hands is general practice).
7. **Let it stop** before you put it down, and unplug it or take its
   battery out before changing the disc.

## Know where your own work stops

- **Repairs inside a tool.** The CPSC says to have damaged cords on
  electric garden tools replaced by a qualified repair facility, and a
  tool that is not working as it should repaired there or thrown away,
  because damaged tools become shock and fire hazards. A tool that has
  been under water is tested at a qualified repair centre before it is
  used again.
- **Any tool that shocks you,** even mildly: out of service at once
  (NIOSH).
- **Powder-actuated tools:** only for people trained on the particular
  tool (OSHA). Not for a household (our recommendation).
- **Felling trees** with a chain saw: training first, as [Firewood](firewood.md)
  sets out; the Forest Service's own course takes 16 to 32 hours.
- **A tool nobody has shown you how to use.** Ask NIOSH's two questions.
- **Cutting into walls with wiring, or ground with buried lines,** before
  the circuits are off or the lines are marked: [Where Your Own Electrical
  Work Stops](where_your_electrical_work_stops.md) and [Square, Level and
  Plumb](square_level_and_plumb.md).

## Traps

- **"The guard gets in the way."** That is its job. OSHA: guards must
  never be removed while a tool is in use.
- **"I'll just hold it."** Clamp it; the holding hand is the nearest one
  to the blade.
- **"It only tingles."** A tingle is a fault reporting itself. NIOSH's
  carpenter changed the cord; the saw killed him.
- **"It's switched off."** The blade is still turning, and a switched-off
  tool on a live cord can be switched on by a careless hand. Unplug it.
- **"Gloves make it safer."** Not near anything that can wind them in.
- **"A bigger disc cuts faster."** A disc rated for less than the
  grinder's speed can fly apart (OSHA), and a bigger one no longer sits
  inside the guard (general practice).
- **"It's cordless, so it's safe."** Only from shock.
- **"Just one quick cut without glasses."** It only takes one chip.

## How the game models it

The game's craftsmanship is hand work, and no tool in it can hurt you.

- **Hand tools for hand crafts.** A craft you make yourself at the
  workbench, forge, anvil or electronics bench names the tools it needs:
  a hammer and hand saw for carpentry, a wrench and screwdriver for
  assembly, a hammer at the forge and anvil, a soldering iron and pliers
  at the electronics bench (`data/crafting/tools.ron`). In every play
  mode the Crafting page's Craft button waits until the recipe's parts
  are in your backpack, or in your home's storage where you can reach
  it, and its tools are in your backpack (`src/gui/pages/crafting.rs`).
  In the Normal play mode, where the game starts, the craft then uses up
  the parts and wears each tool by one use; in Creative, and in Dev if
  you choose it, it uses nothing up and wears no tool while the Inventory
  page's Creative mode switch is on (`src/systems/crafting/mod.rs`). The
  page's "Dev: stock all materials" button, shown only in Dev and while
  the dev cheats switch is on, as it is by default, puts a full stack
  of every recipe's parts in your backpack, but no tools; the starting
  kit holds every tool the recipes name except the soldering iron and the
  chisel (`data/world/player.ron`).
- **The power tools are names only.** The item list includes an Electric
  Drill ("Corded rotary drill") and a Circular Saw ("Powered rotary
  cutting saw"), each with a durability of 200 uses (`data/items.csv`),
  but no recipe uses or makes either and no vendor sells either. The Home
  page's outline of an ideal homestead counts a full power tool set in
  its luxury tier at about 0.5 kWh a day (`data/home_outline.json`). The
  sawmill that turns logs into planks is an automatic machine with no
  power connection (`data/machines/home.ron`).
- **Electric workshop stations need power.** The electronics bench (150
  watts) and the sewing machine (100 watts) are electric, and the
  Crafting page will not start a craft at one that has no power. In the
  default Station-supplied life support mode the ship's reactor covers
  any shortfall aboard, so this shows up at a planet build site with no
  power of its own.

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there
are no cuts, no kickback, no shocks, no noise and no dust, nothing needs
a guard or protective equipment, and no tool has to be learned before it
is used. The only tools in it are hand tools and a soldering iron, which
the item list describes as electric ("Electric solder-melting tip",
`data/items.csv`) but which draws no power of its own and wears like a
hand tool (the electronics bench it is used at does need power), and the
only danger any of them carries is wearing out.

## You own this when

- You read the manual and have been shown how to use a tool before you
  use it alone.
- You check the cord, plug and guards before you start, and you never use
  a tool with a guard removed or wedged.
- You clamp the work, keep both hands on the tool, and keep everyone else
  clear.
- You unplug the tool, or take out its battery, before you change a blade
  or clear a jam, and you wait for the blade to stop.
- You wear eye protection every time, and hearing and dust protection
  when the job calls for it, with nothing loose near the blade.
- You use a GFCI with corded tools, keep them dry, and take any tool that
  tingles out of service.
- You can explain kickback in a circular saw and a chain saw, and how to
  set up a cut so it does not happen.
- You can match a grinding wheel to a grinder and say why it matters.
- You know which tools are not for a household at all.

### Labelled in the text as general practice or our reading, not sourced

- Taking the battery out as the way to disconnect a battery tool, and a
  battery tool keeping every mechanical hazard.
- That OSHA's rules set out what keeps hands whole; that a guard removed
  or wedged has been switched off by a person; that the Labor
  Department's rule shows how the government rates the danger; that the
  holding hand is the one nearest the blade.
- That gloves protect against sparks, hot metal and splinters; a bigger
  disc no longer sitting inside the guard.
- Taping or wedging a trigger; waiting for a blade, bit or wheel to stop
  before lifting it or putting it down.
- Close-fitting gloves or none near anything that can wind them in.
- A drill twisting when the bit catches, the side handle and the clutch;
  clamping small work for a drill.
- That a hand-held circular saw is often itself thrown back by kickback;
  standing beside the line of a hand-held saw's blade as our reading of
  OSHA's advice to stand to the side; setting the depth just through the
  sheet; a sheet held at its ends sagging and closing on the blade; not
  pulling a turning blade back out of a cut; starting the saw clear of
  the wood.
- Never reaching under the work or holding the offcut of a circular saw
  cut.
- Fitting only wheels and discs made for the grinder; clearing the
  sparks' path; both hands on a grinder, the side handle on; a pinched
  cut-off disc kicking the grinder back; never side-grinding with a
  cut-off disc.
- Shutting off the air and letting the pressure out of the hose before
  uncoupling an air tool.
- That carbon monoxide rules for generators fit any engine (our
  extension).
- Switching off the circuits in a wall and finding out what is behind it
  before cutting or drilling.
- The jigsaw's shoe against the work, the blade below and behind it, and
  both hands on a router.
- That powder-actuated tools are not for a household.
