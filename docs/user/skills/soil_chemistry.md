# Soil Chemistry

A soil test report says your potassium is "optimum" and your pH is 5.4.
A bag of fertilizer says 10-10-10. A neighbour says lime "sweetens" the
soil and wood ash "burns" it. All of that is chemistry, and all of it
is about one question: what can a root actually take up, and what
stops it?

This guide answers that question at the level a gardener needs. It
explains where nutrients sit in soil and why some stay put while others
wash away, why pH controls so much, what salt does to a garden, how the
numbers on a bag translate into the elements a plant uses, and how to
handle the materials you buy to change your soil without hurting
yourself or your well water.

It sits between three other guides. [What Soil Is](what_soil_is.md)
explains texture, organic matter and the pH scale itself. [How Plants
Work](how_plants_work.md) lists the elements a plant needs and how to
read a shortage from the leaves. [Testing and Correcting
Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) covers sending a sample to a
laboratory and the amounts of lime, sulfur and fertilizer to use. This
guide is the chemistry in between, so that the report and the bag make
sense.

Its facts come mainly from the US Department of Agriculture's Natural
Resources Conservation Service (NRCS): its Agricultural Waste
Management Field Handbook, a long-standing technical manual for farm
nutrient management, and its soil quality sheets. The safety facts
come from the National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health
(NIOSH), the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) and
the Environmental Protection Agency (EPA). All are United States
government publications, in the public domain. Where something is
general practice or our own reasoning, the text says so.

## First, the materials themselves

Most of the chemistry in this guide happens slowly, in the ground. The
part that can hurt you happens in your hands, when you open a bag.
Read this before you buy anything.

### Not every bag called "lime" is garden lime

Three different chemicals go by the name "lime", and only one of them
belongs in a garden. NIOSH's Pocket Guide to Chemical Hazards tells
them apart:

| What it is | How NIOSH identifies it | What NIOSH says it does to you |
|---|---|---|
| Calcium carbonate | Occurs in nature as limestone, chalk, marble and dolomite, among others | Irritation of eyes, skin and airways; cough |
| Calcium hydroxide | Also called hydrated lime, slaked lime, caustic lime | Irritation, and eye and skin burns; cough, bronchitis |
| Calcium oxide | Also called quicklime, burnt lime, pebble lime, unslaked lime, and simply "lime" | Irritation; skin inflammation; ulcers and perforation of the wall between the nostrils; it reacts with water and gives off heat |

Garden lime is the first one: finely ground limestone (calcium
carbonate), or dolomitic limestone, which also carries magnesium. That
is what the extension services cited in [Testing and Correcting
Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) recommend.

So:

- **Read the chemical name on the bag, not the word "lime".** Buy
  ground limestone or dolomitic limestone for the garden.
- **Leave hydrated lime and quicklime out of a home garden** (general
  practice, following the extension advice to use ground limestone).
  Both burn skin and eyes, and quicklime heats up when it gets wet,
  which, on damp soil or damp skin, is exactly where it ends up.
- **Wear gloves, eye protection and a dust mask** when spreading any
  lime, and spread it on a still day (general practice; NIOSH lists eye
  and airway irritation even for limestone).
- **If lime gets in your eyes,** NIOSH's first aid for all three is to
  irrigate immediately: wash the eyes with large amounts of water,
  lifting the upper and lower lids now and then, and get medical
  attention immediately.
- **If quicklime gets on your skin,** NIOSH says to flush the skin with
  water at once, take off any clothing the chemical has soaked through
  and flush the skin under it, and "Get medical attention promptly."
- **If hydrated lime gets on your skin,** NIOSH says to flush the skin
  at once with soap and water, take off any soaked clothing and flush
  the skin under it, and "If irritation persists after washing, get
  medical attention."
- **If someone swallows either,** NIOSH's first aid is medical
  attention immediately.

Wood ash, which also raises pH, is caustic too; the precautions for it
are in [Testing and Correcting Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md).

### Garden sulfur burns

Elemental sulfur, sold to lower pH, is a fuel. NOAA's CAMEO Chemicals
database calls it flammable and says it burns "with a pale blue flame
that may be difficult to see in daylight", producing toxic sulfur
dioxide gas. It also warns that sulfur reacts violently with strong
oxidising agents, and lists dangerous reactions with chlorates,
perchlorates and calcium hypochlorite, which is sold as pool chlorine
(general knowledge).

So keep garden sulfur dry, away from heat and flame, and stored apart
from pool and other oxidising chemicals, and never mix it with other
chemicals (general practice following CAMEO's reactivity profile).

### Fertilizers, weed-and-feed, and the label

EPA's consumer brochure Read the Label First: Protect Your Household is
written for "household products such as cleaners and pesticides". That
covers the many lawn products that combine fertilizer with a
weedkiller, since a weedkiller is a pesticide. Applying the same points
to plain fertilizer is our application of the brochure, not something
it says:

- The label tells you how to use and store the product safely, gives
  first aid instructions, and gives a number to call for help.
- Keep products in their original containers. In EPA's words, "It is
  very dangerous to put products in food and beverage containers",
  because children think something in a familiar bottle is safe.
- Use the right amount. Using more, the brochure says, can waste the
  product and money and can harm people, pets and the environment.
- Keep children and pets off the treated ground for as long as the
  label says. In EPA's words, "Keep pets and children away from treated
  areas as directed on the label." When a weed-and-feed is spread over
  a lawn, the treated area is the whole lawn (our note).
- Do not apply a product where it can run into ponds, creeks or other
  water supplies, and never pour lawn and garden products down the
  drain.
- Keep your poison control centre's number handy, and have the label
  with you when you call. In the United States the number is
  1-800-222-1222 (general knowledge; it is printed on most product
  labels).

Store garden chemicals locked away or out of reach of children and pets
(general practice, following EPA's warning that such products could
harm them).

### Too much nitrogen ends up in drinking water

Nitrate, the form soil bacteria turn ammonium into, washes easily into
ground water (the reasons are below). EPA's drinking water
standard for nitrate is 10 milligrams per litre, measured as nitrogen,
and EPA explains why: infants below the age of six months who drink
water with nitrate above that level could become seriously ill and, if
untreated, may die, with shortness of breath and blue-baby syndrome
among the symptoms. EPA lists runoff from fertilizer use, leaking
septic tanks and sewage, and natural deposits as the sources.

If your household drinks from its own well, have the water tested for
nitrate, especially if a baby will drink it or it will be used for
formula; [Testing Water](testing_water.md) explains how. And keep
fertilizer, manure and compost heaps well away from the wellhead
(general practice).

**Do not try to boil nitrate out.** Boiling kills germs, so it is the
natural thing to reach for, and here it does the opposite of what you
want. EPA: "Boiling water will NOT reduce nitrate levels. In fact, it
will make the level of nitrate worse because some of the water will
evaporate but the nitrate will not." Until a test shows the well is
below the limit, make a baby's formula with water you know is safe,
such as bottled water (general practice).

## Plants take up ions dissolved in water

A root does not eat soil. It takes up nutrients dissolved in the thin
film of water around the soil particles, and almost all of them are
dissolved as ions: atoms or small groups of atoms carrying an electric
charge. Some ions are positive (cations) and some negative (anions).
NRCS's field handbook lists the common ones a soil exchanges:

| Positive ions (cations) | Negative ions (anions) |
|---|---|
| Ammonium (NH4+), a nitrogen form | Nitrate (NO3-), the other nitrogen form |
| Potassium (K+) | Phosphate (H2PO4- and HPO4 2-) |
| Calcium (Ca2+) and magnesium (Mg2+) | Sulfate (SO4 2-) |
| Iron, manganese, copper and zinc | Chloride, borate and molybdate |
| Hydrogen (H+) and aluminium (Al3+), the acidity | Hydroxide (OH-) and bicarbonate |

Two facts follow straight from that list.

**A nutrient is only food when it is in the water, in the right form.**
[How Plants Work](how_plants_work.md) calls this "presence is not
availability": a soil can be full of phosphorus that no root can reach.

**The plant cannot tell where an ion came from.** NRCS's field handbook
describes how organic nitrogen in manure and compost is turned into
inorganic nitrogen by microbes before plants use it, and names
ammonium and nitrate as the primary nitrogen forms plants take up. An
ammonium ion from compost and one from a bag of fertilizer are the same
ion. The real differences between organic and mineral fertilizers are
how fast the ions appear, what else comes with them (organic matter,
salts), and how easily they are overdone (our reading of the
handbook). [Life in Soil](life_in_soil.md) explains the microbes that
do the converting.

## Cation exchange: the soil's pantry

If nutrients were only ever dissolved in water, every heavy rain would
wash them away. They are not, and the reason is the most important idea
in soil chemistry. NRCS's field handbook puts it directly: "Plants
obtain many of their nutrients from soil by an electrochemical process
called cation exchange. This process is the key to understanding soil
fertility."

Here is how the handbook explains it. Clay particles and humus (well
decomposed organic matter) are extremely fine, so they have an enormous
surface: it says one gram of the clay mineral bentonite has been
estimated to have about 800 square metres. Those surfaces carry a
negative electric charge, so they attract and hold positive ions such
as ammonium, calcium, magnesium and potassium. A root can take those
ions up by exchanging them for other ions, such as hydrogen. Sand
grains are too big to do much of this.

The amount of positive charge a soil can hold is its **cation exchange
capacity (CEC)**, measured in milliequivalents per 100 grams of soil
(meq/100 g). The handbook gives typical values of 6.3 for sand and 27.2
for a clay or loam, and says the higher the CEC, the greater the
soil's potential fertility; [Testing and Correcting
Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) gives NC State Extension's range
for comparison. The point is the gap, not the exact figure: a clay or
an organic soil can hold several times the stock of a sand.

Three things the pantry does and does not do:

- **It holds the positive ions, not the negative ones.** The handbook
  says cation exchange can only store and release positively charged
  nutrients; phosphorus and sulfur, which are anions, are not held this
  way. Soils have some anion exchange capacity too, but the handbook
  says it is relatively low in most soils.
- **It holds some ions harder than others.** The handbook says the pull
  on ammonium is weak compared with potassium, magnesium and calcium,
  so most of the sites are taken by those three.
- **It can be grown.** The handbook lists the practices that raise CEC:
  adding organic material, managing crop residue, rotations, cover
  crops, reduced tillage, and liming an acid soil to raise its pH. Of
  those, building organic matter is the one a gardener controls most.

What this means in a garden (our reading of the handbook): a sandy soil
with a small pantry gives up what you add quickly. Feed it little and
often, and build its organic matter. A clay soil with a big pantry
holds more and changes more slowly, which is good for nutrients and
also means it takes more lime to change its pH (see buffering, below).

## Nitrogen, the nutrient that will not sit still

NRCS's field handbook calls nitrogen the most difficult of the major
elements to manage, because of the many pathways it can follow. Here is
the path, in the handbook's terms.

1. **Organic nitrogen** (in compost, manure, dead roots and soil organic
   matter) is broken down by microbes into **ammonium** (NH4+).
2. Ammonium is a cation, so the soil's exchange sites hold it, and it
   does not readily leach. Plants can take it up, microbes can use it,
   and some can escape as ammonia gas.
3. Soil bacteria convert ammonium to **nitrate** (NO3-), a step called
   nitrification. Nitrate is readily available to plants and is an
   important nitrogen form for most crops.
4. Nitrate is an anion, so it stays in the soil water and moves with
   it. The handbook says nitrate can leach because it has a low
   capacity to stick to soil and does not form insoluble compounds.
5. In waterlogged, airless soil, bacteria can turn nitrate into
   nitrous oxide or nitrogen gas, which escapes to the air. This is
   denitrification.

The handbook sums up the losses: nitrogen is lost from the soil mainly
by leaching with the soil water and by denitrification into the air.

**Where nitrate leaches.** The handbook says soils with high
permeability, coarse texture, or a shallow water table are the most
likely to lose nitrate to ground water, while slowly permeable,
fine-textured or steep soils lose more nitrogen in runoff. It adds that
keeping vegetation with living roots in a field helps capture nitrate
and other nutrients before they are lost.

**Where nitrogen vanishes into the air.** Two places. From a wet soil,
by denitrification (above). And from manure left on the surface: the
handbook says manure on hot, dry soil dries quickly and loses most of
its ammonia within 24 hours, especially in a hot, dry wind, and that
working it into the soil before it dries reduces the loss.

**Why nitrogen fertilizer turns soil acid.** NRCS's soil pH sheet lists
nitrification of ammonium, from fertilizers and from decomposing organic
matter, among the sources of acidity in soil. A bed fed with ammonium
or urea fertilizer drifts acid over the years; [Testing and Correcting
Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) gives the lime needed to cancel
it.

**Why cold soil feeds nothing.** Every step from organic nitrogen to
nitrate is done by microbes, and the handbook says microbial activity
almost stops below 41 F (5 C).

**Worked example: an autumn feed on sandy ground.** You spread a
nitrogen fertilizer on a sandy bed in October, after the crop is
finished, and the winter rains begin. The ammonium is held for a while,
the bacteria turn some of it to nitrate while the soil is still warm,
and the nitrate leaves with the rain, down toward the ground water,
before anything is growing to catch it. Little of what you paid for
reaches a crop, and some of it may reach a well (our reasoning from the
handbook's statements). The handbook's answer is living roots, so sow a
cover crop instead and feed in spring, when a crop is growing (our
application of it).

## Phosphorus, the nutrient that will not move

Phosphorus behaves almost the opposite way. NRCS's field handbook says
it is removed from the soil water by sticking to clay particles and by
combining with carbonates, iron, aluminium and stable organic
compounds, and that phosphorus held this way is considered unavailable
to plants. It also gives the speed of it: "Chemical reactions in the
soil immobilize about half of the added soluble phosphate within the
first day", with more held over the first month.

Consequences:

- **Phosphorus rarely leaches,** except, the handbook says, in sandy
  soils low in clay and organic matter.
- **It travels with the soil instead.** The handbook says erosion and
  runoff carry the attached phosphorus off the field into surface
  water. That is why phosphorus above what crops need is treated as a
  water pollutant, and why [Testing and Correcting
  Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) says to add none when a test
  reads above optimum.
- **Put it where the roots will be** (general practice following the
  handbook): mix phosphorus into the root zone at planting rather than
  scattering it on the surface of an established bed, because it will
  not move far from where it lands.
- **pH matters at both ends.** NRCS's soil pH sheet lists phosphorus
  among the nutrients deficient in acid soils and among those
  frequently deficient in very alkaline ones.

## Potassium, calcium and magnesium

These three are the cations the exchange sites mostly hold. NRCS's
field handbook says potassium is less mobile than nitrogen and more
mobile than phosphorus; that potassium, and to a lesser extent calcium
and magnesium, can leach out of the coarser surface layer but are
usually caught by the clay of the subsoil a few inches lower; and that
potassium leaching is not a ground water hazard.

The handbook also warns against overdoing potassium: excess potassium
in the soil can block the uptake of other nutrients, can add to a salt
problem, and at high rates on clay soils can break up the soil's
crumbs and stop water soaking in. Wood ash is a common source of
potassium at home, which is one more reason [Testing and Correcting
Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) caps how much ash to use.

## pH, the master switch

[What Soil Is](what_soil_is.md) explains the pH scale. Here is what pH
does chemically, from NRCS's soil quality sheet on pH (April 2011).

**Where acidity comes from.** The sheet lists carbonic acid, formed when
carbon dioxide from decomposing organic matter and roots dissolves in
soil water; acid released by roots; aluminium reacting with water;
nitrification of ammonium; sulfur compounds; and rain, including acid
rain. Over time, the leaching of calcium, magnesium and other basic
cations, and their removal in harvested crops, acidify soil. When those
cations are lost, hydrogen ions take their place on the clay and humus.
Below about pH 5, aluminium dissolves and reacts with water to make the
soil more acid still.

**Why dry country is alkaline.** In arid climates, the sheet says,
weathering and leaching are weaker, cations accumulate, and soil
becomes neutral or alkaline. The rock the soil formed from matters too:
it contrasts soils formed in limestone-rich (calcareous) material with
soils formed in granite. [The Ground Under You](the_ground_under_you.md)
explains how to find out what yours formed from.

**What pH does to the nutrients.** In the sheet's words, in acid soils
"calcium and magnesium, nitrate-nitrogen, phosphorus, boron, and
molybdenum are deficient, whereas aluminum and manganese are abundant,
sometimes at levels toxic to some plants." And: "Phosphorus, iron,
copper, zinc, and boron are frequently deficient in very alkaline
soils." It says many crops grow best near neutral, about pH 6 to 7.5,
with a few preferring acid or alkaline soil.

**What pH does to soil life.** The same sheet says bacteria decline at
low pH while fungi tolerate a wide range, and that nitrification and
nitrogen fixation are both inhibited by low pH, which ties pH back to
nitrogen supply ([Life in Soil](life_in_soil.md)).

**What it costs a crop.** The sheet reproduces a published table of
relative yields at different pH, for crops grown in rotation. A few of
its rows:

| Crop | pH 4.7 | pH 5.0 | pH 5.7 | pH 6.8 | pH 7.5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alfalfa | 2 | 9 | 42 | 100 | 100 |
| Barley | 0 | 23 | 80 | 95 | 100 |
| Corn | 34 | 73 | 83 | 100 | 85 |
| Wheat | 68 | 78 | 89 | 100 | 99 |
| Oats | 77 | 93 | 99 | 98 | 100 |

Read across a row and the lesson is plain. Some crops, alfalfa and
barley here, collapse in acid soil; oats lose far less; corn suffers at
both ends. "Raise the pH" is not a universal good. It depends on the
crop.

**Buffering.** The sheet says some soils resist a change of pH more
than others (their buffering capacity), so the amount of lime needed to
raise an acid soil "must be determined specifically for each field". It
says clay and organic matter act as buffers, that adding organic matter
increases buffering capacity, and that coarse-textured soils may
acidify easily compared with clay soils, because they have little
organic matter, a low buffering capacity and a low cation exchange
capacity. Why that should be so is
our explanation, not the sheet's: the reserve of acidity held on the
clay and humus (the same exchange sites as above) has to be neutralised
before the pH in the soil water moves. Either way, a clay or a soil
rich in organic matter takes more lime than a sand for the same change;
[Testing and Correcting Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) explains
the "buffer pH" line on a test report that measures it.

## Salt and sodium

Every soil holds some salts, and in humid regions rain keeps washing
them out. In dry ones it does not.

The Soil Quality Test Kit Guide (Agricultural Research Service and
NRCS, July 2001) says all soils contain some salts, which are essential
for plant growth, but that excess salts hinder plant growth by
affecting the soil-water balance; that salt-affected soils are found
largely in the western arid and semiarid parts of the country, where
low rainfall lets salts accumulate; and that the salt level is measured
as electrical conductivity (EC), with the guide calling values between
0 and 0.8 decisiemens per metre (in a 1:1 mix of soil and water)
acceptable for general crop growth. NRCS's field handbook explains the
harm: high salinity interferes with a plant's ability to take water from
the soil and to exchange nutrients, reducing growth and germination.

Sodium does further damage. The handbook describes the sodium
adsorption ratio (SAR), the amount of sodium relative to calcium and
magnesium, and says an SAR above 13 harms how water enters the soil,
how fast it drains through, and the soil's structure.

In a home garden, salt problems usually come from what is added rather
than from the climate (general practice): too much fertilizer in one
place, which is a salt and can scorch roots and seedlings; heavy
applications of manure, which the handbook notes can be high in salt;
road de-icing salt on beds beside a path or road; and, in some homes,
watering with softened water, which carries sodium. If a soil test
reports high EC or sodium, ask the laboratory or your extension
service what to do; flushing salts out with water works only where the
soil drains (general practice).

## The numbers on a bag, in the plant's terms

[Testing and Correcting Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) explains
how to read the three numbers on a fertilizer bag: the percentages by
weight of nitrogen (N), phosphate (P2O5) and potash (K2O). The middle
and last numbers are not the elements themselves but, by convention,
their oxides. NRCS's field handbook notes that nutrient values are
sometimes given as elemental P and K instead, and gives the conversion
factors:

- pounds of P times 2.3 equals pounds of P2O5;
- pounds of K times 1.2 equals pounds of K2O.

Turned the other way (our arithmetic): divide P2O5 by 2.3 to get
phosphorus, and K2O by 1.2 to get potassium.

**Worked example: a 5-pound bag of 5-10-10.**

| | On the label | As the element |
|---|---|---|
| Nitrogen | 5% of 5 lb = 0.25 lb N | 0.25 lb N (already an element) |
| Phosphate | 10% of 5 lb = 0.5 lb P2O5 | 0.5 / 2.3 = about 0.22 lb P |
| Potash | 10% of 5 lb = 0.5 lb K2O | 0.5 / 1.2 = about 0.42 lb K |

So a bag that looks evenly balanced between phosphate and potash
carries nearly twice as much potassium as phosphorus by the elements.
The label is not misleading you; it is using a different unit. When you
compare a bag with a report, make sure both are in the same unit (our
advice).

## A worked example: one household, two soils

Imagine two beds a short walk apart: one on loose, sandy ground with
little organic matter, and one on a silty clay loam rich in organic
matter. Everything below follows from the handbook and pH sheet
statements above; putting them side by side is our arrangement.

| | Sandy bed | Silty clay loam bed |
|---|---|---|
| Pantry (CEC) | Small: holds little, gives it up fast | Large: holds several times more |
| Nitrate | Leaches quickly in rain | Leaches more slowly; lost as gas if waterlogged |
| Potassium, calcium, magnesium | Washes out of the surface faster | Held on the exchange sites |
| Phosphorus | May leach if low in organic matter | Held tightly; moves with eroded soil |
| pH change | Acidifies easily; needs little lime to correct | Resists change; needs more lime |
| What to do | Feed little and often, build organic matter, keep a cover crop over winter | Feed less often, protect drainage, lime by the test |

If you live around Silverdale, Washington, the locale data in this
project, drawn from the USDA soil survey, shows both kinds within a few
kilometres: glacial outwash sands it sums up as dry, low in fertility
and quick to lose lime and nutrients, and silt loams that hold far more
water. It puts most soils there between about pH 5.1 and 6.5.
[Your Soil, Specifically](your_soil_specifically.md) walks through
them.

## Where your work stops

- **The numbers.** Pantry size, pH, buffer pH, salts and nutrient levels
  come from a laboratory soil test, not from guesswork. [Testing and
  Correcting Soil](testing_and_correcting_soil.md) explains how to get
  one, and the report's recommendation is the amount to use.
- **Salty or sodic soil.** Reclaiming it involves drainage, water
  quality and sometimes amendments such as gypsum; take the test report
  to your extension service (general practice).
- **Your well.** A certified laboratory, for nitrate and anything else
  [Testing Water](testing_water.md) lists.
- **Poisoning or a chemical in the eyes.** First aid as above, then
  medical help or poison control, with the label in your hand.

## Traps

- **"Lime is lime."** Quicklime and hydrated lime are caustic and are
  not garden lime. Read the chemical name.
- **"If a little fertilizer helps, more helps more."** Beyond what the
  crop needs, the extra nitrogen leaches toward ground water, the extra
  phosphorus waits to wash into a stream, and the salts can scorch
  roots.
- **Feeding in autumn.** On light soil, nitrogen applied with nothing
  growing to take it up can be largely gone by spring (our reasoning
  above).
- **Comparing a bag with a report in different units.** P2O5 is not P;
  K2O is not K.
- **"Neutral is always best."** The yield table shows crops differ; so
  do blueberries, which want acid soil.
- **Storing sulfur next to pool chlorine.** Keep oxidisers and fuels
  apart.
- **Boiling well water to make it safe for a baby.** Boiling kills
  germs but concentrates nitrate (EPA). Test the well.

## How the game models it

The garden in this project tracks two pieces of soil chemistry for each
bed, and its numbers come from sources this guide also uses.

- **pH, per bed.** Every soil bed remembers its own pH, starting at 6.5.
  Lime, sulfur and wood ash are counted in grams of calcium carbonate
  equivalent, and it takes about 381 grams of limestone per square metre
  to move the game's loam one pH unit. Each reacts over weeks rather
  than at once, and the stored urine the household uses as nitrogen
  fertilizer slowly turns a bed acid, at 1.8 grams of calcium carbonate
  equivalent for each gram of nitrogen. The pH cannot rise above 8.3,
  the top of the range for soils with free lime in them, and the game
  floors it at 4.0 (`data/garden/soil_ph.ron`).
- **What pH costs a crop.** A crop growing outside its pH range loses
  about 30 percent of its health for each pH unit it is outside, never
  dropping below a floor. That rate is the median taken from the same
  NRCS yield table this guide quotes, which the game carries for corn,
  wheat, oats, barley and soybean (`data/garden/soil_ph.ron`).
- **Nutrients as oxides.** Each bed holds a store of nitrogen, phosphate
  and potash counted as N, P2O5 and K2O, the way a fertilizer bag counts
  them; a growing crop draws it down by what that crop's harvest will
  remove, and a crop is held back by whichever nutrient is scarcest. The
  game's notices name the element, phosphorus or potassium, even though
  the grams are oxides (`src/systems/farming/soil.rs`,
  `data/garden/nutrients.ron`).

What the game leaves out, so you do not learn it from the game: there is
no cation exchange and no pantry, and every bed is the same loam; pH
does not lock nutrients away, it only caps the crop's health; nitrogen
is never lost to leaching, denitrification or ammonia gas; there are no
salts, sodium or micronutrients; and lime, sulfur and fertilizer are
safe to handle.

**Settings that change this.**

- **Soil pH.** Settings > Gameplay > Soil pH is On by default. Off
  freezes every bed's pH and hides it.
- **Play mode.** During development the game starts in the Dev play
  mode (Settings > Gameplay > Play mode), and in Dev, as in Creative,
  materials are free: Garden Lime, Garden Sulfur, Wood Ash, Stored
  Urine and the Fertilizer bag (the game's name for the Composter's
  compost) cost nothing from your pack. Switch Play mode to Normal to
  manage a garden with what you actually have.
- **Starting from the default home.** While Settings > Gameplay >
  "Start every session from the default home" is on, which is also the
  default during development, only your character (name, look and
  outfit) carries between launches. Every bed's pH and nutrient store
  start each session as the default home has them, so a slow
  correction such as sulfur on a blueberry bed cannot be followed from
  one session to the next. Turn the setting off to keep your garden.

## You own this when

- You can tell ground limestone from hydrated lime and quicklime by the
  chemical name, and you know the first aid for lime in the eyes and on
  the skin, and when it needs a doctor.
- You store sulfur dry and apart from pool chlorine and other
  oxidisers, and keep fertilizers in their original, labelled
  containers away from children.
- You can explain cation exchange in one sentence, and say why a sandy
  soil needs feeding little and often.
- You can trace nitrogen from compost to ammonium to nitrate, and say
  where it is lost and why autumn feeding on light soil wastes it.
- You know why phosphorus belongs in the root zone and not in a stream.
- You can read the NRCS yield table and say why "raise the pH" depends
  on the crop.
- You can convert P2O5 and K2O on a bag into phosphorus and potassium.
- If you drink well water, you have had it tested for nitrate, and you
  know that boiling makes nitrate worse.
