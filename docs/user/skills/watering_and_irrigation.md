# Watering and Irrigation

A garden plant is a water pump. Its roots pull water out of the soil and
its leaves let it go again as vapour through the pores they breathe
through. The United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO), whose
method this guide follows, puts it plainly: nearly all the water a crop
takes up is lost this way, and only a tiny fraction stays in the plant.

So the question "how much should I water?" is really "how fast is the
weather pulling water out of this bed?" The answer changes with the sun,
the heat, the dryness of the air, the wind, and how big the plants have
grown. A watering schedule copied from a packet cannot know any of that.
This guide shows you how to work it out, how to check the soil instead of
guessing, and how to deliver the water with as little waste as possible.

Water is measured here as a depth, in millimetres, because that is how
growers and weather services measure it. One millimetre of water spread
over one square metre is exactly one litre, so "5 mm a day" and "5 litres
per square metre a day" are the same thing. Inches are given alongside:
one inch is 25.4 mm, which is 25.4 litres on a square metre, or about 0.62
US gallons on a square foot. Temperatures are in degrees Fahrenheit with
Celsius in brackets.

## Two rules before you start

**Water deeply, then let the top of the soil dry before watering again.**
Iowa State University Extension advises wetting the soil 6 to 12 inches
(15 to 30 cm) deep, where most vegetable roots are, and says that in most
situations one thorough watering a week beats frequent light ones.
Colorado State University Extension's trigger is the top 2 to 4 inches (5
to 10 cm) of soil being dry to the touch. The common mistake, Iowa State
says, is stopping too soon: the surface is wet and the root zone below is
still dry.

**Measure, do not guess.** A rain gauge tells you what the sky gave. A
straight-sided can under the sprinkler tells you what the hose gave. A
finger or a trowel in the soil tells you what is left. All three are
cheap, and all three beat a calendar.

## Where the water goes

Water leaves a garden two ways: it evaporates from wet soil, and it
transpires from leaves. FAO counts the two together as
evapotranspiration, ET for short, and it notes how the balance shifts over
a season: at sowing nearly all of it is evaporation from the soil, and once
the crop covers the ground more than 90 percent of it is transpiration.
That shift is why mulch matters most while plants are small, a point this
guide comes back to.

What drives it, FAO lists as four weather factors: solar radiation, air
temperature, air humidity and wind speed. A hot, bright, dry, windy day
pulls far more water out of a bed than a cool, grey, humid, still one.

## The weather's demand: reference evapotranspiration

To separate the weather from the crop, FAO defines a standard surface: an
imaginary, well-watered lawn of grass 12 cm tall, growing actively and
covering the ground completely. The water that surface would use in
today's weather is the reference evapotranspiration, written ETo. It is a
number about the weather alone, which is what makes it useful: two places,
or two weeks, can be compared by their ETo whatever is growing in them.

FAO calculates ETo from weather records with one standard formula, the
FAO Penman-Monteith equation. You will not need to run it, but you should
know the size of the numbers it gives. FAO's typical ranges, which it
offers for orientation rather than for direct use:

- In a temperate climate that is humid or sub-humid, about 1 to 2 mm a
  day in cool weather (a mean daily temperature near 10 C, 50 F), 2 to 4
  mm at a moderate mean of 20 C (68 F), and 4 to 7 mm when the mean
  passes 30 C (86 F).
- In a temperate climate that is arid or semi-arid, more: 1 to 3, 4 to 7
  and 6 to 9 mm a day for the same three temperatures.
- In the tropics and subtropics: 2 to 3, 3 to 5 and 5 to 7 mm a day for
  the same three temperatures where it is humid, and 2 to 4, 4 to 6 and 6
  to 8 where it is dry.

Note that these are mean daily temperatures, day and night averaged, not
the afternoon high.

## The crop's share: the crop coefficient

A vegetable bed is not a lawn. It is taller or shorter, leafier or
sparser, and its soil may be wetter or drier. FAO folds all of that into
one multiplier, the crop coefficient, Kc:

    crop water use (ETc) = Kc x ETo

The crop coefficient changes as the crop grows, and FAO divides a season
into four stages:

1. **Initial**, from planting until about a tenth of the ground is covered
   by green leaves. The plants are small, so the water use is mostly
   evaporation from the soil. That makes Kc high if you keep the surface
   wet and low if you let it dry.
2. **Development**, from that point until the crop effectively covers the
   ground, which for many crops is around the start of flowering.
3. **Mid-season**, from full cover until the crop starts to mature. Kc is
   at its highest here and stays fairly steady.
4. **Late season**, from the start of maturity to harvest. Kc falls,
   unless the crop is watered right up to a fresh harvest, which keeps it
   higher.

Some of FAO's values for well-managed vegetables (its Table 12, measured
for a sub-humid climate with moderate wind):

| Crop | Kc early | Kc at full size | Kc at the end |
|---|---|---|---|
| Small vegetables as a group (lettuce, cabbage, carrot and similar) | 0.7 | 1.05 | 0.95 |
| Lettuce | | 1.00 | 0.95 |
| Tomato family as a group | 0.6 | 1.15 | 0.80 |
| Tomato | | 1.15 | 0.70 to 0.90 |
| Zucchini | | 0.95 | 0.75 |
| Roots and tubers as a group | 0.5 | 1.10 | 0.95 |
| Potato | | 1.15 | 0.75 |
| Green beans | 0.5 | 1.05 | 0.90 |

Read it this way: a full-grown tomato bed uses about 15 percent more water
than the grass reference, and a bed of new transplants uses little more
than half. On a day when ETo is 5 mm, full-size tomatoes use about
1.15 x 5 = 5.75 mm, which is 5.75 litres for every square metre of bed.

## How much a garden really drinks in summer

Put the two numbers together and the answer to "how much" falls out. The
table below is that arithmetic for a temperate, humid summer, using FAO's
ranges above. It is worked here, not quoted from anyone.

| Situation | ETo, mm a day | Kc | Water use, mm a day | Litres per m2 a week | Inches a week |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| New transplants (the tomato family's early Kc) | 3 | 0.6 | 1.8 | 13 | 0.5 |
| Full-grown bed, mild summer (mean 20 C, 68 F) | 3 | 1.05 | 3.2 | 22 | 0.9 |
| Full-grown bed, warm summer | 5 | 1.05 | 5.3 | 37 | 1.4 |
| Full-size tomatoes, warm summer | 5 | 1.15 | 5.8 | 40 | 1.6 |
| Full-grown bed, hot (mean above 30 C, 86 F) | 7 | 1.05 | 7.4 | 51 | 2.0 |

Three extension services give rules of thumb for their own states, and
they land in the same place:

- **Iowa State** (humid Midwest): most vegetable gardens need about 1 inch
  a week from rain and watering together, which is 25 litres per square
  metre.
- **Colorado State** (semi-arid): vegetables use around a
  quarter of an inch a day in typical summer weather, about 6.4 mm, or 44
  litres per square metre a week, and hot, windy weather raises that
  considerably.
- **Utah State** (arid): most common vegetables need 1 to 2 inches a week,
  25 to 51 litres per square metre.

**The answer, then: a full-grown vegetable bed in summer uses roughly 20
to 50 litres per square metre per week, about 1 to 2 inches of water.**
The low end is a mild, humid summer; the high end is hot, dry and windy.
Young plants use much less, and the amount rises as they grow.

That is what the crop uses. What you have to put on is more, because no
way of watering gets every drop to the roots. FAO's indicative field
application efficiencies, the share of applied water that ends up where
the crop can use it, are about 90 percent for drip, 75 percent for
sprinklers and 60 percent for flooding furrows or basins. To give a warm
summer bed its 37 litres a square metre, you would apply about 41 by drip,
49 by sprinkler, or 62 down a furrow. Rain counts toward it, which is what
the rain gauge is for.

## How much the soil holds, and so how often to water

The soil is a tank between waterings, and its size decides how often you
have to fill it.

**Field capacity and wilting point.** After a soaking, water drains down
through the soil under gravity for a day or so and is gone below the roots.
What the soil still holds when that drainage has slowed, usually 1 to 3
days after rain, is its field capacity. At the other end is the wilting
point, where the soil holds its remaining water so tightly that plants can
no longer pull it out fast enough to recover. The water between the two is
the available water. (NC State Extension's definitions.)

**Texture sets the tank size.** The USDA Natural Resources Conservation
Service gives typical available water capacities by soil texture (its
Program Aid 1619, converted here to millimetres of water per metre of
soil depth, which is also litres per square metre per metre):

| Soil texture | Available water, inches per foot of soil | Millimetres per metre of soil |
|---|---|---|
| Coarse: fine sand, loamy fine sand | 0.6 to 1.2 | 50 to 100 |
| Moderately coarse: sandy loam, fine sandy loam | 1.3 to 1.7 | 110 to 140 |
| Medium: sandy clay loam, loam, silt loam | 1.5 to 2.1 | 125 to 175 |
| Fine: clay, clay loam, silty clay loam | 1.6 to 2.4 | 135 to 200 |

A sandy soil holds about half what a loam does. That is why sandy gardens
need watering more often (Iowa State and Utah State both say so), and clay
ones less.

**Plants do not wait for the wilting point.** Uptake slows well before the
tank is empty. FAO gives each crop a fraction, p, of the available water it
can use before it starts to suffer, set for a crop using about 5 mm a day.
A few from its Table 22, with the root depths it gives (FAO suggests the
smaller root depth for scheduling irrigation, and the larger for deep,
unlayered soil):

| Crop | Root depth, m | Share of available water it can use, p |
|---|---|---|
| Spinach, celery | 0.3 to 0.5 | 0.20 |
| Lettuce, radish, onion | 0.3 to 0.5 (onion 0.3 to 0.6) | 0.30 |
| Sweet pepper | 0.5 to 1.0 | 0.30 |
| Carrot | 0.5 to 1.0 | 0.35 |
| Potato | 0.4 to 0.6 | 0.35 |
| Tomato | 0.7 to 1.5 | 0.40 |
| Cabbage, broccoli, green beans | 0.4 to 0.8 | 0.45 |
| Cucumber, zucchini | 0.6 to 1.2 | 0.50 |

In cooler weather, when a crop uses less, it can draw the soil down a
little further before suffering. FAO's adjustment: add 0.04 to p for every
millimetre a day the crop's use falls below 5, and take it away for every
millimetre above.

**The arithmetic.** Multiply the soil's available water per metre by the
root depth to get the tank, multiply by p to get the share the crop can
use, and divide by the daily use to get the days between waterings. That
share is also how much to put back each time. Worked here for a warm
summer week (ETo 5 mm a day), using the middle of each NRCS range:

| Crop and soil | Tank, mm | Usable, mm | Days between waterings | Water each time |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lettuce, roots 0.3 m, in loamy fine sand | 22.5 | 6.8 | 1.4 | 7 mm (7 L/m2) |
| Lettuce, in loam | 45 | 13.5 | 2.7 | 13 to 14 mm |
| Lettuce, in clay loam | 50 | 15 | 3.0 | 15 mm |
| Tomato, roots 0.7 m, in loamy fine sand | 52.5 | 19.4 | 3.4 | 19 mm |
| Tomato, in loam | 105 | 38.9 | 6.8 | 39 mm (1.5 inches) |
| Tomato, in clay loam | 117 | 43.2 | 7.5 | 43 mm |

(The soils are taken at the middle of each NRCS range: loamy fine sand 0.9
inches per foot, 75 mm per metre; loam 1.8, 150 mm; clay loam 2.0, 167 mm.
Lettuce: p 0.30 and a use of 1.00 x 5 = 5.0 mm a day. Tomato: a use of 1.15
x 5 = 5.75 mm a day, so p is 0.40 - 0.04 x 0.75 = 0.37.)

This agrees with the rules of thumb, and it explains them. Colorado
State's advice to water every four days and put on an inch is four days of
a quarter inch; it works where the root zone can hold an inch of usable
water, as a loam under a deep-rooted crop can. Iowa State's preference for
one thorough watering a week is the tomato in loam. The shallow lettuce in
sand needs water every day or two, in small amounts, because its tank is
tiny.

The root depths are the ones FAO gives for open, unlayered field soil. A
young plant, a shallow raised bed over hard ground, or a pot has a far
smaller tank, and needs water more often. So use the arithmetic to set a
starting schedule, then check the soil.

**Check that the water went deep enough.** Iowa State's method for drip
and soaker hoses: water long enough to wet the soil 10 to 12 inches (25 to
30 cm) deep, then dig a small hole and look. Once you know how long that
takes, you can time every watering after it. Utah State gives a figure for
scale: in a loam, one inch of water goes about 6 inches (15 cm) down per
watering.

## Knowing when the soil needs water

**The finger test.** Push a finger into the soil. If the top 2 to 4 inches
(5 to 10 cm) are dry, water (Colorado State). In a container the rule is
the same with a shorter finger: [Your First Tomato](your_first_tomato.md)
waters when the mix is dry at about 2 inches, the second knuckle.

**A trowel or soil probe.** A finger only reaches the top. Colorado State
suggests pulling up a sample from the root zone at 6 to 8 inches (15 to 20
cm) with a soil probe or a small spade. Under mulch, where the surface
stays damp while the soil below dries, this is the check that counts.

**Feel and appearance.** The Natural Resources Conservation Service
teaches a field method: take soil from the root zone, squeeze it firmly in
one hand several times to form a ball, then look at whether it holds
together, whether it will ribbon out between thumb and finger, whether it
leaves loose grains or a wet stain on your fingers, and how dark it is.
NRCS publishes a chart for each texture group that turns what you see into
the share of available water still left. Its chart for loams (sandy clay
loam, loam and silt loam), lightly condensed:

| Available water left | What the soil does in your hand |
|---|---|
| 0 to 25 percent | Dry; crumbs break away easily, clods crumble when pressed, no stain on fingers |
| 25 to 50 percent | Slightly moist; forms a weak ball with rough surfaces, no water stain, a few grains break away |
| 50 to 75 percent | Moist; forms a ball, very light stain on fingers, darker colour, pliable, a weak ribbon between thumb and forefinger |
| 75 to 100 percent | Wet; forms a ball with well-defined finger marks, a light to heavy coating on fingers, ribbons between thumb and forefinger |
| 100 percent (field capacity) | Wet; forms a soft ball, free water shows briefly on the surface when squeezed or shaken |

Put that beside FAO's p. A tomato can use about 40 percent of the
available water, so water it once the loam in its root zone has dried into
the 50 to 75 percent row (moist, forms a ball, a weak ribbon), before it
reaches the 25 to 50 percent row. Lettuce and spinach, with smaller p,
want water sooner, near the top of that row.

Two honest limits: NC State calls the method subjective, reliable only in
experienced hands, and recommends it only as a last resort; the University
of Minnesota says it takes frequent practice to give consistent answers.
It is still far better than looking at the surface.

**Two tools that mislead beginners.** Colorado State describes the old
farmers' check of pushing a stick or screwdriver into the ground, and
warns that it depends on the soil: a compacted clay resists even when
moist, and a sand lets the screwdriver in wet or dry. It also warns that
cheap soil moisture meters are slightly inaccurate, reading wet in fertile
soil and dry in poor soil, so each has to be learned against your own
ground.

**Tensiometers.** A tensiometer is a sealed, water-filled tube with a
porous ceramic tip at the bottom and a vacuum gauge at the top. Pushed into
the root zone, it works like a mechanical root: as the soil dries it pulls
water out through the tip, and the gauge shows how hard the soil is holding
on, in centibars (one centibar is one kilopascal). Kansas State University
Extension's reading guide:

| Reading, centibars | What it means |
|---|---|
| 0 | Saturated. If it stays there, suspect waterlogging, poor drainage, or a break in the water column inside the tube |
| 5 to 10 | More water than plants need; still draining |
| 10 to 20 | Field capacity |
| 20 to 40 | Plenty of water for medium and fine soils. Coarse sands may already need water at 20 to 30, finer sands at 30 to 40 |
| 40 to 60 | The usual time to start watering: loams at about 40 to 50, clays at about 50 to 60 |
| 70 | Stress range, though the crop is not necessarily damaged |
| 80 | The top of the useful range; the water column inside breaks at about 80 to 85 |

NC State adds the practical detail. Tensiometers suit sands, loamy sands,
sandy loams and the coarser loams, because those soils give up most of
their available water within the instrument's range. Clays and silts hold
much of theirs beyond it, so tensiometers are not recommended there unless
you water before half the available water is gone, which NC State notes is
the normal practice for some vegetables such as tomatoes. Put the tip at
half the depth of the roots: for roots about 12 inches deep, 6 inches down,
in the row. Read it every day at first, twice a day in very sandy soil,
because a sand can dry past 80 centibars in about three days, and once the
water column breaks the gauge reads falsely wet until you refill and
reset it.

**A rain gauge.** Iowa State suggests one for knowing how much the rain
actually supplied and whether you need to water at all. Subtract the
measured rain from the week's need and water the rest.

## When in a crop's life water matters most

Steady moisture matters all season, but some stages are especially
sensitive. Iowa State lists seed germination, flowering and fruit set,
fruit or head enlargement, and rooting, and it notes that young plants,
with small shallow roots, need water more often than established ones. Its
table of the most critical periods:

| Crop | Most critical time for water |
|---|---|
| Tomato, eggplant, pepper | Flowering, fruit set and fruit development |
| Cucumber, melon, squash | Flowering, fruit set and fruit development |
| Bean, pea | Flowering, fruit set and development |
| Broccoli, cabbage, cauliflower | Head development |
| Potato | Tuber enlargement |
| Sweet corn | Tasseling and ear development |
| Carrot, radish, beet, turnip | Root enlargement |
| Onion | Bulb development |

What goes wrong when water runs short or swings between dry and soaked,
from Iowa State and Utah State: blossom-end rot on tomatoes, peppers and
squash; bitter or misshapen cucumbers; bitter, tough lettuce and stringy
celery; split cabbage heads; cracked or forked roots; flowers and young
fruit dropping from beans and tomatoes. Consistency is the lesson: a crop
kept evenly moist does better than one alternately flooded and parched.

## Delivering it: hand, sprinkler or drip

**Hand watering with a hose.** Iowa State calls it probably the least
efficient way to water a vegetable garden. The water comes out faster than
most soils can soak it up, so it runs off, carries soil with it and bares
roots, and most people stop long before the root zone is wet. The problem
Iowa State describes is the rate, which suggests the fix: a watering can
poured slowly at the base of each plant gives the soil time to take it in,
and suits a few containers or a small bed.

**Sprinklers** put water down more slowly than a hose, so it soaks in
better and goes deeper. At ordinary household pressure most deliver about
3 US gallons (11 litres) a minute (Iowa State). The costs, also from Iowa
State: they wet the leaves, which raises the risk of disease, they have to
be moved to reach every corner, and they water the weeds as well as the
vegetables. Part of what they spray also misses the root zone, which is
why FAO's efficiency for them is lower than for drip. Colorado State
discourages them for crops prone to leaf diseases such as early blight (tomatoes, peppers and
potatoes), because splashing spreads the disease and wet leaves let it
start. To learn how long to run one, set a few straight-sided cans around
the bed and time how long they take to collect an inch (Iowa State and
Colorado State both give this method). For scale, Colorado State's figures
for lawn-style heads: pop-up sprays put down about 1.5 inches (38 mm) an
hour, about 40 minutes for an inch, and rotors about half an inch (13 mm) an
hour, about two hours for an inch.

**Drip and soaker hoses** put water on the soil at the plants, slowly, with
little runoff or evaporation and dry leaves. The US Environmental
Protection Agency's WaterSense programme says microirrigation (drip and its
relatives) uses 20 to 50 percent less water than conventional sprinkler
systems; Iowa State gives 30 to 50 percent less than traditional methods
and Utah State up to about 50 percent. The drawbacks, from Colorado State
and Iowa State: emitters clog, so the water has to be clean, which can
rule drip out for rain barrel water and greywater unless it is filtered;
the pressure has to be reduced with a regulator; and soaker hose runs
should be kept to about 25 feet (8 m) or less, or the far end gets less
water than the near end.

To know how fast a drip layout waters, Utah State gives a formula. In
metric it is simple: an emitter's flow in litres an hour, divided by the
area each emitter serves in square metres, is millimetres an hour. For
example, emitters of 3.4 litres an hour (0.9 US gallons an hour) every 30
cm along lines 46 cm apart serve 0.14 square metres each, which is about 24
mm (0.96 inches) an hour. (In Utah State's own units: gallons an hour times
231, divided by emitter spacing in inches times line spacing in inches,
gives inches an hour.)

**When to water.** Iowa State says morning or early afternoon, so leaves
dry quickly and the plants go into the hottest part of the day well
supplied.

**Automating it.** A timer on the hose, or a zone on a lawn irrigation
controller, will run a garden while you are away. Colorado State's one
rule: give the vegetables their own zone, never the lawn's, because the two
need different amounts.

## Mulch

Mulch is a layer over bare soil that stops the sun and wind drying the
surface. FAO's measured effects:

- An **organic mulch** such as straw or crop residue cuts evaporation from
  the soil by about 5 percent for every 10 percent of the surface it covers,
  so covering half the ground saves about a quarter of the soil
  evaporation.
- A **plastic mulch** over drip lines cuts soil evaporation by 50 to 80
  percent. The crop transpires 10 to 30 percent more over the season,
  because heat from the plastic reaches the leaves beside it, but its total
  water use still falls, by 10 to 30 percent on average. FAO also notes
  that growers use mulch to warm the soil and speed crops in cool climates.

Because evaporation from the soil is most of the water use while plants are
small, and a small share once they cover the ground, mulch saves the most
early in the season and between widely spaced plants. Iowa State suggests
several inches of grass clippings, shredded leaves or weed-free straw,
spread once the soil has warmed (early June, in Iowa); Utah State suggests 1
to 2 inches around the plants. Keeping weeds down saves water too, since
weeds drink from the same soil.

## Rainwater and greywater on food crops

Both are real water, and both are often right for ornamentals and trees.
On food, each carries a caution.

**Rain barrel water is not drinking water.** Roofs collect bird droppings,
dust and traces of whatever the roof is made of. Nebraska Extension rules
out drinking it or washing produce in it, and the Lake Champlain Sea Grant
review below adds cooking. Whether it belongs on vegetables at all is where
the sources disagree:

- Nebraska Extension does not recommend it for fruit and vegetable gardens
  at all, and [Collecting Rainwater](collecting_rainwater.md), in this
  Library, follows that cautious path: barrel water for flowers, shrubs,
  trees and lawn, tap water for anything you eat.
- Rutgers Cooperative Extension tested twelve New Jersey barrels on asphalt
  shingle roofs from July to October 2011, sampling 3 to 7 days after rain.
  Lead and zinc were well under the federal guidelines for irrigating
  crops with reclaimed water, and no polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons were
  found. E. coli, a sign of droppings from birds and animals, was over
  the single-sample limit (235 per 100 mL) of the US Food and Drug
  Administration's proposed standard for produce irrigation water in 9
  percent of 47 samples, and failed New Jersey's drinking water standard
  in 66 percent. (That limit was only a proposal, which Rutgers used as a
  yardstick. FDA's final rule of 2015 set a different test for water used
  while growing produce, a geometric mean of no more than 126 and a
  statistical threshold of no more than 410 generic E. coli per 100 mL,
  and in 2024 FDA replaced that numeric testing with farm assessments of
  the water. Checked 27 September 2026.) Rutgers concluded barrel water
  can be used on a vegetable garden if it is treated and used with care.
  Its method: clean the barrel with a 3 percent bleach solution before
  collecting; then each month add unscented household bleach (5 to 6
  percent) at 1/8 teaspoon, about 8 drops, per US gallon of water, which is
  about one ounce for a 55 gallon barrel, more often in rainy spells, and
  wait about 24 hours before using the water; put the water on the soil
  only, never on the plants; water in the morning only; and do not harvest
  right after watering, so that sun and drying can work on anything that
  splashed. Rutgers also judged testing often enough to mean anything
  impractical and too costly for a home garden, and recommends treating the
  water routinely instead.
- A 2026 review of the research and extension guidance by Lake Champlain
  Sea Grant found the same consensus: never drink it, water edible crops
  only at soil level, not onto the plants, and wash your hands after
  gardening and rinse what you harvest. It also found that a first-flush
  diverter, which sends the dirtiest first water off the roof away from
  the barrel, helps.

**Greywater is used household water that has not been through a toilet.**
Which drains count, and what you may do with it, differs from state to
state ([Greywater](greywater.md) compares three neighbours that disagree
about the kitchen sink), so check your own rules before using any. In
Washington State the rule is chapter 246-274 of the Washington
Administrative Code, and its general requirements (WAC 246-274-011) are a
good picture of the concerns everywhere:

- It may be used only for irrigation below the surface of the ground. It
  must not surface in any way, by ponding or by running off: it has to stay
  underground so that people and animals do not come into contact with it.
- It may water plants that produce food, but it must never touch the part
  you eat.
- It may not contain water from washing diapers or similarly soiled or
  infectious material, or waste from a water softener, cleaning car parts,
  washing greasy or oily rags or clothing, rinsing paint brushes, or home
  photo labs.
- The system may be used only during the growing season.
- It may not be put on faster than the evapotranspiration of the ground it
  irrigates. That is this guide's ETc, written into law: greywater may
  replace only what the plants and soil use, and never be enough to drain
  on down past them.

The practical upshot, argued in detail in [Greywater](greywater.md): put
greywater on fruit trees, canes and ornamentals, where the food hangs well
above the soil, and keep it away from root crops and anything eaten raw
from near the ground.

## Signs you are getting it wrong

**Too little.** Iowa State's signs of drought stress: wilting, leaves
curling or rolling, scorched leaf edges, and, later, the fruit problems in
the section above. Stressed plants also catch diseases more easily.

**Too much.** Water beyond field capacity drains below the roots, and
Kansas State notes that it carries nutrients with it, out of the crop's
reach. A tensiometer that sits near zero for days is telling you the soil
is waterlogged or poorly drained. Roots in waterlogged soil cannot get air;
[Your First Tomato](your_first_tomato.md) covers what that looks like in a
pot. And leaves kept wet invite leaf diseases (Iowa State and Colorado
State, above); [Pests and Disease](pests_and_disease.md) covers those.

## A summer week, worked

A 10 square metre bed (about 108 square feet) of mixed, full-grown
vegetables in loam, in a warm week with ETo around 5 mm a day:

- **Crop use:** 1.05 x 5 = 5.25 mm a day, about 37 mm for the week, which
  is 370 litres for the bed.
- **Minus rain:** the gauge caught 10 mm on Tuesday. Counting all of it,
  that leaves about 27 mm, 270 litres, for you to supply.
- **Delivery:** by drip, at about 90 percent, apply about 300 litres. By
  sprinkler, at about 75 percent, 360.
- **Schedule:** the loam holds about 45 mm in the top 30 cm and a mix of
  crops can use roughly a third of it, about 15 mm, before stress. So water
  about every three days, about 15 to 17 mm a time by drip, and check with a
  trowel that it reached 10 to 12 inches.
- **In rain barrels:** a 55 US gallon barrel holds 208 litres. At this
  week's 43 litres a day by drip, a full barrel lasts this bed about five
  days. In a week with no rain at all it would need about 58 litres a day,
  and the barrel would last about three and a half.

## How the game models it

The garden in this project uses the same method, and its numbers come from
the sources in this guide.

- **How much each crop drinks.** Every crop's water per plant in
  `data/plants.csv` is FAO's crop water use: its season-average crop
  coefficient from FAO-56 Table 12, averaged over the four stages, times a
  reference ETo of 3 mm a day (the moderate, humid temperate range in FAO's
  Table 2), times the ground each plant takes. The method and every crop's
  figure are in `data/garden/yields.ron`, under WATER. A grow unit draws that
  times the plants in it.
- **Irrigation.** The home's automated irrigation holds each grow area's
  water level at a steady 80 percent unless you set that area's slider, and
  every litre it uses comes out of the home's tanks. If the cistern runs dry,
  the irrigation stops and the crops start to dry out. A crop planted outside
  any grow area has to be watered by hand, 2 litres from a watering can at a
  time, also from the tanks. Rain waters the outdoor fields and not the
  indoor towers.
- **Thirst costs harvest.** A crop whose water level falls below 20 percent
  loses health, and the harvest is scaled by the crop's health averaged over
  its season. That follows FAO's standard yield response to water
  (Irrigation and Drainage Paper 33): yield falls in proportion to the water
  the crop went without.
- **On the ship the water goes round.** Almost all the water the irrigation
  gives the crops, they breathe out into the grow room's air, just as FAO
  says. The air handlers' cooling coils condense that vapour out of the air
  and pipe it back to the cistern; the crops keep a few percent in their
  tissue. In the three-person home, on a measured day at full planting, the
  irrigation drew 780 litres, the coils returned everything that had gone
  into the air, and the garden's net draw on the tanks was only 91 litres.
  The whole balance, and how it is tested, is in
  [Ship Life Support](../../design/ship-life-support.md).

What the game simplifies, so you do not learn it from the game: a crop's
water level drains at one fixed rate whatever the weather or the crop's
stage, and the reference ETo is a constant 3 mm a day rather than following
the day's sun, heat and wind; every soil holds water alike, where a real
sand holds about half what a loam does; watering loses nothing to runoff
or spray, where FAO's figures above lose 10 percent by drip and 25 by
sprinkler; and there is no rain barrel or greywater, so nothing in the game
teaches their cautions.
