# Teeth

Run your tongue along the outside of your back teeth, up near the gum,
some hours after you last brushed. There is a faint furriness there, a
film that is not quite slime and not quite nothing. That film is dental
plaque, and NIDCR describes it as "a sticky, colorless film of bacteria."
It is the thing this entire guide is about. Everything that destroys
teeth begins with that film sitting somewhere undisturbed for long
enough.

You can feel it right now, which is the useful part. There is no other
disease process in this Library that you can locate with your tongue
before it has done anything to you.

## Why a self-sufficiency library has a chapter on teeth

Because teeth are the part of you that does not heal.

That sentence is the whole strategy, so it is worth being precise about
it rather than treating it as a slogan. Skin heals. Bone heals, and heals
so well that a properly set fracture ends up nearly as strong as it was.
Muscle, liver, gut lining, all of it repairs, because all of it is made
of living cells that can divide and rebuild. The outer surface of a tooth
is not. Mature enamel is acellular and non-vital: there are no living
cells inside it at all, which is why a dental drill on enamel does not
hurt until it reaches deeper tissue. StatPearls, in its histology
reference on the tooth, states plainly that enamel cannot regenerate and
cannot be replaced, and contrasts it with dentin, bone and cementum,
which do contain living cells. (That reference is copyrighted, so the
fact is restated here rather than quoted.)

There is one genuine qualification, and it matters enormously in
practice. Enamel cannot rebuild its structure, but it can take minerals
back. NIDCR puts it this way: "enamel can repair itself by using minerals
from saliva, and fluoride from toothpaste or other sources." So the
mineral content of enamel goes up and down continuously. What cannot come
back is the architecture. Once enough mineral has left that the crystal
structure collapses and a hole opens, that hole is permanent. NIDCR
marks the boundary exactly: at the white-spot stage, "Tooth decay can be
stopped or reversed at this point," but if the process continues, "the
enamel is weakened and destroyed, forming a cavity."

So the honest framing is not "teeth never repair." It is that teeth have
a repair mechanism with a hard limit, the limit is reached silently, and
past it every remaining option is a repair performed by somebody else
with equipment. That is why prevention here is not a virtuous extra. It
is the only part of the process you personally control, and it stops
being available at a moment you will not feel.

**This matters more the further you are from a dentist, not less.** A
person with a dental plan and a twenty minute drive can afford to learn
this the hard way once. A person two hours from anywhere, or without the
money, or both, cannot. The cruel arithmetic of dental disease is that
its consequences fall hardest on exactly the people with least access to
its remedies, and the numbers at the end of this guide say so.

## What this guide will and will not do

It will explain what is actually happening in your mouth, well enough
that the prevention advice stops sounding like nagging and starts
sounding like mechanism. It will tell you what to watch for and in what
order. It will tell you, specifically and without hedging, which findings
mean stop reading and get emergency care.

**It will not tell you how to pull a tooth, how to drain an abscess, or
how to place a filling.** Not a technique, not a tool list, not a "if you
really have no other option" section. This is a deliberate refusal and
the reasoning is given in full further down, because a refusal a reader
does not understand is a refusal a reader ignores.

It is also not a substitute for an examination. A great deal of what a
dentist does on a first visit is look at surfaces you cannot see and take
radiographs of the parts nobody can see, including the space between
teeth and the bone around the roots. Reading cannot do that. Where this
guide says "get it looked at," that is not throat-clearing, it is the
actual answer.

## What decay actually is

Tooth decay is not rot in the sense of something eating the tooth. It is
a chemical process, and it runs in both directions.

Your mouth contains bacteria. Some of them, when they get sugar or
starch, ferment it and excrete acid as waste. NIDCR: "Whenever we eat or
drink something that contains sugar or starch, the bacteria use them to
produce acids," and "These acids begin to eat away at the tooth's hard
outer surface, or enamel." What the acid actually does is dissolve
mineral out of the enamel surface, which is the process called
demineralisation.

That is only half the story, and the half most people are told. The other
half is that the mouth is constantly putting mineral back. NIDCR
describes it as a contest: "On the other team are the minerals in our
saliva...plus fluoride from toothpaste, water, and other sources. This
team helps enamel repair itself by replacing minerals lost during an
'acid attack.'"

So a tooth is not steadily degrading. It is oscillating. Mineral leaves
during and after eating, mineral returns in between. A tooth stays intact
for a lifetime if the returns keep pace with the losses, and it fails if
they do not. NIDCR calls this a tug-of-war, and the metaphor earns its
place: the outcome depends on which side pulls more often, not on how
hard any single pull is.

### The frequency point, which is the most useful thing here

This is the part that changes behaviour, so read it twice.

Each time sugar or starch reaches the bacteria, there is an acid episode,
and then a recovery period during which saliva and fluoride put mineral
back. The recovery takes time. What determines whether a tooth survives
is therefore **how often the acid episodes happen**, because that is what
decides whether recovery ever finishes before the next episode starts.
NIDCR states the consequence directly: "if we eat frequently throughout
the day...the repeated acid attacks will win the tug-of-war." And the
instruction NIDCR draws from it is about frequency, not quantity: "It's
important to keep an eye on *how often* your children eat as well as
*what* they eat."

Work through what that implies, because it is not what "eat less sugar"
implies.

A can of sweet drink swallowed in five minutes is roughly one acid
episode. The same can, sipped across three hours at a desk, is a long
series of them, with no recovery period worth the name in between. The
total sugar is identical. The damage is not remotely identical. By the
same logic, a dessert eaten at the end of a meal is far less costly than
the same dessert grazed on through an afternoon, and a mug of sweet tea
every hour of a working day is, from a tooth's point of view, close to a
worst case.

The practical rule that falls out of this is unglamorous and genuinely
effective: **keep sugar to meals, and let the gaps between meals be
actual gaps.** Not sugar-free. Not joyless. Just clustered rather than
smeared. If you change one thing after reading this guide, change the
sipping.

Two honest limits on that rule. First, it is a rule of thumb drawn from
the mechanism NIDCR describes rather than a dose threshold anybody
publishes: no federal source I could open states a maximum number of
sugar episodes per day, so treat "fewer, clustered" as a direction and
not a number. Second, starch counts too. NIDCR names sugars and starches
together, so crackers, bread and crisps grazed through an afternoon are
in the same category as sweets, which surprises people who think they
have already solved this by avoiding obvious sugar.

### Saliva is the repair system, and some things switch it off

Since saliva carries the minerals that do the repairing, anything that
reduces saliva raises decay risk directly. NIDCR says saliva "contains
minerals such as calcium and phosphate that help keep teeth strong and
fight tooth decay," and that dry mouth "increases the risk for tooth
decay or fungal infections in the mouth because saliva helps keep harmful
germs in check."

The most common cause is medication. NIDCR: "Hundreds of medicines can
cause the salivary glands to make less saliva." This is worth knowing
because it is invisible and it is not the patient's fault. Somebody who
brushed adequately for forty years can start a new prescription and begin
losing teeth without changing a single habit. If your mouth has become
persistently dry, your decay risk has gone up and your prevention needs
to get more aggressive, not stay the same.

Dry mouth is worth raising with whoever prescribed the medicine rather
than treating it as a cosmetic nuisance, because the thing it has
quietly removed is the repair half of the process described above.

### Where decay actually starts

Here the received wisdom and the surveillance data pull in slightly
different directions, and it is worth being accurate rather than tidy.

The everyday claim is that decay starts between the teeth, because that
is where a brush cannot reach. The first half of that is true and
important: NIDCR's hygiene guidance treats cleaning between teeth as a
separate job from brushing precisely because brushing does not do it.

But the largest single location, at least in children and adolescents, is
not between the teeth. It is the chewing surfaces of the back teeth,
which is telling in itself: the established preventive measure for those
surfaces is a physical coating rather than better brushing, which is not
what you would expect if a brush were reaching them properly. CDC's
sealant figures put it starkly: "9 in
10 cavities occur" in the back teeth where sealants are applied, and a
sealant, which is simply a coating painted over those chewing surfaces,
"prevent[s] 80% of cavities over 2 years in the back teeth," still
protecting against about 50 percent of cavities at four years. Children
aged 6 to 11 without sealants have almost three times as many cavities in
their first molars as children with them. Despite that, CDC reports only
42 percent of children aged 6 to 11 and 48 percent of adolescents aged
12 to 19 have them.

So the accurate statement is this. **Decay starts wherever plaque is
allowed to sit undisturbed**, and there are two such places: the pits and
fissures of back-tooth chewing surfaces, and the flat faces where two
teeth touch. The first is the bigger share of childhood cavities and is
addressable with a sealant. The second is the one your toothbrush
structurally cannot reach, at any age, no matter how well you brush,
which is the entire reason cleaning between teeth is a separate
instruction rather than a refinement of brushing.

## Gum disease is a different disease

This is the most common misunderstanding in the subject. Decay and gum
disease are not two grades of the same problem. They have different
mechanisms, different symptoms, different time courses, and they destroy
teeth in entirely different ways. Decay destroys the tooth. Gum disease
destroys what holds the tooth in.

And for adults, the second one is the bigger story. CDC states that
periodontitis is "a leading cause of tooth loss," and that "Tooth loss
can lead to a poor diet, difficulty speaking, and a lower quality of
life."

The mechanism starts the same way, with plaque. NIDCR: plaque not removed
daily hardens into tartar, and these deposits "contain bacteria which can
trigger inflammation that damages the gums and supporting tissues around
the teeth, causing gum disease." Note where the damage is coming from.
The bacteria provoke, and the inflammation does the destroying, which is
why this is a slow disease rather than a dramatic one.

**The distinction that matters is between the stage that reverses and the
stage that does not.**

The early stage, commonly called gingivitis, is inflammation confined to
the gum tissue. The gums are, in NIDCR's words, "red, swollen, or tender"
and bleed easily. And here is the good news, stated by NIDCR directly:
"Early gum disease, caused by plaque buildup, can often be reversed by
daily brushing and flossing." No procedure. No prescription. Cleaning,
done properly and consistently, at the gumline.

The later stage, periodontitis, is when inflammation has moved past the
gum into the ligament and bone that hold the tooth in its socket. NIDCR's
symptom list for the later stage is about the teeth themselves, not just
the gums: recession that makes teeth look longer, teeth that become loose
or sensitive, pain on chewing, persistent bad breath. That lost bone does
not come back on its own. Treatment at this stage is professional deep
cleaning below the gumline, sometimes surgery, and the honest goal is to
halt the loss rather than undo it.

So the practical reading is: **bleeding gums are the warning shot, and
they are the stage you can still fix yourself.** Many people take gum
bleeding as normal, or as evidence they are brushing too hard, and stop
cleaning the area that is bleeding. That gets it exactly backwards. The
bleeding is inflammation from plaque at the gumline, and the response
that resolves it is cleaning that gumline properly and daily, at which
point the bleeding typically settles. If it does not settle after a
couple of weeks of genuinely thorough cleaning, that is a reason to be
seen, not a reason to keep waiting.

### How common it is, and who gets it

NIDCR's analysis of the national health survey, covering 10,683 adults
examined between 2009 and 2014, found 42.2 percent (standard error plus
or minus 1.4) of adults aged 30 or older had total periodontitis: 7.8
percent severe and 34.4 percent non-severe. Those two figures add to the
total exactly, which is worth checking and does check out.

The breakdowns say more than the headline:

- **By age.** 29.5 percent at ages 30 to 44, 46.0 percent at 45 to 64,
  59.8 percent at 65 and over. It accumulates.
- **By smoking.** 34.4 percent of non-smokers, 45.8 percent of former
  smokers, 62.4 percent of current smokers. NIDCR names tobacco as the
  most significant risk factor for gum disease, and this is what that
  looks like in a population.
- **By income.** 60.4 percent of adults below the federal poverty level
  against 28.6 percent of those above four times it. That is a gap of
  roughly 2.1 times, and it is not biology.

CDC adds diabetes to the list, and describes the relationship as running
both ways: "poor periodontal (gum) health can worsen glycemic control
(blood sugar level) in people living with diabetes." NIDCR's risk list
also includes age, genetics, stress, hormonal change, and several
illnesses and immune conditions.

**One caution on bleeding gums specifically.** Plaque is the usual cause
and not the only one. Severe vitamin C deficiency produces swollen,
bleeding gums and loosening teeth, and it can appear within about a month
of a diet essentially free of vitamin C, which is a real risk in a badly
planned winter store rather than a historical curiosity. That case is
covered in [Nutrition That Keeps You Working](nutrition_that_keeps_you_working.md),
and it is worth ruling in or out if bleeding gums appear alongside
fatigue and poor wound healing in someone whose diet has narrowed.

## Fluoride

Fluoride is the single most evidence-backed intervention in this subject
and also the one buried in the most noise, so this section separates what
federal sources currently say from what is genuinely unsettled, and dates
everything, because this is an area where the federal position is
actively being re-examined as of this writing.

### What it does

NIDCR describes fluoride as "a mineral that occurs naturally in soil,
water, and rocks" that "has been shown to prevent cavities, or tooth
decay." The mechanism connects straight back to the tug-of-war: "Fluoride
works by strengthening the tooth's hard outer surface, called enamel and
helps reverse early decay by replenishing lost minerals." It is, in other
words, reinforcement for the side of the contest that puts mineral back.

Sources NIDCR lists: adjusted community water supplies, toothpaste and
mouth rinse, professionally applied varnish or gel, and certain foods and
beverages.

### The number

**The recommended concentration in US drinking water is 0.7 milligrams of
fluoride per liter of water.** CDC states it in those terms and explains
the basis: "The PHS panel that provided the recommendation considered all
sources of fluoride intake and recommended 0.7 mg/L as the concentration
that maximizes fluoride's oral health benefits while minimizing potential
harms, such as dental fluorosis." The recommendation comes from the US
Public Health Service and was published in the Federal Register on 1 May
2015. CDC's page carrying it was current when this guide was written in
September 2026.

Two things that number is not. It is not a mandate: CDC says plainly that
it "does not mandate community water fluoridation," and whether a system
fluoridates is decided by state and local government. And it is not a
legal limit, which is a separate number entirely, covered below.

On effectiveness, CDC states that "Drinking fluoridated water keeps teeth
strong and reduces cavities by about 25% in children and adults," and
gives the economics as an average return of 20 dollars for every dollar
spent in communities of 1,000 or more people, with an average saving of
32 dollars per person per year in avoided treatment.

### The legal limits are different numbers, and the gap is the point

EPA, not CDC, sets what is enforceable, and it sets two values:

- **4.0 mg/L is the enforceable maximum contaminant level.** This is a
  health-based limit that public water systems must meet.
- **2.0 mg/L is the secondary standard,** which EPA describes as set
  "based upon a balancing of the beneficial effects of protection from
  tooth decay and the undesirable effects of excessive exposures leading
  to discoloration."

Secondary standards are normally unenforced guidance about taste, colour
and appearance. Fluoride is treated unusually: "While SMCLs are not
federally enforceable, EPA requires a special notice for exceedance of
the fluoride SMCL of 2.0 mg/L," and systems above 2.0 but below 4.0 must
notify the public within twelve months of learning of the exceedance.

Hold those three numbers together, because a reader who only hears "0.7"
will misread a water report. The recommended level is 0.7. The level that
triggers a public notice is 2.0, roughly 2.9 times the recommendation.
The enforceable ceiling is 4.0, roughly 5.7 times it. A supply can be
entirely legal at a concentration several times the level anybody
recommends.

This is also a correction worth carrying to
[Testing Water](testing_water.md), which lists fluoride in the table of
secondary standards. That listing is correct, but fluoride is the
exception to the sentence that introduces such tables: it is the one
entry on the secondary list that also carries an enforceable primary
limit, so unlike the rest of that table, fluoride does have a health
number attached. [Water Chemistry](water_chemistry.md) covers what
treatment does and does not remove.

### Finding out what is in your own water

If you are on a public system, ask the system. CDC's position is that
"Local water utilities are the best source of information on fluoride
levels in your water," and CDC runs a lookup tool, My Water's Fluoride,
at https://nccd.cdc.gov/DOH_MWF/Default/Default.aspx, which reports data
from utilities that choose to share it.

**If you are on a private well, none of that applies to you.** No utility
is testing it, no report is coming, and the municipal answer for your town
is not your answer. CDC is unambiguous: "The fluoride content of well
water can be determined only through laboratory analysis. Your local
public health department can tell you where to have your home well water
tested."

This is not a hypothetical around the canonical locale. The shipped
groundwater record for Silverdale, in `data/locales/silverdale_wa/water.json`,
describes a typical domestic well near Silverdale as about 30 m deep,
roughly 100 ft, with half of local wells falling between about 12 m and
78 m, computed from real USGS well records in Kitsap County rather than
estimated. A great many households in that landscape are drinking
groundwater that nobody has ever measured for fluoride, which can be
naturally high or naturally near zero depending on the rock. The only way
to know is a test. [Testing Water](testing_water.md) covers how to get one
and how to read the result.

Bottled water is its own trap: CDC notes that "Some, but not all, bottled
water contains fluoride," and that anything labelled de-ionized, purified,
demineralized or distilled contains no fluoride or only traces. A
household that switched entirely to such water has quietly removed one of
the two mineral sources from the repair side of the equation.

### Fluorosis, which is the established downside

Dental fluorosis is a change in the appearance of enamel caused by too
much fluoride while teeth are still forming. CDC gives the age window:
"This condition only develops in children while teeth are developing
under the gums (up until about age 8)." An adult cannot develop it. CDC
associates it with water above 2.0 mg/L and notes that "Less than 1% of
the U.S. population has water from private wells or drinking systems with
fluoride higher than 2.0 mg/L."

Two practical consequences. NIDCR advises that fluoride toothpaste should
not be used for children under two unless a dentist or doctor has advised
it. And CDC notes that ready-to-feed infant formula contains little
fluoride and does not cause fluorosis, while powdered or concentrate
formula mixed with fluoridated water can raise the risk of mild
fluorosis. If you are mixing formula and your water is fluoridated, that
is a question worth asking a clinician rather than resolving from a
guide.

### What is established and what is contested

Established, and not seriously disputed in the federal literature: that
fluoride reduces tooth decay, and that excessive fluoride during tooth
formation causes fluorosis. Both of those appear above with sources.

Genuinely unsettled, and currently under active federal review: whether
fluoride exposure affects children's neurodevelopment, and at what
concentration. Here is the state of it, with dates, because the dates are
what make this legible.

**August 2024.** The National Toxicology Program, part of the National
Institute of Environmental Health Sciences at NIH, published Monograph
08, a systematic review of studies through October 2023. Its conclusion,
held with moderate confidence, was that "Higher levels of fluoride
exposure, such as drinking water containing more than 1.5 milligrams of
fluoride per liter, are associated with lower IQ in children." The review
was explicit about its own limits. It stated that "there were
insufficient data to determine if the low fluoride level of 0.7 mg/L
currently recommended for U.S. community water supplies has a negative
effect on children's IQ," that it "was not designed to evaluate the
health effects of fluoridated drinking water alone," and that it found
"no evidence that fluoride exposure had adverse effects on adult
cognition."

Read that carefully, because it is routinely reported as both more and
less than it says. It is a finding about concentrations above 1.5 mg/L,
which is more than twice the recommended 0.7 and well below EPA's
enforceable 4.0. It is not a finding that 0.7 is harmful. It is also not
a finding that 0.7 is safe. It is a statement that at the recommended
level the data were insufficient to answer the question.

**May 2024.** CDC's scientific statement, still posted, says expert
panels "have not found convincing scientific evidence linking community
water fluoridation with any potential adverse health effect or systemic
disorder such as an increased risk for cancer, Down syndrome, heart
disease, osteoporosis and bone fracture, immune disorders, low
intelligence, renal disorders, Alzheimer disease, or allergic reactions."
That statement predates the NTP monograph by three months and does not
address it.

**August 2026.** EPA released the protocol for a fluoride human health
toxicity assessment under the Safe Drinking Water Act, the step before a
draft assessment goes out for public comment. EPA framed it as acting "In
light of new science and real concerns from the public," said the
assessment will examine how much exposure causes harmful effects
including neurodevelopmental effects and dental fluorosis in children,
and stated that it "will not prejudge any outcomes of the assessment."

So the honest summary for a reader deciding what to do: the cavity
benefit is well established; the enforceable federal ceiling of 4.0 mg/L
sits above the 1.5 mg/L concentration the NTP review associated with
lower IQ, which is arithmetic on two federal numbers rather than a
conclusion any agency has drawn; and the federal government is currently
in the middle of re-examining exactly this. Anyone who tells you the
neurodevelopmental question is definitively settled, in either direction,
is ahead of the published federal position as of September 2026. What is
not in dispute is that knowing your own water's concentration is useful,
and that on a private well nobody knows it unless you test.

## Mechanical care that actually works

Brushing and cleaning between teeth are not two versions of the same
task. They reach different surfaces, and neither substitutes for the
other.

**Brushing.** NIDCR: "Brush your teeth twice a day with a fluoride
toothpaste," and "Use fluoride toothpaste. Fluoride is what protects
teeth from tooth decay (cavities)." The technique instructions are
specific and worth following exactly, because the common errors are
predictable:

- "Angle the bristles toward the gumline, so they clean between the gums
  and teeth." The gumline is where gum disease starts, and it is the spot
  people miss by brushing the flat middle of the tooth.
- "Brush gently using small, circular motions. Do not scrub hard back and
  forth." Hard scrubbing is not more thorough. It is how people abrade
  their own gums and tooth necks.
- "Brush all sides of each tooth."
- "Brush your tongue."

**Cleaning between teeth.** NIDCR: "Clean between teeth regularly, aiming
for once a day," and "Floss to remove plaque, and food particles, from
between your teeth." NIDCR lists several tools as acceptable: dental
floss, a floss holder, a floss threader, a water flosser, and interdental
brushes. That list is deliberately plural, and this guide recommends no
product and no brand. The surface that needs cleaning is the flat face
where two teeth touch, and the correct tool is whichever one you will
actually use daily on that surface.

**Sealants,** covered above, are the answer for the other high-risk
surface. They are applied professionally and are the single most
cost-effective preventive measure in CDC's oral health data.

**What to expect from doing this.** NIDCR's own summary is about as
optimistic as federal health writing gets: "If you protect your oral
health with good oral hygiene practices (brushing and flossing), the odds
are in your favor you can keep your teeth for a lifetime." CDC's
prevention list adds two more items to brushing and flossing: "Limiting
foods and beverages that are high in added sugar," and regular checkups
and professional cleaning.

**On timing, and being honest about the edges.** NIDCR gives frequency,
twice a day, and technique, but I could not open a federal page stating a
minimum brushing duration, a position on whether to brush before or after
breakfast, or a recommendation on whether to rinse with water after
brushing. That last question, whether rinsing washes away fluoride that
would otherwise keep working, is actively studied and I found research on
it, but no federal guidance I could open takes a position. So this guide
does not state one. Twice daily with fluoride toothpaste, angled at the
gumline, plus once daily between the teeth, is the part that is actually
published, and it is the part that carries nearly all of the benefit.

Hand hygiene belongs here too in one narrow respect: anything that goes
into your mouth repeatedly, including your fingers when using floss, is a
route in. [Handwashing and Personal Hygiene](handwashing_and_hygiene.md)
covers that properly.

## Recognising trouble, in order of how much time you have

This sequence is the practical core of the guide. Each step gives you
less time than the one before it.

**1. Sensitivity to sweet, hot or cold.** NIDCR notes that early decay
produces no symptoms at all, and that as it advances a person may get "a
toothache (tooth pain) or tooth sensitivity to sweets, hot, or cold." A
brief twinge that stops as soon as the stimulus is removed is the mildest
signal there is. This is the stage where the problem is still cheap. It
is also the stage almost everyone ignores.

**2. A visible white spot, or a visible hole.** A chalky white patch is
mineral loss without structural collapse, and NIDCR is explicit that
decay can still be stopped or reversed there. A hole or a dark pit is not
reversible. Neither is going to heal and both are reasons to be seen, the
first because it can still be turned around and the second because it
cannot.

**3. Pain on biting.** When pressure on the tooth hurts, or the tooth
feels slightly raised or "high" when you close, the trouble has reached
the tissues around the root tip. Restating the StatPearls description of
apical periodontitis: pain, tenderness to pressure, difficulty eating,
and a sensation that the tooth is elevated are early features once
infection has passed beyond the tooth into the surrounding bone. This is
no longer early.

**4. Pain that arrives on its own.** Spontaneous pain, with no hot drink
or sweet to trigger it, and pain that lingers well after a stimulus is
gone, mark inflammation of the pulp that has passed the point of
recovery. Restated from the same reference: where mild pain provoked by a
stimulus and settling quickly suggests a reversible state, constant,
spontaneous, poorly localised pain indicates the irreversible one.

**5. Pain that wakes you at night.** Pain severe enough to wake somebody
from sleep is a recognised feature of the irreversible stage. Treat this
as "be seen promptly," not "see how it goes."

**6. The pain stops. This is the one people misread, and it is the most
important sentence in this guide.**

When a tooth has hurt badly for days and then stops hurting, the usual
reading is that it is getting better. It usually is not. What has
typically happened is that the pulp, the living tissue inside the tooth,
has died. Restating StatPearls on oral facial infection of dental origin:
irreversible pulpitis may progress to loss of blood supply and death of
the pulp, and this often leaves the tooth without symptoms even though
the underlying disease is still there. The nerve that was generating the
pain is gone. The infection is not.

That silent tooth is now a sealed chamber of dead, infected tissue with a
direct route into the bone at the root tip. The same reference notes that
chronic lesions at the root tip are typically symptom-free, and that they
may spread through bone into the tissue spaces of the face and neck, or
open a channel to the surface. That channel is the small bubble or pimple
that appears on the gum, which is a drainage point for an infection that
never resolved. The A.D.A.M. encyclopedia on MedlinePlus names exactly
that finding, a throbbing toothache that will not go away or a bubble on
the gum, as a reason to contact a dentist. Its content is copyrighted, so
that is restated rather than quoted.

**Relief of dental pain without treatment is not recovery. Assume it is
progression until a professional says otherwise.**

**7. Swelling.** Swelling means the infection has left the tooth and is
in the soft tissue. This is where the hazard rating of this topic comes
from, and it gets its own section.

## The abscess, and when to stop reading and get help

NIDCR notes that untreated decay can lead to an abscess, with "pain,
facial swelling, and fever." That undersells where this can go, so here
is the rest of it, plainly.

An infection that starts in a tooth does not stay in the tooth. Restating
the StatPearls guide for medical practitioners: infection from a lower
molar can spread into the tissue spaces under the tongue and under the
jaw, producing Ludwig angina, a condition that obstructs the airway if it
is not managed in time. Restating the StatPearls reference on Ludwig
angina itself: about 90 percent of cases originate from infections of the
lower molars, swelling of the critical airway structures can develop
within about 30 minutes of symptom onset, and airway compromise is the
leading cause of death from it. The first priority in treatment is
securing the airway.

That is the reason this topic is rated serious. A neglected tooth is, in
a small number of cases, an airway emergency with a half-hour fuse.

**The findings below mean call emergency services now. Not the dentist in
the morning. Now.** Each one is on its own line because each one is
independently sufficient.

- **Swelling that is spreading across the face rather than staying in one
  spot.**
- **Swelling that is closing the eye, or spreading around the eye.**
- **Swelling under the tongue, or a floor of the mouth that feels firm or
  raised.**
- **Swelling that has reached the neck, or a neck that looks uniformly
  thickened.**
- **Difficulty swallowing, or drooling because swallowing has become hard.**
- **Any difficulty breathing, or noisy breathing.**
- **A muffled or thickened voice.**
- **Trismus, meaning you cannot open your mouth normally.**
- **Fever, particularly above about 38.5 degrees C, which is about 101.3
  degrees F.**
- **Confusion, drowsiness or altered alertness in someone with a dental
  infection.**

Those findings are restated, never quoted, from the copyrighted
StatPearls references named above. Marked trismus, difficulty swallowing,
drooling, limited tongue mobility, a swollen or firm floor of the mouth,
a muffled voice, fever, facial swelling and redness, and respiratory
distress all appear in the warning list for oral facial infection of
dental origin, with altered mental status and difficulty breathing
flagged as the findings that should cause immediate concern. The firm,
uniform neck swelling comes from the Ludwig angina reference.

**One item on that list needs its provenance stated honestly: the eye.**
Swelling closing or surrounding the eye did not appear in the red flag
lists on the pages I opened. I have included it because the same
reference names clot formation in a venous sinus at the base of the skull
as a rare complication of these infections, which is the route by which a
dental infection reaches the eye. So treat it as a strong reason to be
seen urgently, which it plainly is, rather than as an item I can trace to
a published warning list. Everything else above I can.

**Three things about that list are worth saying explicitly.**

First, **this is an emergency services problem, not a dental one.** The
correct call is the emergency number, and the correct destination is a
hospital, because the immediate threat is the airway and the treatment is
airway management and intravenous antibiotics. A dental surgery is not
equipped for that and may not be open.

Second, **do not wait to see whether it settles overnight.** The whole
danger of this condition is the speed at which the airway can close.
Someone who looks manageable at bedtime is exactly the case the thirty
minute figure is about.

Third, **fever plus facial swelling in someone with a bad tooth is enough
on its own.** You do not need to wait for breathing difficulty to justify
going. Breathing difficulty is late.

For everything below that threshold, a painful tooth with local swelling
and no systemic signs, the answer is still urgent dental care, just not
an ambulance. Restating the StatPearls description of management: draining
the infection, antibiotics, pain control and dealing with the source
tooth, with hospital admission where there is fever, breathing difficulty
or airway involvement and urgent dental referral otherwise. Note that the
source tooth is on that list alongside the antibiotics. Antibiotics are
one component of the management described, not the whole of it.

## What you cannot do at home, and why

**This guide will not tell you how to extract a tooth, how to drain an
abscess, or how to place a filling.** That is a decision, not an
oversight, and here is the reasoning.

The space around a tooth root is not a place an untrained person can work
safely, and the reason is that it is not a sealed compartment. Restating
the StatPearls reference on dental abscess: infection in the pulp tracks
down through the root canal and then into the lower jaw or up into the
upper jaw, depending on which tooth it is. From there, as the previous
section set out, it can reach the tissue spaces under the tongue and jaw
and turn into an airway emergency, and the guide for medical
practitioners also names clot formation in a venous sinus at the base of
the skull as a rare complication.

That is the anatomy an improvised procedure is taking place inside: a set
of connected spaces with the airway at one end. I did not open a source
giving the distances from particular tooth roots to the sinus above them
or the nerve canal below them, so this guide states no figures for those.
The argument does not need them.

Now consider the failure modes specifically, because "it might go wrong"
is too vague to change anybody's behaviour:

- **A tooth that breaks during an attempted extraction** leaves retained
  roots and an open socket. You have not removed the infection, you have
  opened a larger door into the bone for it, and you have made the real
  extraction considerably harder.
- **Cutting into a swelling** without knowing the anatomy risks opening
  the infection into the tissue planes rather than out of them, and there
  are large blood vessels in the neck that do not tolerate error.
- **Sealing a cavity that contains infected pulp** traps the infection
  under a lid. The pressure has nowhere to go but the bone. This is why
  "just fill it to stop the pain" is actively dangerous rather than
  merely ineffective.
- **Pain control that lets you ignore it longer** is the quiet version of
  the same mistake, and it is the one most people actually make.

The general principle, which the Library applies elsewhere too: where a
mistake's failure mode is worse than the untreated problem, and where the
skill cannot be acquired from reading, the responsible thing a document
can do is say so and stop. [Bleeding and Wounds](bleeding_and_wounds.md)
draws the same line around wound closure for the same reason.

Field extraction is a real thing that real clinicians do in places with
no dental chair. It is done by people trained to do it, with anaesthetic,
instruments designed for the job, and a plan for the complications. None
of those three things is available to a reader of a guide, and the first
one, local anaesthetic, is not something this document is going to
explain either.

## Temporary measures that are genuinely defensible

These are the things you can legitimately do while arranging care. None
of them treats the underlying problem, and all of them are bridges rather
than solutions.

### Pain control, within the label

Over-the-counter analgesics used exactly as labelled are reasonable and
are often what a clinician would recommend anyway while you wait.

The important hazard here is not the dental one. It is acetaminophen,
known as paracetamol outside the US, because it is in hundreds of
products and the toxic dose is not far above the therapeutic one. FDA:
"The maximum total amount of acetaminophen taken in 24 hours should not
be more than 4,000 mg for adults and children 12 years of age and
older." FDA also warns that "Taking too much acetaminophen can cause
liver failure and death."

**The specific trap is double-dosing without realising it.** FDA: "Do not
use more than one acetaminophen-containing product at a time.
Acetaminophen is in many products. You could accidentally take too much
if you use more than one product containing acetaminophen at a time."
Someone with a bad tooth who takes a dedicated painkiller, plus a cold
and flu sachet, plus a night-time preparation, can reach a dangerous
total while believing each individual dose was correct. Read every label.

FDA also warns of "severe liver damage" if you have three or more
alcoholic drinks a day while using acetaminophen, and says: "Never take
more acetaminophen than what the label says, even if you still have fever
or pain." That last instruction is written for exactly this situation,
because dental pain is the kind that tempts people to exceed the label.

Two further points. **Do not put aspirin, or any tablet, against the gum
or the tooth.** The A.D.A.M. encyclopedia entry on tooth abscess makes
this point and it is restated here rather than quoted; a tablet held
against the mucosa can burn the tissue, and it does nothing useful for
the tooth, because these drugs work after absorption and not on contact.

**And do not use benzocaine gels on an infant or young child.** FDA is
direct: "Using benzocaine products can lead to a serious, and sometimes
fatal, condition called methemoglobinemia," a condition in which, in
FDA's words, "the oxygen-carrying capacity of red blood cells is greatly
reduced." FDA names the affected product types and advises against
topical oral viscous lidocaine for teething pain in infants and young
children as well. For teething, FDA's advice is mechanical: "gently rub
or massage the gums with your finger, or give the child a firm (not
liquid-filled) teether made of rubber to chew on," and it warns against
teething jewellery, citing reports of strangulation and choking.

### Keeping the area clean

Keep brushing, including the sore tooth, unless it is so painful that you
cannot. Plaque left on an already-inflamed area makes gum inflammation
worse, and the reversible stage of gum disease resolves with cleaning and
not with rest. Warm saltwater rinses are commonly suggested for comfort,
including in the A.D.A.M. encyclopedia entry noted above, and they are
harmless, but treat them as symptom relief and not as treatment. Nothing
you rinse with reaches the inside of a tooth.

### A lost filling or a crown that has come off

When a filling falls out or a crown comes off, the tooth underneath is
exposed. It will usually be sensitive, it can be sharp against the
tongue, and food will pack into it.

What over-the-counter temporary dental cement is actually for is worth
stating precisely, because people expect too much of it. The device
labelling registered with the FDA device database, published by NLM,
describes a temporary dental cement as being for "cementating temporary
crowns and splints. Retention of temporary crowns, sealing dressing in
teeth during root canal therapy procedures," with intended uses including
temporary luting of prefabricated crowns and bridges, temporary filling
of small cavities, and trial cementation of restorations before final
placement.

Read what that says and what it does not. The material is a placeholder:
it holds something in position, keeps a space sealed against debris, and
makes the tooth comfortable until the real repair happens. **It is not a
treatment for decay and it is not a treatment for infection.** Cementing
a crown back onto a tooth that is throbbing does not address why it is
throbbing, and a temporary material over an infected tooth has the
sealing-in problem described earlier.

I could not open a federal consumer-facing instruction for how to use a
retail lost-filling repair kit, so this guide does not give a procedure
for one. The package insert is the instruction, and it is written by the
manufacturer for that product. What is defensible to say from the device
labelling is what the material class is for, which is the paragraph
above.

### A knocked-out permanent tooth

This is the one genuine emergency in this guide where what a bystander
does in the first minutes changes the outcome, so it is worth getting
exactly right.

The tooth's chance of surviving depends on keeping alive the periodontal
ligament cells on the root surface. Those cells dry out fast in open air,
and everything below follows from protecting them. The figures, restated
from the StatPearls reference on avulsed teeth: most teeth can be
successfully replanted if the time out of the mouth and dry is under 30
minutes; the ligament cells are irreversibly damaged somewhere between 30
and 60 minutes; beyond about 90 minutes, special handling is required.

The handling steps, restated from the A.D.A.M. encyclopedia first aid
entry on a broken or knocked-out tooth (copyrighted, so restated rather
than quoted) and consistent with the StatPearls reference:

- **Pick the tooth up by the crown, the chewing end. Do not touch the
  root.** The cells you are trying to save are on the root surface.
- **Do not scrape or wipe the root to clean it,** and do not clean the
  tooth with alcohol or hydrogen peroxide.
- **Do not let it dry out.** This is the single most important
  instruction.
- **If you can, put it back in its socket straight away,** level with the
  neighbouring teeth, and bite gently on gauze or a damp teabag to hold
  it there.
- **If you cannot, put it in milk.** Milk is the recommended storage
  medium because its acidity, salt concentration and nutrient content
  suit the ligament cells. Saline or the person's own saliva will also
  do, including holding the tooth between the lip and gum in a person old
  enough not to swallow it.
- **Do not store it in water.** Plain water damages the ligament cells
  because it is too dilute relative to the cells.
- **Get to a dentist immediately.** The delay is the thing that decides
  the outcome.

**One important exception: a knocked-out baby tooth is not replanted.**
Restated from the StatPearls reference: primary teeth should not be
replanted, because doing so risks damaging the developing permanent tooth
underneath. Still get the child seen. Just do not push the tooth back in.

Milk being the recommended medium is a genuinely useful piece of
knowledge precisely because it is counterintuitive and because milk is
usually within reach. If you remember one thing from this section:
**crown only, never dry, milk not water, go now.**

## Access, honestly

A guide that treated "see a dentist" as a solved step would be useless to
a large share of the people reading it, because in the United States
dental care is the kind of care people go without most.

That is not a rhetorical flourish, it is a measured fact. Using national
survey data for 2019, CDC's Health, United States reports: "In 2019,
14.7% of people aged 2 and over did not receive needed dental care due to
cost in the past 12 months." The comparison numbers on the same page are
8.5 percent for medical care and 5.6 percent for prescription drugs.
Dental is the highest of the three, at roughly 1.7 times the medical
figure.

And it tracks income almost exactly: "The percentage of adults who did
not receive needed dental care due to cost was highest for adults living
below 100% of FPL (30.7%) and at 100%-199% of FPL (30.6%)." Nearly a
third of adults near the poverty line went without dental care they
needed because of what it cost. A separate CDC analysis of the 2019
national survey found 19.2 percent of women and 15.6 percent of men aged
18 and over went without needed dental care because of cost, peaking
around ages 30 to 64.

Set that beside the gum disease numbers from earlier, where periodontitis
ran 60.4 percent below the poverty level against 28.6 percent well above
it, and the shape of the problem is clear. The disease is commonest
exactly where the care is least reachable.

The consequences are not confined to the mouth. CDC attributes about 34
million lost school hours and 92 million lost work hours a year to dental
care needs, and estimates roughly 46 billion dollars of US productivity
lost yearly to untreated oral disease, in 2015 dollars.

### The routes that exist

NIDCR maintains a page on finding dental care, and it is the most useful
single federal document in this whole guide for a reader without money.
What it lists, in its own words:

- **Dental schools.** "Dental schools often have clinics that allow
  dental students to gain experience treating patients while providing
  care at a reduced cost." NIDCR points to a program finder at
  https://coda.ada.org/find-a-program/search-dental-programs.
- **Dental hygiene schools.** These "may also offer supervised, low-cost
  care as part of the training experience." This route covers cleaning
  and prevention rather than fillings or extractions, which given how
  much of this guide is about prevention is not a small thing.
- **Federally funded health centers.** "You may also be able to find care
  at federally funded health centers where you pay what you can afford,
  based on your income." The finder is at
  https://findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov/. These are the HRSA-supported
  community health centers, and the charge is set on a sliding scale by
  income rather than being a fixed price.
- **State and local health departments.** "Call your local or state
  health department to learn more about their financial assistance
  programs." NIDCR also suggests calling 2-1-1 to find local services.
- **Medicaid.** "Medicaid is a state-run program that provides medical
  benefits, and in some cases dental benefits."
- **CHIP,** for children in families earning too much for Medicaid but
  unable to afford private insurance.
- **Medicare,** which "only covers dental services related to certain
  medical conditions or treatments." Do not assume Medicare covers
  routine dental care, because generally it does not.
- **United Way,** which "may be able to direct you to free or
  reduced-cost dental services in your community."
- **Clinical trials,** where researchers "may provide study participants
  with limited free or low-cost dental treatment for the particular
  condition they are studying."

**The Medicaid split is worth understanding, because it catches people
out.** For children, dental coverage is required. Medicaid.gov states
that dental services are covered for all child enrollees as part of the
Early and Periodic Screening, Diagnostic and Treatment benefit, and that
this must at minimum include relief of pain and infections, restoration
of teeth, and maintenance of dental health. For adults it is optional:
states "have flexibility to determine what dental benefits are provided
to adult Medicaid enrollees," and "There are no minimum requirements for
adult dental coverage." So an adult and their child, in the same
household on the same programme, can have entirely different dental
entitlements, and what an adult gets depends on which state they are in.

I could not open HRSA's own pages describing the sliding fee scale, as
the server refused the request, so this guide describes the health centre
route in NIDCR's words rather than HRSA's, and does not state the
specific income thresholds or the number of health centres. The finder
URL above is the one NIDCR publishes.

**The practical advice, which costs nothing:** call before you are in
pain. Dental schools and health centres often have waiting lists, and the
worst possible time to start navigating an unfamiliar system is the night
a tooth starts throbbing. Prevention is free; a place in a queue is free;
an emergency is neither.

## What the simulation models, and what it does not

The game's medical data lives in `data/medical.ron`, and four records
relate to this document. The mapping is worth setting out, because the
simulation currently teaches a shape that is partly right and partly
missing the point of this guide.

What it represents well:

- **`tooth_decay`** is described as "Cavities and enamel erosion causing
  increasing pain," lists `dental_extraction` and `dental_filling` as its
  treatments, and carries `untreated_progression: Some("tooth_abscess")`.
  That progression is the correct one and it is the spine of the
  "recognising trouble" section above.
- **`tooth_abscess`** in turn progresses to `infection_bacterial`, which
  progresses to `sepsis`, which is marked `critical` and described as
  fatal within days without treatment. That chain, decay to abscess to
  systemic infection to death, is medically the right escalation and is
  unusual to see modelled at all.
- **`dental_extraction`** is a level 3 procedure requiring
  `med_kit_advanced_0`, and **`dental_filling`** is level 4. Both
  requiring an advanced kit is right: neither is a field-improvised
  action, which is the same conclusion this guide reaches.

What it does not yet cover:

- **There is no gum disease in the file at all.** No gingivitis, no
  periodontitis, no record mentioning gums. Searching `data/medical.ron`
  for gum, gingiv or perio returns nothing. Given that periodontitis
  affects 42.2 percent of adults over 30 and is a leading cause of adult
  tooth loss, the simulation currently models the disease that dominates
  childhood cavities and omits the one that actually takes adults' teeth.
  Half of this guide has no counterpart in the data.
- **There is no prevention mechanic.** Nothing represents brushing,
  cleaning between teeth, fluoride, sealants, saliva or sugar frequency.
  Because `tooth_decay` has `symptoms: []` and no precondition, decay is
  something that happens to a character rather than something their
  behaviour drives. The central teaching claim of this document, that
  frequency of sugar exposure determines whether teeth survive, is not
  expressible in the current schema.
- **`tooth_decay` carries `recovery_time_s: 3600`,** one hour. Whatever
  that field means elsewhere, applied to decay it encodes the opposite of
  the fact this guide is built on. Enamel structure does not recover, at
  any timescale. A filling is a patch, not a recovery. If that field is
  read as "time until healed," it is teaching the single most important
  thing about teeth backwards.
- **`tooth_decay` is `severity: "minor"` and `tooth_abscess` is
  `"moderate"`,** while the topic's own hazard rating is serious.
  Moderate is defensible for a typical abscess, but nothing in the data
  expresses the airway emergency that gives this topic its rating, and
  `tooth_abscess` offers `dental_extraction` as a treatment option with a
  0.92 success rate and no representation of a spreading infection that
  extraction alone does not solve.

None of that makes the existing records wrong to have. It means the
reading layer currently carries teaching the data cannot yet back, which
is worth knowing when the two are compared.

### What I could not source, and what I did instead

- **HRSA's own description of the sliding fee scale.** Three HRSA URLs
  (hrsa.gov/get-health-care, hrsa.gov/get-health-care/affordable and the
  Bureau of Primary Health Care compliance manual) all returned HTTP 403,
  and findahealthcenter.hrsa.gov returned only its page title. So this
  guide describes federally funded health centres in NIDCR's words
  instead, and states no figures for the number of health centres,
  patients served, or income thresholds.
- **A federal consumer instruction for using an over-the-counter lost
  filling repair kit.** None found. The guide therefore states only what
  the device labelling says the material class is for, and gives no
  procedure.
- **The fluoride concentration range that 0.7 mg/L replaced in 2015.** A
  search result asserted it was 0.7 to 1.2 mg/L, but neither the CDC
  recommendations page nor the MMWR report I opened states it, so the
  guide does not claim it.
- **Whether to rinse with water after brushing, and minimum brushing
  duration.** No federal page I opened takes a position on either, so the
  guide says so rather than filling the gap.
- **A published threshold for how many separate sugar exposures per day
  is too many.** NIDCR gives the mechanism and the direction but no
  number, and the guide presents the clustering rule as a rule of thumb
  drawn from the mechanism rather than as a published limit.
- **Swelling around the eye as a published red flag for dental
  infection.** It is not on the warning lists of the pages I opened. The
  guide keeps the finding, because the venous sinus complication named on
  those same pages is exactly the route to it, but says at the point of
  the claim that it is reasoning from a named complication rather than a
  citation to a warning list.
- **PubMed Central articles on avulsed tooth storage media
  (PMC5571385).** Blocked by a reCAPTCHA on two attempts, via both the
  pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov and www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov addresses. Not cited. The
  storage medium facts in this guide come from the StatPearls avulsed
  tooth article, which did load.
