# Fire Performance Fuels: What Is in the Can, and How to Handle It

This guide covers the liquids people burn on fire props: what each one
actually is, what it is called in different countries, what its flash
point means for handling it, how it tends to burn, which fuels fire
departments and festival fire teams allow, which fuels are never used,
and how to store and handle all of them. It pairs with
[Building a Fire Staff](fire_staff_materials.md), which covers the prop
itself, and [Clothing for Fire Performance](fire_performance_clothing.md),
which covers what to wear.

One hard boundary before anything else: **do not light a fire prop
without a trained spotter, and do not learn fire spinning from a
document.** Learn the movement unlit, then learn fire safety in person
from experienced spinners. This guide tells you what is in your fuel can
and how that can behaves. It cannot make you safe.

Two warnings about the numbers. First, fuels change between brands,
countries and even years, so **the safety data sheet (SDS) for the bottle
in your hand beats every number below.** An SDS is the maker's official
sheet listing what is in a product, its flash point and its hazards, and
it is free online for almost every fuel. Second, much of what spinners
say about fuel (brightness, smell, burn time, wick wear) is experience,
not measurement. Every number here traces to something in the Sources
list at the bottom. Where a figure comes only from makers, retailers or
practitioners, it is labelled **community figure** in the text, and it
means what spinners tend to report, not something anyone tested.

## The short answer

- **Almost every fuel in this guide is a flammable liquid in law.**
  OSHA's flammable liquids standard counts anything with a flash point at
  or below 93 C (199.4 F), and that takes in camp fuel, alcohol, kerosene
  and most lamp oils. The exceptions are the highest-flash paraffin lamp
  oils (Lamplight's Ultra-Pure, at 97 to 121 C on its sheets) and Isopar M
  (93.5 C minimum), which sit above the line and are not regulated as
  flammable liquids. They still burn readily once they are in a wick.
- **The split that matters for handling is whether a fuel gives off
  ignitable vapour at ordinary temperatures.** Camp fuel and Zippo
  lighter fluid do at any temperature you will spin in. Ronsonol lighter
  fluid and alcohol do on anything but a cold day (their sheets give
  flash points of 2 C and about 7 C, 36 F and 45 F), so handle all of
  them as though the vapour is always there. Kerosene, lamp oil and the
  isoparaffins do not until they are heated or held in a wick.
- **"Naphtha" is a family name, not a product.** Camp fuel is a naphtha.
  The naphtha sold as paint thinner in US hardware stores is a different,
  heavier naphtha, and nobody sells it as a spinning fuel.
- **"Lamp oil" is two different products.** Some are refined paraffins
  with flash points far above kerosene; others are kerosene-type
  distillates. Read the SDS.
- **Many US spinners use camp fuel** (**community figure**), straight or
  blended with lamp oil, for fast lighting and a clean burn. Kerosene is
  cheaper and burns longer but smokes and smells more. At least one city
  permit, Seattle's, turns that round and allows only kerosene-grade or
  heavier fuels by default.

## Flash point, and what it means in your hands

OSHA defines flash point as the lowest temperature at which a liquid
gives off enough vapour to form an ignitable mixture with the air near
its surface. Below its flash point, a fuel needs to be heated (or
spread thin on a wick, which amounts to the same thing) before it will
light. Above it, the fuel is already surrounded by vapour that will
catch from a spark.

What that means in practice:

- **For camp fuel, "above its flash point" is every temperature you will
  ever spin in.** Coleman's sheet gives its camp fuel's flash point as
  below -18 C (0 F). The vapour is always there.
- **Alcohol is above its flash point on any mild day.** Retail denatured
  alcohol flashes at about 7 C (45 F) on its own sheet.
- **The vapour is heavier than air and it travels.** The Coleman,
  Klean-Strip denatured alcohol and Hess kerosene sheets, and NOAA's
  CAMEO Chemicals database, all say the same thing in different words:
  the vapour spreads along the ground and can reach an ignition source
  some distance away, then flash back to the container. The flame that
  lights your fuel can does not have to be near it.
- **Mists and sprays can light below the flash point.** The Firefly
  paraffin lamp oil sheet warns that the oil can form an explosive
  mixture when sprayed or atomised, and an older Coleman sheet, reproduced
  in the Kindle Arts Society guidelines, says mists may be flammable
  below the normal flash point. A freshly dipped wick being spun throws
  exactly such a mist.
- **What counts is the temperature of the fuel, not the air.** Kerosene
  sold to the US specification can flash as low as 38 C (100 F), and
  kerosene-range fuel in general from about 35 C (95 F) on CAMEO's
  figures. EPA reports that conventional asphalt reached surface
  temperatures up to 152 F (67 C) at midday in an Arizona study. A spill
  on sun-baked pavement, or a can left in the sun, can push a "safe"
  fuel past its flash point.
- **Fuel soaked into something is a wick.** A puddle of lamp oil on cool
  ground will not flash from a spark, but the same oil soaked into grass,
  dirt, a rag or your sleeve will burn if a flame reaches it.
- **Evaporation follows the same order.** Camp fuel and alcohol are gone
  from skin, clothes and spills within minutes. Kerosene and lamp oil
  barely evaporate: Lamplight's Ultra-Pure lamp oil sheet gives a vapour
  pressure below 0.01 mmHg at 20 C, against 5.3 psi at 38 C for Coleman
  camp fuel. So a splash of kerosene or lamp oil stays wet in fabric,
  and, we infer, stays flammable long after camp fuel would be gone. That
  is our reading of the evaporation figures; no test of it was found.

**How the regulation groups them.** Under OSHA 29 CFR 1910.106,
Categories 1 and 2 are liquids flashing below 23 C (73.4 F), Category 3
covers 23 to 60 C, and Category 4 covers above 60 up to 93 C. Anything
flashing above 93 C is not a flammable liquid under the standard at
all. Camp fuel, lighter fluid, alcohol and paint-aisle naphtha fall in
the first two categories. Kerosene sold to the US specification, and
Isopar G and H, fall in Category 3, although NIOSH's range for kerosene
(38 to 72 C) runs up into Category 4. Firefly paraffin lamp oil, clear
lamp oils such as Medallion, and Isopar L fall in Category 4. Ultra-Pure
paraffin lamp oil and Isopar M flash above 93 C and sit outside the
categories: Lamplight's 2007 Ultra-Pure sheet calls the product
nonhazardous under OSHA's hazard communication rules and not regulated
for transport. That is a statement about the liquid in the bottle, not
about a soaked wick. NIOSH uses the older labels for the same idea: it
classes VM&P naphtha, methanol and ethanol as Class IB flammable liquids
and kerosene as a Class II combustible liquid.

## What each fuel actually is

### Camp fuel, white gas and Coleman fuel

These are three names for the same kind of product: a light, very
volatile petroleum naphtha blend sold for camp stoves and lanterns.
"White gas" today means this purpose-made product, not gasoline.

The recipe is proprietary and changes. Coleman's current US sheet (2022)
lists 60 to 100 percent of a light hydrotreated petroleum distillate,
plus pentane, isopentane and cyclohexene at 5 to 10 percent each, and
hexane at 1 to 5 percent. Its flash point is below -18 C (0 F). It is
classed as highly flammable, carries warnings for aspiration (see the
poisoning note below), drowsiness and possible skin irritation, and is
labelled **suspected of damaging fertility or the unborn child** because
of the hexane. The same sheet calls for static precautions and says to
use it only outdoors or in a well-ventilated area. It ships as a Class 3
hazardous material, so it is awkward to buy online.

Those hazard labels describe handling the liquid and its vapour, not a
measurement of what a burning wick gives off. They are still a good
reason for gloves, for standing with the wind at your back when you pour,
and for keeping flames away from the downwind side of your fuel.

**Wick lighter fluid is a close cousin.** Zippo's EU sheet (2022) lists
the same main ingredient as Coleman camp fuel, with a flash point below
-6.5 C (20 F). Ronsonol's EU sheet (2018) describes a slightly heavier C7
to C9 hydrocarbon with a flash point of 2 C (36 F). Formulas differ by
market, so read the sheet for your bottle.

**Australia and New Zealand: Shellite.** Shellite is the local camp-fuel
equivalent, but brands differ a great deal. The Diggers (Recochem) sheet
(2022) gives a flash point of -30 C (-22 F), lists n-hexane at up to 30
percent, and carries the labels may cause cancer, may cause genetic
defects, and causes damage to organs through prolonged or repeated
exposure. Its cancer classification is Category 1A, the highest there
is: under OSHA's hazard communication rules, 1A means known to have
carcinogenic potential for humans, based largely on human evidence.
Hunter's Shellite (2021) is a heptane, cyclohexane and n-hexane blend with a
flash point of -15 C (5 F) and n-hexane at 15 to 20 percent. Compare
that with 1 to 5 percent hexane in US Coleman fuel. With Shellite, gloves,
standing upwind and minimal skin contact matter even more.

### Paint-aisle naphtha (VM&P naphtha) is not camp fuel

The can labelled "naphtha" in a US hardware store is usually VM&P
(varnish makers' and painters') naphtha, sold as a paint thinner.
Klean-Strip's sheet gives its intended use as thinning oil-based paint,
enamel and varnish, and its flash point as 21 C (69 F). NIOSH gives VM&P
naphtha a flash point of -7 to 13 C (20 to 55 F) and describes it as
typically about 12 percent aromatic compounds. So plenty of cans give
off ignitable vapour at room temperature, and none of it is sold or
tested as a spinning fuel.

The rule: **camp fuel is a naphtha-type fuel, but not all naphtha is camp fuel.**
The chemical identity numbers (CAS numbers) printed on these sheets name
broad refinery streams, and the same number turns up on products with
very different flash points. Compare flash points, not names.

### Kerosene

In the US, kerosene is sold to a published specification, ASTM D3699,
in two grades. No. 1-K is the low-sulphur grade (at most 0.04 percent
sulphur by mass), made for unvented heaters and wick lamps. No. 2-K
allows up to 0.30 percent sulphur. **Both must flash at 38 C (100 F) or
higher** in the edition that Maryland's kerosene regulation reproduces.
ASTM's public summary of the current edition (D3699-25) does not state
the figure, so that is the edition this guide can confirm it for. Real
products sit at or above that line: Hess gives above 38 C,
the Imperial Oil sheet reproduced by Kindle Arts gives 40 C (104 F), and
NIOSH gives a range of 38 to 72 C (100 to 162 F). CAMEO Chemicals starts
its range lower, at 35 C (95 F), because it describes kerosene-range
material in general. For fuel sold as kerosene in the US, the
specification minimum of 38 C is the figure to use; for fuel bought
elsewhere, or anything off specification, allow for the lower one. In
the UK and South Africa this fuel is called paraffin.

Spinners report that it burns longer than camp fuel, smokes and smells
more, and leaves more residue (see How each fuel burns).

Its sheets carry health labels too, and they are no gentler than camp
fuel's. Hess classes kerosene as causing skin irritation and as a
suspected carcinogen (Category 2, OSHA's class for suspected human
carcinogens), and lists 0.04 percent naphthalene. Klean-Strip's
"Kerosene" sheet (for a product that was really mineral spirits, see
below) adds may cause genetic defects, suspected of damaging fertility
or the unborn child, and possible damage to the central nervous system
through prolonged or repeated exposure. Gloves and pouring with the wind
at your back apply to kerosene as much as to camp fuel.

One label quirk worth knowing: Klean-Strip's now-discontinued "Kerosene"
listed its main ingredient on its own sheet as Stoddard solvent, which is
mineral spirits, with up to 5 percent trimethylbenzene. A product sold
as kerosene is not guaranteed to be kerosene.

### Lamp oil: two different products under one name

This is where the most common advice goes wrong. **Lamp oil is not
always kerosene, and the difference is large.**

- **Paraffin lamp oil** (look for "paraffin" on the label) is a refined
  paraffinic (alkane) hydrocarbon, a mix of branched and straight-chain
  molecules, with a flash point well above kerosene's. Firefly Paraffin
  Lamp Oil (2023) is more than 95 percent C12 to C15 alkanes, branched
  and linear, with a flash point of 83.5 C (182 F). Lamplight's
  Ultra-Pure gave 97 C (207 F) on its 2007 sheet and 121 C (250 F) on its
  2002 sheet, so even one brand's own sheets disagree. Lamplight's 2007
  sheet says it is not regulated for transport and is only mildly
  irritating to skin with prolonged contact. **Treat the 2007 sheet's
  figures as approximate:** it contradicts itself, describing the oil as
  water white with a mild hydrocarbon smell in one section and as a
  yellowish liquid with a citronella smell in another, which suggests
  parts of it were carried over from a scented product. (Its C5 to C20
  composition line is a broad refinery-stream name; the 2002 sheet names
  a narrower C14 to C16 paraffin mix.) Read the current sheet for your
  own bottle.
- **Clear "lamp oil" that is not paraffin** is often a kerosene-type
  distillate. Lamplight's Medallion lamp oil, for example, is a
  hydrotreated light distillate with a flash point of 63 C (145 F).
  What this guide says about paraffin lamp oil does not automatically
  apply to it.

Paraffin lamp oil can also freeze in cold weather: the 2007 Ultra-Pure
sheet gives 0 C (32 F), the 2002 sheet 8 C (47 F).

### Isoparaffins (Isopar, Shellsol T, Pegasol)

Highly refined synthetic fuels with almost no aromatics and very little
smell. ExxonMobil's sales specifications for Isopar G and Isopar L allow at
most 0.01 percent aromatics by weight, and the grades differ a lot in
flash point. Minimums on the sheets: Isopar G 40 C, Isopar H 49 C,
Isopar L 62 C, Isopar M 93.5 C. **Know your grade.**

Portland Fire & Rescue lists isoparaffin oil, naming Shell Sol T, as a
common fuel, and a New Zealand retailer recommends Isopar H for
professional indoor shows, while telling everyone else never to spin
fire indoors (**community figure**). NAFAA's guidelines say any
petroleum fuel burned on an open wick gives off toxic fumes and should
be very limited indoors, even with ventilation. Isoparaffins are
petroleum fuels too: ExxonMobil's Isopar G sheet, for example, names its
only ingredient as a hydrotreated petroleum naphtha. So they are the
purified end of that family, not an exception to the rule. This guide
found no published measurement of how these burn on a wick. In the US
they appear to be sold mainly through chemical distributors in pails
and drums, but that was seen only in search listings and not verified;
no consumer price could be confirmed.

### Denatured alcohol and methylated spirits

Ethanol made undrinkable by adding other chemicals. **What it is mixed
with matters more than the alcohol.** Klean-Strip Denatured Alcohol, a
common US brand, lists 30 to 90 percent ethanol and **5 to 60 percent
methanol** on its 2023 sheet, and labels it toxic if swallowed, in
contact with skin and if inhaled, with a warning that it may cause
blindness. The same sheet notes that symptoms of methanol poisoning can
be delayed by 6 to 30 hours. NIOSH lists optic nerve damage (blindness)
among methanol's effects and treats skin contact as a real route of
exposure.

Methylated spirits ("meths" in the UK, "metho" in Australia) is the same
idea, but denaturing recipes differ by country, brand and year. Some
contain methanol and some use other denaturants. The sheet for your
bottle tells you which.

**Flash point: about 7 C (45 F)** on Klean-Strip's sheet for the retail
product. NIOSH's 11 C (52 F) and 13 C (55 F) are for pure methanol and
pure ethanol. Use the retail product's lower figure.

The flame is dim and bluish and hard to see in daylight. That is the
real hazard: you may not be able to tell whether something is still
burning.

### Gel fuels

Portland's list names one brand of gel fire fuel. Be careful with the
word "gel": common chafing-dish gels are gelled alcohol, and some are
mostly methanol. Sterno's Handy Fuel is sold as a methanol gel, and its
sheet (2013) lists methanol at 50 to 100 percent. **Never use a
methanol gel on a prop.** No sheet for a purpose-made fire-arts gel could
be found.

Gel fuels have their own record of harm. In 2011 the US Consumer Product
Safety Commission announced a recall of pourable gel fuels for firepots
after 65 incidents, two deaths and 34 people hospitalised with burns.
The pattern: the flame was hard to see, someone poured more fuel into a
pot that was still burning, and burning gel splattered onto people. That
is the same failure as dipping a prop you think has gone out.

### Blends

Mixing camp fuel with lamp oil is common, to get easy lighting and a
longer burn. Ratios people use include 1:1 and 1:2, 60 to 75 percent
white gas, or three parts Coleman fuel to one part lamp oil (**community
figures**, from Dark Monk, Temple of Poi and Home of Poi). Camp fuel with
kerosene is also used and is reported to smoke more than camp fuel alone.

This guide's rule, which is our own inference and not a published
standard: **anything with camp fuel in it gives off camp-fuel vapour, so
store and handle any blend exactly like camp fuel.** Label every blend.

"Always" in the last column means the flash point is below freezing, so
the vapour is there at any temperature you will spin in. "Above" gives
the fuel temperature at which the vapour appears, for fuels that flash
between freezing and room temperature, so at room temperature they
always do. "No"
means no at ordinary temperatures. Every one of these fuels will burn
once it is in a wick, soaked into something, or sitting on something
hot.

## How each fuel burns

Everything in this table is a **community figure**: performer reports
and maker estimates, not measurements. Wick size, wind and how hard you
spin off change burn time a lot.

| Fuel | Lighting | Flame and soot | Burn per dip | Residue and smell |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Camp fuel | Instant | Bright, least soot of the petroleum fuels | About 3 to 4 min | Least residue; evaporates off props and clothes |
| Kerosene | Takes a few seconds to catch, slower in the cold | Smokiest and sootiest of the common fuels | About 5 to 7 min; one performer's review put it at about 40 percent longer than white gas | Most smell, and the most residue on props |
| Paraffin lamp oil | Slow to light | Smaller flame; some smoke on an open wick, whatever the label says | 5 min or more | Slippery, oily film on floors and props; its soot can be hard to wash out of clothes |
| Isoparaffins | Depends on grade | Reported to be clean, with little smoke | Not reported | Close to odourless |
| Denatured alcohol | Instant | Dim and bluish, hard to see in daylight | Short | Evaporates very fast |

Two notes on the table.

**Kerosene burn time is where sources disagree.** A few older guides say
kerosene is quick to light and short to burn. Most sources, including
one performer's side-by-side review, say it burns longer, and that fits
how slowly it evaporates. This guide goes with longer.

**On "white gas burns hotter".** [Building a Fire Staff](fire_staff_materials.md)
explains why flame temperature is not a useful way to compare these
fuels. What genuinely differs is soot, and how fast each fuel feeds the
flame. Candle-flame research attributes a flame's visible glow to
thermal radiation from hot soot particles, so for flames of the same
size a sootier one is brighter and, it follows, radiates more heat at
you and your prop. Kerosene soots heavily; alcohol barely soots, which
is why its flame is so hard to see. Camp fuel is still reported to look
bright (**community figure**) although it soots less than kerosene.
Our reading, not a measurement, is that this comes from size: camp fuel
evaporates fast and feeds a large flame, while a sootier fuel glows
more for a flame of the same size. A Kevlar wick maker reports that
kerosene-based fuel at least doubles wick life compared with white gas
(**community figure**).

## Why many US spinners reach for camp fuel

It lights on the first touch, looks bright, leaves less soot than the
oily fuels, and is gone from your clothes by the time you pack up. The
trade-offs are short burns, harder wear on wicks, a hexane warning on the
label, and vapour that is always ready to flash. **Fuel discipline at the
depot matters most with this fuel.**

It is not allowed everywhere. Seattle's fire performance permit bans
Class I flammable liquids, naming white gas and gasoline, and allows
only kerosene-grade (Class II) or heavier fuel unless the Fire Prevention
Division approves a small amount in advance. Lighter fluid and alcohol
are Class I liquids too, so the same rule shuts them out. Check your own
city's conditions before you buy a gallon.

Elsewhere the default differs. In the UK, fire-arts advice to use
"paraffin" means kerosene. A New Zealand retailer recommends a
de-aromatised solvent (Pegasol 3440) for spinning and says the Shellite
grade is for quick flash effects, not spinning (**community figure**).

**Cost, as a snapshot checked Sept 24, 2026:** Coleman camp fuel was
$19.99 a gallon at one farm supply store and $24.99 at a hardware store,
where it was in-store pickup only. One flow-arts page (undated) quotes
about $9 to $10 a gallon (**community figure**), so do not compare it
with the dated prices. Prices move constantly and vary by region; check
before you plan around them. No kerosene price could be confirmed on the
same day.

## When and why some use kerosene

It is cheap, sold almost everywhere, burns longer, and is reported to be
gentler on wicks (**community figure**). A small spill on cool pavement
will not give off ignitable vapour, and one teacher recommended it to
beginners for exactly that reason (**community figure**).

That changes on a hot day. Kerosene sold to the US specification can
flash as low as 38 C (100 F), and kerosene-range fuel in general from
about 35 C (95 F) on CAMEO's figures. Sun-baked pavement or sand, or a
can left in the sun, can exceed either. Keep it shaded, and treat any
hot-weather spill as flammable. And a spill soaked into grass, dirt or
clothing burns like a wick whatever the weather.

The downsides are smoke, smell, sticky residue, the health labels
described above, and fuel that stays wet in your clothes. Portland allows
kerosene outdoors only, while Seattle's permit makes kerosene grade the
default and camp fuel the exception. A Canadian fire performance society
says white gas or lamp oil should be used before kerosene, preferring
the more purified fuel, and a New Zealand retailer calls it a last
resort.

## What fire departments and festival fire teams allow

A fire department's permit conditions are the closest thing to an
official answer. This guide found two cities' permit conditions and
three performance organisations' guidelines, and the two cities disagree
about the most common fuel. **Local fire codes vary and always win**, so
ask your fire marshal or the event's fire team.

Two extinguishers rated at least 2A-40BC and a 2.5-gallon bucket of water; 15 ft clear between audience and spinners; fuel stored in an approved manner under constant supervision; no fire breathing |
| Burning Man Fire Conclave (2021) | White gas (also called naphtha, Coleman fuel, camping fuel), lighter fluid (Ronsonol, Zippo), lamp oil, kerosene, alcohol | Original or clearly marked fuel containers, never water bottles; at least one 5 lb ABC extinguisher; never dump fuel on the ground |
| NAFAA performer safety guidelines, Revision 3.0 (reprinted by the Eagle River Fire Protection District, 2019) | Prefers high-proof alcohol indoors, and the most purified fuel available when petroleum fuel is used | Petroleum fuels on open wicks always give off toxic fumes and smoke; keep them very limited indoors even with ventilation; a sheltered, windless outdoor spot can be as bad as indoors |
| Kindle Arts Society, Version 207 (2015) | White gas, kerosene, isopropyl alcohol, ultra-pure lamp oil | White gas or lamp oil before kerosene |

Portland's policy also shows what a full set of permit conditions looks
like. Performers must be 18 or older, or 16 to 18 at the fire marshal's
discretion with written parental consent and direct adult supervision.
Fire must stay 20 ft from the audience and from anything combustible,
with 5 ft more when several performers share the space. Two safety
people are required, one of them a spotter whose only job is watching
the performance, the audience and the fuel area. Each spotter needs a
2A-10BC extinguisher nearby, a wet cotton towel or a wool, Nomex or
duvetyne blanket, and access to a 5-gallon bucket of water for cooling
burns. Excess fuel must be spun off into a spin-out bucket before
lighting, and a drip pan goes under the fuel area. Clothing must be
snug, natural fibre or fire resistant, and never synthetic. Indoor
venues need automatic sprinklers and at least a 12 ft ceiling.

A caution on the alcohol entries. Portland and NAFAA list or prefer
alcohol (Seattle's Class II rule excludes it by default), but US
denatured alcohol can be up to 60 percent methanol and its sheet says to
use it outdoors or with good ventilation. One flow-arts shop's safety
guide says never to use alcohol at all (**community figure**). If you
use it, know exactly what is in your bottle.

## Never in the dip can

| Fuel | Why not |
|---|---|
| **Gasoline or petrol** | More volatile than camp fuel: flash point -43 C (-45 F). The Hess sheet lists benzene at 0.1 to 4.9 percent and classes gasoline as a carcinogen and mutagen; EPA has required refiners to average 0.62 percent benzene since 2011, which is lower but not zero. The sheet also says to use it only as a motor fuel. Portland prohibits it outright |
| **Diesel** | Not because it is volatile: ExxonMobil's sheet gives a flash point above 38 C (100 F), despite retailer claims otherwise. It is sooty and hard to light, the sheet labels it a suspected carcinogen, and it says not to use it for anything but motor fuel |
| **Straight methanol**, including Canadian methyl hydrate and methanol gels | Burns with a flame CAMEO describes as invisible; toxic through the skin as well as by swallowing and breathing; can blind |
| **Flame-colouring chemicals** mixed into any fuel | One performance troupe reports that colouring chemicals mixed into alcohol fuel, such as lithium chloride, copper chloride and boric acid, may give off toxic vapours and tend to destroy wicks (**community figure**) |
| **Scented torch fuel** | TIKI citronella torch fuel is a kerosene-type distillate (flash point 63 to 135 C), but scent additives are not on any fire authority's list and one prop maker advises against torch fuel (**community figure**). Torch fuels vary: some are kerosene-type distillates, and some, such as TIKI Clean Burn, are methyl esters, a biodiesel-type fuel (its sheet gives 90 to 100 percent coconut fatty acid methyl esters, flash point above 93.9 C). No torch fuel is on any fire authority's list. If you use one anyway, read its sheet, and leave out anything with scent or additives |
| **Paint thinners** (VM&P naphtha, mineral spirits, white spirit), **charcoal lighter fluid, acetone, jet fuel** | None is on any fire department or festival list found. Their contents vary widely between brands, and nobody publishes how they behave on a wick. Some "odourless mineral spirits" are isoparaffins, but ordinary mineral spirits are a different, more aromatic solvent (NIOSH gives Stoddard solvent a flash point of 39 to 43 C). Only treat a thinner as an isoparaffin if its sheet says so; otherwise stay with fuels on the lists |
| **Anything you cannot positively identify**, including the unlabelled jug in the garage | You do not know its flash point, so you do not know how it behaves |

## Storage and handling

**Containers.** The original container with its cap on, or a metal can
made for fuel. Never glass (Portland bans it), and never a drink bottle
or food container. Label everything and keep the sheet with it.

**Children, and swallowed fuel.** Store fuel locked away, somewhere cool
and ventilated, not in the house or a hot car. Poison Control warns that
to a child, coloured lamp oil looks like a drink and clear lamp oil looks
like water. Every petroleum fuel here carries a warning that swallowing
it can let it into the lungs, which can be fatal: NIOSH lists chemical
pneumonitis for kerosene, and ATSDR's case reports on kerosene
aspiration include deaths. Methanol-containing alcohol is poisonous even
in a mouthful, and symptoms can be delayed by hours. **If anyone
swallows fuel, do not make them vomit** (the Coleman, Hess and Firefly
sheets all say so). Call Poison Control (in the US, 1-800-222-1222),
your local poison centre, or emergency services straight away. Children
can enjoy LED props; adults handle the fuel.

**Amounts.** Bring only what you will use. Portland's limit on site is 1
gallon.

**Heat and sun.** Keep fuel shaded and away from heat and flame.

**Distances.** Sources give 10 to 30 ft, and more is better. Burning
Man's storage rules keep stored fuel at least 10 ft from anything
combustible and any ignition source, and open flames at least 25 ft
away while filling or pouring. They count shade structures and tents as
combustibles, and allow shade to be built over propane storage but not
over liquid fuel storage. That rule keeps combustible structures away
from the fuel; it is not a reason to leave the can in the sun. Pick a
shaded spot for the fuel rather than building a tarp or canopy over it.
NAFAA keeps the audience and smoking 30 ft from the fuel station.

**The fuel depot.** Before anything is lit, have a fire extinguisher
rated for flammable liquids at the depot (NAFAA says at least class B,
ABC preferred), a damp cotton towel or a dry duvetyne safety cloth (its
flame-retardant treatment washes out, so it is kept dry and never
washed), and a sober person who
watches the depot until the fuel is packed away. Put the depot outside
and behind a hard wall where you can; if there is no wall, a spotter
stands between fuel and fire (NAFAA). One Canadian guideline describes a
short barrel with a heavy hinged lid for fuelling, so a fire inside can
be smothered by slamming it shut. **Never throw water on burning fuel.**
The sheets warn that a stream of water can spread a fuel fire and may
not put it out: Klean-Strip's denatured alcohol sheet says so directly,
Coleman's label says to use something other than water (its
firefighting section does allow water fog, which is not a bucket), and
CAMEO warns that water may be ineffective on naphtha and inefficient on
kerosene. Use an extinguisher, a towel or a lid. Keep a bucket of clean
water for cooling burns (Portland) and, where a permit asks for one, for
putting out a prop (Seattle), never for throwing on burning fuel.

**The dip can.** A small metal can with a tight or self-closing lid,
sitting in a drip pan. Seal every fuel container before anything is lit,
and never light up at the dip station.

**Dipping.** One to two seconds is plenty (**community figure**). Keep
grips and handles out of the fuel.

**Spin-off.** Into a spin-off can, bucket or zip-top bag, away from foot
traffic, people and flames. Return reclaimed fuel to its can. **Never
dump fuel on the ground.**

**Hot props.** Never dip a prop right after it goes out. Wait several
minutes, longer for metal-heavy heads and staff ends (**community
figure**). Make sure the wick is really out (alcohol flames can be
nearly invisible in daylight) and the metal near the wick is cool enough
to touch. Here is why this applies to every fuel, not just the volatile
ones. Hot metal heats the fuel it touches past its flash point, so even
kerosene or lamp oil starts giving off ignitable vapour right at the dip
can (our reading of the flash points above; nobody has published a
measurement of a staff end under a burning wick). And a wick that looks
out can still be smouldering inside (**community figure**). Camp fuel is
still the worst case, because its vapour is always sitting over the can.

The sheets also give an autoignition temperature: the lowest
temperature at which the fuel lights in hot air inside a heated test
flask, with no flame. For the heavier fuels (kerosene, lamp oil, torch
fuel) the sheets put it at roughly 200 to 230 C: 210 C for Hess
kerosene (CAMEO gives 229 C), 216 C on the 2002 Ultra-Pure sheet (the
2007 sheet says only above 200 C), and above 220 C for TIKI citronella
torch fuel. The heavier fuels have lower autoignition temperatures than
you might expect. The lighter fuels have higher ones, 260 C on the
Ronsonol sheet and 280 C on the Diggers Shellite sheet (Zippo's sheet
gives only a lower bound, above 200 C), but their vapour is already
ignitable from a spark, which is worse. A hot surface in open air
usually has to be well above this figure before it lights anything, so
read these numbers as a floor, not a trigger. NIOSH researchers note
that a liquid fuel's real hot-surface ignition temperature is higher
than its flask figure, and a 1991 American Petroleum Institute
publication, as summarised by Shell and UK Health and Safety Executive
researchers, concluded that a hot surface in open air should not be
assumed to light a hydrocarbon unless it is about 200 C above that
figure.

**Static.** Most of the sheets here, from camp fuel to paraffin lamp oil,
call for precautions against static discharge. Touch bare metal before
handling fuel, and keep phones away from the dip area (**community
figure**). When refilling the dip can, set
both containers on the ground and keep the spout touching the can you
are filling. NIOSH's warning on filling fuel cans is written about
gasoline at the pump, but the mechanism is the same: a container sitting
on a plastic bed liner or carpet cannot shed its charge, so **never fill
a container while it sits in or on a car or truck, tailgate included.**

**Spills of camp fuel or alcohol.** Stop dipping and call it out. Anyone
with a lit prop puts it out or moves well away, because the vapour creeps
along the ground towards flames that are already burning. Keep people
out, stay upwind and out of low spots, and let it clear before anything
is lit again. Soak up anything bigger than a splash with sand or cat
litter.

**Spills of kerosene or lamp oil.** Soak it up with sand, cat litter or
rags. The puddle stays slippery and stays flammable, and fuel soaked into
the ground or a rag burns like a wick. Treat fuel rags like soaked wicks:
keep them in a lidded metal can away from the fire (Portland), and mop up
any residue between performers (NAFAA).

**Skin and clothes.** Wear nitrile or neoprene gloves and eye protection
when pouring. If fuel gets on your clothes, even a splash, change before
you light up: kerosene and lamp oil barely evaporate, so the splash
stays wet and, we infer, stays flammable long after camp fuel would be
gone (no test of this was found). Wash fuel off hands and arms with
soap and water first. Wear snug natural fibres or fire-resistant
clothing, never synthetics; [Clothing for Fire Performance](fire_performance_clothing.md)
covers what to wear and why.

**After the burn.** Let wicks cool completely, and let the leftover fuel
evaporate, before covering or bagging them: one prop maker warns that
covers and bags soak up the fumes and become more flammable
(**community figure**). Store wicks dry. Keep fuel off grips: silicone
swells, softens and loses strength while petroleum fuel is in it, then
returns to its former state once the fuel has gone (Shin-Etsu, a
silicone maker), so the harm is in the soak. Other grip rubbers and tape
adhesives are covered in [Building a Fire Staff](fire_staff_materials.md).

## Fire breathing and fire eating

Fire breathing and fire eating are separate disciplines, with stricter
rules and their own fuel requirements, and this guide does not cover
them. Fuel breathed into the lungs causes a pneumonitis serious enough
to have its own name in the medical literature, "fire-eater's
pneumonia". Learn those
disciplines only in person from an experienced teacher.

## You own this when

- You can say what is in your fuel can, by name and by flash point,
  because you read its sheet.
- You know whether your fuel gives off ignitable vapour at the
  temperature you spin in, and you handle the can accordingly.
- You know that "naphtha" and "lamp oil" are families, not products, and
  you would not swap in paint thinner or an unlabelled jug.
- You can name the fuels that are never used and give the reason for
  each.
- Your depot has a sealed metal can in a drip pan, a flammable-liquid
  extinguisher, a damp towel or a dry duvetyne cloth, and one sober person minding
  it, with open flames well away.
- You never dip a prop that has just gone out, and you never fill a can
  that is sitting in a vehicle.
- You know that swallowed fuel means calling Poison Control, not making
  someone vomit.
- You have read your own city's or event's fire performance conditions,
  because two cities can disagree about the very fuel in your can.

When all eight are true, you understand the liquid half of fire
performance. The skill transfers well beyond spinning: reading a safety
sheet, knowing that vapour travels, and treating any fuel you cannot
identify as unknown are the same habits that keep a garage, a workshop
or a camp kitchen from catching fire.
