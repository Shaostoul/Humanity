# Clothing for Fire Performance

What you wear while spinning fire decides how bad a mistake gets. This
guide covers which fabrics to choose and why, the plastic that hides in
clothing labelled as natural, fuel on clothing, hair, flame-retardant
sprays, how to test an unlabelled fabric safely, what to do if clothing
catches, and the smoke that burning materials give off.

The hard boundary first: **no fabric is fireproof, and no clothing
choice replaces a trained, sober safety spotter standing by with a fire
blanket, or a blanket big enough to wrap a person.** A small wet towel
is for putting out a prop, not a person. Learn fire spinning in person
from experienced performers, rehearse lit only with a spotter watching,
and treat everything below as the thing that buys you a few seconds, not
the thing that keeps you safe. A costume that has never been rehearsed
lit has never been tested.

Every number here traces to something in the Sources list at the
bottom. Much of what circulates about fire costumes comes from retailers
and performers rather than from anyone who measured it. Where a figure
comes only from that world, it is labelled **community figure** in the
text; treat it as what experienced people report, not as a tested
value.

## The one fact behind every choice: melting, not catching

People expect the difference between good and bad fabrics to be how
easily they catch fire. It mostly is not.

Health Canada's guide to textile flammability sorts fibres into three
broad groups. Cotton, rayon, ramie, acetate and triacetate catch easily
and burn fast. Acrylic, nylon, polyester, olefin and silk are harder to
light, but the synthetics among them melt and drip. Wool and
modacrylic tend not to keep burning once the flame is taken away.

So plain cotton actually catches **faster** than polyester. The reason
every fire-performance rule still bans polyester is what happens next.
A plastic fibre melts into a hot liquid that sticks to skin, keeps
delivering heat to the same spot, and leaves plastic in the wound. A
natural fibre does not melt: it chars and leaves ash rather than molten
plastic in the wound. A 2008 USDA Agricultural Research Service article
put the contrast simply: the ash from burning wool is soft and will not
lodge in an open wound, while synthetics can bead and drip into one.

But a burning natural-fibre garment does not fall away from you. It
keeps burning against you until someone smothers it. Health Canada puts
cotton among the fibres that light easily and burn fast, and in the
informal performer test described below, a tightly woven cotton T-shirt
that finally caught was hard to put out and just kept going
(**community figure**). That is why the spotter matters more than the
fabric.

The evidence that plastic fabric melts onto skin is not only from the
fire arts:

- **US Forest Service flame tests** on undergarments, each tested
  underneath a layer of the flame-resistant aramid fabric wildland
  firefighters wear, found that the 100 percent cotton and 100 percent
  wool undergarments did not ignite, melt or char, while large parts of
  the polypropylene shirt and a 79 percent polyester, 21 percent
  elastane athletic shirt melted. The aramid layer took the flame, so
  this shows which fabrics melt even under protection, not that bare
  cotton resists flame. The authors noted that melting material can
  stick to the skin and make burns worse. Wildland firefighters are allowed only 100 percent natural
  fibre (cotton, wool or silk) or flame-resistant material such as
  aramid next to the skin.
- **OSHA's rule for electrical workers exposed to flame** forbids
  clothing that could melt onto the skin or ignite and keep burning, and
  names acetate, nylon, polyester, rayon and polypropylene, alone or in
  blends, as covered unless treated or worn so the hazard is removed.
- **The US Marine Corps** barred polyester and nylon athletic clothing
  off base in Iraq in April 2006. A Navy surgeon described a Marine
  whose polyester shirt melted onto him in a roadside bomb blast,
  leaving burns over 70 percent of his body.

The rule that follows is not "natural fibres do not burn". It is:
**choose a fabric that will not melt onto you, then choose a heavy,
smooth, tightly woven version of it, then fit it close to the body.**
Fibre, weight and fit together decide how many seconds you have.

## What the fire codes say

Two US city fire departments that issue fire-performance permits spell
out clothing, and they are the clearest official statements found.

- **Portland Fire and Rescue** (policy revised August 2018): clothing of
  performers **and** spotters should fit snugly and be made of
  fire-resistant material or natural fibre, with wool, leather and
  cotton given as examples. Synthetic clothing may not be worn.
- **Seattle Fire Department** (permit conditions revised March 2025):
  the same wording for performers and spotters, and the same ban on
  synthetic clothing.
- **The Utah State Fire Marshal's** performer safety compilation lists
  heavy denim, leather and tightly woven natural materials such as
  cotton and wool as reliable, and says to avoid pleather, nylon,
  polyester and other "poly" fabrics, lightweight, lace and loose-weave
  fabrics, stretchy or fuzzy fabrics, feathers and fur. It also says to
  tie shoes tight or cover them with trouser legs so falling embers
  cannot get in.

Behind the city rules sits NFPA 160, the national standard for flame
effects before an audience. Portland lists it among the references for
its policy, Seattle's clothing condition cites an NFPA section and the
same permit names NFPA 160 elsewhere, and the North American Fire Arts
Association (NAFAA) describes its own performer guidelines as
supplementing it.
The standard is paywalled, so this guide quotes the city rules rather
than the standard itself.

None of these sets a percentage. You will see "at least 95 percent
natural" quoted online as a rule; it comes from performer blogs, not
from any code or standard found for this guide. It is a **community
figure**. The conservative rule, and the one the codes actually state,
is 100 percent natural fibre or genuine flame-resistant material.

## Fabric by fabric

The ignition times in this section come from one informal test by a
performer, first published by a poi retailer and reprinted in the Utah
State Fire Marshal's compilation. One tester, a hand count, no
controlled method: every time in it is a **community figure**, useful
only for comparing fabrics with each other.

| Fabric | Melts? | How it behaves | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Wool, heavy and tight** | No | Hardest natural fibre to light; chars; tends to go out by itself | The best natural choice |
| **Heavy cotton: denim, canvas, duck, twill** | No | Catches eventually, burns to ash, can keep glowing | The everyday choice |
| **Real leather and suede** | No | Chars and curls rather than burning through | Excellent outer layer |
| **Linen and hemp** | No | Burn like cotton; light linen goes up very fast | Heavy, tight weaves only |
| **Silk** | No | Harder to light than cotton, but thin silk still catches in seconds | Heavy raw silk only, or skip |
| **Rayon, viscose, modal, lyocell, "bamboo"** | No | Burn fast, like cotton, and are usually thin and drapey | Skip |
| **Acetate and triacetate** | **Yes** | Catch fast like cotton **and** melt | Never |
| **Polyester, nylon, acrylic, spandex and other plastics** | **Yes** | Often slower to light, then melt and stick | Never |

### Wool

The champion among ordinary fabrics. The wool industry's own fact sheet,
citing Australia's national science agency CSIRO, gives wool a limiting
oxygen index of 25.2 against 18.4 for cotton. That index is the share of
oxygen in the surrounding air a fibre needs to keep burning, and ordinary
air is about 21 percent oxygen, so wool sits above the line and cotton
below it. The same sheet puts wool's ignition temperature at 570 to 600
C, against 255 C for cotton, and says wool does not melt or drip. Health
Canada independently groups wool with the fibres that tend not to keep
burning once the flame is removed. In the informal performer test,
blanket-weight wool did better than denim (**community figure**).

Treat "goes out by itself" as a tendency, not a promise. Thin, brushed
or fuzzy wool, and any wool with fuel on it, can keep burning.

**Downsides:** warm, which matters in summer; shrinks and felts if washed
hot or agitated; moths eat it; often costs more. And garments sold as
"merino" are often blended with nylon, polyester or elastane (a
**community figure** from a wool retailer). Read the
label and look for 100 percent wool.

### Heavy cotton, and smooth against fuzzy

Cotton is cheap, breathable and easy to sew, which is why it is what
most performers actually wear. Plain cotton does catch easily. What
changes the picture is weight and surface. Health Canada notes that a
lightweight fabric is more flammable than a heavier one of the same
fibre, and that the looser the weave, the faster flame travels over it.
In the informal test, lightweight cotton gauze and a cheap T-shirt went
up in about 3 seconds, while unfrayed denim and heavy tightly woven
cotton of the khaki kind held for the full 10 seconds (**community
figures**).

Two warnings that apply to all cotton:

- **It can smoulder after the flame is out.** The sewing-industry burn
  guide notes that plant fibres sometimes glow before they finally go
  out. A patted-out sleeve may still be burning inside. Make sure it is
  actually out.
- **Fuzzy surfaces flash.** Health Canada singles out raised-fibre
  surfaces such as fleece, flannelette and terry towelling: air
  circulates around the loose fibres and flame can sweep across the
  whole surface in a moment. The same is true of frayed edges and loose
  threads. Pile fabrics such as corduroy scorch on the raised surface
  first; in the informal test, cotton corduroy held for the full 10
  seconds, about as well as denim, once the flame got past those raised
  fibres (**community figure**). Choose smooth.

**Read the label on work trousers and jeans.** Work trousers, "khakis"
and canvas garments are sold in polyester and cotton blends as well as
pure cotton, and stretch jeans contain elastane: Levi's describes its stretch denim as 1 to 3
percent elastane.

### Leather and suede

Real leather does not melt. In the informal test it curled into a
charred roll at the edge after about 20 seconds without the centre
catching (**community figure**). OSHA accepts leather as protective
clothing for welding and cutting work, but **only when it is free of
oil, grease or solvents.** For a fire performer that means fuel. A
fuel-splashed leather jacket is not protective, and the Coleman
kerosene safety data sheet goes as far as saying contaminated leather
gloves and shoes should be laundered or thrown away.

**Downsides:** hot, heavy and expensive. And be sure it is real: faux
leather is plastic (see below), and "bonded leather" is ground leather
fibre mixed with non-leather binders. The FTC's leather guides, which
cover bags, shoes and belts sold on their own but not clothing, say
bonded leather may not be sold simply as leather and must state its
share of leather fibre. For garments there is no such guide, so check
for yourself that it is real leather.

### Linen and hemp

The same plant-cellulose family as cotton, and they burn the same way:
the sewing-industry burn guide groups cotton, linen, hemp and rayon
together as fibres that burn to a soft grey ash without melting. They
are cool, strong and breathable. But light, airy linen is one of the
fastest fabrics to go up: flax lasted about 3 seconds in the informal
test (**community figure**). Use heavy, tight weaves only.

### Silk

Silk does not melt, and the burn guide describes it as burning slowly
and tending to put itself out. Health Canada, though, puts it in the
moderately flammable group, and in the informal test light, shiny silk
caught in about 5 seconds while heavy raw silk held for about 8
(**community figures**). Thin silk is not a fire fabric. Heavy raw silk
is acceptable; it is also expensive.

### What to skip: rayon, viscose, modal, lyocell and "bamboo"

These are made by dissolving plant pulp and spinning it back into fibre.
US law defines rayon as regenerated cellulose, and the FTC says most
fabric sold as "bamboo" is really rayon and must be labelled as such.
They do not melt, but they burn fast: the burn guide says rayon burns
rapidly, and Health Canada puts it in the fast-burning group with
cotton. They are usually thin and drapey, and often blended with
spandex or polyester. There is no reason to choose them over heavy
cotton.

**The exception that proves "plant-based" means nothing: acetate and
triacetate.** They also start as wood pulp, but the burn guide lists
acetate among the fibres that melt and burn. They catch like cotton and
melt like plastic, the worst of both. They are common in costume satins
and linings.

**A quick check for any fabric:** if you can see light through it, it
is too thin for fire. This is a **community figure**, repeated widely by
performers and teachers, and it matches the weight rule above.

## Fit

Every source agrees on fit, from the city codes to performer guides.

- **Snug, close to the body.** Loose fabric has air all round it and can
  swing into the flame or tangle with the prop.
- **Nothing dangling:** no loose or bell sleeves, floppy collars, wide
  necklines, scarves, fringe, tassels, tulle or frayed edges.
- **Nothing fuzzy** on the surface, for the flash reason above.
- **Shoes tied tight or covered** by trouser legs so embers cannot drop
  in.

The Utah compilation allows loose or fuzzy pieces for effect only if the
performer has rehearsed lit in them first. That is the general rule for
any costume: the North American Fire Arts Association's performer
guidelines (Revision 3.0) say to check each part of the costume for
flammability and to practise lit in the actual costume several times
before performing in it.

## Fuel on clothing

**Fuel on clothing burns, whatever the fabric.** Wool that would put
itself out, leather that would only curl, even aramid: soaked in fuel,
any of them is a wick.

- **Fuel up and spin off well away from people and anything lit.**
  Portland requires a spin-out bucket to take excess fuel off before
  lighting.
- **Watch the spray.** Spinning off can mist fuel onto your own clothes
  and skin, and onto anyone near you.
- **If fuel splashes on you, change before going near flame.** The
  Coleman kerosene safety data sheet says to take off fuel-soaked
  clothing and launder it before wearing it again.
- **Keep costume and fuel apart in storage and transport.**

## Hair, face and the small things

**Hair** catches fast and sits right next to your face. Tie up long
hair, and cover it with a snug cap of the same natural fabric as the
costume. The Utah compilation says wetting hair is common, but hair that
is long, loose or sprayed must be covered, with no tassels or long ties,
in something you can pull off fast. **No hairspray, gel or oily
products** on performance days.

**Wigs, braids and extensions** are almost always synthetic. Kaneka's
Kanekalon braiding fibre, for example, is a modacrylic, and US labelling
law does not require wigs to carry fibre content at all, so you often
cannot know what a packet holds. "Heat-friendly" on a wig means it
tolerates a styling iron, not a flame. Loose, fine hair near fire is a
bad idea whatever the packet says (see the modacrylic note below).

**Costume glue:** spirit gum is shellac dissolved in alcohol, about 36
to 66 percent ethanol by Mehron's safety data sheet for its spirit gum.
The same sheet gives a flash point of 54 F (12 C), below room
temperature, which means it gives off ignitable vapour while wet. Let
any adhesive dry fully, well before lighting up.

## Hidden plastic in "natural" clothing

A "100% cotton" label is a statement about the main fabric, not about
everything sewn into the garment. The FTC's labelling rules under the
Textile Fiber Products Identification Act explain what is left out.

| Where plastic hides | What the rule allows |
|---|---|
| **Small fibre amounts** | Fibres under 5 percent of the weight may be listed only as "other fiber" (wool must always be named) |
| **Trim** | Collars, cuffs, waistbands, wristbands, binding, tape, braiding, gussets and similar trim need no fibre disclosure at all, with no size limit and no phrase on the label. Rib-knit cuffs and collars on a "100% cotton" top can be any fibre |
| **Decoration** | Decorative trim or designs making up no more than 15 percent of the surface may be left out with "exclusive of decoration" |
| **Ornamentation** | Ornamentation making up no more than 5 percent of the fibre weight may be left out with "exclusive of ornamentation" |
| **Elastic** | Elastic covering no more than 20 percent of the surface may be left out with "exclusive of elastic" |
| **Sewing thread** | The thread holding the garment together does not have to be disclosed |
| **Structural linings** | A lining that is only structural does not have to be disclosed (one added for warmth does) |
| **Non-fibre parts** | Zips, buttons, beads, sequins, leather patches and painted designs are outside the rule entirely |
| **Hats, wigs, secondhand** | Headwear, wigs and obviously secondhand items are not covered |

What that means in practice:

- **Thread.** The label never tells you what the seams are sewn with,
  and thread that looks like cotton may be mostly plastic. The thread
  maker American and Efird describes its corespun threads as a
  continuous polyester core, about 60 percent of the thread, wrapped in
  either cotton or polyester fibre, and recommends them for work as
  heavy as denim. The thread maker Coats says nylon and polyester seams
  start to weaken around 350 F, and recommends aramid thread for
  anything near fire. Coats also advises against cotton thread near
  fire: it does not melt the way polyester does, but it burns. If
  aramid thread is not available, cotton is the lesser evil only
  because it will not melt onto skin. Read the spool either way.
- **Elastic** is a stretchy core of rubber or spandex, and the yarn
  covering it can be polyester: Dritz, a sewing-notions maker, lists its
  braided elastic as 55 percent polyester and 45 percent rubber.
  Spandex itself is a polyurethane under US law. Either way elastic
  carries meltable plastic, so keep it minimal and covered.
- **Zips.** Plastic teeth melt, and a metal zip usually still runs on
  polyester tape. Even YKK's organic-cotton-tape metal zip lists its tape
  as 9 percent polyester plus a corespun yarn. Buttons, lacing or
  hooks of metal, horn, wood, bone or shell are safer closures.
- **Hook-and-loop (Velcro)** is nylon or polyester. An industrial
  insulation maker gives both a temperature tolerance of about 280 F
  (138 C). Avoid it.
- **Prints and patches.** Rubbery screen prints are usually plastisol
  ink, which a commercial printer describes as a PVC-based ink that
  remelts when heated. Heat-transfer vinyl is PU or PVC film on a
  melt adhesive (a **community figure** from a vinyl seller). Plain
  fabric, or a print that soaks into the fibres, is the safe choice.
- **Sequins, glitter and metallic trim** are plastic film (see the list
  below).
- **The same T-shirt can change fibre with its colour.** As a snapshot
  checked on Sept 24, 2026, Gildan lists its "Heavy Cotton" T-shirt as
  100 percent cotton in solid colours, 90 percent cotton and 10 percent
  polyester in Sport Grey, 99 percent cotton and 1 percent polyester in
  Ash Grey, and 50 percent cotton and 50 percent polyester in its
  heather colours and its "safety" colours (Safety Green, Safety Orange,
  Safety Pink). "Safety" in a colour name says nothing about fire.
  Check the exact colour you are buying, every time.

A note on the federal clothing flammability standard, 16 CFR 1610: it
measures how fast flame spreads across a fabric, and it exempts from
testing plain fabrics of 2.6 ounces per square yard or more and fabrics
made entirely of acrylic, modacrylic, nylon, olefin, polyester or wool.
Nothing in its test grades melting. A garment that is perfectly legal to
sell can still melt onto you.

## Never wear plastic fabric while spinning fire

This is the one rule with no exceptions for ordinary clothing. Plastic
fabric melts, sticks, keeps burning you, and leaves deeper burns with
plastic in them. Some of it (polyester, nylon) is slower to light than
cotton; acrylic, by contrast, is listed in the burn guide as melting and
burning rapidly. **Blends are not a compromise.** Health Canada describes
a scaffolding effect in polyester and cotton blends: the charred cotton
holds the melting polyester in the flame, so some blends burn rapidly
and give off more heat than 100 percent cotton. The Forest Service's
50/50 test garments did not melt when worn under an aramid outer layer,
but the authors said their tests were not thorough enough to recommend
blends, and the firefighting standard still calls for 100 percent
natural fibre, aramid or other flame-resistant material.

Under the FTC rule a US fibre label must give the generic fibre name,
and a trade name such as Lycra may appear only beside it in lettering of
the same size. So the label is the only thing that settles it.
Marketing words are not fibre names. Plastic fabric is sold under many
names:

| Kind | Names you will see |
|---|---|
| **Generic plastic fibres** | Polyester, PET, "recycled polyester", nylon, polyamide, acrylic, modacrylic, polypropylene, olefin, polyurethane (PU), PVC, vinyl, vinyon, saran, and acetate and triacetate |
| **Polyester brands** | Dacron, Terylene, Fortrel, Trevira, Kodel, Polartec, Polarguard, Capilene, Thermolite, Coolmax |
| **Nylon brands** | Supplex, Tactel, Cordura (mostly nylon; the brand also covers blends), Taslan (a texture, usually on nylon, sometimes polyester) |
| **Acrylic and modacrylic** | Orlon, Acrilan, Creslan, Dynel, Kanekalon, "faux wool" |
| **Stretch** | Spandex, elastane, Lycra, "scuba" knit |
| **Fuzzy and plush** | Most fleece and microfibre, minky, faux fur, faux suede such as Ultrasuede |
| **Fake leather** | Pleather, PU leather, leatherette, Naugahyde, "vegan leather", including plant-based leathers, which are usually bound or coated with plastic |
| **Shiny and sparkly** | Lamé, Lurex, metallic fabric, Mylar, sequins, glitter |
| **Sporty words** | Performance, athletic, technical, moisture-wicking, quick-dry, Dri-FIT |
| **Outerwear** | Gore-Tex and other waterproof shells, softshells, windbreakers, rain jackets, puffers (a down puffer still has a nylon or polyester shell) |
| **Blends** | Poly-cotton, CVC, tri-blend, "cotton-rich" (only means some cotton), heather colours |
| **Hair** | Synthetic wigs, braids and extensions |

Where these come from: the generic fibre definitions are the FTC's. The
brand-to-fibre matches are from US Customs and Border Protection's list
of fibre trade names, which itself warns it is not complete and dates
from 2006, and which lists some brands under more than one fibre, so a
brand name alone is never proof. Coolmax is described by its maker as a
polyester fibre, Ultrasuede by its maker as polyester microfibre bound
with polyurethane, and Naugahyde by its maker as vinyl-coated fabric.
Nike's own help page describes Dri-FIT as a polycotton fabric, which
still means polyester. Sequins moved from gelatin to acetate, then to
Mylar polyester film, and then to vinyl, as the Smithsonian's history of
them records. The "scuba", minky and Taslan entries rest on retailer and
maker listings and are **community figures**.

**Take jackets off before anyone lights up, spotters included.** Almost
every modern jacket, waterproof or insulated, has a plastic shell.

**Weave names are not fibres.** Satin, velvet, velour, chiffon, organza,
tulle, taffeta, jersey, crepe, georgette, charmeuse, mesh and ripstop
describe how a fabric is made, not what it is made of. Any of them can
be silk or cotton; in a shop, they are often polyester or nylon. Read
the fibre label.

## The exception: aramid and certified flame-resistant workwear

"Never wear synthetics" is right for everything above, but not literally
for every synthetic. **Aramids** such as Nomex and Kevlar (the same
fibre family as your wicks) are made not to melt. DuPont's Nomex guide
says the fibre does not melt and drip but chars, gives it a limiting
oxygen index of about 28, and says its flame resistance is built into
the fibre and does not diminish over its life. The US Forest Service
allows aramid undergarments alongside cotton, wool and silk.

**Certified flame-resistant workwear** is the other exception. NFPA 2112
is the standard for flame-resistant clothing that protects industrial
workers from flash fire. As an FR clothing maker explains it (the
standard itself is paywalled), certified fabric must survive five
minutes in a 500 F oven without igniting, melting, dripping or
separating, and shrink no more than 10 percent, on top of flame and
heat-transfer tests.

Two cautions:

- **"Flame retardant" or "FR" on a tag is not the same thing.** Health
  Canada notes that synthetics often melt and drip even when they put
  themselves out. A fabric that stops burning can still melt onto you.
  Look for aramid in the fibre content or a named certification such as
  NFPA 2112, not a loose "FR" claim.
- **Fuel soaked into any fabric still burns,** Nomex included.

**Modacrylic is the awkward case.** It is a synthetic and sits on the
never-wear list above, yet it resists melting. Kaneka says its
Kanekalon modacrylic puts itself out once the flame is removed and
resists melting and dripping, Health Canada groups modacrylic with wool
among the fibres that tend not to keep burning, and flame-resistant
modacrylic blends are made into commercial protective work garments
(the flash-fire study by Mackay and colleagues tested several). None of
that makes an ordinary modacrylic costume piece, such as a wig, braids
or faux fur, into fire wear: loose, raised fibres are exactly the
surface that flashes, a maker's claim is not a certification, and
burning modacrylic gives off hydrogen chloride as well as cyanide (see
the smoke table). Wear it only as part of a garment certified to a named
standard such as NFPA 2112.

## Flame-retardant sprays

A flame-retardant spray on a natural fabric can make it slower to
catch. That is all it does.

- **It does not stop plastic melting.** Never use it to make a synthetic
  costume "safe".
- **It wears out.** The Utah compilation says all retardants lose
  effectiveness with time and need reapplying. Washing, sweat and rain
  take it out; treat any washed or soaked garment as untreated.
- **It does not make the smoke safe.** In one 1979 animal study a
  retardant treatment doubled the toxicity of cotton smoke (it slightly
  reduced acrylic's).

The Utah compilation's own check for treated fabric is a short vertical
test with a lighter: anything that keeps burning gets re-treated or
replaced. Treat a spray as a backup on natural fabric, never a fix.

## The burn test, done safely

For fabric with no label, a burn test tells you whether it melts. It
does not tell you the exact fibre, and the sewing-industry guide it
comes from warns that it cannot reliably identify blends. Read labels
where they exist.

**Setting up:**

- **Outdoors, on a calm day,** or at least with windows open, and keep
  your face out of the smoke (stand to the side or upwind).
- **Pre-wash** the fabric so finishes do not confuse the result.
- **A small swatch,** about 2 inches square.
- **Long tweezers,** and keep the metal tips out of the flame; hot metal
  burns.
- **A non-flammable dish underneath:** a glass dish or metal baking pan.
- **Water within reach,** long hair tied back, and a small flame such as
  a lighter or unscented candle.

**Doing it:** ease one edge of the swatch into the flame and straight
out again. Blow it out if it keeps burning. Let the residue cool before
you touch it; melted beads stay hot.

| Result | Smell | Residue | Fibre |
|---|---|---|---|
| Burns steadily, may glow before going out | Burning paper or leaves | Soft grey ash | Cotton, linen, hemp, rayon |
| Burns slowly, curls away, tends to go out | Burning hair | Dark bead that crushes to powder | Wool, silk |
| Melts, shrinks away, may drip | Chemical (sweet, celery, vinegar, fishy, rubbery) | Hard bead that will not crush; spandex may leave black ash | Polyester, nylon, acrylic, acetate, spandex |

If it melts, it is out. If it leaves a hard bead, it is out.

**No visible melting does NOT prove the fabric is pure natural fibre.**
The sewing-industry guide warns that the burn test cannot identify
blends with certainty, so a blend can pass for natural fibre. An
unlabelled
fabric that passes is still unverified, so use it only if it is heavy,
tight and smooth, and prefer fabric labelled 100 percent natural fibre.

## If your clothes catch

The spotter, not the fabric, is your main protection. You may not know
your back is alight; they will. The steps below are in order.

1. **Put lit props down away from you, then stop, drop, cover your face
   with your hands, and roll.** The spotter tells you once a blanket is
   ready, and you stop rolling so they can smother the flames, as the
   Utah compilation's procedure puts it. The spotter covers you so the
   flames are pushed away from your face and airway, not up into them
   (the Home of Poi text reprinted in the same compilation). MedlinePlus
   gives the same core advice for anyone whose clothes are alight:
   smother the flames under something heavy, such as a wool or cotton
   blanket, coat or rug. Once the fire is out, open the blanket, because
   it traps heat against the body (Utah compilation).
2. **Call emergency services straight away** if a burn from burning
   clothing is about the size of the person's palm or larger or looks
   deep, if they breathed in smoke, and whenever you are not sure how
   serious it is. MedlinePlus's call-911 list includes palm-sized or
   larger burns, deep (full-thickness) burns, breathing in smoke and not
   being sure, and for a major burn it puts the call among the first
   steps, not after first aid is finished. It also counts second-degree
   burns on the face, hands, feet, groin, buttocks or over a major joint
   as major burns that need urgent medical care, so get help for those
   too. A clothing fire can easily cause a burn that size, so be ready
   to call.
3. **Take off what is hot, leave what is stuck.** As soon as you can,
   remove hot or smouldering clothing that is not stuck to the skin
   (remember that cotton can keep smouldering after the flame is out),
   and any rings, watches or other tight items near the burn, before
   swelling makes that impossible (ANZCOR's burns guideline; MedlinePlus
   says to make sure the person is no longer touching anything burning
   or smoking). **Do not pull off anything stuck to the skin.** Melted
   fabric stays where it is for a medical team to deal with.
4. **Cool the burn with cool running water, not cold water and never
   ice, for 20 minutes,** while help is on its way. ANZCOR and the
   burn-care guidelines summarised by CADTH both call for at least 20
   minutes and warn against ice or ice water. Run the water over the
   burn; do not put the person in a cold bath. MedlinePlus's "5 to 30
   minutes" is its advice for minor burns, and for a severe burn it
   warns that putting it in cold water can cause shock. **Keep the rest
   of the body covered and warm while you cool.** ANZCOR gives this to
   reduce the risk of hypothermia, which small children can develop
   quickly, and the more of the body you are cooling, the more it
   matters.
5. **Nothing on the burn:** no butter, oil, ice, creams or household
   remedies. After cooling, cover it loosely with a clean, dry dressing
   or cloth (MedlinePlus).
6. **Smaller burns still get checked** if they are deep or on the face,
   hands, feet, genitals or a joint. The American Burn Association's
   referral criteria cover deep burns to the face, hands, feet,
   genitals, perineum or over any joint.

**Smoke or hot gas near the face** needs its own attention. If clothing
or hair burned near someone's face, get them into fresh air and treat it
as a possible airway injury. The American Burn Association counts burns
on the face, singed facial hair and smoke exposure as signs of possible
inhalation injury that warrant a burn-centre consultation, and
MedlinePlus lists breathing in smoke as a reason to call emergency
services. MedlinePlus's page on inhalation injuries lists coughing, a
scratchy throat, shortness of breath, chest pain or tightness, headache
and stinging eyes among the usual symptoms.

## Smoke and fumes

The smoke from burning materials can be toxic, and not only from
plastics.

**All smoke is harmful.** NIST's chapter on combustion products starts
from the point that every combustible material makes smoke that is
hazardous in high enough concentration, and that carbon monoxide comes
from both smouldering and flaming. You cannot see or smell carbon
monoxide.

**Natural fibres are not clean.** Smouldering cellulose (cotton, linen,
paper) produces acrolein, a strong eye and airway irritant, according to
the same chapter. ATSDR lists hydrogen cyanide as a combustion product
of nitrogen-containing materials, naming wool, silk and plastics;
hydrogen cyanide stops the body using oxygen.

**Some plastics add specific gases.**

| Material | Found in | What it releases when overheated or burnt |
|---|---|---|
| **PVC and vinyl** | Pleather, many sequins, plastisol prints, vinyl costume pieces | Hydrogen chloride, which NIST says comes off readily at about 225 to 275 C, before the material even burns; it turns to acid on the moist lining of the nose and lungs |
| **Nylon** | Fabric, zips, hook-and-loop | Hydrogen cyanide (CPSC art safety guide) |
| **Polyurethane** | PU leather, foam, spandex | CPSC's art safety guide says not to heat urethane plastics at all because of toxic release |
| **Acrylic and modacrylic** | Fabric, faux fur, synthetic wig hair | Built on acrylonitrile, which contains nitrogen, so hydrogen cyanide. In a flash-fire test of flame-resistant work garments, the modacrylic blends also gave off marked hydrogen chloride (Mackay and colleagues, 2015). One 1979 animal study found acrylic smoke about 7 times as toxic as cotton smoke |

**Keep it in proportion.** NIST stresses that smoke toxicity depends on
how much burns, whether it flames or smoulders, ventilation and
distance, not only on the material. A full-body test of
flame-resistant coveralls in a 3 to 4 second flash fire found the gases
produced were short-lived and not a significant risk to the wearer,
while heat injury to the airway was. For a brief flare-up in open air,
the melt-and-stick burn is the bigger harm. The rule is still simple:
**do not breathe any of it.**

**Fuel smoke is toxic too.** The North American Fire Arts Association's
guidelines (Revision 3.0) say petroleum fuels burning on open wicks
always make toxic fumes, worse in enclosed spaces, and that
semi-enclosed, low-wind outdoor spots can be as bad as indoors. The
Coleman kerosene data sheet lists carbon monoxide and irritating
aldehydes and ketones among its breakdown products. Spin in open air, not under roofs, awnings or in tents.

What to do:

- **Burn tests outdoors,** small snippets, face out of the smoke.
- **Put out anything smouldering and step away** from its smoke rather
  than standing over it.
- **Do not rely on a dust mask or N95.** A well-fitted N95 can filter
  smoke particles, but not the gases that matter here (carbon monoxide,
  hydrogen cyanide, hydrogen chloride); Cal/OSHA states that N95 masks
  do not protect against gases or vapours. Fresh air and distance are
  the protection.
- **Prop builders:** CPSC's art safety guide warns that heating or
  cutting plastics can release toxic breakdown products, naming
  hydrochloric acid vapour from PVC and hydrogen cyanide from nylon.
  Heat-form, hot-wire or laser-cut foam and plastic outdoors or with
  extraction pulling fumes away from your face. Never laser-cut or
  hot-wire PVC, vinyl or pleather, and do not let PVC scorch when heat
  bending it.
- **Never burn costume scraps, foam or plastic in a fire pit.** The EPA
  lists dioxins, fine particles, carbon monoxide and other pollutants
  among the products of burning trash in the open.

## Dress the spotter the same way

The codes quoted above apply the same clothing rule to spotters as to
performers, and NAFAA's guidelines say spotters should dress with the
same care as a performer. The spotter stands closest to a fire going
wrong, and a jacket with a plastic shell is as bad on them as on you.

**What the spotter holds matters as much as what they wear.** Portland's
policy requires each spotter to have at least one wet cotton towel of at
least 15 by 24 inches, or a wool or Nomex blanket or duvetyne, ready to
put out the **props**. Portland specifies the towel wet; duvetyne in
particular must stay dry and never be washed, because its
flame-retardant treatment washes out. A towel that size is hand-towel size: it is for a
wick, and it cannot smother a burning back or a pair of legs. For a
person, the spotter needs something that can wrap a body. The Home of
Poi text reprinted in the Utah compilation says fire authorities regard
fire blankets as the best way to deal with someone whose clothing is
alight, and the compilation's improvised alternative is a large wet
cloth such as a cotton sheet, a cotton tarp or a wool blanket. A large
wool or Nomex blanket does both jobs.

The codes require more than the blanket, and none of it is optional
where they apply:

- **Portland** requires at least one extinguisher rated 2A-10BC or
  better close to each spotter, and a 5-gallon open-topped bucket of
  water, at least three-quarters full, that spotters can reach to cool
  accidental burns. It also makes spotting someone's only job during the
  performance, with a second person keeping the perimeter.
- **Seattle** requires at least two extinguishers rated 2A-40BC or
  better, one each side of the stage, and a 2.5-gallon bucket of water
  for putting out the props; wet towels are allowed for that only with
  the fire department's approval.
- **Sober.** The Utah compilation's chapter on substance abuse rules out
  alcohol and drugs in fire performance and expects the people working
  alongside you, spotters included, to be clear-headed and alert.

## You own this when

- You can say why polyester, which catches more slowly than cotton, is
  still the fabric you never wear, and why "plant-based" proves nothing.
- Your performance clothes are 100 percent wool, heavy tight cotton,
  real leather, heavy raw silk or genuine aramid, smooth-faced, and
  snug, and you checked the label for the exact colour you bought.
- You know where the hidden plastic is (thread, elastic, zip tape,
  prints, sequins, hook-and-loop) and have replaced or covered it.
- You change out of anything with fuel on it before going near flame.
- Your hair is tied up and covered in natural fabric, with nothing
  sprayed or oiled in it.
- You can run a burn test outdoors on an unlabelled fabric and read the
  result.
- You know that a fabric which does not melt in a burn test can still
  be a blend, and you prefer labelled 100 percent fabric.
- You know what to do in the first minute if clothing catches: when to
  call for help, why the water is cool and not cold, what comes off and
  what stays, and why nobody peels off melted fabric.
- Your spotter is dressed by the same rules and holds a fire blanket or
  a large blanket that can wrap a person, not just a towel for the
  props, and you have rehearsed lit in this exact costume.

When all of those are true you own a costume that fails slowly instead
of badly, and you understand why. The same reasoning, reading what a
material will do when it gets hot rather than what the label promises,
applies to every piece of equipment that goes near fire.
