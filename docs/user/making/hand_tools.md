# Hand Tools: Where the Other Hand Is

Put a scrap of board flat on a table. Hold it down with your left hand.
Now take a chisel in your right, set it on a corner, and push.

One of three things happens. The board slides. Or the board tips up. Or
the chisel goes in, meets a hard bit of grain, skips sideways, and
arrives somewhere you did not choose. In all three cases the tool did
exactly what you asked. The problem was never the chisel. The problem
was that the work was free to move and your other hand was in front of
the edge.

That is the whole subject in one sentence, and it is why this guide is
organised the way it is. **Hand tool injuries start with work that
moved**, far more often than with a sharp edge or a failure of skill.

Be careful how hard you lean on that, because it is not a published
statistic and this guide will not pretend otherwise. **No source opened
for this guide breaks hand tool injuries down by whether the work was
secured.** What is published is the next best thing, and it is striking
once you notice it. In the federal hand tool manual this guide leans on,
the chisel procedure, the block plane procedure, the bench plane
procedure, the hacksaw procedure and the general saw safety list all
begin with the same instruction: secure the work so it cannot move.
Five procedures, four different tools, one first step.

Holding the work is the part that gets least attention and the part
those procedures put first.

The decision you are actually making, every single time you pick up a
tool, is this: **what am I trying to do to this material, what holds it
while I do that, and where does the tool go if it slips?** Three
questions. Ask them in that order and most of this guide becomes
obvious.

## What this will and will not do

This will give you the motions, the grips, the hazards attached to each
motion, and enough of the mechanism that you can pick up a tool you have
never seen and work out what it wants.

This will **not** teach you to saw a straight line. There is no
arrangement of words that does that. The Forest Service says so in its
own trail tools manual, in a sentence worth reading twice: the keys to
safe work are sharp tools, proper tool use, team work and awareness of
hazards, and **such knowledge cannot come from a manual**. Its next
instruction is to ask an experienced person for help. A federal manual
about hand tools, telling you that a manual is not enough, is the most
honest thing in this guide, and it is not a disclaimer. It is a finding.
There is a section at the end that says exactly which parts of this you
will only get from a person and which parts you will get from ruining
wood.

Where a number appears here it comes from a publication that specifies
it, and every one traces to the Sources list at the bottom. The best
source on this subject by a wide margin is a joint Army, Navy, Air Force
and Marine Corps manual called *Use and Care of Hand Tools and Measuring
Tools*, TM 9-243, which is in the public domain and which walks through
fifty-odd tool families with a warning block on each. Where a statement
is shop custom rather than a specified value, it says so in the text.
There is a fair amount of shop custom here, because hand tool practice
is full of confident lore that nobody ever wrote down properly.

Two sibling guides carry load that this one deliberately does not.
[Sharpening](sharpening.md) owns edges: what an edge is, the burr,
angles, stones, stropping, heat and temper. It will not be repeated
here, and when this guide says "sharp" it means the thing that guide
describes. [Working with Wood](working_with_wood.md) owns the material:
grain direction, moisture, movement, how much a fastener can hold and
why boards split. Read that one if you want the numbers behind "wood is
strong one way and weak the other two", which is the fact underneath
half of what follows.

## The organising idea: there are only about eight things you can do

Do not learn tools. Learn what you are doing to the material. There are
roughly eight operations, and every tool you will ever meet is a
particular answer to one of them:

1. **Measure and mark.** Decide where the change goes.
2. **Hold.** Stop the work moving.
3. **Sever.** Cut through, across the fibres or along them.
4. **Pare and shear.** Take a controlled slice off a surface.
5. **Bore.** Make a hole.
6. **Strike.** Deliver a blow.
7. **Fasten and join.** Make two pieces into one.
8. **Abrade and finish.** Wear the surface down.

This matters practically. When you are handed a tool you do not
recognise, you do not need its name. You need to know which of those
eight it does, and that is usually visible: an edge at an acute angle
pares, a row of teeth severs, a spiral flute bores, a mass on a stick
strikes. Then the rules for that operation apply, and the rules for that
operation are mostly about your other hand.

It matters for a second reason too. Tools are sorted in shops and
catalogues by trade and by shape, which is why a beginner ends up owning
four things that do the same job and nothing that does a job they need.
Sorted by operation, the gaps are obvious.

## 1. Measure and mark

Nothing in this section can hurt you, which makes it the section people
skip. It is also the one that decides whether the rest of the work is
possible, because **you cannot cut to a line that is not there.**

### The reference face and the reference edge

Pick one face of the board and one edge. Mark them. From then on, every
measurement on that piece comes from those two surfaces and from nothing
else.

This is shop custom, taught in every joinery tradition, and not
something found as a specified procedure in any of the federal manuals
opened for this guide. But the reason for it is arithmetic, and
arithmetic you can check.

Say you are laying out six shelf positions on an upright. If you measure
each one from the last one, every mark carries every previous error. Say
you are consistently one thirty-second of an inch long, which is a very
ordinary pencil error. Six marks at one thirty-second each is six
thirty-seconds, which is three sixteenths of an inch, a bit under five
millimetres. Your last shelf is three sixteenths out and every shelf
after the first is out by a different amount, so nothing is parallel to
anything.

Measure all six from the same end instead and each mark carries only its
own error. The last shelf is out by one thirty-second. Same tool, same
hand, same care, six times less error, because you did not let the
mistakes stack.

**This is the whole argument for a reference surface, and it applies
everywhere.** It is why a marking gauge has a fence that rides one edge.
It is why you plane one face flat first and call it the face side. It is
why machinists dimension a drawing from a datum. The tool that makes it
physical is the marking gauge: TM 9-243 describes it as a graduated beam
with a head that slides and locks, and a steel spur projecting about one
sixteenth of an inch, used to mark guidelines parallel to an edge, end
or surface. Its instruction for using one is exactly the principle in
miniature: press the head firmly against the edge of the work, tip the
gauge forward with a wrist motion until the spur touches, then push it
along the edge **keeping the head firmly against the work**. The fence
against the reference edge is doing the measuring. Your accuracy comes
from the contact, not from your eye.

### Why a knife line beats a pencil line

A pencil line is a stripe of graphite sitting on top of the fibres. It
has width. A sharp pencil makes a line perhaps half a millimetre wide,
and a blunt one makes a line twice that, which means "cut to the line"
is already an ambiguous instruction: the near side, the far side or the
middle of it?

A knife or spur line is a severed groove in the wood. It has essentially
no width, and it has a second property that matters more: the chisel or
plane blade can physically drop into it, so the tool registers on the
line instead of you aiming at it.

**The second half of that is shop custom and is labelled as such.** What
is sourced is narrower and still useful: TM 9-243's scratch awl is
described as being used to scribe marks on soft wood, its scriber
procedure is to lay a steel rule or straight edge beside the line and
hold the scriber like a pencil, and its marking gauge cuts its line with
a steel spur. Every federal marking tool in that manual cuts rather than
draws. The manuals do not explain why, and this guide is not going to
invent a citation for a conclusion that the tools themselves make
plainly enough.

One real caution, which is the reason pencils survive: **a knife line is
permanent.** It is a cut. If you mark in the wrong place you now have a
groove to plane out. Common practice is to mark rough layout in pencil
and only knife the lines you are about to cut to.

### The square, and checking that it is square

A try square or combination square transfers a right angle from an edge
onto a face. Everything about a square depends on its stock sitting flat
against the reference edge, which is the same principle again.

**Squares are not automatically square.** A cheap one may never have
been, and any square can be dropped. Testing one takes ten seconds and
needs no equipment: hold the stock against a known straight edge, scribe
a line along the blade, then flip the square over so it faces the other
way, put the stock against the same edge at the same place, and scribe
again. If the square is true the two lines lie on top of each other. If
it is out by half a degree the two lines diverge by a full degree,
because flipping it doubles the error, and you will see a wedge of light
between them. This flip test is standard shop practice rather than a
procedure in any of the manuals read here.

TM 9-243's own contribution on measuring tools is blunter and worth
keeping: a gauge strained out of shape will result in inaccurate
measurements, small scratches or nicks will result in inaccurate
measurements, and you should not drop any gauge. A measuring tool that
has been used as a lever or a scraper is not a measuring tool any more.

If you are working between metric and imperial, or converting a plan,
read [Units and Converting Them](../skills/units_and_converting_them.md)
before you cut anything. A unit error does not look like an error, which
is exactly what makes it expensive on a board you have already bought.

## 2. Hold

This is the section that prevents injuries, so it comes before any
cutting tool.

Look again at the federal instructions in the sections below, and notice
how many of them begin the same way. For a woodworker's chisel, TM 9-243
says to place the work securely in a vise so that **the wood should not
be able to move in any direction**. For a block plane and again for a
bench plane: secure work with a vise or with clamps to prevent slippage.
For a hacksaw: secure the material in a vise or other holding device to
avoid vibration. In its general saw safety list: using clamps or vises,
steady or secure any loose material to be cut.

Five instructions, four tools, three chapters, one first step. The
manual is not being repetitive. Workholding is the first step of every
cutting operation because it is the step that the other steps depend on.

### Why a hand is not a clamp

A hand can hold a board still against a light, steady, predictable
force. It cannot hold a board against a force that is intermittent,
because intermittent force is what makes things slip, and it cannot hold
a board against a force larger than your grip, which is a much smaller
force than you imagine when the surfaces are smooth and there is dust
between them.

More importantly, a hand holding work is a hand **in the work area**.
That is the geometry that turns a slip into an injury. The tool is
moving under force toward a place your hand is occupying. TM 9-243's
chisel warning states the requirement in the only terms that are
reliable: **keep both hands back of the cutting edge at all times.** You
cannot obey that instruction while your other hand is the thing holding
the workpiece. So something else has to hold it.

### What actually holds work

**A bench.** The single most useful workholding device is a surface that
does not move, at a height that lets you get your weight over the tool.
It does not need to be a joiner's bench. It needs mass, or it needs to
be fixed to something with mass, and it needs to not rack when you push
sideways.

**A vise.** TM 9-243's instructions are for the machinist's bench vise
but the rules carry: make sure the vise is bolted securely to a bench or
table and the swivel base is locked; work should be held firmly in
place, but the jaws should not be so tight that they mar the finish; use
brass or copper caps, or rawhide or leather, to protect soft or polished
material. And two rules that get broken constantly: **do not strike the
vise with a heavy object, and do not try to hold large work in a small
vise.** An overloaded vise does not usually fail dramatically. It just
lets go under load, which is worse, because you were pushing hard at the
time.

Its care note has a detail most people get backwards: **never oil the
swivel base or swivel joint, because it decreases holding power.** Oil
the screw, not the thing that is supposed to grip.

**Clamps.** TM 9-243 describes C-clamps as being for work that cannot be
held in a vise, or that has to be held for extended periods. Select one
with an opening about one and a half to two inches wider than the
material. Protect polished surfaces with brass shims or wooden blocks.
Check that the clamp applies even pressure over the entire surface. And
the caution that saves clamps and knuckles both: **do not use wrenches
or bars to tighten clamps.** A clamp tightened with a cheater bar is a
clamp being loaded past what its frame was designed for, and the frame
is cast.

Hand screw clamps, the wooden-jawed kind with two screws, get their own
caution in the manual: **use only on wood**, keep the jaws parallel, and
properly clamped work will form a square. Their virtue is a wide flat
jaw that grips without denting.

**Holdfasts, bench hooks, stops and wedges.** A holdfast is a bent bar
dropped through a hole in the bench and tapped to lock; a bench hook is
a board with a lip at each end, one hooking over the bench edge and one
stopping the work. Neither is in any manual opened for this guide, so
this is custom: they persist because they engage and release in a
second each, and **a workholding method that is slow to use is one you
will skip when you are in a hurry.**

**Your own body, carefully.** Kneeling on a board, standing on it,
trapping it against your hip: all legitimate, all used constantly, and
all subject to the same test. Where does the tool go if it slips? If the
answer is "into the leg that is holding the work", find another method.

### The hazard, stated plainly

The mechanism is always the same: the tool was loaded with force that
the workpiece was resisting, the workpiece went away, and that force is
now unopposed and travelling. A dull tool makes it worse, because you
were pushing harder to begin with, which is the argument
[Sharpening](sharpening.md) makes at length.

## 3. Sever: saws

A saw cuts by removing a strip of material as dust. The strip it removes
is the **kerf**, and its width is set by the thickness of the plate plus
the amount the teeth stick out either side.

### Rip and crosscut are different geometries for different fibres

TM 9-243 puts the distinction in one line: there are two categories of
handsaw, and **the ripsaw is designed to cut with the grain of wood,
while the crosscut saw is designed to cut against the grain.**

The reason is that the two jobs are not the same physical problem.
Cutting across the grain means severing a bundle of long fibres
crossways; the tool needs to slice them at the edges of the cut or it
tears them out and leaves a ragged wall. Cutting along the grain means
splitting between fibres and then shovelling the split material out; the
tool needs to scoop, not slice.

The Forest Service's crosscut saw manual describes the crossways case
precisely, for a big two-man saw where the two jobs are done by two
different teeth: **the cutting teeth sever the fibres on each side of
the kerf, and the raker teeth, cutting like a plane bit, peel the cut
fibres and collect them in the sawdust gullets and carry them out of the
cut.** Sever the edges, then peel the middle, then carry it away. That
is the crosscut operation, and a handsaw's crosscut teeth do all three
jobs with one tooth shape instead of two.

**The corresponding statement for rip teeth, that each acts as a tiny
chisel taking a shaving straight along the grain, is shop teaching and
appears in none of the sources opened for this guide.** Take it as
custom.

The practical consequence: **using a crosscut saw to rip a long board is
slow, and using a rip saw across the grain leaves a torn edge.** Neither
is dangerous. Both are the ordinary experience of somebody who owns one
saw and does not know there are two.

### Set, and why a saw binds

To **set** a saw is to bend the tip of each tooth slightly away from the
plane of the blade, alternate teeth bent opposite ways. The Forest
Service manual gives both the definition and the purpose: setting bends
the tips away from the plane of the saw, **causing the kerf to be wider
than the saw**, and it **helps prevent binding**.

That is the mechanism for a problem you will absolutely meet. A saw that
cuts a slot exactly its own width has no clearance, so the moment the
cut is not perfectly straight, or the wood closes on it, the plate is
gripped. Set buys clearance.

The manual is equally clear that more is not better: **a saw should be
set only as much as required to keep it from binding. More set than
necessary results in more work to make a wider kerf and a saw that flops
in the cut with the possibility of a curving cut.** The amounts are for
large crosscut saws and do not transfer to a handsaw, but they show the
scale: the set required varies from almost nothing for a taper-ground
saw in dry hardwood up to 0.030 inch for the same saw in soft, punky
wood, with 0.010 inch given as a good preliminary figure. Ten thousandths
of an inch is about a quarter of a millimetre. This is a small
adjustment with large consequences.

The same manual lists what makes a cut curve, and the list is a useful
diagnosis chart: **a kinked saw, too much set allowing the saw to flop
in the cut, uneven set that pulls the saw to one side, or a sawyer
bending or twisting the saw as he cuts** (which it notes is not a fault
of the saw, but a problem that might be blamed on the saw). Its
description of a saw that is working right is the standard to aim at: it
cuts without jumping or catching, does not bind, makes a straight cut,
and makes thick shavings.

### Starting the cut without the saw jumping

This is where beginners bleed, because the natural way to start a cut is
to put a thumb next to the teeth and push, and a saw that skips out of a
cut at the start travels straight across that thumb.

TM 9-243 gives a procedure for a handsaw and every part of it is doing a
job:

1. **Place the work on a level just below the knees**, with a sawhorse,
   bench or other support fairly close to the line of cut. Support near
   the cut stops the wood flexing, and flexing is what makes a saw jam.
2. **Place your thumb against the side of the saw blade, being careful
   to hold it well above the teeth.** Note what that says. The thumb
   guides the *side* of the plate, and it is *above* the teeth, not
   beside them. It is a fence, not a grip.
3. **Start the cut by drawing back a few inches of teeth at the heel of
   the saw a few times.** Backwards, at the heel, under almost no
   pressure. You are scratching a groove for the saw to sit in, not
   cutting. Once there is a groove the saw cannot jump out of it.
4. **At approximately a 45 degree angle, saw only on the downward stroke
   applying pressure evenly.** The manual gives 45 degrees for the
   crosscut handsaw and gives no angle for ripping. The steeper angle
   commonly taught for rip cuts is shop convention and is not stated in
   any source here.
5. **Keep the blade at a right angle to the work surface, checking
   occasionally with a try square.** Your eye will not tell you. A
   square will.
6. **When nearing the end of the cut, hold the waste side of the wood to
   prevent the board breaking off unevenly.** The offcut's weight tears
   out the last inch if you let it fall.

The pressure rule is the one to internalise: **cut on the working
stroke, lift the pressure on the return.** TM 9-243 makes this explicit
for the hacksaw, where you cut on the push stroke and draw the blade
back using no pressure each time, and it caps the rate at 40 to 50
strokes per minute. That rate is slower than people expect and it is
there because speed is heat and heat kills teeth.

### Push versus pull

A western handsaw cuts on the push stroke and is stiffened to survive
being pushed. A blade being pushed is in compression along its length,
which is the direction a thin plate buckles, so it has to be thick
enough not to. A blade being pulled is in tension, which a thin plate
handles beautifully, so a pull saw can have a much thinner plate and
therefore a much finer kerf.

There is a sourced example of a pure pull saw in the crosscut manual:
**symmetric two-man crosscut saws are pulled by each sawyer, and there
is no push stroke.** The same manual explains the force difference in a
one-man cut: with the handle up, the push stroke increases the downward
force, so the teeth sink deeper, the cut is deeper and it takes more
energy, while on the pull stroke a slight upward force is applied
instead.

Japanese pull saws, which are the pull-stroke tool most people encounter
today, are not covered by any source read for this guide. The physics
above is general. The specific claims made about those saws are not
sourced here.

### The rest of the saw rules that matter

From TM 9-243's saw safety and care lists, all of them earned:

- **Inspect before using. Do not use a damaged or broken saw.** If a saw
  is dropped, inspect it immediately before reusing.
- **Never use a woodcutting saw to cut through nails or other metal.**
  One nail takes out several teeth at once.
- **Do not force a saw that binds. Use a wedge to spread the cut.** This
  is worth its own note, because a bound saw is the exact moment people
  start heaving, and heaving on a stuck tool is covered in the body
  position section below for a reason.
- **Between cuts, place the saw flat on a bench** where the teeth cannot
  be damaged, and do not put heavy objects on a blade, which distorts
  it.
- **Do not allow pointed or edged tools to lie around where they may
  injure someone**, and be careful not to let fingers or other parts of
  the body get in the line of cut.

For carrying saws any distance, the Forest Service adds rules from
actual trail crews: lay the saw flat across one shoulder with the teeth
guarded and facing away from the neck, carry it on the downhill
shoulder, and transport saws at the rear of a line of workers. The
general rule for carrying edged tools in company is the useful one:
carry them at your side, blades forward and handles behind, and **if you
slip, drop the tool to the side to prevent falling on the blade.**

If the wood you are cutting is fuel rather than lumber, the units and
the drying matter more than the saw does, and
[Firewood](../skills/firewood.md) covers both.

## 4. Pare and shear: chisel, knife, plane

This is the most dangerous section in this guide, and the reason is a
single property of the operation.

**A chisel does not slip sideways. It slips along the cut.** It is
already moving in the direction of the cut, under force, when it loses
its bite. Whatever is in front of it, in line with the cut, is what it
reaches. For a saw or a hammer the dangerous zone is roughly where the
tool is. For a paring tool the dangerous zone is **everything in front
of the edge, out to the distance your arm can drive it.**

So the rule is not "be careful". The rule is geometric, and TM 9-243
states it as a warning in capitals: **wear eye protection, and keep both
hands back of the cutting edge at all times.** Followed by a second
warning: **never cut toward yourself with a chisel.**

Read those as one instruction. Draw an imaginary line forward from the
edge, along the direction of cut, and keep everything made of you out of
it. That includes the hand steadying the work, the thigh the work is
resting on, and the hand that is about to move into position.

### The chisel, step by step

TM 9-243's woodworking chisel procedure, which is short and complete:

1. **Mark the area to be cut** with rule and pencil.
2. **Examine the grain and place the work securely in a vise so you are
   cutting with the grain.** The wood should not be able to move in any
   direction. Cutting with the grain matters because the fibres
   underneath either support the cut or split away ahead of it, and when
   they split away the cut runs on and takes a chunk with it.
3. **Rough cuts are made bevel side down. Smooth finishing cuts are made
   bevel side up.**
4. **Start the cut about one eighth of an inch from the guide line, and
   cut toward the centre, the waste area, to protect the edge.** One
   eighth of an inch is about three millimetres. You are deliberately
   leaving material to remove later, because a cut that overshoots
   cannot be put back.
5. **Make small thin cuts. This prevents breaking or splitting of the
   work.** Check your guide marks often to prevent overcutting.
6. **Remove the last one eighth of an inch with chisel and mallet** to
   finish.

And the caution attached to the driving: **use short, rapid mallet blows
to control depth and length of cut.** Many small controlled inputs, not
one large one. That is the theme of the entire operation.

### Bevel up and bevel down, and what it is actually doing

The manual gives the rule without the mechanism, so here is the
mechanism, labelled as the explanation it is rather than a sourced
claim.

A chisel has a flat back and a bevelled front. Which one faces the work
decides where the tool goes when you push.

**Bevel down**, with the flat back up, the bevel rides on the wood
beneath and acts as a ramp. Push and the ramp lifts the edge out of the
cut. That self-limiting behaviour is what you want when hogging waste
out of a recess: the cut cannot run away and dive deeper.

**Bevel up**, with the flat back down on the work, the back registers
against the surface you already have and the edge takes a thin slice
parallel to it. That is what you want for a finishing cut, because the
reference is the finished surface itself.

Both of those follow from the shapes, and both match what TM 9-243
prescribes. The mechanism is not in the manual.

**The back of a chisel is half the edge.** [Sharpening](sharpening.md)
covers this properly and it is not repeated here, but the practical
consequence belongs in this section: a chisel whose back is not flat
cannot register on the work in the bevel-up position, so it cannot take
a controlled finishing cut no matter how sharp the bevel is.

### Struck or pushed

TM 9-243 distinguishes two chisel constructions and the distinction is
about how much force the handle can take. A **socket** chisel has the
blade and a socket forged in one piece with a wooden handle dropped into
the socket, and is used for heavier cutting where a hammer or mallet may
be required for additional driving force. A **tang** chisel has the
handle drilled and fitted over a tang and reinforced with a metal band,
and **the driving force required is hand pressure only.**

That last clause is the one to notice. Some chisels are not meant to be
hit at all.

### Knives

TM 9-243's knife rule is short and has exactly one exception: **always
cut away from the body**, the exception being the draw knife, which is
built to be pulled. Its awl warning is the same idea in different words:
awls are very sharp and must be used with extreme caution, and points
should be covered when not in use.

The same forward-line rule as the chisel applies, for the same reason. A
knife that slips travels along the cut.

### Planes

A plane is a chisel held at a fixed angle in a body that controls how
deep it can go. Everything that makes a plane easy is that control, and
everything that makes it frustrating is the setting of it.

TM 9-243 names two and gives their geometry: the **block plane** is the
smallest, **designed to cut end grain with the cutting blade set between
12 and 20 degrees**, usually usable in one hand with a toe knob for when
extra pressure is needed. The **bench plane**, of which the jack plane
is the common example, is **designed for cutting with the grain with its
blade set at approximately 45 degrees**, for all-purpose planing.

The low angle for end grain and the higher angle for long grain is the
same distinction as rip versus crosscut teeth, arriving again: severing
fibre ends wants a slicing geometry, lifting a shaving along the grain
wants a scooping one.

Its procedure, with the reasons:

- **Secure the work with a vise or clamps to prevent slippage.** Again,
  first step.
- **Ensure the blade is extremely sharp and set to produce a fine cut.**
  A plane set coarse does not cut more, it jams more.
- **Right hand on the handle, left hand on the knob**, reversed if you
  are left handed. Two hands means the tool is steered by your body
  rather than your wrist.
- **Check that the plane is placed on the work so that the wood grain
  points in the direction the plane will go.** Plane against the grain
  direction and the blade lifts fibres rather than slicing them, and it
  tears out.
- **Push with a steady, even stroke along the length of the board, then
  raise the plane from the work after each stroke and return to the
  starting point.** Lifting on the return is the same rule as the saw.
  Dragging an edge backwards over the work dulls it for nothing.
- For end grain specifically: **plane from either end to prevent the
  grain splitting, or plane a chamfer on the far end first.** This is
  the fix for a specific failure. Plane straight off the far end of a
  board and the last fibres have nothing behind them, so they break out
  rather than cut, and a corner of your work disappears. Either stop
  short and come in from the other side, or knock a chamfer off the far
  corner first so there is no fragile corner left to break.

The plane safety list adds a few: use each plane only for the job it is
intended for, keep cutting tools in good condition, inspect for damage
before using and repair or replace immediately, and **plane work
smoothly to avoid splinters that may be left to cause injury.** The
splinter is a real hazard, it is just a small one, and it is the most
likely thing to actually happen to you in this section.

Its care rule is the one that marks out somebody who has been taught:
**place the plane on its side to protect the cutting edge when not in
use**, and withdraw the blade into the mouth for storage.

## 5. Bore

Boring is the most forgiving operation here, with one specific failure
that ruins work.

The hand tools are the **brace**, a cranked frame with a chuck that
turns a bit with a lot of torque and very little speed, the **breast
drill** and **hand drill**, which are geared so the chuck turns faster
than your hand does, and the **awl** and **gimlet**, which make small
holes by displacing wood rather than cutting it out.

TM 9-243's descriptions carry the useful distinctions. The brace is used
to drill holes in wood and, with a screwdriver bit, to drive and remove
screws, which is worth remembering because a brace applies more
controlled torque to a stubborn screw than any screwdriver. The breast
drill has an adjustable breast plate that gives you something to lean
against, and a speed shifter so you can start a hole slowly at a 1 to 1
ratio to prevent marring the surface, then shift to a 3.5 to 1 ratio to
finish. Both it and the hand drill have a **side handle** whose stated
purpose is to steady the drill and ensure the bit is boring a straight
hole.

That side handle is the answer to the main difficulty of hand boring,
which is that a hole goes where the tool is pointed and nothing about
the tool tells you where that is. Two hands on a tool with a side handle
gives you two reference points and a much better chance of perpendicular.

### Breakout on the far side

Here is the failure. As a bit approaches the far face there is less and
less wood left to support the fibres ahead of the cutting edges. At some
point that remaining skin is too thin to resist, and instead of being
cut it is pushed out, taking a ragged crater of splinters with it. The
hole is fine. The surface around it is destroyed.

**Neither TM 9-243's manual drill chapter nor any other source opened
for this guide describes breakout or gives a remedy for it.** The
standard fixes are shop custom and are offered as such: clamp a sacrificial
backing board tight against the far face so the fibres there are
supported by wood instead of by air, or bore until the bit's lead screw
or spur point just breaks through, then stop, turn the work over, and
finish the hole from the other side using that small hole as the centre.

What is sourced is the same principle arriving in two other chapters,
which is why you should believe it. The plane chapter says to plane a
chamfer on the far end first to prevent the grain splitting. The saw
chapter says to hold the waste side when nearing the end of the cut to
prevent the board breaking off unevenly. **Unsupported fibres at the
exit of a cut break instead of cutting.** That is a general property of
wood, it has a general remedy, which is to support them, and it shows up
in every operation that goes all the way through.

### Boring hazards, which are mild but real

A hand brace cannot spin fast enough to catch and wrap, which is the
serious drilling hazard and the reason this section is short. What does
happen: a large bit that binds can twist the work out of a weak hold, so
hold the work properly. A bit that has been working hard gets genuinely
hot, and grabbing a hot bit is an easy and completely avoidable burn. If
you do get one, the first minute is the part that matters and
[Burns](../skills/treating_burns.md) covers exactly that.

## 6. Strike

Striking is the operation where the energy is stored up in advance and
released all at once, which changes the nature of the mistakes. A paring
mistake takes off a shaving. A striking mistake arrives at full speed.

### The hammer itself is a hazard before you swing it

TM 9-243 has a warning about hammers in capital letters, and it is worth
taking literally: **never, never use an unsafe hammer. Before using,
check for a loose head or a cracked handle. Do not use the handle as a
pry bar, or to knock sharp edges together.**

Then its specific checks:

- **Inspect the faces of steel hammers for wear, dents or chips. They
  can be dangerous if chips fly off.** Replace the hammer if you find
  them. A chip off a hardened hammer face leaves at high speed and is
  the reason for eye protection on an operation with no obvious dust.
- **Make sure the handle is tight in the head.** And the instruction
  that gets ignored most: **do not tape a cracked handle. Replace it.**
  Tape hides a crack. It does not carry load.
- **Make sure the wedges are in the handle**, keeping the head tight,
  and use only correct wedges.
- **Make sure the striking face is free of oil.** An oily face slides
  off whatever you hit.

Federal construction regulation says the same thing with legal force. 29
CFR 1926.301 is four sentences long and three of them are about tool
condition: employers shall not issue or permit the use of unsafe hand
tools; **impact tools, such as drift pins, wedges and chisels, shall be
kept free of mushroomed heads**; and **the wooden handles of tools shall
be kept free of splinters or cracks and shall be kept tight in the
tool.** The general industry rule, 29 CFR 1910.242, adds that each
employer is responsible for the safe condition of tools used by
employees, **including tools which may be furnished by the employees**.
Your own tool being your own problem is not how the law sees it.

**Mushrooming** deserves the explanation the regulation does not give.
When you repeatedly strike the soft end of a struck tool, a punch or a
cold chisel, the metal spreads sideways into a flared lip with an edge
overhanging the shaft. That lip is work-hardened, brittle, and
unsupported, and eventually a piece of it leaves at the speed of a
hammer blow. TM 9-243 gives the same instruction twice: inspect mallets
for mushrooming and file the edges back down to the original shape, and
replace punches that have mushroomed ends.

### How to hold and where to aim

TM 9-243, on holding any hammer: **the proper way to hold any hammer is
near the end of the handle. The handle is shaped for gripping without
slipping from your grasp at this position, and gives the best control
and impact with least effort.**

Choking up on the handle feels more controlled and is the standard
beginner instinct. It costs you leverage, which means you compensate
with more muscle, which means less control, not more.

On aiming: **strike the nail or tool squarely and on centre to prevent
the hammer from glancing off.** That is the whole of hammer accuracy.
Square and on centre, because a face that lands at an angle converts
part of the blow into sideways motion, and a glancing blow is how a
hammer arrives somewhere unplanned, usually the hand holding the nail.

On how much of your body to use: **the wrist and arm motion depends on
the power of the impact required. Small nails require light blows almost
entirely from a wrist motion. Heavy blows come from the wrist, forearm
and shoulder.** Match the body segments to the job. Driving a brad with
a shoulder swing is how you bend brads and hit thumbs.

### Starting a nail without hitting your fingers

TM 9-243's sequence, which solves the problem properly:

1. **Support the nail between thumb and first finger about halfway up
   the nail**, not at the head. A piece of paper or a comb can hold
   small nails, which puts your fingers entirely outside the strike
   zone.
2. **Tap the nail head until the nail will remain standing by itself.**
   Light taps. You are only setting it.
3. **Remove your fingers and drive the nail flush.** Fingers leave
   before full-force blows begin.
4. And the instruction people miss: **make sure the hand supporting the
   work is not in direct line with the hammer blows.**

### The claw, and the wrong hammer for the job

The claw is for pulling nails and prying boards. TM 9-243: slip the claw
under the nail head **as far as it will go**, to prevent bending the
nail head up; pull the handle to a vertical position to withdraw the
nail; and for longer nails, **place a block of wood under the head for
better leverage.** The block does two things: it increases leverage, and
it stops the hammer head crushing the surface of your work.

Its caution about what a claw hammer must not hit is specific and
important: **never use a claw hammer on a steel punch or on hardened
steel-cut or masonry nails. The face is too soft and could chip.** A
carpenter's hammer is built to hit relatively soft nails. Hardened steel
against a claw hammer face damages the face, and the damage leaves as a
fragment.

### Mallets, and why a chisel handle gets a mallet

TM 9-243 lists mallets separately from hammers and gives the carpenter's
mallet a cylindrical wooden head, often bound with thin metal bands, and
a stated use: **driving dowels, small stakes, wooden handled chisels,
and forming and shaping sheet metal.**

That is the sourced part: the tool for driving a wooden-handled chisel is
a mallet, and a federal manual says so directly. The chisel procedure in
the same manual specifies chisel and mallet at every driving step.

The reason usually given, and it is reasoning rather than a quotation
from any source here, is about contact area and hardness. A steel hammer
face is small, hard, and concentrates its blow on a tiny patch of a
wooden handle; a mallet face is broad and softer, so the same energy
arrives spread out. A wooden handle struck repeatedly with steel
splinters and splits, and the regulation quoted above requires wooden
handles to be **free of splinters or cracks**, so the tool that causes
that damage is the tool that puts your chisel out of service.

Some modern chisels have handles designed to be struck with a hammer,
and those exist precisely because this is a real limitation of the
wooden ones.

## 7. Fasten and join

The wood does the holding, not the fastener. That subject belongs to
[Working with Wood](working_with_wood.md), which sets out the Wood
Handbook's measured numbers: how much a nail holds, how density changes
that, how badly end grain performs, what moisture cycling does to
holding power, and the lead hole and spacing rules that stop a board
splitting. **Those numbers are not repeated here.** If you are deciding
how to join something, read that guide first, because it answers the
question this one cannot.

What belongs here is the motion.

### Pilot holes

TM 9-243's instruction is unambiguous: **before attempting to drive a
screw into wood, you first make a pilot hole**, using a screw starter or
gimlet, an awl, a nail or a drill. The gimlet is described simply as a
threaded tip used to make a pilot hole in wood for wood screws.

A screw is a wedge with a thread on it, and wood is weakest in tension
across the grain, which is the direction a wedge loads it. That
mechanism, and the numbers for it, are in the wood guide. The practical
version: **a screw driven into a pilot hole pulls two pieces together; a
screw driven without one can split the piece it is trying to hold.** The
closer to an end or an edge, the truer that is.

### Driving a screw

TM 9-243, briefly: insert the screw in the pilot hole, insert the tip in
the slot, **keep the screwdriver in line with the screw**, and use your
other hand to keep the blade steady if needed.

In line with the screw is the whole technique. A screwdriver held at an
angle transmits part of its force sideways, which is what makes the tip
climb out of the slot, which chews the slot, after which no screwdriver
will ever grip it again.

**Do not use pliers to turn a screwdriver.** For genuinely stubborn
screws the manual says to use a square-bladed screwdriver designed for
heavy duty work with a wrench that fits the blade, which is a tool built
for the load rather than a bodge applied to one that is not.

The screwdriver safety warning is one of the sharpest in the manual:
**handle the screwdriver
carefully, a greasy handle could cause an accident; do not carry a
screwdriver in your pocket unless it has a pocket clip;** and **do not
use a screwdriver for prying, punching, chiselling, scoring or
scraping.**

Every one of those five misuses loads the blade sideways or shock-loads
a tip that was hardened for torsion. TM 9-243's general safety chapter
adds the consequence: **a battered screwdriver may slip and spoil the
screw slot or cause painful injury to the user.** The manual does not
say what that injury is. The mechanism makes it a puncture, and the
thing in front of a driver that slips out of a slot is usually the hand
holding the work, which is the argument for holding the work some other
way.

### Glue surfaces

Glue joins by getting into two surfaces and setting. That means it wants
surfaces that are clean, close-fitting and freshly prepared, and it
wants pressure while it cures. TM 9-243's hand screw clamps are
described for exactly this: two wooden jaws, used for **holding wood
while gluing**, kept parallel so the pressure is even.

**Beyond that, no source opened for this guide covers adhesive
selection, open time, clamping pressure or joint design, so this guide
does not.** Those are real subjects with real numbers and they deserve
their own document rather than a paragraph of remembered lore here.

## 8. Abrade and finish

Abrading is removing material with many small hard particles instead of
one edge. It is slow, it is controllable, and it is how you fix
something that is very nearly right.

### Files

TM 9-243: every file has a point, an edge, a face of cutting teeth, a
heel and a tang. American pattern files are for fast removal where a
precision finish is not required. Its selection rules give the pattern
that applies to all abrasives: **for heavy rough cutting use a large
coarse double-cut file; for finishing cuts use a second-cut or
smooth-cut single-cut file.** Coarse to shape, fine to finish.

Its file safety list is three items and every one is a specific
mechanical failure:

1. **If a file is designed to be used with a handle, do not attempt to
   use it without the handle. Holding the sharp tang in your hand while
   filing can cause serious injury.** A bare tang is a spike pointing at
   your palm, and when the file catches, your hand keeps going.
2. **Do not use a file for prying. The tang end is soft and it bends
   easily. The body of the file is hard and very brittle. A light
   bending force will cause it to snap.** Hard and brittle is the same
   trade the sharpening guide describes for edges: the hardness that
   lets a file cut hardened steel is exactly what stops it bending
   instead of breaking.
3. **Do not hammer on a file. This is very dangerous because the file
   may shatter.**

A file used on wood loads up with fibres quickly. A rasp, which has
individual raised teeth rather than rows of cut ridges, is the wood
version of the same idea. Rasps are not covered in any source opened
here.

### Abrasive paper

Grit numbering is covered in [Sharpening](sharpening.md), which sources
the definition properly: a grain number is a sieve mesh count, so a
bigger number means smaller particles and a finer finish. The
progressions people recommend are convention rather than standard.

The rule that matters for finishing is that **each grade only has to
remove the scratches left by the grade before it.** Skipping several
grades means the fine paper is trying to erase coarse scratches, which
it will do eventually and slowly, or more usually will not do, leaving
scratches that only appear once a finish goes on.

### The hazard that is actually serious here

Sanding produces almost no visible mess and a very large amount of
respirable dust, which is exactly backwards from how people judge risk.

The National Institute for Occupational Safety and Health lists wood
dust as a **potential occupational carcinogen** with the cancer site
given as **nasal cancer**, and sets a recommended exposure limit of
**1 milligram per cubic metre** as a time-weighted average. The OSHA
permissible limit is 15 milligrams per cubic metre for total dust and
5 for the respirable fraction, so the health-based figure is fifteen
times stricter than the enforceable one for total dust and five times
stricter for the respirable fraction. The other listed effects
are eye irritation, nosebleeds, dermatitis, respiratory
hypersensitivity, granulomatous pneumonitis, asthma, cough, wheezing,
sinusitis and prolonged colds, with the eyes, skin and respiratory
system as target organs, and skin and eye contact as exposure routes
alongside breathing it.

What that does not mean: it is not a reason to avoid working wood, and
the limit is a workplace average over a shift, not a threshold where
harm begins.

What it does mean, in Silverdale and anywhere else on this coast, is
covered in [Working with Wood](working_with_wood.md), which notes the
detail that matters locally: **western red cedar dust shares the same
NIOSH entry as wood dust.** The wood most worth building with outdoors
here is on the respiratory hazard list.

## How to tell whether a tool is any good

You are in a second-hand shop, or somebody has handed you a box of tools
from a garage. Here is how to sort them.

### Judge it in your hand first

The NIOSH and Cal/OSHA guide to selecting non-powered hand tools makes a
point worth starting from: **a tool becomes ergonomic only when it fits
the task you are performing and it fits your hand.** There is no such
thing as a well-designed tool in the abstract.

It gives dimensions, and they are the only published numbers this guide
found for how a tool should fit a hand:

| What you are gripping | For power (high force) | For precision (low force) |
|---|---|---|
| Single-handle tool, handle diameter | 1 1/4 to 2 inches | 1/4 to 1/2 inch |
| Double-handle tool, grip span closed | at least 2 inches | at least 1 inch |
| Double-handle tool, grip span open | no more than 3 1/2 inches | no more than 3 inches |

One and a quarter to two inches is about 32 to 51 millimetres. A quarter
to a half inch is about 6 to 13 millimetres.

And its handle length rule, which explains a specific pain: for
high-force tasks the handle should be **longer than the widest part of
your hand, usually 4 to 6 inches**, because if the handle is too short
the end presses into the palm, against the nerves and blood vessels
there, and may cause injury. That is why a short screwdriver used hard
leaves a sore spot in the middle of your palm.

Its other selection points, each a yes or no you can check by picking
the tool up: no sharp edges or finger grooves on the handle (finger
grooves fit one hand size and fight every other); a non-slip surface; a
soft coating if possible; spring-loaded handles on pliers and cutters so
they reopen by themselves; usable with either hand; and usable **while
keeping your wrist straight**. On handle angle it gives a real rule
rather than a slogan: **bent handles are better when the force is
applied horizontally, straight handles are better when the force is
applied vertically.**

Be honest about what that guide does and does not cover. It says
explicitly that it **does not address tool weight, tool balance,
vibration or tool maintenance.** Those are exactly the things a
traditional tool buyer talks about most, and they are not in it.

The injuries it is about are also a different class from the rest of this
guide: not cuts but musculoskeletal disorders, carpal tunnel syndrome,
tendonitis and muscle strain, which it notes **do not happen because of
a single event** but build from repetitive movement over weeks, months
or years. Its list of early symptoms is the useful part, because they
are easy to dismiss: tingling, numbness, swelling in the joints,
decreased grip strength, decreased ability to move, continual muscle
fatigue, sore muscles, pain from movement or pressure or cold, and
change in the skin colour of your hands or fingertips.

### What is fixable

Most of what makes a second-hand tool look terrible is cosmetic or
routine.

- **A dull or chipped edge.** Fixable, always, and it is the normal
  condition of a tool that has been used. It costs time and a little
  steel. [Sharpening](sharpening.md) is the whole procedure.
- **Surface rust.** Fixable. The crosscut saw manual's method for a
  rusty or pitchy plate is to lay it flat and work it with an ax stone
  or a pumice grill stone, dousing it liberally with a citrus-based
  solvent to dissolve pitch and stop the stone clogging.
- **A loose handle.** Fixable, and there is a real distinction between
  the temporary and permanent fix. The Forest Service notes that a loose
  handle **can be temporarily tightened in the field by soaking the head
  in water or linseed oil**, because the wood swells to fit the eye, and
  then says immediately: **make permanent repairs as soon as possible.**
  The folk version of this trick usually omits the second half. Swelling
  wood with water in a steel eye is a way to finish the day, not a
  repair.
- **A dry, grey, splintery handle.** Fixable. Remove any varnish or
  paint and apply raw linseed oil lightly and regularly to protect
  against drying and cracking.
- **A handle that is the wrong shape for you.** Fixable, carefully.
  Handles may be shaved to fit individual grips to reduce impact shock
  and hand and arm cramps, and the Forest Service's caution is the right
  one for all irreversible work: **proceed slowly, because it is better
  to remove too little and have to trim again than to remove too much
  and have a weak or unusable handle.**
- **A kinked saw plate.** Partly fixable, and this one is commonly
  misreported as fatal. The crosscut manual says **few saws are
  completely straight** and that slight kinks or bumps will not cause
  much trouble, then gives a whole procedure for finding kinks with
  paired straightedges and hammering them out. So a kink is a
  specialist repair with specialist tools rather than a scrapped saw.
  What it costs you if you leave it is real: a straight saw requires
  minimum set, is less likely to buckle on the push stroke, and cuts
  straighter.

### What is not

- **A cracked casting.** A cracked plane body, a cracked clamp frame, a
  cracked vise. Cast iron does not bend before it breaks, and it breaks
  under exactly the load you were applying when you needed it. No
  source read for this guide addresses repairing castings; the judgement
  that they are not worth repairing is ordinary shop opinion, but the
  behaviour of cast iron under load is not.
- **A twisted plane body.** The sole of a plane is its reference
  surface, the same role as the marking gauge fence. A sole that is not
  flat cannot register on the work, and correcting it means removing
  metal across the whole sole.
- **A mushroomed struck tool that has been left too long.** Filing the
  lip back to the original shape is the specified repair, but if the
  flare has been there long enough that cracks run back into the shaft,
  the regulation's instruction is the right one: it does not say repair,
  it says such tools **shall be kept free of mushroomed heads**, and TM
  9-243 says replace punches that have mushroomed ends.
- **A hammer face with chips or dents.** TM 9-243 does not offer a
  repair. It says inspect for wear, dents or chips, and **replace the
  hammer if these conditions are found.**
- **A tool that was never made properly.** Nothing you do to the edge
  fixes a square that is not square, a plane whose mouth is cast off
  centre, or a chisel whose steel will not hold an edge.

The general test TM 9-243 gives is the one to carry round a second-hand
shop: **never use damaged tools**, because a battered tool slips, and a
measuring tool strained out of shape gives wrong answers while still
looking like a measuring tool. **A tool that lies to you is worse than a
tool that does not work**, because you find out later.

## What keeps a tool working

Almost all tool life is decided between uses.

**Rust.** Rust attacks the thinnest, most exposed metal first, which is
the edge. TM 9-243's care instructions across every chapter converge on
the same answer: apply a light coat of oil to metal surfaces before
storing, store in a dry place, and use a rust-preventive compound for
long storage. The Forest Service's saw rule is to coat the blade with a
protectant before storing and **never store a saw in a wet sheath**,
which is the most reliable way to rust a blade there is, because you
have sealed damp against steel.

**Storage that protects the geometry, not just the surface.** This is
the part people miss. Store saws straight, hung or laid flat, because
**storing a saw in a bent position will bow it**. Place a plane on its
side and withdraw the blade into the mouth. Fit protective covers on
chisel edges and store them in racks where they cannot be chipped. Keep
awl points covered. When a vise is not in use, bring the jaws lightly
together and leave the handle vertical, which protects the jaws.

**Handles.** Raw linseed oil, lightly and regularly, on bare wood.
Remove varnish first. The Forest Service also mentions that some workers
drill short holes in the base of a handle and periodically fill them
with linseed oil so it penetrates through the pores.

**Sharpness, which is maintenance and not a project.** Both the saw
chapter and the brush tool chapter say the same thing in different
words: touch up the teeth with a file occasionally rather than waiting,
and repair nicks and dulled edges immediately. The procedure is
[Sharpening](sharpening.md). The scheduling advice is the Forest
Service's: **at the end of the day, identify tools that need maintenance
and schedule time for repairing or replacing them.** Maintenance that is
not scheduled does not happen.

**Tool habits.** TM 9-243's general chapter is unfashionable and
correct. Keep each tool in its proper storage place, because a tool is
useless if you cannot find it. Keep tools free of rust, nicks, burrs and
breaks. **Use each tool only on the job for which it was designed.** Keep
them where they cannot fall on the floor. And its framing for the whole
subject, which is the right attitude: **a tool may be efficient,
essential, time-saving or even convenient, but it is also dangerous.**

## Force, leverage and body position

This is the part that transfers to everything physical you will ever do.

### Never pull hard on something that can come free

The principle: when you pull, your body is already accelerating
backwards against the resistance. If the resistance disappears, nothing
stops you. When you push, the resistance disappearing means your hands
move forward into the space where the work was, which is usually
survivable, and your body weight is already forward and stable.

This is why so many of the specific rules above are about not heaving.
**Do not force a saw that binds; use a wedge to spread the cut.** Do not
use wrenches or bars to tighten clamps. Do not use pliers to turn a
screwdriver. Do not use a hammer handle as a pry bar. Every one of those
is a case of a tool being loaded past what it was built for, and the
failure mode of an overloaded tool is sudden release.

The sudden release is the hazard, not the load. A wrench with jaws
sprung to the point that slippage occurs is forbidden by 29 CFR
1926.301(b) for exactly this reason: a wrench that slips at full effort
puts your knuckles into whatever is next to the fastener at the speed
your arm was pulling. **The prohibitions are all sourced; stating the
common principle in terms of pulling is reasoning, not a quotation.**

### A plane stroke is a whole-body movement

Push a plane with your arms alone and you run out of stroke at arm's
length, and the pressure varies through it, so the cut is deeper in the
middle than at the ends and the board ends up convex. Use your legs and
your weight and the stroke is long and even. TM 9-243 asks for a
**steady, even stroke along the length of the board**, and even is the
hard word, not steady. Evenness comes from a body moving as one unit,
not from a wrist correcting.

The NIOSH guide makes the related point: awkward postures make you use
more force, and relaxed shoulders and elbows make it easier to drive
downward force. Its two suggestions are about the bench rather than the
tool, which is why people forget them: **reposition the work piece, or
work on a lower surface.** If you are fighting a tool, the answer is
often to move the work rather than to grip harder. TM 9-243's
instruction to saw with the work **just below the knees** is the same
idea: that height puts your shoulder above the cut, so your arm swings
in the plane of the saw and your weight is available.

### Match the body segments to the force

The hammer instruction is the clearest statement of this in any of the
sources: small nails take light blows almost entirely from the wrist,
heavy blows come from the wrist, forearm and shoulder together. The
general rule is that you recruit the smallest set of joints that will do
the job, because small joints are precise and large ones are powerful,
and using a shoulder for a wrist job costs you accuracy.

The NIOSH guide has the grip version of the same trade: a **pinch grip**
holds the tool between thumb and fingertips and gives control for
precision; a **power grip** wraps all the fingers round and gives
maximum force. **A pinch grip will produce much less power than a power
grip**, so exerting force through a pinch grip means working harder to
get the job done. If you find yourself squeezing a precision grip hard,
you have chosen the wrong grip or the wrong tool.

## The honest limit

Here is where reading stops, stated as precisely as I can.

**What a document can give you** is everything above: the operations,
the order of the steps, the hazards attached to specific motions, the
reason behind each rule, and enough vocabulary to ask a useful question.
That is not nothing. Most beginner injuries come from not knowing that a
rule exists, not from failing to execute one.

**What you will only get from somebody watching your hands** is the
calibration. Nobody can tell you in words how hard to push a plane,
because the answer is a feeling in your arms that corresponds to a
shaving of a particular thickness. Nobody can tell you in words when a
saw has started to wander, because you feel it in the handle a stroke or
two before you can see it in the kerf. Nobody can watch you hold a
chisel and say "your left thumb is going to be in front of the edge in
about four seconds" except a person who is actually watching you. That
last one is the reason supervision matters most for the paring tools.
The Forest Service's own sentence stands as the authority here: such
knowledge cannot come from a manual, so ask an experienced person.

**What you will only get from ruining wood** is judgement about the
material. How a particular board's grain runs and which way you should
therefore plane it. How close to an end you can drive a screw before
this species splits. What a cut looks like when the saw has drifted off
square, versus when your line was wrong to begin with. These are not
teachable in advance and they are not dangerous to learn, which is a
useful combination. Buy cheap softwood, make deliberate mistakes in it,
and learn what each mistake looks like when it is happening rather than
when it is finished.

**What you should not learn alone**, where the cost of a mistake is
disproportionate: anything swung, anything felled, and anything where
the work is supported by your own body. The Forest Service is explicit
that you need training, experience and in some cases certification
before bucking or felling with a crosscut saw, and notes that using an
ax exposes you to similar hazards. That advice does not stop at the
trailhead.

One structural point worth ending on. The best study found for this
guide looked at ten years of United States emergency department records
for upper-limb injuries from home workshop equipment: 30,336 recorded
cases between 2010 and 2019, weighted to national estimates of about
168,795 injuries in 2010 falling to 147,584 in 2019, a decline of
roughly an eighth. Men were 27,762 of those cases and women 2,574, which
is 91.52 percent male. Amputations climbed steeply with age, from about
4 percent of injuries among people in their teens to about 24 percent
among people in their sixties.

The finding that matters here: **for everyone from their teens through
their forties, manual tools were the leading cause, ahead of power
saws.** Only from the fifties upward did powered saws overtake them. The
paper's manual tools are handheld saws, screwdrivers, hammers, manual
drills, chisels, nail guns and pliers.

Be careful what you take from that. It counts emergency visits, not
rates per hour of use, so it says nothing about which tool is more
dangerous to use for an hour, and the authors note that the coding
depends on individual coders' preferences. Younger people may simply do
more work with hand tools. But the belief that hand tools are the safe
option and the dangerous ones all have cords is not supported by the
one dataset examined here, and that belief is worth losing.

## You own this when

- You name the operation before you reach for the tool, and the tool
  choice follows from the operation.
- Your first action on any cut is to make the work immovable, and you
  notice when you have skipped it.
- You can point at the line the tool would travel if it slipped right
  now, and nothing of yours is on it.
- You measure everything from one reference face and one reference edge,
  and you can say why in terms of accumulated error.
- You start a saw cut with backward strokes at the heel and your thumb
  on the side of the plate, above the teeth.
- You know which way the grain runs before you plane, and you chamfer
  the far corner before you run off the end.
- You back up a hole before the bit breaks through, because you know
  unsupported fibres break instead of cutting.
- You hold a hammer at the end of the handle, strike square and on
  centre, and reach for a mallet when the thing being struck is a wooden
  handle.
- You check for a loose head, a cracked handle and a mushroomed end
  before the first blow, not after the incident.
- You can look at a rusty tool in a junk shop and say which of its faults
  are an evening's work and which mean leave it there.

When those are true, the specific tools stop mattering very much. You
will be able to pick up an unfamiliar one, work out which of the eight
operations it performs, infer how it wants to be held and where it would
go if it slipped, and be roughly right. That transfer is the actual
skill. It is also why this guide is organised by operation and not by
tool: **the tools are infinite and changing, and the operations are
eight and ancient.**

  https://www.fpl.fs.usda.gov/documnts/fplgtr/fplgtr190/chapter_08.pdf

  either. Where fastener mechanics were needed, this guide points the
  reader at [Working with Wood](working_with_wood.md), which cites the
  Wood Handbook directly, rather than restating numbers from a page that
  could not be opened this session.
- **That hand tool injuries usually begin with work that moved.** This
  is the organising claim of the whole guide and **it is not a published
  statistic.** No source opened here breaks injuries down by whether the
  work was secured. What is sourced, and is said in the text instead, is
  that five separate procedures in TM 9-243 begin by securing the work.
  That is evidence about what the manual thinks matters, not evidence
  about injury rates, and the difference is stated where the claim is
  made.
- **The reference face and reference edge convention.** Universally
  taught and not found as a specified procedure in any source read here.
  The arithmetic argument for it given in the text is arithmetic, not a
  citation, and can be checked.
- **Why a knife line beats a pencil line.** That marking tools cut
  rather than draw is sourced, from the scriber, awl and marking gauge
  chapters. The explanation that a blade drops into the severed groove
  and registers on it is shop custom.
- **The flip test for checking a square.** Standard shop practice, not
  in any manual read here.
- **Rip tooth geometry.** The crosscut mechanism is sourced in detail.
  The corresponding description of a rip tooth as a small chisel is
  shop teaching and is labelled as such. The steeper starting angle
  commonly taught for rip cuts is likewise unsourced; only the 45
  degrees for crosscutting is specified.
- **Japanese pull saws.** Not covered by any source read here. The
  general point about a pushed blade being in compression and a pulled
  one in tension is mechanics; the specific claims usually made about
  those saws are not sourced.
- **Breakout on the far side of a bored hole, and its remedies.** The
  backing board and the bore-from-both-sides methods are shop custom.
  The underlying principle, that unsupported fibres at the exit of a cut
  break rather than cut, is sourced twice in other operations, from the
  plane chapter's chamfer instruction and the saw chapter's instruction
  to hold the waste side near the end of a cut.
- **Holdfasts and bench hooks.** Not in any source read here, and
  presented as custom.
- **Why a mallet rather than a steel hammer on a chisel handle.** That
  the mallet is the correct tool is sourced directly. The contact-area
  and hardness explanation is reasoning, and the link to the regulation
  requiring handles free of splinters and cracks is this guide drawing a
  connection, not a statement either source makes.
- **The bevel-up and bevel-down mechanism.** The rule is sourced. The
  explanation of the bevel acting as a ramp is not in the manual.
- **The rule against pulling hard on a tool that can come free.** The
  individual prohibitions are all sourced. The general principle stated
  in terms of pulling is reasoning.
- **Adhesives.** Deliberately not covered beyond clamping, because
  nothing opened for this guide specifies adhesive selection, open time
  or clamping pressure.
- **Rasps.** Mentioned once and not covered, because no source read here
  describes them.
- **That a cracked casting is not worth repairing.** Ordinary shop
  opinion. The behaviour of cast iron, which fails without much warning
  deformation, is not controversial, but no source here was consulted on
  repairing tool castings.

### A note on what is not cited

Tool manufacturers, retailers and woodworking publications carry an
enormous amount of hand tool advice, a good deal of it excellent and
almost none of it an authority on a dimension, an angle or an injury
figure. This subject is unusually full of confident lore that traces
back to nothing. Where a number here exists only in that literature it
has been left out, and where a practice exists only there it has been
labelled shop custom. Nothing in this guide rests on a vendor's claim
about a product.
