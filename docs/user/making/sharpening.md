# Sharpening: Why the Dull Blade Is the Dangerous One

Most people believe a sharp blade is the hazard and a dull one is
merely annoying. It is the other way round, and getting this backwards
is how people end up in an emergency room.

The decision you are actually making is this: **every time you pick up
a cutting tool, you are choosing between spending five minutes on the
edge and pushing harder than the job needs.** Pushing harder is the
dangerous option. Everything below serves that one choice.

Here is the mechanism, and it is not complicated. A dull edge does not
start the cut. You lean on it. When it finally goes, it goes with all
that stored force behind it, and it goes somewhere you did not plan,
which is usually into the hand holding the work. A sharp edge starts
where you put it, needs almost no push, and if it does slip it is not
carrying much energy. Sharpening is therefore a safety skill first and
a quality skill second.

Where a number appears here it comes from a government publication or
a peer-reviewed study that measured it. The best sources on this
subject are unusually good: the USDA Forest Service has published
detailed public-domain manuals on filing axes and crosscut saws, and
the Army and Navy jointly publish a hand tool manual that is better on
sharpening stones than most books you can buy. Every number traces to
something in the Sources list at the bottom. Where a figure is trade
practice rather than a measured value, it says so in the text.

## What the evidence actually says, and what it does not

Be careful here, because the argument at the top of this page is made
confidently all over the internet and the supporting evidence is
partial.

**The force half is measured.** Researchers instrumented knives and
followed fifteen professional meat cutters through lamb shoulder
boning, beef rib trimming and beef loin trimming in two packing
plants. Sharper blades required statistically significantly lower peak
and mean cutting moments, and lower grip forces, than dull knives. The
numbers give a sense of scale: peak cutting moments reached 17.2
newton metres on shoulder boning, and grip forces averaged 28.3% of
each worker's maximum voluntary contraction with peaks at 72.6%. A
dull knife pushes those numbers up. That is a real measurement, not a
saying.

**The authority half is stated but not quantified.** OSHA's hand and
power tools guidance puts it plainly: knives and scissors must be
sharp, and dull tools can cause more hazards than sharp ones. The same
document tells you to direct saw blades and knives away from
walkways and away from anyone working near you.

**The injury half is inference.** No study found for this guide
directly measures injury rate against edge sharpness. What is measured
is the scale of the problem: an analysis of the federal injury
surveillance system found an estimated 434,259 knife-related injuries
treated in United States emergency departments every year, about 1,190
a day, or 1.56 per thousand people per year. Fingers and thumbs were
hurt in 66% of cases, and 94% of injuries were lacerations. The same
paper opens by noting that knives cause more disabling injuries than
any other type of hand tool. Pocket and utility knives accounted for
47% of the injuries where a knife type was recorded, and kitchen
knives for 36%.

So: the force increase is proven, the hazard claim is the position of
the federal safety regulator, the mechanism is obvious, and the direct
injury comparison has not been published. That is an honest summary.
It is still more than enough to sharpen your tools.

## What an edge actually is

An edge is not a thing added to a blade. It is a **geometric
coincidence**: two surfaces meeting so precisely that the line where
they meet has almost no width. That line is the **apex**.

This matters because it changes what you think you are doing. You are
not putting an edge on. You are **removing metal from two surfaces
until they intersect cleanly**. Every stroke of every stone is
subtraction. If the two surfaces do not yet meet, no amount of
polishing will make them cut, and this is the single most common
reason a beginner sharpens for twenty minutes and ends up with a blade
that still will not cut paper. They polished two surfaces that never
touched.

How small is small? A straight razor is honed to a wedge with a total
included angle of about 17 degrees and a tip radius of roughly 40
nanometres. Forty nanometres is smaller than many of the individual
structural features inside the steel itself.

That last fact is the key to how edges die. Research published in
*Science* in 2020 followed razor blades cutting human hair inside an
electron microscope. The blades did not simply wear smooth. They
**chipped**: microscopic cracks started where the steel's internal
structure varied from point to point along the edge, and a chunk of the
apex broke away. The chipping happened before any appreciable wear had
occurred. The authors are careful that the conditions have to line up,
so it is not every stroke, but it is what took the blade out.

That study is about razors and hair, so do not stretch it into a law
about every blade. What it does establish is that **an edge can go
from sharp to useless by fracturing rather than by rounding over**, and
two practical consequences follow:

- **A dull edge is often a damaged edge, not a worn one.** You may not
  be restoring a gently rounded curve at all. You may be cutting back
  past a chipped line until you reach sound metal, which takes more
  removal than people expect.
- **Thinner is sharper and also more fragile.** The Forest Service
  crosscut saw manual says this about saw teeth in exactly these
  terms: the more pointed a tooth is filed, the deeper it sinks and
  the hungrier the saw is, but a sharply pointed tooth wears faster
  than one less sharply pointed. That trade is the whole of edge
  geometry, in one sentence, for every tool you own.

## The burr: your one reliable checkpoint

This is the most useful thing a beginner can learn, and it turns
sharpening from guesswork into a procedure with a test you can pass.

When you grind one side of a blade far enough that the two surfaces
finally meet, the metal at the apex has nowhere left to go. It folds
over to the far side as a thin ragged lip. This is the **burr**, also
called a wire edge or a feather edge. You cannot usually see it. You
can feel it by sliding a fingertip **off the edge, across it, never
along it**.

A burr is not a defect. It is proof. **It is the only unambiguous
signal that you have removed enough metal to reach the apex at that
point on the blade.** No burr means the surfaces have not met yet and
you are not finished, however long you have been at it.

The procedure the Forest Service ax manual gives, which is the same
procedure for a kitchen knife, is:

1. Work one side until **a burr can be felt along the whole length of
   the edge**. Not part of it. The whole length, from heel to tip. If
   the middle burrs and the tip does not, keep going on the tip.
2. Turn the blade over and work the other side until **the burr flips
   back across to the first side**, again along the whole length.
3. Move to a finer abrasive and repeat, more lightly. The burr gets
   smaller each time.
4. Remove the last of it, which is what stropping is for. More on that
   below.

The ax manual is blunt about step 1: stop filing once you have filed
one side so that a burr of metal can be felt on the back side, then
turn the ax over and continue on the other side until the burr goes
back over to the side where you started.

The crosscut saw manual adds a warning worth carrying over to every
tool. Remove the burr periodically as you go, because **a burr can
hide the true shape of the edge** and make you think you are further
along than you are. Its instruction for saw teeth is to knock the burr
off with a whetstone, a light stroke of the file, or even a piece of
hardwood.

## Angle: consistency beats the number

The exact number matters far less than people think. Holding the same
number for every stroke matters enormously, because a wobbling angle
grinds a rounded shoulder instead of a flat surface, and a rounded
shoulder never meets its partner at a clean apex.

The Forest Service says this in the plainest possible way in its
handtool manual: blade and edge bevels may vary with individual
preference and with the task at hand, an ax bevel for softwoods might
be thinner than one for hardwoods, and experience and experimentation
will determine the right bevel for each tool and job. The crosscut saw
manual says the same about saw teeth, that the consensus is less bevel
on a cutter tooth for hardwood than for softwood.

Read that as permission to stop agonising. Pick an angle, hold it, and
adjust later if the edge chips or will not bite.

The working ranges below give the total included angle where that is
what the source specifies, and the per-side angle where that is the
convention. Read the last column carefully.

| Tool | Working angle | Where this comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Straight razor | About 17 degrees total | Measured, from published research on razor steel |
| Kitchen knife | About 15 to 20 degrees per side | **Trade practice.** No federal source found. Softer steels and heavier work want the higher end |
| Woodworking chisel or plane iron | About 25 degrees primary, often a 30 degree micro-bevel at the very tip | **Trade practice.** No federal source found |
| Axe | About 25 degrees, and **slightly convex**, filed back 2 to 3 inches from the edge | USDA Forest Service ax manual, which supplies a printed gauge template |
| Machete | Keep the factory bevel | USDA Forest Service handtool manual: use a mill bastard file or whetstone to maintain the factory edge bevel |
| Hoe, McLeod, grub hoe | 45 degrees, outside bevel | USDA Forest Service handtool manual |
| Shovel | About 45 degrees, bevel on the inside (top) face | USDA Forest Service handtool manual |
| Twist drill | 118 degrees included, that is 59 degrees each side of the axis, with 8 to 12 degrees of lip clearance | US Army machine tool manual: the general purpose drill point |

**Convex, flat or hollow.** The ax manual asks for a rounded, slightly
convex profile and a fan shape spreading back over the cheek of the
head. The handtool manual explains why: when you grind, arc the blade
toward its midpoint so it ends up with a full-width convex bevel, and
be careful not to hollow grind, which leaves the bevel dished to the
radius of the grinding wheel. A hollow bevel is thinner just behind
the apex, which is exactly where an impact tool needs steel. The
crosscut manual makes the same point from the other direction: a saw
tooth whose filed surface is flat develops a concave cutting edge and
a thin, weak point.

Short version: for anything you swing or lever, convex. For anything
you push through a controlled cut, flat is fine and easier to hold.

## Abrasives, honestly

Every abrasive does the same job, which is scratching metal off. They
differ in how fast, how flat they stay, how much mess they make and
what they cost.

The Forest Service handtool manual publishes a comparison of
sharpening stones with a speed rating for each. The prices are from
2005 and are useless now, so they are left out; the ranking is not.

| Stone | Natural or synthetic | What it is for | Speed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Washita | Natural | Shaping and roughing | Fast |
| Soft Arkansas | Natural | Sharpening | Medium |
| Hard white Arkansas | Natural | Edge finishing | Slow |
| Black hard Arkansas | Natural | Edge polishing | Very slow |
| Silicon carbide | Synthetic | Roughing to sharpening | Fast to medium |
| India | Synthetic | Roughing to sharpening | Fast to medium |
| Crystolon | Synthetic | Roughing to sharpening | Fast to medium |
| Diamond on nickel | Synthetic | Sharpening hard steels | Medium |
| Silicon carbide in rubber | Synthetic | Deburring | Fast |

That manual's own recommendation is **synthetic**, because synthetic
abrasives are harder than natural stones, their uniform particle size
gives an even surface, and they cost less. If you want a natural stone
for the final polish, do the rough work on a synthetic first so you do
not wear out the expensive stone on work it is not needed for.

What the common types mean in practice:

- **Oilstones.** Light oil floats the swarf, the metal and grit, off
  the surface so it does not lodge in the pores. Use oil on an
  oilstone. If it glazes because the pores have filled, clean it with
  a citrus-based solvent and a stiff brush.
- **Waterstones.** Water does the same floating job. They cut fast and
  polish well, and the ax manual warns they are much softer than
  oilstones and tend to cup and wear faster. They need flattening
  often.
- **Diamond plates.** Steel plate with diamond grit bonded to it. They
  stay flat, they cut hard steels that ordinary stones struggle with,
  and there is nothing to glaze. They are the low-fuss choice.
- **Ceramic.** Very hard, very slow, almost no wear. A finishing
  abrasive, not a shaping one.
- **Sandpaper on glass.** Wet-and-dry paper laid on a sheet of glass or
  a machined flat. The Forest Service uses exactly this arrangement to
  reshape worn stones: rub the stone over coarse sandpaper, or over
  glass sprinkled with carborundum powder, keeping it wet. If it will
  flatten a stone it will sharpen a chisel. It is cheap, it is flat,
  and the paper is consumable.
- **Files.** For axes, mower blades, mattocks, machetes and anything
  else too soft and too battered to bother a stone with. A 10 inch
  mill bastard file is the Forest Service's all-round recommendation.

**Understanding file cut.** Files are graded two ways at once and the
labels confuse everyone. The pattern of teeth: **single-cut** files
have one series of parallel teeth set at 60 to 80 degrees to the edge
and are for finishing; **double-cut** files have two crossing series
at 45 degrees to each other and are for restoring shape. The
coarseness, for files of the same length: **bastard** is coarsest,
then **second cut**, then **smooth**. Longer files of the same grade
are coarser than shorter ones.

**Grit numbers.** An abrasive grain number is a sieve mesh count: grain
number 40 means the grains passed through a sieve with about 40
openings to the linear inch. Bigger number, smaller grit, finer
finish. That is the definition. The specific progressions people
recommend, such as 1000 then 4000 then 8000 on waterstones, are trade
practice, not standards, and any three reasonably spaced grits work.

**When coarse is the right answer.** This is where beginners waste the
most time. If the edge is chipped, rolled, or has simply never met
itself, a fine stone cannot fix it, because a fine stone removes
almost no metal. More strokes on a fine stone is not equivalent to
fewer strokes on a coarse one. The rule:

- **No burr after a few minutes on a fine stone?** You are on the wrong
  stone. Drop to coarse.
- **Visible chips or a rolled edge?** Coarse, or a file.
- **Edge is there but not keen?** Fine, then finer.
- **Edge was sharp yesterday and is merely tired?** No stone at all.
  See the keeping-it section.

The general shape of this, as the Army and Navy tool manual describes
a two-sided stone, is that the coarse face removes nicks and reshapes,
and the fine face completes the job and puts a keen edge on.

## Stropping is not sharpening

A strop is a piece of leather, canvas, or a block of soft clear wood.
Drawing the blade **backwards**, spine first, along it does two things:
it tears off the last of the burr, and it aligns and burnishes the
very apex.

The Forest Service ax manual gives the direction precisely: strop by
drawing the ax toward the edge, the opposite of the direction used
during sharpening, on finished leather or a piece of soft clear wood
such as pine. The Army and Navy manual says the same about any blade,
that the wire edge left by a stone may be removed by stroking both
sides on a soft wood block, canvas, or leather.

What a strop does **not** do is remove meaningful metal or change the
geometry. If a blade will not cut, stropping will not save it. Strop
after sharpening, and strop between sharpenings to keep an edge
pleasant, but do not mistake it for the work.

Compounds, the waxy abrasive bars sold to charge a strop with, do add
a little cutting action. That is trade practice rather than a
specified process, and a bare leather strop already does the job the
Forest Service describes.

## Tool by tool

### Kitchen knife

The easiest place to learn, because the steel is soft enough to
respond quickly and the angle is forgiving. Medium stone until you
raise a burr along the entire edge, flip, raise it back, go to a fine
stone, strop. Hold the same angle; a guide clip or a folded matchbook
under the spine is a legitimate beginner's crutch.

### Chisel and plane iron

The only tools here where flatness is as important as the bevel. The
back of a chisel is one of the two surfaces that form the apex, so if
the back is not flat and polished near the edge, the edge cannot be
sharp no matter what you do to the bevel. The usual practice, which is
trade practice rather than a specification, is to flatten the back
once, properly, on a coarse abrasive, and then to sharpen only the
bevel from then on.

### Axe

The best-documented tool in this guide, because the Forest Service
wrote a manual about it. Clamp the head so both hands are free. Wear
leather gloves. Fit the file with a knuckle guard, which can be made
from leather, wood, a piece of fire hose, or old linen hose. File
**into** the edge, toward the middle of the handle, because that
creates the least burr to remove on the other side. A single-cut file
only cuts on the push stroke, so lift it clear on the return; sawing
back and forth loads the file with metal and ruins it. Work back 2 to
3 inches from the edge with a fan shape across the cheek, keeping the
profile rounded, chasing the burr from side to side, then hone with a
stone and strop.

### Machete and brush tools

Keep the factory bevel with a mill bastard file or a whetstone. Repair
nicks and dull spots **immediately**, before the next use, and do not
work with a dull or defective brush tool at all, because a brush tool
that skates off a springy stem goes somewhere unpredictable at swing
speed.

### Scissors and shears

Sharpen, do not grind. The Army and Navy manual is specific and
useful here: dull shears can usually be sharpened on an oilstone or
with a file; do not grind them if sharpening is all that is needed,
because **most shears become useless after two or three grindings**.
Grind only if the edges are actually nicked, damaged or distorted. And
never touch the flat inside faces, because two scissor blades cut by
shearing past each other and that contact plane is the mechanism.

### Drill bit

Sharpenable by hand on a grinder, with practice, to 118 degrees
included with 8 to 12 degrees of clearance behind each lip. Both lips
must come out the same angle and the same length. The Army manual
spells out what happens otherwise: if the lip angles differ, only one
lip cuts as the drill revolves, the hole comes out oversized, and the
drill wears very rapidly. A drill point gauge, set at 59 degrees,
removes the guessing, and the manual says to use it frequently to
check both lip angle and lip length. This is the tool most likely to
be ruined by heat, so read the temper section below before trying.

### Saw

Depends entirely on which saw. A traditional handsaw is sharpenable
with a triangular file and is a reasonable second-year skill. A
crosscut felling saw is a trade in itself: the Forest Service manual
walks through cleaning, hammering out kinks, jointing the teeth to a
circle, fitting and swaging the rakers, pointing up the cutter teeth,
and setting the teeth to about 0.010 inch for a felling saw or 0.015
inch for a heavy bucking saw, each step with its own gauges.

Two saws you cannot sharpen at all, and this is **trade knowledge**
rather than anything sourced here: a modern hardpoint handsaw has
hardened teeth and is sold as a consumable, and a carbide-tipped
circular blade needs diamond wheels and a machine.

For any saw, the Army and Navy manual's maintenance advice still
applies: never cut nails or other metal with a woodcutting saw, do not
force a saw that binds but spread the cut with a wedge, and touch up
the teeth with a file occasionally rather than waiting until it will
not cut.

### What a beginner should not attempt yet

Only the shears entry below comes from a manual. The rest is
judgement, offered as trade practice.

- **Crosscut and bandsaw blades.** Not because they are dangerous to
  sharpen, but because a badly filed saw is worse than a dull one and
  you will not know which mistake you made. Learn from a person.
- **Scissors and shears you care about.** See above. The material
  budget is two or three grindings for the life of the tool.
- **Carbide anything.** Wrong abrasive, wrong machine.
- **Serrated knives.** Each scallop needs a tapered rod of the right
  diameter, and the flat side must never be ground.
- **Reshaping a good chisel or plane iron on a dry bench grinder.**
  The next section explains why.

## Testing sharpness, safely

**Do not run a thumb or finger along the edge.** It is the commonest
test and it is how people cut themselves while checking whether they
might cut themselves.

Three tests that do not require touching the apex with soft tissue:

- **The light test.** Look straight down into the edge with a light
  over your shoulder. A properly honed edge has no flat at the apex,
  so there is nothing to reflect, and **it reflects no light**. Any
  bright line or bright dot is a flat spot or a nick. The Forest
  Service ax manual uses exactly this test, and notes that a single
  point of light is usually a ding that will disappear over several
  sharpenings and need not be chased.
- **The fingernail test.** Rest the edge lightly **across** your
  thumbnail, not along it, and try to slide it. A sharp edge bites in
  and stays. A dull one skates. The ax manual gives this as the safer
  alternative to the traditional test of shaving hair off the back of
  the hand, and calls it equally effective.
- **Cutting something.** Paper held by one corner, or a tomato skin,
  or for a woodworking edge a slice of end grain in softwood. If it
  needs a push, it is not sharp.

## Keeping it, which is most of the work

Sharpening is the small part. Nearly all edge life is decided by what
happens in between.

**A steel or ceramic rod is not a sharpener.** A rod realigns an edge
that has rolled over rather than removing metal to make a new one.
That distinction is trade practice rather than a measured
specification, but the working consequence is solid: a rod restores an
edge that is tired, and does nothing for one that is chipped or
genuinely blunt. Ten seconds on a rod before each use, and a real
sharpening when the rod stops helping.

**What ruins edges.** The mechanism in each row is just relative
hardness or repeated impact. The ordering is judgement about ordinary
domestic use, not data.

| Cause | What it does |
|---|---|
| Cutting on glass, stone, ceramic or steel | Chips the apex outright. Glass, stone and ceramic are harder than blade steel; a steel surface usually is not, and there the apex fails against something that simply will not yield |
| Cutting down onto a plate | Same, in miniature, every meal |
| Cutting through bone, staples, nails or wire | One impact, one chip |
| Letting blades touch each other in a drawer | Random nicks along the whole edge |
| Dishwashers | Heat, alkaline detergent and knocking about |
| Rust | Pits the apex, which is the thinnest and most exposed metal |

Cutting boards of wood or soft plastic, and never on a plate, is the
single largest change most people can make. Which board material is
kindest to an edge is trade practice and not something this guide can
source, but the general rule that a surface harder than your steel
will chip your steel is simply hardness.

**Storage** should stop the edge touching anything hard. A sheath, a
block, a magnetic bar, an edge guard. The Forest Service, the Army
and the Navy all say some version of the same thing: protect
sharpened edges at all times, store to protect the cutting edge, coat
metal with light oil for long storage, and never store a saw in a wet
sheath.

**Two conditions worth knowing.** The Army and Navy manual warns that
in very cold weather a chopping tool should not be used before the
blade is warm, because a cold blade is brittle and will break easily.
And a rusty tool is a dull tool, because rust attacks the thin metal
at the apex first.

## Safety

### The injuries sharpening itself causes

Sharpening puts your fingers next to a freshly sharpened edge on
purpose, repeatedly, for an extended period. The four defences, all
from federal manuals:

- **Gloves.** Wear leather gloves throughout, on both hands. The ax
  manual is explicit that this is because the blade becomes
  razor-sharp during the process, and the handtool manual repeats it
  for all filing.
- **A file guard.** A knuckle guard on the file keeps your fingers off
  the edge when the file skips, which it does. Leather, wood, a piece
  of fire hose or old linen hose all work.
- **Secure the tool, free both hands.** Clamp the head to a bench at a
  comfortable height, or wedge it. Holding a tool in one hand and a
  file in the other is how the file slips.
- **Choose the stone that does not put fingertips in the path.** The
  ax manual notes that traditional round pocket stones can be
  dangerous because your fingertips are always in jeopardy, and that
  the version with a finger groove in the middle exists precisely to
  fix that.

### Which way to stroke

**Always cut away from the body.** The Army and Navy manual gives that
rule for knives with a single named exception, the draw knife, which
is designed to be pulled. OSHA's guidance adds the other half: direct
saw blades, knives and other tools away from walkways and away from
anyone working near you.

Sharpening has its own directions, and they are not the same:

- **Filing an axe:** file **into** the edge, because it raises the
  least burr.
- **Using a stone on a blade:** stroke with **the sharp edge
  trailing**, in a circular motion, light even pressure.
- **Stropping:** draw the blade **backwards**, spine first, away from
  the edge.
- **A wet pedal grindstone:** rotate it **toward you and into the
  blade**.

### Power grinders

A bench grinder is the fastest way to reshape a damaged tool and the
fastest way to destroy one. Both federal regulation and the shop
manuals converge on the same short list.

- **Eye protection, always.** The Army machine tool manual calls eye
  protection the most important safety rule in the shop and specifies
  safety glasses with side shields, with goggles worn over
  prescription glasses. The Army and Navy hand tool manual's warning
  on bench grinders is to wear eye protection and watch the fingers,
  and to hold the work firmly so it cannot catch in the wheel. The
  Forest Service adds heavy leather gloves, long sleeves and a leather
  apron against sparks and hot shavings, plus shields and hood guards
  on the grinder itself.
- **Ring test every wheel before you mount it.** Tap it gently with a
  light non-metallic implement. A ringing note means sound. A dull
  note means cracked, and OSHA says a wheel that sounds dead shall not
  be used.
- **Check the speed.** OSHA requires the machine's spindle speed to be
  verified before mounting, so it cannot exceed the maximum operating
  speed marked on the wheel.
- **Set the work rest close.** OSHA sets a maximum opening of one
  eighth of an inch between rest and wheel, to stop work being dragged
  into the gap. The Army manual says one sixteenth to one eighth. The
  adjustable tongue guard at the top must never sit more than one
  quarter of an inch from the wheel.
- **Keep the guards.** OSHA's exposure figures put the open arc of a
  bench or floor stand wheel at not more than 90 degrees, one quarter
  of the periphery, and a portable grinder at not more than 180
  degrees with the top half of the wheel enclosed at all times.
- **Never grind on the flat side of a wheel**, which weakens it and
  can break it, and never clamp a hand-held grinder in a vice.

### Heat: the failure you cannot undo

This is the one that ruins tools permanently, and it is silent while
it happens.

Tool steel is hard because it was **hardened**, heated and quenched,
and then **tempered**, reheated to a lower temperature to take the
brittleness out. Tempering is a one-way trade: the higher the
tempering temperature, the softer the steel becomes. That is the
whole mechanism. A grinding wheel puts heat into a thin edge faster
than the mass of the tool can carry it away, and the edge tempers
itself further, in seconds, with nobody deciding to do it.

**You cannot put it back by cooling it down.** The steel is
permanently softer, and it will not hold an edge again until the
softened metal is ground away.

**The colour tells you.** Heat grows an oxide film on bright steel,
and the film changes colour as it thickens. The colour these two
manuals name, and the only one you need, is **blue**.

The Army machine tool manual, on grinding a drill: overheating turns
the drill edges blue, which indicates the drill's temper has been
lost, and **the blue area must be ground completely away** to get back
to sound steel. The Forest Service ax manual: you can always tell when
the temper is drawn because the colour of the steel at the edge turns
blue, and at minimum you have drawn the temper for at least three
sixteenths of an inch back from the edge.

So blue is not a stain to polish off. It is a map of the metal you now
have to remove.

The Forest Service's advice on grinders is worth quoting in
substance because it is stronger than most people expect. Never use an
electric high-speed dry bench grinder to sharpen an axe, because that
type of grinder will almost certainly draw the temper and ruin it, and
very few people have the skill to avoid it. The grinder it recommends
instead is an old-style pedal grindstone kept wet by a constant
application of water. Failing that, a file and a whetstone, which is
how most quality axe sharpening is done today.

The handtool manual is a little more permissive and gives the
conditions: electric grinders reshape damaged tools quickly but
improper grinding ruins both the bevel and the temper; high speeds
remove metal fast, which can leave the blade weak and prone to
chipping, and they let heat build in the head until the temper is
destroyed and the blade can no longer hold an edge. Its instructions
are to grind slowly, keep the blade cool, inspect frequently so you do
not take off too much, and dip the tool in water or let it cool in
open air.

One trap: the Army manual warns that a drill which has already got too
hot can **crack** when dipped into cold water or coolant. Do not let
it get hot in the first place; cool it early and often rather than
rescuing it late.

### Dust

Dry grinding produces very fine airborne particles of both the metal
and the wheel. The Army machine tool manual's instruction is direct:
some machines have a vacuum dust collector, and when operating a
grinder without one, wear an approved respirator; use coolant whenever
possible, because it also controls dust.

Where the material contains crystalline silica, the dust is a
recognised long-term hazard rather than a nuisance. That is the case
for concrete, stone and masonry, which is what the federal guidance
below was written about, and it is worth assuming for a sandstone
grinding wheel or a quarried natural stone until you know otherwise.
NIOSH sets a
recommended exposure limit for respirable crystalline silica of 0.05
milligrams per cubic metre as a time-weighted average and designates
it a **potential occupational carcinogen**, with lung cancer the
associated site. The listed effects are cough, shortness of breath,
wheezing, decreased lung function and progressive respiratory symptoms
of silicosis, with the eyes and respiratory system as target organs.

The federal engineering answer for silica dust from a handheld grinder
is one of two things: **water applied continuously to the wheel**, or
**a shroud with vacuum dust collection**. OSHA also says not to clean
up with compressed air, because it puts the dust back in the air, and
to clear slurry before it dries out and becomes dust again.

There is a happy coincidence here. Working a stone wet, which is what
the oil or water on a sharpening stone is for anyway, is also the dust
control. Keep the stone wet and you have very little to breathe.

### If you do cut yourself

From the National Library of Medicine's public first aid guidance:

- Wash your hands, then wash the cut thoroughly with mild soap and
  water. Use direct pressure to stop the bleeding. Apply an
  antibacterial ointment and a clean non-stick bandage.
- For a puncture, rinse for 5 minutes under running water, then wash
  with soap. Look for objects in the wound but do not poke around, and
  if something is stuck in there do not pull it out.
- **Call emergency services** if the bleeding is severe or will not
  stop, for example after 10 minutes of pressure, if the area has lost
  feeling or does not work properly, or if the person is otherwise
  seriously hurt.
- **Contact a healthcare provider right away** if the wound is more
  than a quarter inch, 0.64 centimetre, deep, is on the face, or
  reaches bone, because stitches may be needed; if it was caused by a
  rusty object or a fishhook; if debris is stuck in it; if it shows
  signs of infection such as warmth, redness, throbbing, fever,
  swelling, a red streak spreading from the wound, or pus-like
  drainage; or if you have not had a tetanus shot in the last 10
  years. A tetanus vaccine is generally recommended every 10 years.

### One legal note if you employ anyone

Federal regulation makes each employer responsible for the safe
condition of tools and equipment used by employees, **including tools
and equipment furnished by the employees themselves**. A worker's own
dull, mushroomed or cracked tool is still the employer's
responsibility. OSHA's guidance adds the related rules: impact tools
such as chisels, wedges and drift pins must be kept free of mushroomed
heads, wooden handles must not be splintered, cracked saw blades must
be taken out of service, and wrenches with sprung jaws must not be
used.

## You own this when

- You can say out loud that a dull tool takes more force, and that
  more force is what makes a slip dangerous.
- You think of sharpening as removing metal until two surfaces meet,
  not as adding something to a blade.
- You do not stop working a side until you can feel a burr along its
  entire length, and you know that no burr means not finished.
- You pick the coarse stone when the edge is damaged, instead of doing
  four hundred more strokes on a fine one.
- You know that stropping finishes an edge and never makes one.
- You test with light and a fingernail, never with a fingertip along
  the edge.
- You have never seen blue on an edge you ground, because you cooled
  it before it got there.
- Your edges are stored where nothing hard can touch them, and you cut
  on wood, not on a plate.

When those are true you can put a working edge on almost anything,
which is one of the few skills that makes every other physical skill
cheaper and safer at the same time. The underlying habit transfers
well past blades: find the signal that tells you the job is actually
done, trust that signal instead of elapsed effort, and stop when it
appears.

### What could not be sourced, and is labelled in the text

Several things in this guide are trade practice rather than measured
or specified values, and each says so where it appears:

- **Bevel angles for kitchen knives, chisels and plane irons.** The
  figures everyone quotes, roughly 15 to 20 degrees per side for a
  kitchen knife and 25 to 30 degrees for a chisel, do not appear in
  any federal publication found for this guide. The sourced angles in
  the table are the axe, the hoe, the shovel and the drill.
- **That a steel or ceramic rod realigns an edge rather than removing
  metal.** Universally stated by makers and cooks, not found as a
  measured claim in any authority cited here.
- **Grit progressions.** The definition of a grit number as a sieve
  mesh count is sourced. The specific ladders people recommend are
  convention.
- **Which cutting board material is kindest to an edge.** The general
  rule that a surface harder than the blade will chip the blade is
  just hardness, but the ranking of boards is not sourced here.
- **The full temper-colour sequence.** Straw, brown and purple before
  blue are commonly quoted and are not in any source opened for this
  guide. Blue is, from two of them, and blue is the one that matters.
- **Angle guides, hardpoint saws and serrated knives.** The
  suggestion of an angle guide or a folded card under the spine, the
  statement that a hardpoint handsaw is a consumable, and the method
  for serrations are all ordinary shop knowledge, labelled as such in
  the text.

One further limitation is worth stating plainly rather than hiding in
a footnote: **no study was found that directly compares injury rates
between sharp and dull blades.** The force difference is measured, the
hazard statement is the federal regulator's, and the step between them
is mechanical reasoning. Nothing in this guide depends on that step
being more than reasoning.

### A note on what is not cited

Knife retailers, sharpening-equipment makers and woodworking
publications carry a very large amount of sharpening advice, some of
it excellent. None of them is an authority on an angle, a temperature,
or an injury statistic, and where a number here exists only in that
literature it has been left out or labelled as trade practice.
Nothing in this guide rests on a vendor's claim about a product.
